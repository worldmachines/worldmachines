Dating the Steppe's Eclipse: A Chaffetz Check on the Modernity Machine

Commons · Entities · updated 23 September 2026 · source on GitHub
David Chaffetz's Raiders, Rulers, and Traders argues that two of the Modernity Machine's ~1600 moving parts, the final eclipse of steppe military power and the Silk Road's obsolescence, are dated one to three centuries too early; this page records the friction and three ways to read it.

Extends modernity-machine with material from chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders.

Dating Check: The Steppe's Eclipse and the Silk Road's End (Chaffetz)

The "Moving Parts Turned On (~1600, per Rao 2025)" list on modernity-machine includes "Final eclipse of steppe nomad military power" and, under "Moving Parts Turned Off," "The Silk Road (effective obsolescence)." David Chaffetz's Raiders, Rulers, and Traders (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders) is a book-length argument that both items are dated one to three centuries too early, and it is worth recording the friction rather than smoothing it.

On the steppe's eclipse. In Chaffetz's account the decisive subordination of the horse-breeding peoples happens in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, not around 1600, and it is done by two land empires rather than by gunpowder as such. chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch10-empires-strike-back argues the Qing succeeded where the Tang, Song and Ming had all failed not through superior weaponry but through accumulated scale — a population risen from fifty to two hundred million, a bureaucracy capable of running Mongol famine relief fifty-five times in one reign, and multi-year provisioning at Hami and Turfan that the book ranks above Louis XIV's or Napoleon's contemporary European logistics. Where patronage failed, the answer was extermination: the Oirat campaigns killed over a million by smallpox, massacre and enslavement. Russia's half of the story is a matter of absorption over two centuries — Cossacks modeled on the Kazakh and Tatar pattern, the Crimean khanate destroyed, the Kalmyk flight of 1771.

The evidence that steppe cavalry remained decisive well past 1600 is specific. Nader Shah takes Delhi in 1739 with horse power (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch09-riding-the-whirlwind). The second Battle of Panipat in 1761 turns on horseflesh — Ahmad Shah's Turkmen mounts running down the Marathas' lighter Kathiawar and Arab stock. Ranjit Singh's Sikh Empire, founded in the early nineteenth century, is described as consciously the last cavalry-founded state "in India—and, indeed, the world." Qing bannermen crushed Yakub Beg's Xinjiang revolt with undiminished effectiveness in the 1870s, having lost to European rifles at Tianjin in 1860 only where marshy coastal terrain denied them room to manoeuvre (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch11-the-great-game). The formal closure Chaffetz names is the 1881 Treaty of St Petersburg — the first border ever drawn across the steppe — and Skobelev's destruction of the Turkmen the same year.

On the Silk Road. chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch05-silk-road-or-horse-road argues the route's dominant commodity was never silk, and the-horse-road collects the case: the Tang spending ten percent of the state budget on imported horses, Indian courts importing up to seventy thousand a year, silk functioning as a currency rather than a cargo because China lacked monetary silver until the sixteenth century. On that reading the trade did not become obsolete around 1600 at all. It ran on through the Mughal supply chain via Kabul and Kandahar, was the object Britain went looking for when Moorcroft rode to Bukhara in 1819, and closed only under twentieth-century border regimes — after which Central Asia went from wealthy emporium to one of the most isolated regions on earth (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch12-last-horse-powers, chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-epilogue).

How to hold this. Three readings are available and the modernity-machine page should not pick one prematurely. (a) The ~1600 list is Eurocentric in a way the dawn-day-dusk-machine-typology page already flags as a gap ("no engagement with non-Eurocentric machine periodizations"), and the dates describe when these things stopped mattering to Europe. (b) The list marks when the seeds of eclipse were planted — Qing and Russian demographic and administrative buildout does begin around then — with the harvest arriving over the following two centuries, which is entirely consistent with how this framework treats machine construction elsewhere. (c) The list is simply wrong on these two items, and a machine periodization that gets the end of steppe military power wrong by two hundred and fifty years has a real calibration problem.

Reading (b) is the most charitable and probably the most accurate, but it should be stated rather than assumed, because it changes what the list is claiming. And it is worth noticing that Chaffetz's own decisive break — Britain in India winning paramountcy with money and diplomacy rather than horseflesh, five hundred cavalry against hundreds of thousands (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch11-the-great-game) — is a Modernity Machine victory in that page's own terms: fiscal-administrative legibility substituting for a material resource. The MM did end the horse's political era. It just took until the nineteenth century to do it.
