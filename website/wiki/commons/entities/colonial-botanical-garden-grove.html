The Colonial Botanical Garden (Grove)

Commons · Entities · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Grove's colonial botanical garden: an institution that was at once a re-created Paradise, a medical and commercial station, a symbol and instrument of control over global nature, and a node in a network that moved plants one way and environmental ideas the other, from Leiden and the Cape through Pamplemousses and St Vincent to Calcutta.

Source: grove-green-imperialism. In the Conclusion Grove calls the colonial garden the basis of “a new kind of learning, information collecting and networking,” and describes gardens generally “as symbolic texts, centres of calculation and repositories of information and expertise” (grove-green-imperialism-conclusion).

Paradise and control at once

The old Zoroastrian Pairidaeza was gradually transmuted into more “rigorously botanical or experimental concepts” along a Sanskrit–Arab–Venetian route (grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s1-early-conservation). The apothecaries' gardens of Padua, Pisa, Leiden and Heidelberg tracked the growth of oceanic trade, and their spread was “an accurate indicator in microcosm” of Europe's expanding knowledge of the global environment. Grove reads them on two registers: “re-creations of Paradise” and “landscape 'texts'” signifying “a particular type of ecological control that had not previously been available” (grove-green-imperialism-ch02-s1-south-west-india). The 1654 Cape Company Garden's collections were “symbolic of the degree of economic or colonised control which was being acquired over the global environment” (grove-green-imperialism-ch02-s3-botanical-networks). The Dutch put the idea into heraldry, a lion above a walled garden (grove-green-imperialism-ch03-s2-dutch-cape-mauritius). At the Cape a “planned Eden could be constructed far more comprehensively than in Europe” (grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s5-aesthetic-transitions).

Never single-purpose

On Mauritius Grove braids three motives: commercial and subsistence (Labourdonnais's manioc as slave food), Edenic, and medical-academic (Aublet's garden from 1753). Only by holding all three does the garden's role as an information network make sense (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s2-labourdonnais-anxieties). The Calcutta garden began as Kyd's 1776 famine-relief proposal to spread drought-resistant sago “to every village.” It survived only once he recast it as a commercial asset (grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s4-famine-calcutta-garden). Saharanpur and Sibpur were founded on or revived from Mughal garden sites, and Bishop Heber found Sibpur answering “Milton's idea of Paradise” (grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s2-roxburgh-1).

A portable form and a network

  • The Leiden model was reproduced at Paris, Oxford and Edinburgh; Grove credits Leiden's unusual religious tolerance (grove-green-imperialism-ch02-s3-botanical-networks).
  • The Cape garden was institutionalised by the 1676 appointment of a medical botanist, which Grove dates sixty years ahead of the French on Mauritius and over a century ahead of the British in India. Its influence on French and British practice was “out of all proportion to its small area” (grove-green-imperialism-ch03-s4-het-paradijs). Poivre saw its free seed and instruction for settlers in 1749 and carried the template to Mauritius (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s4-poivre-origins).
  • Kingstown, St Vincent (1765), the first botanical garden in the Americas, came out of Society of Arts prizes (grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s1-caribbean-forests). Grove treats it and Pamplemousses as “elements of a single institutional construct or network” (grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s2-soame-jenyns). Under Anderson it became a local “centre of calculation” doing “autonomous intellectual work” (grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s4-anderson-advocacy).
  • The Calcutta–St Vincent axis, via St Helena, moved plants west to east and ideas east to west. By 1790 about fifteen men ran the empire's tropical gardens, within a cadre of some 130 collectors and superintendents between 1770 and 1820. Grove's gloss is that “environmental centres of calculation” were “succeeded by an information network” (grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s4-famine-calcutta-garden).
  • Banks recommended German-trained naturalists to the East India Company (grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s3-forster-st-helena). In the 1840s Gibson ran the Dapoori garden while corresponding with Hooker at Kew (grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s3-lobbying).

Fragile and double-edged

The network was precarious. After 1830 a Retrenchment Committee recommended cutting Calcutta's funding, and the Hookers' lobbying, not any economic case, kept the Indian gardens alive (grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s2-roxburgh-2). Linnaean classification, which Linnaeus himself compared to a map, is presented as heir to the Renaissance ambition of reassembling the world's flora in one place (grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s1-banks-professionalisation). Grove's twist is that the same infrastructure armed critics as well as administrators: the garden men were often the people who diagnosed the damage their employers were doing (grove-green-imperialism-conclusion).

World Machines resonances, with their hedges

The notes read the garden network as information infrastructure of the modernity-machine and as a case of legibility-as-machine-core. Each adds that Grove's gardens are also paradisal, prestige and medical institutions, and that knowledge often flowed from periphery to centre rather than top-down (grove-green-imperialism-ch02-s1-south-west-india, grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s4-famine-calcutta-garden). The “centres of calculation” vocabulary is Latour's (bruno-latour), borrowed by Grove. The notes also compare the garden as epistemic device with the-english-garden-as-epistemic-object only loosely: Grove's gardens are tropical acclimatisation and medical stations, not the English landscape garden (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s2-labourdonnais-anxieties).
