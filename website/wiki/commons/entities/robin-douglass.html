Robin Douglass

Commons · Entities · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Robin Douglass, author of Rousseau and Hobbes (2015), whose method pairs historical contextualism (the French and Genevan reception of Hobbes) with philosophical reconstruction of Rousseau's thought, and who reads Rousseau as neither Hobbesian nor simply anti-Hobbesian.

Robin Douglass is the author of Rousseau and Hobbes: Nature, Free Will, and the Passions (Oxford University Press, 2015), summarized at douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes. This page records only what the reading notes show about his method and his positions. It contains no biography.

Method

  • Historical contextualism. Douglass holds that scholarship on Rousseau and Hobbes has proceeded at a "predominantly theoretical and ahistorical level". He sets out to reconstruct the eighteenth-century French and Genevan reception of Hobbes, which he calls "strikingly absent from existing scholarship" (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-french-reception-opening). He places himself in the line of Skinner's remark that Rousseau's reading of De cive is under-researched (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-hobbes-and-rousseau).
  • Reception, not derivation. He declines to say what Rousseau took from Hobbes and asks what Rousseau thought was at stake in targeting him. Resemblance is not derivation "unless otherwise stated" (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-methodological-problems).
  • Philosophical reconstruction. He also argues for the coherence of Rousseau's thought around three themes (nature, free will, the passions) while rejecting a "mythology of coherence" (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-nature-free-will-passions, douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-methodological-problems).
  • Selective, without a grand narrative. His reception history follows "threads" leading to Rousseau. It defines "French" by language and covers only thinkers Rousseau named or could hardly have missed (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-french-reception-opening).
  • Evidential caution. He argues probabilistically that Rousseau knew De cive, probably in Sorbière's translation, and places the burden of proof on anyone claiming Rousseau read Leviathan (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-methodological-problems, douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-engagement-in-context).

Stance

  • Hobbes versus Hobbism. His guiding distinction is between Hobbes's texts and the French caricature. He repeatedly corrects the caricature: for example, the conflation of Hobbes's justice with his laws of nature, and the "straw Hobbes" of positivism (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-diderot-encyclopedie, douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-law-nature-denaturing). See hobbism-douglass.
  • Neither a Hobbesian nor an anti-Hobbesian Rousseau. He disputes the Strauss, Derathé, Davy and Jouvenel readings, and reverses Tuck's method by using Hobbes to understand Rousseau (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-hobbes-and-rousseau, douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-engagement-in-context).
  • Against proto-Kantian readings. He reads Rousseau's free will as conscious choice among inclinations, not Kantian self-legislation. He concedes openly that this is a weaker notion of autonomy (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-free-will-and-virtue, douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-oppositions-and-affinities).
  • Interpretive middle positions. On private versus general will he places himself "somewhere in-between" the pluralistic and total-unity readings (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-freedom-preserved). He sets aside the debate over whether civil religion is totalitarian (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-unity-civil-religion).
  • A sober verdict. He concludes that Rousseau's republic was unsuitable for "the children of Adam", against Hanley's "moderate and modern" Rousseau, while keeping Rousseau's critique of commercial society live (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-republics-naturally-good).

Douglass is not a machine theorist. Any World Machines use of his work is a reader's extension, not his claim.
