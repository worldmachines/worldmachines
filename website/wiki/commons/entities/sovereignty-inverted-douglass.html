Sovereignty inverted (Douglass)

Commons · Entities · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Robin Douglass's thesis that Rousseau kept Hobbesian absolute, rights-free sovereignty and the body-politic vocabulary but inverted Hobbes by lodging sovereignty inalienably and unrepresentably in the people's general will, reaching republican conclusions (law above man) through natural-law tools and a theory of the will grounded in free will.

"Sovereignty inverted" names robin-douglass's thesis that jean-jacques-rousseau's political problems in Du contrat social were Hobbesian but his answer radically inverted Hobbes's (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes). Rousseau keeps what is most Hobbesian about sovereignty and moves its seat permanently to the people. In doing so he turns the modern discourse of sovereignty and contract, "so often used to justify the submission of the people", toward classically republican ends (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-sovereignty-inverted). Jouvenel's phrase for Rousseau's theory, "Hobbism turned inside out", is one of the Hobbesian readings Douglass reports and qualifies (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-hobbes-and-rousseau).

What Rousseau keeps from Hobbes

  • Total alienation to the whole. The single clause of the contract is the total alienation of each associate, with all his rights, to the whole community. Douglass says Rousseau goes beyond Hobbes by denying even a right of self-preservation against the sovereign.
  • No rights against the sovereign. Rousseau is, for Douglass, one of the few who grasped Hobbes's real worry: that private judgements of good and evil, appeals to conscience included, dissolve the commonwealth. Barbeyrac and other critics missed or evaded this point.
  • The body-politic vocabulary and the citizen/subject double relation, which Douglass traces from Grotius through its full development in Hobbes's De cive to Pufendorf and Rousseau (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-sovereignty-inverted).
  • Unity as the problem. Both thinkers held that the sovereign will must be absolute and incontestable and that religion threatens unity (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-sovereignty-and-law-opening, douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-unity-civil-religion).

What Rousseau inverts

  • Inalienability. Sovereignty is "only the exercise of the general will". Power can be transferred but will cannot. Hobbes and the whole natural-law tradition let the people bind its future will by instituting a superior who speaks for it (Pufendorf's second pact; Sorbière's De cive). Rousseau denies that will can be transferred, alienated or represented. Douglass adds two limits. The argument applies only to contracts that transfer will. And it concerns the freedom of the people as a collective body, not of individual citizens (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-sovereignty-inverted).
  • Indivisibility with a division of powers. Sovereignty, the moral power, is only the ratifying of general laws. Government, the physical power, executes them. Rousseau's sovereign is "absolute in power but limited in the form of its actions". Douglass calls the response largely definitional (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-sovereignty-inverted, douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-unity-civil-religion). In the conclusion he calls the resulting division of legislative and executive powers "more Lockean" (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-oppositions-and-affinities).
  • No representative sovereignty. The people can be represented only by all citizens freely assembled. Any Hobbes-style representative sovereignty is an illegitimate alienation (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-oppositions-and-affinities).

Why the inversion rests on free will

The hinge is the moral/physical distinction. Force is physical and yields only necessity, not obligation. Freedom, as a gift of nature grounded in free will, cannot be alienated without removing morality from one's actions. Rousseau took this criticism of Hobbes from Pufendorf-style natural lawyers and turned it against them: their two-pact theories also left the people dependent on a superior will, so they were no better than Hobbes (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-free-will-slavery-obligation, douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-sovereignty-and-law-opening). The inversion "undercuts the main distinctions between Hobbes and his natural law critics" (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-unity-civil-religion).

From the collective to the citizen

Collective inalienability is necessary but not sufficient. Individual freedom is preserved by equal legislative right ("each citizen gives himself to all, he gives himself to no one") and by the general will being each citizen's own will as citizen. Obedience to law is then dependence on things, not on men, which is Rousseau's formula for placing law above man. Douglass admits that Du contrat social only partly solves the conflict between private and general wills. The rest falls to the legislator and the cultivation of love of fatherland (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-freedom-preserved; see love-and-fear-douglass).

The origins of the general will

Rousseau's general will took shape against Diderot's. Diderot's "Droit naturel" offered a universal general will of the human race against a Hobbesian "violent interlocutor". Already in the Discours sur l'économie politique Rousseau confines the general will to particular bodies politic (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-diderot-encyclopedie). In the Manuscrit de Genève he faults Diderot for giving the independent man no reason to be just (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-state-of-nature-to-political-society).

Republican ends, natural-law means

Placing law above men, defining liberty as freedom from dependence on others' wills, and keeping legislative right with citizens are republican conclusions. Rousseau reached them through the problems of modern natural law (sovereignty, will, contract). Douglass suggests this suited Geneva, where natural-law arguments were current, and treats the two traditions as complementary in Rousseau, not opposed (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-sovereignty-inverted).

Resonance (hedged)

A single public person with one will, made by the act of association, is a design for making a people one. The notes compare it only loosely to the legibility ambitions of the modernity-machine, and Douglass makes no such claim. See making-a-people-one-douglass.
