Richard H. Grove

Commons · Entities · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Richard H. Grove, the environmental historian whose Green Imperialism relocates the origins of Western environmentalism from the metropole to the colonial periphery, working through named institutional chains of surgeons, gardens and despatches and arguing against several standard historiographies.

Author of Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion, Tropical Island Edens and the Origins of Environmentalism, 1600–1860 (1995); see grove-green-imperialism. This page records only what the reading notes support about his argument, method and stance.

The argument

Grove's thesis is that a scientific critique of “development” and a programme of state conservation were fully formed by the early nineteenth century, and that they came from the colonial periphery: from small tropical islands where degradation was visible within decades, and from the company surgeons and botanical gardens that carried the lesson to the Caribbean and India (grove-green-imperialism-introduction, grove-green-imperialism-conclusion). He names his target directly. The Anglo-American pantheon of Marsh, Thoreau and Roosevelt has obscured “the central significance of the colonial experience in the formation of western environmental attitudes and critiques” (grove-green-imperialism-introduction).

Method

  • Discourse construction, not origin-hunting. He refuses to identify “the earliest instances of consciously conservationist interventions,” because such practices cannot be separated from ordinary resource arrangements. He traces how the Western discourse was built (grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s1-early-conservation).
  • Named chains. His characteristic unit is a traceable relay of individuals and institutions: Hales to Duhamel du Monceau to Poivre; Forster to Sprengel to Humboldt; Hooker meeting Dalhousie on the voyage out (grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s6-dalhousie). The Introduction note contrasts this with C. A. Bayly's broader global political economy (grove-green-imperialism-introduction).
  • Environmental history as the frame. He proposes environmental history, rather than conventional history of science or colonial-discourse analysis, as the right frame for contact between Asian and European natural knowledge, and borrows Latour's “centres of calculation” (grove-green-imperialism-ch02-s1-south-west-india; compare bruno-latour). He extends the phrase himself: centres of calculation “succeeded by an information network” (grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s4-famine-calcutta-garden).
  • Risk. In the Conclusion he invokes Mary Douglas and The Tempest: the colonial scientist's authority came from interpreting risk in an unfamiliar environment and among an unfamiliar population at once (grove-green-imperialism-conclusion).

Stances he argues against

  • “Judaeo-Christian attitudes are inherently destructive.” He attributes rapid ecological change to colonising capital instead (grove-green-imperialism-introduction).
  • Monolithic ecological imperialism. The colonial state was “heterogeneous and ambivalent” and open to scientific lobbying (grove-green-imperialism-introduction).
  • Enlightenment and Romanticism as the decisive turn. He treats these as minority labels and places the transition in a longer seventeenth-century process (grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s5-aesthetic-transitions). He also rejects the reading of mechanistic science as simply anti-ecological, since Hales turned it into a critique of deforestation.
  • Single-centre world-system diffusion. He qualifies Wallerstein as Eurocentric, pointing to Tokugawa forest conservation and to autonomous change in India and China (grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s6-ecological-pressures).
  • Rousseau as a mere “pre-environmentalist”. He argues against LaFreniere that Rousseau was tied more directly to a practical conservation programme than recognised (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s7-defoe-rousseau-commerson).
  • “Merrie India”. He calls the picture of pre-British India as an ecological golden age of common property “inherently Orientalist in the Saidian sense” (grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s1-company-forests-1). He argues that pre-colonial forest practice supplied colonial forestry with “models (rather than just a legitimation)” (grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s6-dalhousie).
  • Marsh as founder. Man and Nature reinforced rather than founded the colonial desiccationist consensus (grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s7-wider-perspective).

He states his own synthesis in his own voice: early environmentalism was not Newtonian reductionism suppressing a Rousseauist harmony with nature but their fusion, driven by anxiety about the moral state of Western society and about humanity's capacity to destroy nature and climate (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s11-revolution-global-context).

How the World Machines notes read him

The notes read many of his cases as instances of Legibility and especially of legibility-as-precondition-not-cause, and they say each time that the frame is theirs, not his. He does not write machine theory.
