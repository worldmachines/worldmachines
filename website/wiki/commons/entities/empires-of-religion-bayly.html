Empires of Religion (Bayly)

Commons · Entities · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Bayly's argument that the nineteenth century saw not religious decline but a reorganized expansion in which world religions centralized authority, fixed canons, rode print and steam, policed syncretism and came to resemble nation-states, while heterodoxy multiplied beneath them.

“Empires of Religion” is the title of chapter 9 of bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world. It names Bayly's counter-claim to the secularization thesis. After 1815 the great world religions underwent a “triumphal reemergence and expansion”. They transformed doctrine, organization and authority on a global, mutually entangled scale, using the same technologies of print, travel and bureaucracy as the modern state (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-new-style-religion). Chapter 8 sets this up by arguing that competing global religious systems did more to shape everyday mental life than the secular doctrines did (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch08-righteous-republics).

Against decline

  • The secularization thesis as artifact. Bayly traces it from Voltaire and Gibbon through Mill, Marxist “false consciousness”, Weber's disenchanted world and Chadwick's “secularisation of the European mind”. The consensus was pervasive enough that even anti-colonial Marxist historians largely ignored religion (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-new-style-religion).
  • Tactical retreat. Religious authorities gave up indefensible ground, such as disestablishment and the end of religious tests, “in order to conquer new areas of cultural and social life”. Secularization “was the fly in the wheel, not the wheel itself” (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-new-style-religion). Disestablishment acted as a tonic, freeing clergy to become “guardians of the poor” (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-imperial-religions).

How the empires were built

  • Formalizing authority. Religions answered the “ancien régime in religion”, with its tangled jurisdictions, and the crisis of 1780–1820 by seeking clearer lines of authority. Examples include Papal Infallibility (1870), the Anglican Lambeth conferences, and Sunni curricula and the al-Azhar network (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-imperial-religions).
  • Inventing bounded religions. Missionaries such as James Legge and reformers such as Kang Youwei and Swami Dayananda treated diffuse bodies of text and practice as bounded “religions” comparable to Christianity. Confucius became an “uncrowned king”. Before 1780, Hinduism had been at best “a huge extended family” of beliefs (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-imperial-religions).
  • Uniformity pushed down and out. Authorities disciplined Sufi orders, monkhoods and other internal currents, while missions and pilgrimage pushed into “decentralized spirituality” (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-new-style-religion).
  • Print, pilgrimage and building. Print did for doctrine what benedict-anderson said it did for the nation. Print first “allowed Hinduism to be arrayed in textual form in one single bookcase”. Steam made the Hajj a mass institution. Mosque, church and temple architecture converged on a few canonical styles that could be exported (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-pilgrimage-print-nation).
  • Agency from below and from the periphery. Schoolmasters, clerks and women did much of the work. Krio pastors, not British ones, evangelized West Africa, and Maori chiefs converted “on their own terms”. Following Susan Bayly, the question becomes how Christianity itself “was converted” (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-new-style-religion).

Mirroring the nation-state

Reformed world religions “spawned larger bureaucracies” and policed syncretism the way states policed dual loyalty (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-imperial-religions). Vivekananda's capture of the 1893 World Parliament of Religions for Indian nationhood is the clearest fusion of empire of religion and nationalism (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch06c-new-imperialism-and-internationalism). By 1914 the Catholic hierarchy, Ottoman-sponsored pan-Islamism and the Hindu Aryan Society came to resemble “states and empires”. Uniform doctrinal claims sharpened boundaries against Jewish, Armenian and other minorities, which Bayly treats as groundwork for later violence but not as making it inevitable (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch13-uniformity-complexity-and-the-road-to-1914).

Never complete

The same print and travel infrastructure let heterodoxy spread: Babi-Bahai universalism, the Ahmadiyya, theosophy, spiritualism and messianic revolts. Against Weber, rationalization reached only “the small international middle class” (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-pilgrimage-print-nation). Spirit cults, witchcraft and millenarian movements persisted or expanded (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-new-style-religion, bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch13-uniformity-complexity-and-the-road-to-1914). The irony runs in the arts too: religion's expansion as formal doctrine came with its withdrawal from art, while state and market took on a quasi-religious aura (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch10-arts-politics-and-hybridity).

World Machines resonances, with their hedges

  • legibility-as-machine-core. Making doctrines, congregations and jurisdictions countable and bounded resonates strongly, carried from the state into religion. But Bayly's religious authorities pursue doctrinal purity and salvation, not administrative control as such. The technologies and the uniformizing effect rhyme; the aims differ (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-new-style-religion, bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-imperial-religions).
  • imagined-communities. Chapter 9 is in effect the religious half of Anderson's story: the imagined community here is confessional as much as national (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-imperial-religions).
  • Where the rhyme stops. Heterodox movements do not so much resist legibility as adopt its idiom for dissenting purposes (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-pilgrimage-print-nation).
