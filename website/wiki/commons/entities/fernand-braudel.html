Fernand Braudel

Commons · Entities · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
French historian (1902–1985) of the Annales school whose work on long-duration (longue durée) historical temporalities and the coupling of human societies to non-human machines (grain, climate, geography) is a key empirical illustration in Bryant's temporal topology.

French historian (1902–1985), central figure of the Annales school. His major works — The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II (1949) and The Structures of Everyday Life (1981, vol. 1 of Civilization and Capitalism) — develop a multi-temporal historiography that distinguishes the short term (events), medium term (conjunctures), and long term (longue durée: geological, climatic, and structural time).

The Longue Durée

Braudel's most influential concept: history operates at multiple timescales simultaneously. The history of events (battles, reigns, crises) unfolds on the short-term scale of years and decades. But beneath it are structural temporalities — climate, geography, agricultural rhythms, demographic patterns — that change across centuries and millennia, and which shape the possibilities within which short-term events occur.

This is the empirical, historical correlate of Bryant's temporal topology: not all historical machines operate on the same timescale, and the mismatches between them are crucial to understanding historical outcomes.

Grain Temporalities and Social Organization

Bryant's most extended use of Braudel in Ch. 6 of Onto-Cartography concerns the temporal rhythms of wheat and rice (Structures of Everyday Life, 1981: 108–57):

Wheat (one harvest per year, disease-vulnerable):

  • Structures European societies around intense planting/harvest periods with long fallow intervals
  • Necessitates food preservation technologies (granaries, salting, fermentation)
  • Enables family-farm agriculture because large labor forces are not required
  • Produces a seasonal rhythm of intense labor followed by relative leisure
  • Leaves time for individual crafts and cottage industries

Rice (two to three harvests per year, labor-intensive):

  • Requires large collective labor forces for planting and harvest
  • Pushes societies toward collective farming organization
  • Generates stratification: a priestly or noble class that tracks rice reserves and distributes them
  • Leaves farmers with little time for non-agricultural activities
  • Produces surplus that enables denser social stratification

The temporal rhythm of a grain is a machine that structures social organization — not metaphorically but materially. Grain temporality is a slow machine to which human social machines become structurally coupled.

Braudel and Temporal Mismatch

The Braudel example illustrates Bryant's point about temporal mismatch: human institutions and practices (family farming, class structure, religious calendars, seasonal festivals) are shaped by the temporal machines of agricultural ecology, climate, and geography. Understanding why European and Asian societies organized differently requires attending to these non-human temporal machines rather than seeking explanations solely at the level of culture or ideology.

Key Works

  • The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II (1949)
  • The Structures of Everyday Life: Civilization and Capitalism, Vol. I (1981)
  • The Wheels of Commerce: Civilization and Capitalism, Vol. II (1982)
  • The Perspective of the World: Civilization and Capitalism, Vol. III (1984)

The Structures of Everyday Life: three economies, not one

The first volume of Civilization and Capitalism has its own canon page, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1. Its origin story matters for reading Braudel as a whole. A 1952 commission from Lucien Febvre for a short summary of pre-industrial European economic history grew into an "interminable venture" once direct research made Braudel doubt the classical account (Sombart, Kulischer and the economists generally). That account treats "the economy" as a single, statistics-ready reality moving toward the market, the firm and capitalist investment until the Industrial Revolution "neatly divides human history in two" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-introduction).

In its place Braudel proposes three coexisting, partly antagonistic strata. Material life is an "infra-economy" of routine, self-sufficiency and barter within a small radius. The market economy is the "transparent" layer that economic science was built to observe. Capitalism is a "shadowy zone" of privileged actors, monopoly and action at a distance. Braudel expected the separation of capitalism from the market economy to be the book's most controversial claim. The scheme organizes the trilogy: Volume I weighs material life, Volume II (The Wheels of Commerce) separates the market economy from capitalism, and Volume III (The Perspective of the World) is the chronological history built on both (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-introduction, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion). See braudel-three-storey-house.

Volume I's organizing question is its subtitle, the limits of the possible. This is a "border which in every age ... separates the possible from the impossible," set by food supply, population pressure, low labour productivity and weak control over nature. Between the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries it "hardly changed at all" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-preface). See limits-of-the-possible.

The longue durée as practised in Volume I

Volume I shows the multi-temporal method described above at work on everyday life.

  • Repetition makes structure. Reducing the scale of observation moves attention from "History with a capital letter" down into material life. There, a happening that is merely repeated becomes "a generality or rather a structure" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-preface). The Conclusion calls anecdotes (Maximilian eating with his fingers, the bandit Cartouche choosing wine over coffee at his execution) "the dust of history," drawing on Georges Gurvitch's micro-sociology. Each is trivial alone, but "by indefinite repetition" they form "linked chains." Such series supply "horizons and vanishing-points" and behave as near-constants. Braudel quotes Georges Lefebvre: "'A law' ... 'is a constant'" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion).
  • Long term plus world scale. Comparison runs through the long term and the dialectic of past and present, and across as wide a space as possible. Braudel names debts for Islam (a decade in Algiers), the Americas (four years in Brazil), Japan, China and India (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-introduction). The aim was "if not to see everything, at least to locate everything, and on the requisite world scale" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion).
  • The present as evidence. After the 1973–4 crisis, barter, moonlighting and home work resurfaced at a scale some economists put at 30–40% of GNP. Braudel reads this as proof that material life is a permanent substrate, not a pre-industrial relic (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-introduction). He read the 1970s oil shock as another encounter with the "ceiling of the possible" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch06-transport-and-history-of-technology). A childhood memory of a Lorraine village (a mill wheel, a house rebuilt in 1806, hemp retted in the stream) becomes evidence that the material past persists as residue, disappearing "slowly, and never in the same manner" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion).
  • Plateaus and bursts. The long term is not smooth. Technology "gradually reaches new forms of equilibrium on higher levels than in the past, only to remain there for a long time": first the accelerator, then the brake (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch06-transport-and-history-of-technology). The energy chapter borrows Pierre Léon's formula: "first came evolution (a slow rise) and then revolution (an acceleration): two connected movements" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch05-wind-water-wood-coal).

Braudel's own reading of wheat and rice

The grain section above follows Bryant's use of Braudel. The Volume I notes add Braudel's own terms, which put more weight on yields, land and the state.

  • Plants of civilization. Wheat, rice and maize are choices made once, early and almost irreversibly. Everything or almost everything "would thereafter depend" on them, and they produce "almost ineradicable structures" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-wheat-cultivation-and-yields).
  • Wheat as a locked system. Wheat was never grown alone. It sat in a rotation-livestock-manure complex that Ferdinand Lot called "so firmly cemented together that no fissure was possible," and it yielded about 5:1 (4:1 net of seed) for three centuries (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-wheat-cultivation-and-yields).
  • Rice as the harder twin. A hectare of paddy yields roughly five times the calories of a hectare of wheat: about 7,350,000 against 1,500,000, and 340,000 for meat. So Far Eastern vegetarianism "certainly does not spring from idealism." But rice is "an even more tyrannical and enslaving crop than wheat." The paddy needed a system "firmly integrated and supervised from above," and the extent of rice fields tracked state control and the concentration of villages. Paddy agriculture also led China to largely give up its highlands (no pastoralism, dairy or forestry), a trade-off against Europe's mixed arable and pasture (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-rice).
  • Double harvests arrived slowly. Early-ripening Champa rice was imported around the start of the eleventh century. It spread province by province and became a settled double-harvest system only by the thirteenth century, behind the great demographic expansion of southern China (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-rice).
  • Braudel's own caveat. He reasons about rice through wheat because, as a Westerner, that is the frame available to him. The note reads this as an explicit acknowledgment of the limits of cross-civilizational comparison (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-rice).

World-scale comparison against "Western nationalism"

  • Correcting European priority. Gunpowder was made in China from the ninth century. Braudel blames "a kind of 'Western nationalism'" for minimizing Europe's debt and discards the attribution to Roger Bacon (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch06-gunpowder-and-artillery). Credit instruments run from Babylon through Rome and the Cairo geniza to Chinese bank notes of the ninth century (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch07-paper-money-and-credit). "Europe therefore neither discovered America and Africa, nor first penetrated the mysterious continents." The habitable world "already had an owner before 1500" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch01-scale-of-reference).
  • A synchronous world. World population roughly doubled between 1300 and 1800. Because the growth was synchronous in China, Russia and the Americas, Braudel argues that European explanations cannot account for it. He offers shared climatic fluctuation as a hypothesis, not a proof (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch01-guessing-world-population).
  • Numbers bound history without deciding it. "I am not suggesting that the path of history ... has been entirely dominated by numbers" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch01-many-against-the-few).
  • Civilizations as different planets. Civilizations bind "thousands of cultural possessions" into an order. Each great centre of population "has worked out a set of elementary answers" and clings to them by "force of inertia." Humanity "was divided between different planets," and "even when they were close together, these solutions never combined" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion).
  • The China question. China's technical precocity, followed by a plateau, recurs through the volume without a settled answer. See braudel-china-precocity-puzzle.

Socio-economy, Marx and the state

Braudel judges "society" too vague a word and proposes "socio-economies" instead. He takes up Marx's question of who owns the land, ships, machinery and the "leading positions in society." But he insists on adding the state, "simultaneously cause and consequence." The result is "a typology of world socio-economic systems: those based on slavery, those with serfs and overlords, those where there are businessmen and pre-capitalists." He walks "some of the way" with Marx but rejects the deterministic succession of modes of production. At the close of the volume he names inequality as the engine: "the inequalities, the injustices, the contradictions large or small ... made the world go round." Capitalism's power lies in its "comparative freedom of movement" over the "inflexible" floors beneath it (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion).

World Machines readings of Volume I

The notes read Volume I against the world-machines frame, and each rhyme carries a hedge. Material civilization is a slow-changing, barely visible infrastructure beneath events, which is close in shape to a world machine. But it is "inert and cyclical rather than a machine moving through a Dawn–Day–Dusk lifecycle" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-preface). The three floors loosely echo three machines in friction, but they are "synchronic strata within one social formation, not sequential thousand-year eras" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-introduction). The structure-versus-event move "near-exactly anticipates" the history-machine contrast between paradigms that assemble silently and event-centred history. Braudel makes the move from the side of economic and social history, not institutional paradigm formation (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-preface).

The Wheels of Commerce: building the upper floors

The second volume, The Wheels of Commerce, has its own canon page, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2. Volume I weighed material life. Volume II climbs to the market economy and to capitalism, which Braudel defines as "a shadowy zone, a twilight area of activities by the initiated" and at root "an accumulation of power ... a form of social parasitism" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-foreword). Its clearest formula places capitalism in "the zone of the anti-market, where the great predators roam," above a transparent market economy and a "non-economy" beneath it (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch02-locating-the-market). See braudel-three-storey-house.

He defends the anachronistic word openly. "Capitalism" matured only around 1900, but historians coin retrospective labels routinely, and he needed a name for "this zone which is not the true market economy, but indeed often its exact opposite" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-foreword). "If capitalism is thrown out of the door, it comes in through the window" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch03-capital-capitalist-capitalism).

Method in Volume II

  • Comparison as experiment. "Observation — repeated until the eyes ached," borrowings from the human sciences, and "systematic comparison" in Marc Bloch's tradition. Because pre-industrial systems changed slowly, comparable cases across centuries are "virtually a series of experiments" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-foreword).
  • Price series over definitions. Against Polanyi's dating of a self-regulating market to the nineteenth century, Braudel offers an empirical test: synchronized price movement, found across Europe by the twelfth century. He dismisses Polanyi's reliance on ethnographic exceptions as "an almost theological taste for definition" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch02-locating-the-market). For profit rates he calls for "a methodical model, the model of a model" built on Simiand's and Labrousse's price history (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch03-industrial-profits-overland-transport).
  • Words checked against things. Following Henri Berr and Lucien Febvre, he traces capitale from twelfth-century Italy, capitalist from 1633 and capitalism from Louis Blanc (1850) and Sombart (1902). He then drops the words and asks what capital materially is (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch03-capital-capitalist-capitalism). Of the Weber–Sombart quarrel he says "we must move out of the charmed circle of words, and look to real life" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch05-retrospective-geography-rationalism-florence).
  • Several causes, never one. "Economics played a part, politics played a part, society played a part, and culture and civilization played a part" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch04-capitalist-mentality-and-long-distance-trade). He treats the Protestant ethic as a real but overstated correlation (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch05-christendom-usury-puritanism) and explains the North's rise by "retrospective geography" and costs: "There are more things in the world than the Protestant ethic" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch05-retrospective-geography-rationalism-florence).
  • Society as a "set of sets." Economy, hierarchy, state and culture are held together as a heuristic, not a metaphysics: "the point of a definition is to provide an approach to a problem." He is willing to state a constant he cannot explain. The concentration of power in a tiny elite is "a fact" stated "without entirely understanding it" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch05-opening-hierarchies-vertical-elevation).
  • An unfinished voice. The chapters were written and rewritten four or five times and first given as lectures. Braudel compares them to Matisse's discarded drafts and admits doubts about his own clarity (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-foreword).

Against Western "pioneering genius"

Volume II extends Volume I's correction of "Western nationalism" from techniques to the instruments of capitalism itself.

  • The Mediterranean, not Amsterdam, is "the cradle of the stock market," with negotiable state loan stocks in Venice, Florence and Genoa and a forward market in Verona's 1318 statutes (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch01-the-exchanges).
  • Capitalism's toolkit was borrowed. Bookkeeping, the bill of exchange and the commenda were "cultural goods, which travelled and were exchanged like any other," largely from a more mature medieval Islamic trading world. Admitting this means "turning one's back on traditional accounts of the history of the West as pioneering genius, spontaneous inventor" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch05-state-culture-incomplete-entity-islamic-model).
  • The evidence itself is unequal. Europe invented historians and used them as evidence for itself, while the history of non-Europe is still being written (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch01-few-exchanges-many-fairs-concluding-hypotheses).
  • No destined winner. "Why should one civilization be more intelligent and rational than another for all time?" He imagines Chinese junks rounding the Cape in 1419 and refuses to "rewrite world history in support of a cause." Capitalism "no longer appears to us to be the last word in historical evolution" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch05-retrospective-geography-rationalism-florence).
  • The comparative answer he does give. Capitalism needs a market economy, which China had, plus a society that lets upper classes accumulate and transmit wealth across generations, plus world trade. Europe and, partly, Japan met these conditions "in their own separate ways" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch05-capitalism-outside-europe-norman-jacobs-politics, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-conclusion).

The Perspective of the World (Volume III)

The third volume of Civilization and Capitalism has its own canon page, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3. Braudel frames it as a gamble. Having built the model of material life, market economy and capitalism, he "abandons himself to the march of time" and lets chronological narrative confirm or invalidate it, because history, unusually among the human sciences, offers itself as both explanation and the only real means of verification outside pure deduction (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-foreword). The volume's central category is the world-economy, an economically autonomous fragment of the planet with slow-moving frontiers, one dominant city at a time and a hierarchy of core, middle zone and periphery. Braudel builds it in open dialogue with Immanuel Wallerstein, whose model he calls "certainly a valid one" while dating the European world-economy's birth to thirteenth-century Italy rather than the sixteenth century (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-ch01-world-economies-ground-rules, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-ch01-an-order-among-other-orders). See braudel-world-economy. Its narrative spine is the succession of centres from Venice to London (braudel-succession-of-dominant-centres). It then turns to national markets, the non-European world and the industrial revolution, and ends by testing the model against the twentieth century (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-conclusion-past-and-present).

Layered time made explicit in Volume III

Volume III spells out the medium term that the longue durée section above only names. Braudel inventories the economic cycles of his day: Kitchin, Juglar, Labrousse's intercycle, Kuznets and Kondratieff. These are "synchronic," overlapping to intensify or dampen the general trend. Above them all is the secular trend, "barely visible in everyday life, but plodding inexorably on." He rejects Keynes's "in the long run we shall all be dead" as banal: "we all live in both the long-term and the short-term" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-ch01-divisions-of-time). He also maps Kuznets's split between "what makes economic growth possible" and "the way it actually happens" onto his own longue durée and conjoncture. The industrial revolution becomes conjunctural fireworks on a structural fuse lit centuries earlier (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-ch06-growth-and-division-of-labour).

The Foreword adds world time, a superstructure that links some regions into a shared rhythm and leaves others as "zones of silence" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-foreword). Two passages show Braudel using this apparatus on his own present. Writing inside the 1972–4 crisis, he asks whether it is an ordinary Kondratieff downswing or a reversal of the secular trend, while admitting the cycles' mechanism is a mystery (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-ch06-finance-conjuncture-living-standards). The Conclusion still refuses a strong claim: "I make no claim to explain the present in the light of history" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-conclusion-past-and-present).

Against great-man explanation in Volume III

Volume III repeatedly moves explanation off individuals and onto structure. Charles V's dream of universal monarchy could not decide the fate of a world-economy whose capitalism was already present in Antwerp, Lisbon, Seville and Genoa (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-ch01-an-order-among-other-orders). On the pound's survival in 1621 Braudel asks: "can one man, however brilliant and far-sighted, have been responsible for a monetary process affecting the entire English economy?" He answers with "not one single explanation but a series of explanations" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-ch04-england-island-and-pound). England's economy "was not moving towards any goal, rather it encountered one" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-ch06-long-distance-trade-inland-transport). The notes read each case as close to the history-machine pattern of distributed assembly, with the hedge that Braudel's subject is economic structure, not historiographical paradigms.
