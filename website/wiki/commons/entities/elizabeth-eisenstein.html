Elizabeth L. Eisenstein

Commons · Entities · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Historian of the shift from script to print whose method replaces periodization labels with a traceable communications shift, treats printing as “an agent, not the agent” of change, and in her 2005 Afterword answers book historians and critics of technological determinism by narrowing rather than abandoning her claims.

Elizabeth L. Eisenstein is the historian whose The Printing Press as an Agent of Change, abridged with a 2005 Afterword as eisenstein-printing-revolution, put the shift from script to print on the agenda of general history. This page records only what the batch notes support about her, her method and her stance.

How the project began

She tells the story herself. Her graduate training was in a quasi-Marxist social history that had no place for a “communications revolution”, and her reading lists in medieval and early modern French history barely mentioned printing (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-1). Dissatisfied with Bridenbaugh's “collective amnesia” and with marshall-mcluhan's Gutenberg Galaxy, she took from McLuhan only his question: what did the shift from script to print change? Finding “not even a small literature” on it, she made that gap the project (eisenstein-printing-revolution-introduction). Her first articles predate book history as a field and were never meant as contributions to it. Her unit of study was the disruption of Western Christendom and the periodization of Renaissance, Reformation and Scientific Revolution. The innovation on her mind as she wrote was the photocopier, and she credits the Internet with giving the book “an unexpectedly long lease on life” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-1).

Method

  • A traceable event instead of a label. She declines the “definitionally hopeless” debate over when the Middle Ages ended. She anchors instead on printers' workshops appearing city by city, because “printing deserves to be put first” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch05-permanent-renaissance-1). In the conclusion she retires “modernity” itself as a moving target and tracks “a definite communications shift” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-conclusion-1).
  • An agent, not the agent. Printing is “an agent, not the agent, let alone the only agent, of change”. She does not substitute one variable for many; she explains why long-present variables “began to interact in new ways” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-introduction). Its use was “overdetermined”, a convergence of profit, mission, fame and statecraft producing an irreversible “change of phase” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-conclusion-2).
  • Concrete, group-by-group history. She praises McLuhan's insight and rejects his abstraction, tracing how the same innovation affected different groups in “markedly different ways” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch04-s1-reading-public). She warns that compressing consumption effects into “any single statement or neat formula” leads astray, while insisting this does not mean nothing changed (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch02-defining-initial-shift-2).
  • Name the intermediaries. She dissolves the “mystery” of the Ninety-Five Theses by tracing the printers, translators and distributors between church door and public clamor (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch06-christendom-disrupted-1). The same move gives publication its own geography in the history of science (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s3-galileos-trial-1).
  • Incentives, not virtue. Honest citation spread because it satisfied “mixed motives”, not because scholars became better people (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s4-data-collection). Publication was fueled “not by love of God or Mammon but by love of God and Mammon” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s3-galileos-trial-1).
  • Order of causes. Asking whether material or spiritual factors mattered more is pointless. What matters is what came first, and printing should be placed ahead of the Protestant Revolt (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch06-christendom-disrupted-3).
  • No dialectical endings. She rejects the Hegelian and Marxist “impulse to end tales that are still unfolding” and lets contradictory trends stand indefinitely (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-final-remarks).

Characteristic interlocutors

Her habit is to quote a scholar in order to reattribute his observation to print.

  • Panofsky. His permanent Renaissance is right as description and wrongly explained. Fixity, not Renaissance culture, supplies permanence, and the printer's shop, not the studio, is where scholars and artisans met (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch05-permanent-renaissance-1, eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch05-permanent-renaissance-3).
  • Burckhardt. His Renaissance individual is real, but “much more is owed to the advent of printing and much less to local circumstances in Italy” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch05-permanent-renaissance-2).
  • Kuhn. The data available to astronomers was transformed, in transmission (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s4-data-collection, eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s2-copernican-revolution).
  • Sarton. He was wrong to fold dissemination into standardization (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s1-wide-dissemination). She endorses his view that a great library is a scientific instrument (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s1-great-book-of-nature).
  • Mannheim. Men of letters were not confined to “the secluded study”. The printing shop was their “vital and functioning segment of society” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch04-s2-men-of-letters).
  • Koestler. His mystical Kepler omits the press on the Rudolphine frontispiece (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s2-copernican-revolution).
  • Marxist historiography. There are good reasons to associate printers with merchants, “none for detaching them from association with professors and friars” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-conclusion-1).

Replies to critics in the 2005 Afterword

Her consistent move is to concede the phenomenon, narrow the claim and hold the line.

  • Being misread is not being wrong. The “McLuhanesque” label misattributes a remark she aimed at McLuhan's “typographical man”, not at bibliography (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-1).
  • Gradualists and the longue durée. Nonbook production and the scribe-turned-printer (Peter Schoeffer against Vespasiano da Bisticci, who “closed shop”) show a “veritable metamorphosis”. Maufer's 1477 shop was closer to the printers of 1788 than to contemporary manuscript dealers (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-1).
  • The communicative spectrum. Against McKenzie and Love she argues that print altered the balance among speech, script and print, not their existence (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-1, eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-2).
  • Barnard. She regrets “impact” and adopts “cooperative transition” or “change of phase”. Hand copying was also linked to politics and religion; what changed was “the character of the linkage itself” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-2).
  • McKitterick, Duguid and Nunberg. Manuscript persisted in “specialized functions”, but the shift was “all in one direction” and print arrested and reversed loss (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-3).
  • Adrian Johns. He refutes a claim she never made. Early printed output was “more standardized than were hand-copied ones”, a relative gain (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-3).
  • Michael Warner. Printing and culture are “fused”. Only “in this restricted sense”, the skills presswork demanded, did “the character of the tool(s)” determine the new labor force (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-3).

She also records her own concessions. Silent reading predates print (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch04-s1-reading-public). Handwriting was never a casualty (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-2). The ordinary “cramped, dirty, noisy” print shop deserves more attention, and information overload “should have been given more emphasis” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-3). Her first chapter's title, “An Unacknowledged Revolution”, is now “somewhat out of date” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-1).

For World Machines readers

Eisenstein is a historian of the book, not a machine theorist. The notes repeatedly find her “agent, not sole cause” stance close to legibility-as-precondition-not-cause, and her silent, multi-generational revolution a partial rhyme with history-machine. Both resonances should carry her own hedge: she describes one medium's cumulative effects, not a theory of civilizational infrastructure (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch04-s2-men-of-letters, eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-3).
