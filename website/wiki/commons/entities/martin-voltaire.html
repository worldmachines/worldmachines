Martin (Candide)

Commons · Entities · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Martin, the persecuted scholar Candide picks at Surinam as the most miserable man in the province, a self-declared Manichaean whose pessimism is as total a frame as Pangloss's and turns out, in Paris and Venice, to be practical competence.

Martin is the old scholar who becomes Candide's companion from Surinam to the end of voltaire-candide, and the book's counter-voice to pangloss. Everything here is his own claim or the narrator's report of him; the notes are firm that his pessimism is Martin's, not the narrator's verdict.

Chosen for misery

Robbed and fined at Surinam, Candide advertises a free passage for the most unhappy man in the province, hears twenty candidates, and picks a scholar who has been robbed, beaten, abandoned, fired and persecuted as a Socinian, and who has worked for booksellers, "no employment could be more detestable" (voltaire-candide-ch19-surinam-and-martin).

The Manichaean system

At sea he declares himself a Manichaean: God "has abandoned" the globe "to some evil being", El Dorado excepted. His evidence is cities and families wishing each other's destruction, the poor hating the rich, "a million regimented assassins" roaming Europe, and the claim that private griefs are worse than public calamities. To "there is some good in the world" he answers "it has escaped my knowledge"; asked why the world was formed, "To drive us mad". When a Spanish ship sinks the pirate who robbed Candide, he splits the verdict: "God has punished the knave, and the devil has drowned the rest." Asked whether men have always been vicious, he asks whether hawks have always eaten pigeons; Candide's "free will—" is cut off by landfall. The note reads his frame as "as total an explanatory frame as Pangloss's": after fifteen days they are "just as far advanced as the first moment they began", their only gain "the satisfaction of talking… and of comforting each other". He follows Candide to Venice because "I have none, but you have" (voltaire-candide-ch20-21-at-sea-with-martin).

Pessimism as competence

In Paris he throws out the priest who wants a note payable in the other world, observes that he did well when poor with no friends or physicians, and generalizes the Abbé's picture of Parisians into contradiction in government, courts, churches and spectacles (voltaire-candide-ch22-paris-1). At supper he answers Candide's "It is men who make these blemishes, and they cannot do otherwise" with "Then it is not their fault". At the arrest it is Martin who works out the plot (woman, Abbé and officer all cheats) and advises the bribe, "more of a Manichaean now than ever"; the note calls his pessimism here "practical competence, not a mood" (voltaire-candide-ch22-paris-2).

Wagers and diagnoses

In Venice he bets that Pacquette and Friar Giroflée are unhappy and wins, predicts that Candide's money "will only make them wretched", and allows only that a gondolier's life is trivially preferable to the doge's; the note observes that his method is as anecdotal as Candide's (voltaire-candide-ch23-24-portsmouth-paquette-giroflee). He largely agrees with Pococuranté's opinions yet sees the flaw Candide misses, "a pleasure in having no pleasure", and meets Candide's hopes for Cunégonde with "It is good to hope" (voltaire-candide-ch25-pococurante). He watches the six kings without a word (voltaire-candide-ch26-six-kings), then calls dethroned kings "a very common thing". Asked whose misfortune is worst, he declines to rank: he would "need to enter the heart of each man", and he asks what "scales" Pangloss would use (voltaire-candide-ch27-voyage-to-constantinople).

On the farm

He proposes drowning the Baron, answers the old woman's question by saying that people live either in convulsions of misery or in the lethargy of boredom, and takes Candide's garden as the "only way to make life bearable" (voltaire-candide-ch30-conclusion-garden). The notes' reading is that the book lets neither Martin nor Pangloss win.

See panglossian-optimism-voltaire for the doctrine he opposes.
