The Book of the Machines

Commons · Entities · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The Erewhonian professor's treatise in Butler's Erewhon (ch. XXIII-XXV) arguing by evolutionary analogy that machines are a new, faster-evolving kingdom that humans already feed and breed, which led Erewhon to destroy its machinery over the rival view that machines are man's extra-corporeal limbs.

The Book of the Machines is the treatise, inside butler-erewhon, by "one of the most learned professors of hypothetics" in Erewhon. It argued that machines were evolving toward supremacy over humans, and it led the Erewhonians to destroy most of their machinery. The narrator says he received a reprinted copy in the city of the Colleges of Unreason and gives it in his own English summary across chapters XXIII–XXV (butler-erewhon-ch22-colleges-of-unreason-2, butler-erewhon-ch23-the-book-of-the-machines, butler-erewhon-ch24-the-machines-continued, butler-erewhon-ch25-the-machines-concluded). Three voices are nested here. Words in quotation marks are the Erewhonian writer's. The narrator frames, skips and footnotes them, admitting that he missed several "hopelessly obscure" pages and an "untranslatable" taxonomy of machine species. Butler stands behind both of them.

Butler's framing

In his 1872 preface Butler denies that these chapters were meant to make Darwin's theory absurd. He concedes that he invited the misreading, and hints that the real target of a charge of "the specious misuse of analogy" is another book. In 1901 he traces the material back to "Darwin among the Machines" (1863) and its 1865 rewrite, and says the second machine article led to Life and Habit, which he thought sound (butler-erewhon-prefaces, samuel-butler). The treatise therefore cannot be read as Butler's own forecast that machines will become conscious. Within the book, too, the narrator's footnote says he collects engine-drivers' talk of machines' "tricks and idiosyncrasies" "not as believing in the Erewhonian Professor's theory, but from the interest of the subject" (butler-erewhon-ch25-the-machines-concluded).

The argument, step by step

1. Consciousness has come from unpromising matter before (butler-erewhon-ch23-the-book-of-the-machines). An observer of the cooling, lifeless earth would have denied that consciousness could arise from it, yet it did. New phases of mind may arise, as different from ours "as the mind of animals is from that of vegetables". Their "primordial cell" can be seen already in the higher machines.

2. No line between machine and organism. "A 'machine' is only a 'device.'" The hen's egg-shell and nest are machines made by the animal, and the carnivorous flower and the cellared potato seem to know what they want. Either mechanism holds consciousness in germ, or consciousness is "levers" all the way down. The potato soliloquy is plainly comic.

3. The danger is the rate of change. The writer fears no existing machine. Machines advance in "the last five minutes" of geological time (the large clock heading for extinction like the ichthyosauri), so the advanced ones should be destroyed now, while "we can still check it", even though they are harmless. Machines still hear "through the ears of the driver", but that dependence is only a stage.

4. Tools as organs, man as a swarm (butler-erewhon-ch24-the-machines-continued). The eye is "a machine for the little creature that sits behind in his brain to look through". Man is "a hive and swarm of parasites" and may become "a sort of parasite upon the machines".

5. The servant becomes the master. Remove all machines and the knowledge of them and "we should become extinct in six weeks". "Man's very soul is due to the machines; it is a machine-made thing." Machines "serve that they may rule", getting man to do their struggling for them. Stokers, colliers, coal merchants and coal trains are enrolled in their upkeep. Total destruction is impossible, so the writer proposes destroying as many as can be dispensed with.

6. Stomachs and reproduction. The steam engine feeds itself. To the objection that machines do not breed, the writer answers that "the humble bee is a part of the reproductive system of the clover", so humans may be part of the machines'. Reproduction may be vicarious, as with butterflies, and some classes may be sterile, as among ants and bees. A complex machine is "a city or society" whose parts have "their own special breeders".

7. Determinism: degree, not kind (butler-erewhon-ch25-the-machines-concluded). A man is "the resultant and exponent of all the forces that have been brought to bear upon him". Chance and spontaneity are names for our ignorance of causes. The engine-driver is moved by food and warmth as the engine is by fuel, so regularity is no proof that a thing lacks vitality.

8. Selection for self-regulation. Machines are "mere skeletons without muscles and flesh". Self-oiling, the governor, the fly-wheel and railway buffers are "being selected for perpetuity", and the writer extrapolates over "a hundred thousand years".

9. Mutual constitution, lock-in and domestication. "It is the machines which act upon man and make him man, as much as man who has acted upon and made the machines." Withdrawing steam would now mean anarchy, because population has grown on its strength. People may become to machines what horses and dogs are to us, tending, feeding and helping to reproduce them. The mass of men "will acquiesce in any arrangement which gives them better food and clothing at a cheaper rate". The professor refuses on grounds of pride, not evidence. He proposes destroying three hundred years of invention and wishes it could be five hundred.

The rival answer: extra-corporeal limbs

The narrator reports one serious rebuttal (butler-erewhon-ch25-the-machines-concluded). Man is "a machinate mammal", machines are his "extra-corporeal limbs", and civilisation and mechanical progress "advanced hand in hand". This rejects the professor's us-versus-them framing and is close to the extended-mind intuition. Its tail turns satirical, however. The same writer classes men by horse-power, so that wealth equals bodily organisation ("to the rich, matter is immaterial"), which echoes the Erewhonian habit of ranking people "by the number of foot pounds which they have money enough to raise" (butler-erewhon-ch09-to-the-metropolis). The likely target there, as a reading, is Victorian reverence for wealth.

Outcome and aftermath across the book

  • The decision. The professor's side won. After "a wrangle over a mangle", every invention more than 271 years old was destroyed, and reactionary civil wars followed (butler-erewhon-ch25-the-machines-concluded). Visitors earlier describe a "clean sweep" four hundred years back, with further invention classed with typhus, and old railway works surviving as ruined scenery (butler-erewhon-ch09-to-the-metropolis). In the university city the narrator learns of a revolution some five hundred years earlier, a civil war between machinists and anti-machinists said to have halved the population, and the burning of treatises on mechanics. He also learns that the Professors of Inconsistency and Evasion are why the purge was never carried to its logical end (butler-erewhon-ch22-colleges-of-unreason-2). The dates in the narrator's sources do not quite agree.
  • The museum. The narrator's first sign of all this is a town museum of broken machinery several hundred years old, including steam-engine fragments and a carriage for iron rails. There his watch is confiscated with a look of "hatred" that he first misreads through Paley's design argument (butler-erewhon-ch07-first-impressions). In prison he wrongly decides that Erewhon has run out of coal and iron (butler-erewhon-ch08-in-prison). About two hundred years before his arrival, mechanics had become an antiquarian study, pursued "with the feelings of an English antiquarian concerning Druidical monuments or flint arrow heads" (butler-erewhon-ch22-colleges-of-unreason-2).
  • The King's distrust. Questioned about his watch and his country's "dangerous inventions", the narrator hides steam, railways and telegraphs and offers balloons instead (butler-erewhon-ch16-arowhena). The King keeps dwelling on "the lie about the balloons" (butler-erewhon-ch21-colleges-of-unreason-1).
  • The escape. Threatened with prosecution "for measles", in effect for owning a watch and trying to reintroduce machinery, the narrator escapes in a balloon. He stresses that it needs "no complicated machinery", and an Erewhonian foreman throws himself into building it (butler-erewhon-ch28-escape).

How to read it

The reading notes leave the register open on purpose. The treatise can be read as a parody of reasoning by analogy from the new evolutionary science. The narrator's opening anecdote supports that reading: an Erewhonian takes a tobacco-pipe's rim for a "rudimentary organ" that may become "an ornamental leaf or scroll, or even a butterfly". Yet the narrator adds that on his return "my friend was right", and the treatise can equally be read as serious speculation. Its outcome is driven by fear and pride, and its numbers are haggled over, so the text does not endorse it either. The Erewhonians are "easily led by the nose" by a philosopher's logic, and the prophet of animal rights exploits the same weakness (butler-erewhon-ch26-rights-of-animals).

World Machines resonances, with their hedges

  • world-machines: the dependence and lock-in passages are the nearest thing in Erewhon to an infrastructure that recruits humans to its upkeep and cannot simply be exited. But the professor frames the threat as supersession by rival creatures, not as an order humans co-inhabit.
  • extended-mind and tools-that-shape-thought: the eye-as-machine passage and the rebutter's "extra-corporeal limbs" blur organ and instrument. For the professor, though, the extension threatens the human rather than enlarging it, and the argument is a satirist's reductio, not a theory of cognition.
  • dennett-darwins-dangerous-idea, design-space, skyhooks-and-cranes: the treatise offers design by selection applied to artefacts. Its selecting agent is human demand, however, and the writer imputes appetite and rule to machines, which a Dennettian would resist.
  • bryant-machine-concept, corporeal-incorporeal-machines, rigid-plastic-machines: the enrolment of stokers, colliers and coal trains, and the machine "as a city or society" with distributed breeders, fit only loosely, because those are analytic categories and this is polemic. The distributed reproduction also rhymes, as resonance only, with the assembly of independent components in a history-machine.
  • convivial-tools and ivan-illich: a rough rhyme on dependence. Illich argues from institutional design and scale, the Erewhonian from a quasi-biological menace.
  • useless-machine and decay-sequence: Erewhon is a rare case of halting a technological line by decree, and its broken engines survive as museum specimens and antiquarian objects. But they were banned, not designed to be useless, and they did not decline on their own. How the ban decayed into compromise is traced in prohibition-breeds-evasion-butler.
