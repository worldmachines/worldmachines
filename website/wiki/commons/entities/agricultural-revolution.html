The Agricultural Revolution (Appleby's account)

Commons · Entities · updated 19 July 2026 · source on GitHub
The seventeenth-century shift from fallow-field farming to Dutch four-part crop rotation, adopted and extended by English freeholders and improving landlords, which Appleby treats as capitalism's causal keystone — breaking the Malthusian food ceiling, cutting England's farm workforce from 80 to 40 percent, and freeing the labour and capital that industry later used.

In The Relentless Revolution the switch from fallow-field farming to continuous crop rotation is the causal keystone of capitalism's emergence — the change Appleby argues "did more to make capitalism possible than trade in spices or the parade of industrial machines that gets the credit" (appleby-relentless-revolution-ch03b-dutch-and-english-farming-improvements). This page collects that account across the notes that develop it.

The trap it broke

Premodern Europe sat in a demographic-agricultural vise. Roughly 80 percent of the population was needed just to grow food, leaving structurally too little surplus labour or spending money for manufacturing, trade or luxury — disposable income reached perhaps 15 percent of society, and the sixteenth century's occupational structure was in this respect no different from Rome's. Fear of famine made grain the most surveilled commodity in the economy: English law criminalized engrossing, forestalling and regrating, because "the growing and marketing of grains were as much social as economic activities." Malthus later formalized the catch-22 — population's power "superior to the power in the earth to produce subsistence" — while missing Europe's own quiet counter-mechanism, a uniquely late marriage age that functioned as a voluntary population brake, reconstructed only afterwards from parish registers (appleby-relentless-revolution-ch03a-basket-of-consumables-and-population).

The Columbian Exchange widened the "basket of consumables" — maize, potatoes, and above all sugar, acting "like so many insurance policies against famine" — but unevenly and at a cost. The potato drove earlier marriage and denser population in Ireland, Poland and China, then supplied a single point of failure when the 1840s blight killed or displaced a third of Ireland's population; sugar built a slave-labour plantation economy in the Caribbean before it reached the ordinary European pantry (appleby-relentless-revolution-ch03a-basket-of-consumables-and-population).

The mechanism

Dutch farmers broke the bind between feeding people and feeding soil. Instead of fallowing a third or a quarter of arable land, they discovered that clover and other legumes fix nitrogen and rotated grain, turnips, hay and clover through four-part cycles — "the virtuous circle of growth replaced the vicious circle of decline." English farmers copied and adapted it (four-field rotation in some regions, "up-and-down husbandry" in others) while exploiting marl, sea sand and ground seashells according to local soil (appleby-relentless-revolution-ch03b-dutch-and-english-farming-improvements).

Why it was slow

Appleby is emphatic that improvement was not opportunity meeting a natural drive to self-betterment. Custom, risk aversion and even the "biorhythms of premodern men and women," unaccustomed to sustained daily labour, worked against adoption; being a rational economic actor "didn't make a lot of sense" when risk routinely outpaced reward. Only freehold farmers and landlords who had converted fixed rents into market-rate leases were positioned to gamble on new technique, and it was their conspicuous success — bigger harvests, falling grain prices that punished the unimproved — that eventually built momentum (appleby-relentless-revolution-ch03b-dutch-and-english-farming-improvements).

Consequence, not cause

The chapter's argumentative spine reverses the standard Marxist story. Rather than landlords coercing tenants into market discipline, "new social relations were the consequence, not the cause" of agricultural transformation — an unplanned, five-or-six-generation "slow, untidy sorting out" running from cottagers to great lords. Enclosure raised productivity while dissolving the network of community obligation that had bound villages, provoking pamphlet wars (the Reverend John Moore's charge that enclosure abandoned Christian duty to the poor against the Reverend Joseph Lee's defence of private incentive) and prompting the Elizabethan Poor Laws as the institutional replacement for communal absorption of the vulnerable. Local markets unified into a national one — legible, interchangeable food distribution that contemporaries found disorienting, as one lament over having to "carry our Corn God knows where" records (appleby-relentless-revolution-ch03b-dutch-and-english-farming-improvements).

This is a history-machine-shaped account: a paradigm assembling itself out of thousands of independent farm-level decisions, visible as a "revolution" only in retrospect. The grain-regulation apparatus it displaced, and the national market it built, are both instances of legibility-as-machine-core operating on food — first to prevent riot and starvation, then to move a commodity.

What it made possible

England and the Netherlands were, on Appleby's account, the only European states to expand population and income simultaneously in the seventeenth century, crossing the threshold of freedom from famine that separated them from their own past and from a Europe where war, static technique and (in Russia and Poland) serfdom kept agriculture stagnant (appleby-relentless-revolution-ch03b-dutch-and-english-farming-improvements).

The industrial payoff is specific. The agricultural revolution cut England's farm workforce from 80 to 40 percent, driving up wages for the labour that remained; a domestic putting-out-system was already dispersing capital and manufacturing skill through the countryside; and abundant cheap coal substituted for depleting timber. High wages plus cheap fuel is Appleby's stated arithmetic behind inventing "machines using fuel that could vastly increase the output of human laborers" (appleby-relentless-revolution-ch05a-slavery-labor-and-industrial-inventions).

A later, different episode

The twentieth-century Green Revolution is a separate event with a partly inverted moral. High-yield seeds doubled Asian cereal production and rural incomes, but favoured farmers who could afford the new inputs — widening rural inequality — and later stalled as governments cut irrigation and research spending (appleby-relentless-revolution-ch12b-eastern-europe-and-indias-transformation). Where the seventeenth-century transformation freed labour and capital for something else, this one distributed its gains along an existing gradient of means.

World Machines resonances

The pre-1650 pattern Appleby describes — population growth pressing against a food ceiling, then collapse and recovery, with the Black Death killing roughly half the Continent — is the substrate secular-cycles models; her account of the break is a claim about a cycle being exited rather than completed. The energy framing (coal substituting for depleting timber, fuel-burning machines substituting for human and animal muscle) connects to machines-as-energy-regimes and vaclav-smil.
