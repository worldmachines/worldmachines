Alibis for Aggression

Commons · Entities · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Peter Gay's coinage for the respectable rationales (scientific, religious, political, moral) through which the nineteenth-century bourgeoisie licensed aggression while still seeing itself as constructive; plural, contested, and often leaky, they “cultivated hatred, in both senses of the term.”

Alibis for aggression is the key coinage of peter-gay's The Cultivation of Hatred (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3). It names the religious, political, and especially scientific rhetoric that let the nineteenth-century bourgeoisie license militancy while still seeing its own “attacks” on markets, nature, and empire as “purely constructive activities” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-front-introduction). An alibi does not deny aggression. It relocates responsibility for it onto nature, God, duty, the victim's own good, or the enemy's wickedness. Gay's central pun applies: alibis “cultivated hatred, in both senses of the term,” licensing aggression while also channelling it (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-apotheosis-conflict-spencer-darwin).

Alibis are plural, contested, and leaky

Gay insists these rationales were never a settled consensus. They competed with each other, and they provoked guilt in some of the people they licensed, such as Beatrice Webb's “new consciousness of sin.” They were argued over as fiercely as the aggressions they justified (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-apotheosis-conflict-spencer-darwin). Some failed on their own terms. Manliness was so imprecise (“sometimes a weasel, sometimes a whale”) that Gay calls it “an uncertain mechanism for the liberation of aggressive impulses” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-manliness-ideal-ambiguity). Others absorbed their own contradictions: Carnegie and Rockefeller preached Darwinian competition while building monopolies to escape it, then recycled the same providential logic into philanthropy (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-apotheosis-conflict-nietzsche-sumner).

The three canonical alibis

Gay opens Part One with a three-alibi scheme (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-apotheosis-conflict-spencer-darwin):

  1. Competition. What was later labelled “Social Darwinism” predates Darwin. Its sources include Hobbes, Smith's invisible hand, Malthus's population arithmetic, and Spencer's Social Statics (1850) and “survival of the fittest” (1862). Darwin himself mocked the claim that he had proved “might is right.” Spencer, who called the starving of the weak “a merciful provision,” also wrote “Aggression of every kind is hateful to me.” Sumner's version licensed domestic economic struggle but stopped hard at war and empire. Nietzsche folded injury and appropriation into the definition of life. Jack London and Frank Norris produced “embodied alibis for literally mortal competition.” The alibi finally hardened into law through the Fourteenth Amendment and Lochner v. New York, against Holmes's dissent that the Amendment “does not enact Mr. Herbert Spencer's Social Statics” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-apotheosis-conflict-spencer-darwin, gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-apotheosis-conflict-nietzsche-sumner).
  2. The convenient Other. Hatred projected onto a collective enemy, and dressed after mid-century in race science. See the-convenient-other.
  3. Manliness. The Victorians “democratized the courtly ideal of prowess” into a virtue for middle-class men, whose battlefields were commerce and politics. The century settled the word's incoherence by redefining the true manly man as “at once self-assertive and self-controlled” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-manliness-ideal-ambiguity). Thomas Hughes softened his own ideal. Kingsley's pugnacity looked to contemporaries like a mask for its opposite. The harsher cult of honor in the American South, the Mediterranean, and duelling Germany shows what the alibi becomes when “society was the superego” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-manliness-hughes-honor-code). Theodore Roosevelt's “strenuous life” is manliness as counterphobic defense against a sickly childhood (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-tr-extremist-of-the-center).

The wider catalogue

The volume keeps finding the same structure in other domains:

  • Honor. In the Burschenschaften, “one of the most seductive alibis” was the conviction that a principled, honorable rebel may take the law into his own hands. Sand's 1819 murder of Kotzebue is the extreme case (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-front-mensur-2-politics-adolescence).
  • Punishment. Retribution, deterrence, and rehabilitation were each “only too often an alibi for unacceptable, even deranged vengeance.” James Fitzjames Stephen supplied “the frankest justification that the century produced for legalized aggression”: criminals “should be hated, and their punishment should express that hatred.” Moral certainty itself worked as a license, as in Charles Holmes's rage after Lincoln's assassination (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-civilized-rationales). Flogging survived on duty, biblical warrant, endangered manliness, and even utilitarian arithmetic, as when the Boston School Committee cited “the greatest good of the greatest number” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-pleasures-pain-flogging-sadism-masochism).
  • Nature and domesticity. Separate-spheres naturalism and the “power of the powerless” were “two alibis for the male monopoly on aggression.” They looked like opposites and did identical work (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p4-domesticity-alibis-power-paradox). The appeal to “Nature” was the late century's master rhetorical resource against women's claims, escalating into Moebius's pseudo-anatomy and recruited even by the anticlerical left (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p4-time-for-tentativeness). A mirror set of alibis licensed women's subordination by making exclusion look voluntary (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p4-domesticity-education-womens-magazines).
  • Sexual violence. “Domestic frustration” was the “convenient and plausible alibi” for rape. Gay calls it the least credible, since rape delivers “a rabid display of raw power” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-domestic-violence-rape-suicide).
  • Humor. “It's only a joke” shields boasting, self-punishment, and outward attack alike (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p5-varieties-of-laughter).
  • Caesarism. Love for the leader is the price of license to hate the excluded (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p3-modern-caesars-napoleon). See caesarism-gay.
  • Character. Self-control itself was marketed as “aggression disciplined and sublimated” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p6-character-obsessional-neurosis-freud).
  • Nationalism, “the alibi of alibis.” In 1914 it proved the master alibi because it could absorb and override every other identity at once: class solidarity, cosmopolitanism, and socialist internationalism (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-epilogue-august-4-1914).

Occasionally a contemporary named the mechanism outright. Jean Finot wrote that race science “was, in a word, an alibi for the ugliest aggression” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-convenient-other-colonial-violence).

World Machines resonance (hedged)

The notes read alibis as loosely related to what world-machines calls an era's legitimating infrastructure. Each time they qualify the match: Gay's alibis are moral rhetoric internal to a class's self-image, not the technological and institutional architecture a world machine names (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-front-introduction). Manliness in particular was unusually leaky, so the resonance is with legitimation in general rather than a clean case of it (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-manliness-ideal-ambiguity). The Lochner episode is the strongest bridge. There an alibi migrates from rhetoric into durable legal infrastructure (gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-apotheosis-conflict-nietzsche-sumner).
