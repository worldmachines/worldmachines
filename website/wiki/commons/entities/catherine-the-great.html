Catherine II

Commons · Entities · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Catherine II of Russia as John Morley shows her with the philosophers: Diderot's imperial benefactress and host in 1773–74, who in Morley's account courted the Encyclopædists for vogue, fame and political cover, borrowed from Montesquieu, and judged Diderot's reforming principles fine books but very bad business.

This page draws on Volume II of john-morley's diderot-and-the-encyclopaedists (morley-diderot-v2), Chapter IV and the Conclusion. Morley builds his portrait mostly from diplomats and men of affairs, and the notes keep his conclusions apart from the contemporaries he quotes. The broader pattern is discussed at philosophers-and-enlightened-despots-morley.

Patron of the philosophers

In 1773 Diderot, at sixty, resolved to visit his imperial benefactress; Princess Dashkow and Prince Galitzin prepared the way. Morley's central claim is that “It was the vogue of the philosophers, and not their philosophy that made Catherine their friend.” He cites her own confession that voltaire “made me the fashion” and that the “learned men in ist” wearied her, and Gunning, the English chargé d'affaires, on her “insatiable and unbounded thirst for fame”. She founded academies and left them unfinished. Comparing her correspondence to a Reptile fund, Morley concludes it was “in some measure the result of political calculation”. The Duchesse de Choiseul's letter, which Morley quotes and endorses only in part, holds that the men of letters were flattered dupes who covered her crimes. Morley notes the contrast with France: the Sorbonne burned Marmontel's Belisarius while Russian courtiers translated it, and Diderot, refused the French Academy, was an honoured guest in Russia (morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-1).

The code and the crimes

Catherine pillaged Montesquieu for her Instruction, but Morley says Montesquieu's own method would have taught her that absolute power cannot force a free-labour civil system onto a society resting on serfdom; he suggests she privately valued the code less than she told a Parisian philosopher. When she championed the Polish Dissidents, the philosophers saw toleration on her banners and “asked no further questions”. With the “significant exception of Rousseau”, they did not criticise the Partition or the death of her husband; Voltaire called these “family affairs with which I cannot possibly think of meddling” (morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-1).

Managing opinion

Catherine tried to buy Rulhière's account of the 1762 coup and then to have him imprisoned. Diderot, who had warned Rulhière that not everything true is fit to be told, offered her two ways to read the book, as a satire on her or as a portrait of a great princess. Falconet came at Diderot's urging to cast the statue of Peter the Great (morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-1).

Diderot at her court

At St Petersburg Catherine banished etiquette and talked with Diderot daily; he struck the imperial knees and she sheltered behind a table. Foreign diplomats assumed the access must be political. Years later she told Ségur that his principles “would make fine books, but very bad business”: he worked “on paper”, she “on the human skin”. The notes stress that this is her own retrospective self-presentation. Morley adds that she rated Diderot “at his true worth as a great talker”, without wholesale enthusiasm or disparagement. Grimm proved the smoother courtier, sending her quarterly packets of daily letters and serving as an unofficial intermediary with Versailles until 1792. In 1792 she banished Voltaire's busts to the cellars (morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-2).

The University plan and a rebuke

Diderot's Plan of a University for the Government of Russia put the sciences before letters. On religion he took no position of his own and said only that her subjects should conform to her views, concessions Morley treats as made to “her majesty's political religion”. In an anecdote Morley hedges as a story, Catherine caught Diderot flattering her after he had denounced the flatterers of kings (morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-2).

World Machines resonances, with the notes' hedges

Morley's Russia, where the tenth and eighteenth centuries coexist, reads as a laboratory of forced, artificial modernisation and resonates loosely with the modernity-machine's imposed legibility, though Morley's frame is linear civilisation, not machine lifecycles (morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-1). The diplomats' misreading of Diderot's visit is, in the notes' gloss, a small case of states mistaking a network of ideas for an instrument of policy (morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-2).
