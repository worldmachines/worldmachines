Custom As Second Nature Montaigne

Commons · Entities · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne's account of custom as “a second nature, and no less powerful”: a slow, unnoticed power that forms the senses, character, belief, conscience and law from infancy, makes the foreign look barbarous and the familiar look rational, and that he would have judgment see through while conduct mostly obeys it.

Custom is one of the most persistent themes of Montaigne's Essays (montaigne-complete-essays). The phrase “Custom is a second nature, and no less powerful” comes late, in Of Managing the Will, where he uses it to explain why at his age he cannot change (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch10-managing-will-1). The idea is worked out from Book I onward.

How custom works

The essay on custom opens with the woman who carried a calf every day until it was an ox. Custom is a teacher whose authority arrives “by little and little, slily and unperceived” and then turns tyrannical (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch22-custom-should-not-easily-1). Montaigne traces it through a series of layers:

  • Senses. Smiths stop hearing their trade, his perfumed doublet stops pleasing him after three days, and the great bell in his tower is now barely heard.
  • Character. Cruelty and treason have their roots in what parents applaud in children. He reports, of himself, an aversion to cheating at cards that his upbringing gave him.
  • Body. An armless man uses his feet as hands.
  • Belief. No opinion is so extravagant that custom has not made it law somewhere, and reason then backs it. “The laws of conscience, which we pretend to be derived from nature, proceed from custom.”

He carves religion out of this claim, saying that errors there lie beyond human reason; the notes warn against reading the passage as an attack on religion (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch22-custom-should-not-easily-1).

Barbarism is other people's custom

The principle points outward. Darius's Greeks recoil from eating their dead and his Indians from burning theirs: “use veils from us the true aspect of things” (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch22-custom-should-not-easily-2). In Of Cannibals, everyone “gives the title of barbarism to everything that is not in use in his own country”, and we have “no other level of truth and reason than the example and idea of the opinions and customs of the place wherein we live”. Montaigne does not flatten this into pure relativism: the Brazilians remain barbarous “in respect to the rules of reason”, though not in respect to Europeans, who exceed them in cruelty (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch30-cannibals). Of Coaches applies the same move to the conquest of Mexico and Peru as an indictment (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch06-coaches-2). The Apology goes further: “We are Christians by the same title that we are Perigordians or Germans”. This is a claim about how believers in fact hold their faith, offered as a criticism of them, not a claim that the faith is false (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-1). The Apology also finds the New World sharing customs with Europe (circumcision, crosses, a flood story). Montaigne reads the same evidence both as convergence and as diversity, and calls Christian-looking parallels “vain shadows of our religion” (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-13).

Law and the missing natural law

“Laws derive their authority from possession and custom”: like rivers they grow by running, and their sources turn out to be “but a little spring” (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-14). The philosophers' natural laws fail a universality test, since every candidate is contradicted by some nation. Montaigne nonetheless allows it “credible that there are natural laws for us”, lost because reason confounds the face of things (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-13). To go “according to nature” is merely to go “according to our understanding”, and what we cite as natural law is a “municipal law” (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-8). Of Experience concludes that laws “keep up their credit, not for being just, but because they are laws; 'tis the mystic foundation of their authority” (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-1). For what he does with this politically, see relativism-and-conservatism-montaigne.

Custom as a tool, not only a veil

Montaigne also uses custom deliberately:

  • Against fear of death. Book I prescribes making death familiar so as to disarm its novelty (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch19-study-philosophy-learn-die-1), and locates the terror of dying in the ceremonies of the deathbed, a mask to be taken off (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch19-study-philosophy-learn-die-2).
  • Courage by usage. His plague-stricken peasants reach by usage a courage equal to “the most studied and premeditated resolution” (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch12-physiognomy-2).
  • The body's regimen. Custom is “the potion of Circe”: it can mould us to any form, and its best lesson is “to change and variation” (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-2). Being harmed by long-accustomed things is something he believes impossible, and changing a sick man's diet does harm (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-3).
  • Keeping habit loose. Precautions harden into dependence: the warm cap becomes a quilted hat, then a hare's skin, “there is no end on't”. He “avoid[s] the invariable” for this reason (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-4).
  • Travel and self-invented shame. Abroad he holds that “every usage has its reason” and mocks compatriots who call foreign manners barbarous because they are “not French” (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch09-vanity-4). He turns the theme against shame about sex and self-punishing austerity: people follow “the laws of thy parish” while ignoring “those of God and the world” (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch05-some-verses-virgil-4).

The sceptic lives by custom

In the Apology, the Pyrrhonian who suspends judgment still acts. In practice he follows natural inclination, “the constitution of laws and customs” and the tradition of the arts. Here custom is defended as the sceptic's working guide, not unmasked (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-6). The same faculty of custom therefore does three jobs in the Essays: it is the veil that makes the local seem natural, the discipline that can make death and pain bearable, and the practical rule left once reason has been humbled.

World Machines resonances, hedged

The reading notes repeatedly link this theme to habit-and-identity, glossing custom as the invisible machinery behind what feels natural. They add qualifications: Montaigne treats custom as a force over whole peoples as well as individuals; in places he uses it deliberately rather than exposing it; and in the Apology's natural-law passages he exempts revealed Christianity from the relativizing (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-13). The claim that laws and religions are “a bond to their society” touches the world-machine idea of infrastructure that feels natural from inside, though his frame is individual and sceptical rather than a cycle on the scale of eras. The cannibals material sits at the textual root of the later noble-savage figure, hedged by second-hand testimony and by his frank account of the Brazilians' warfare and ritual eating.

Custom dulls wonder

That It Is Folly to Measure Truth and Error by Our Own Capacity finds custom at work in our sense of the marvellous. Things we see daily we neither admire nor ask the causes of; it is custom, not knowledge, that takes away their strangeness, and they would seem incredible if newly shown to us. A Lucretian verse makes the point that each person takes the largest river he has seen for the largest that exists. Montaigne draws a double rule from this. Credulity and blanket incredulity are twin errors, and of unlikely things attested by trustworthy people “we ought at least to leave them in suspense”. He confesses that he once pitied believers in ghosts and witchcraft, and says reason, not new experience, changed his mind. The essay ends in religious conservatism: one must submit wholly to the Church's authority or throw it off, not decide how much obedience is owed, and he reports finding neglected rules well grounded once he discussed them with learned men (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch26-folly-measure-truth-error). The note reads the river passage alongside habit-and-identity and the way familiarity makes infrastructure invisible, with the hedge that Montaigne's concern is epistemic humility, not a theory of eras.

Custom in devotion, burial, affection and death

Several shorter essays show custom governing private and sacred acts. “We pray only by custom and for fashion's sake”, Of Prayers complains, mocking the man who gives “One hour to God, the rest to the devil”; the target is prayer detached from amendment, not prayer (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch56-57-prayers). On funerals his own view is that each should suit the matter to his fortune, and “I should wholly refer the ordering of this ceremony to custom”. The note reads custom here as a convenient default for an indifferent matter rather than the invisible machinery of belief (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch03-05-affections-carry-themselves). The natural affection of parents, to which we give such authority, “has but very weak roots”: wet-nurses come to love foster children more than their own, and a goat-suckled child he saw refused any other goat. He would leave inheritance to “the custom of the country”, since the laws have considered the matter better than we can (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch08-affection-fathers-children-2). The custom of Cea makes the same point about death. Marseilles kept hemlock at public charge for those who gave their reasons to its senate, so that an act punished in one regime is a regulated rite in another; Montaigne adds no verdict of his own (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch03-custom-isle-cea). In the civil wars custom is “a great bounty of nature”, numbing the senses to evils that would otherwise be unbearable (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch09-vanity-3).
