Simón Bolívar

Commons · Entities · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Liberator of northern South America (1783-1830), whose revolution Wulf presents as fought with Humboldt's statistics and Humboldt's imagery as much as with guns — and whose aftermath supplied Humboldt his sharpest disillusionment.

Simón Bolívar (1783–1830) led the wars that expelled Spain from northern South America between roughly 1807 and 1822. In Andrea Wulf's telling he is also the clearest case of Alexander von Humboldt's ideas becoming political ammunition: a revolution fought, as the reading note puts it, as much with Humboldt's ideas as with guns (wulf-invention-of-nature-ch12-revolutions-and-nature).

The meeting, and Humboldt's misjudgment

The two met in Paris in 1804–05, when Bolívar was twenty-one and Humboldt had just returned from five years in the Americas (wulf-invention-of-nature-ch09-europe). They agreed on the immorality of colonial rule. They did not agree about what came next: Humboldt considered the colonies "ripe for a revolution" in principle but believed there was "no one to lead them," and dismissed Bolívar as "a dreamer."

The structural half of that diagnosis is sharp — Humboldt's account of why revolution was blocked (three centuries of deliberately stoked racial mistrust, and a creole elite that preferred Spanish rule to shared power) is the kind of analysis the rest of the book earns him credit for. The personal half was simply wrong about the man in front of him. Bolívar's own turning point comes shortly after, in the Monte Sacro oath in Rome: a dandy becomes a revolutionary, an old identity dying before a new legitimacy exists to replace it — the gramsci-gap interregnum running inside a single biography rather than an institution.

Nature as political language

Bolívar does not merely fight in the Andean landscape; he argues in it. Storms, volcanoes, gangrene, locusts, and abysses carry his rhetoric of liberty, and the note traces the idiom directly to Humboldt — "as if writing with Humboldt's pen." After the 1812 Caracas earthquake he answers the ground itself: "If Nature itself decides to oppose us… we will fight and force her to obey." His prose poem "My Delirium on Chimborazo" climbs Humboldt's own mountain (wulf-invention-of-nature-ch07-chimborazo) and turns the ascent into an allegory of liberation, walking in Humboldt's tracks and then deliberately leaving them. The organizing claim is stated outright: nature is the "infallible teacher of men."

Data as ammunition

Humboldt's Political Essay on the Kingdom of New Spain — tables of silver production, agricultural yields, trade, demography — is read by Bolívar not as travel writing but as a handbook of facts about his own continent, and as an indictment of what Humboldt called "the odious monopoly" of colonial extraction. The same book was read at the same time by Jefferson, as a wary calculus about a future rival (wulf-invention-of-nature-ch08-politics-and-nature). One survey, two opposed uses: see legibility-cuts-both-ways.

Buffon, degeneracy, and rank

The war has an ideological front that Bolívar, Jefferson, and Franklin all fight on the same terrain: Buffon's claim that American nature — and by extension American peoples — was smaller, weaker, and more degenerate than Europe's. Humboldt demolishes it from direct observation: Caribs "like bronze statues of Jupiter," pre-Inca astronomical manuscripts, languages rich enough to hold abstractions like eternity. For Bolívar this is not academic; dismantling Buffon dismantles the scaffolding of colonial contempt. The degeneracy thesis is a period instance of exactly the operation cosmopolitan-time-queue names — coexisting societies ranked on one line — and the rebuttal is a bid for what the canon calls coevalness.

The war, unromanticized

The notes are blunt about the cost. The 1812 earthquake read from pulpits as divine punishment; the "War to the Death" decree condemning Spaniards en masse; Boves's "Legions of Hell" and some 80,000 dead; a leader charming enough to seduce a continent and capable of ordering mass executions. Slavery is a hinge rather than a footnote: the pact with Haiti's Pétion ties the revolution to abolition, and the "black veil" persists after Bolívar's own emancipation decree. Victory at Boyacá (1819) founds Gran Colombia.

After

The aftermath is where Humboldt's disillusionment lands. Bolívar's pan-American congress fails and his own vice-president turns against him (wulf-invention-of-nature-ch15-return-to-berlin); he later declares a dictatorship, which Humboldt files alongside American slavery and Napoleon III's self-coronation in the ledger of revolutions that curdled (wulf-invention-of-nature-ch20-greatest-man-since-the-deluge). One earlier episode shows the limits of the cosmopolitan-science ideal that connected the two men: Bolívar's revolutionaries recruit Aimé Bonpland as a kind of soft-power scientist, and when Paraguay's dictator Francia kidnaps and imprisons him on suspicion of "agricultural espionage," Bolívar's and Humboldt's combined standing cannot get him out (wulf-invention-of-nature-ch14-going-in-circles).

Bayly: Bolívar inside the world crisis

Wulf's portrait above is biographical. C. A. Bayly's bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world places the same revolution inside a global chain.

  • One portable doctrine. The “rights of man” formulas were received as self-evident and freestanding: “no king, no divine authority, no imperial interest, no superiority of race or creed could nullify them”. That is why Caribbean slaves, Bengali readers of Paine and the Latin American revolutionaries around Bolívar could each seize the doctrine for local emancipations. Bayly treats these as instances of one idea, not as scattered imitation of Europe (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch03-world-crisis-anatomy).
  • A link in the chain. The fall of Spain triggered the independence wars. These disrupted Mexican and Peruvian silver output, and the disruption rippled into West Africa and into a China that had come to depend on that silver (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch03-climacteric-sapping-legitimacy).
  • The aftermath as a pattern. Bolívar's constitutional vision for Spanish America collapsed into “contending pseudo-nations” ruled by caudillos. Bayly cites this as a specific instance of the broader post-1815 wreck (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch04-wreck-of-nations-and-trade). It is a structural counterpart to Humboldt's personal disillusionment recorded above.
  • A cult as hybrid legitimacy. The new republics invoked Aztec and Inca descent and, later, a Bolivarian cult. Bayly counts these among the patched-together legitimacies of the restoration era (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch04-losers-legitimacy-and-state). The Latin American republics are also his examples of nationalisms manufactured by newly created states (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch06a-theories-of-nationalism).
  • The image. The revolutionary iconography of David and Girodet travelled, and Bolívar was refigured as the Brutus or the Napoleon of Spanish American independence (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch10-emerging-nation-arts-and-outside-west).

Set beside Wulf, this suggests that the gramsci-gap running through Bolívar's own biography had a continental counterpart. Neither Bayly nor the notes make that connection.
