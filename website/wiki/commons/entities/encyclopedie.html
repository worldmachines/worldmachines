The Encyclopædia (Encyclopédie)

Commons · Entities · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The Encyclopædia edited by Diderot and D'Alembert (text completed 1765, plates 1772), which John Morley reads as the symbol and organ of an insurrection joining the scientific idea to the social idea: a Baconian survey of knowledge and the arts that exposed the old regime's abuses by plain statement, made under persecution, connivance and self-censorship.

This page draws on Volume I (Preface, Chapters I–VIII) of john-morley's study diderot-and-the-encyclopaedists; Volume II material is added in later sections. Morley spells the work Encyclopædia, and so does this page.

The symbol of a new insurrection

For Morley the Encyclopædia is “the great symbol” of a phase of revolt that joined the scientific idea to the social idea, aiming at a “new social synthesis resting on a purely positive basis”. It was a protest against the old organisation as much as the old doctrine. Its moral, that human nature is good, the world can be made habitable and evil comes from bad education and institutions, was in his phrase a “wonderful gospel” then and a truism now, and the antecedent of all social improvement. Churchmen resented it because the Church taught a fallen man, and because curiosity about the earth and the spirit of improvement undercut both doctrine and abuse. Morley calls it an “informal, transitory, and provisional organisation of the new spiritual power” (morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary; see men-of-letters-as-spiritual-power-morley).

Precedents and origin

Morley's test of a real encyclopædia is whether it presents knowledge as a totality of organically connected parts. By that test Aristotle first glimpsed the idea, Pliny was “presided over by no definite idea of informing order”, Roger Bacon's Opus Majus was the best medieval attempt, and Alsted merely arranged manuals under a verbal and logical division. Chambers's Cyclopædia (1727) had the right aim, things considered “relatively, or as they respect each other”, but not the grasp. The true parent was francis-bacon, acknowledged in Diderot's Prospectus and D'Alembert's Preliminary Discourse; Chambers was only the occasion (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-1; Morley had already called Bacon “the direct source” of the undertaking, morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-2).

The project began as a French translation of Chambers, passing from Mills and Sellius through the bookseller Le Breton and the Abbé Gua de Malves. Diderot, needy, took over the stalled scheme and saw that the encyclopædic form could gather “all that new thought and modern knowledge” existing in “unsystematic and uninterpreted fragments” into a whole, demoting Chambers to “a mere starting-point”. Le Breton brought in three further booksellers for capital, and Diderot, admitting his weakness in mathematics and physics, recruited jean-le-rond-dalembert (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-1).

Editors, contributors and the labour

The roll of writers, which Morley opens with Voltaire's tribute, ran from the famous (Montesquieu, Turgot, Rousseau, Buffon, later Haller and Condorcet) through specialists who supplied whole fields (Forbonnais, Quesnai, Holbach on chemistry and mineralogy, Duclos, Morellet) to a flood of unsought volunteers, among them clergymen who were unbelievers at heart. The editors were “constantly embarrassed” by competing articles on one subject. Turgot left when he saw the scheme “coloured by that spirit of sect”. The workhorse was Jaucourt, writing and correcting thirteen or fourteen hours a day, whom Diderot credited with getting the work across the line (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-2). The corvée article was written by a sub-engineer of roads and bridges at Tours, evidence for Morley that the work gathered active-minded specialists (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-1). voltaire hailed the first volumes as the glory of France, sent small contributions and pressed for method: etymologies, definitions, examples, clearness, brevity (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-2).

Morley concedes that the “single body with a common doctrine” was a logical fiction, but holds the popular impression practically sound. He credits Comte with seeing the value of the “encyclopædic workshop” that united destructive schools in a constructive task, and notes how the editors cultivated a sense of collective authorship with contributor lists, éloges for the dead and the metaphor of an Academy's transactions (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-1). Diderot's share of the labour included proof-sheets ten hours a day and eyes worn out over plates and diagrams (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-1), and its demands competed with his lavish gifts of time to friends while proofs waited at Le Breton's (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-3).

Publishing history and suppressions

The booksellers' interest in the work helped secure Diderot's release from Vincennes in 1749 (morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-3). The clergy's first attack was indirect: the abbé de Prades, Diderot's friend and a suspected author of the theology articles, was condemned for his Sorbonne thesis in January 1752, and the council's decree of 7 February 1752 suppressed the first two volumes. Diderot's papers and plates were seized, but, as Grimm put it, the Jesuits forgot to take his head and his genius, and the government had to ask the editors to resume. The third volume appeared in 1753 with D'Alembert's fable of the grasshoppers in its preface. Subscribers grew from barely two thousand to three thousand by the fourth volume and about a thousand more by the seventh (1757). D'Alembert's article on Geneva, which praised the Socinian pastors as a veiled reproach to French churchmen, and Helvétius's De l'Esprit (1758) brought the crisis: in March 1759 the Council of State withdrew the privilege of 1746. Yet fifty compositors kept setting the forbidden book (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-3).

D'Alembert then retired, citing affronts, protected satires, sermons at Versailles and new censors, as well as dissatisfaction with the booksellers' finances. Voltaire first urged resistance, then urged Diderot to finish abroad; Diderot refused, partly because to abandon the work was to do what the persecutors wanted. For seven years he edited alone, until he discovered that Le Breton and his foreman had secretly cut passages likely to provoke the government from the final ten volumes and burned the manuscripts and proofs. The text was completed in 1765 and the plates in 1772. Voltaire's anecdote of the book at the king's supper presents it as a practical compendium of the arts, from the pin to the gun. Foreign editions, imitators and the Methodical Encyclopædia followed. Morley gives the costs, the subscription price and Diderot's pay, about three thousand livres a year and sixty thousand in all, per Grimm, against the army contractor's twenty thousand livres a day that Voltaire complained of (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-4).

What the articles said: abuses stated plainly

Morley's walk through the contents is meant to show the work constructive, not a “gospel of negation”. Articles on agriculture (a quarter of the cultivable land unbroken or abandoned, grain-trade restrictions, arbitrary imposts, drift to the towns), on the gabelle and taille (absurd residence rules, the rejected taille proportionnelle of Lisieux), and on Privilegé (a bourgeois buying a salt-office place to escape taxes, burdening the poor) amount in his phrase to “a formal indictment” without rhetoric, whose grievances reappear in the cahiers of 1789. The corvée and militia articles anticipated or paralleled Turgot's Limousin reforms; the Foundations article, by Turgot in Morley's account, is the most masterly discussion of endowments; Foire and Maîtrises expose regulatory mania and guild monopoly; the game articles were unusually direct (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-1). Morley credits the Encyclopædists with attacking colonial tyranny, the slave trade, the fiscal system, venal justice and the penal code, while hedging that they did not originate these ideas; what changed was that “political ideas have been grasped as instruments” (ibid.; see speculative-to-social-attack-morley).

Industry, the arts and the plates

Morley compares the work to Virgil's Georgics as a “glorification of labour”. Diderot, “the son of a cutler”, visited workshops, worked at looms and had machines taken apart in order to instruct others, aiming at a storehouse of the arts detailed enough to be reconstructed “after a deluge”; Morley reads the plates as “an animated scene of human life”. The emphasis marks two things for him: honour transferred from knights and friars to “pacific labour”, the “recognition of the basis of a new society” whose neglect by nobles and churchmen “was the fatality of the Revolution”; and “the substitution of interest in things for interest in words”, completing the movement of Bacon and Descartes (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-2). Even here much was book-knowledge at second hand, which Morley accepts as an encyclopædia's legitimate method so long as the writer passes it through his own mind (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-2).

Classification and the Preliminary Discourse

The genealogical tree of knowledge borrowed from Bacon is faulty, Morley says, because it follows the faculties of the understanding rather than objective relations, so that printing lands under Logic and architecture is scattered. Yet the union of secular knowledge in one work gave it a “massive and sumptuous front” that made theology look “a shrivelled thing”, and a “guiding thread” survives the objections. The Preliminary Discourse, mostly D'Alembert's, conveys “ordered growth and measured progress” from Bacon and Descartes to Newton, Locke and Leibnitz, and sets two conditions of success, disregard of authority and avoidance of premature system: “the spirit of system, yet never to construct a system”. Both told against theology (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-2).

Religion, discussion and toleration

Morley holds that accounts of the Encyclopædia as preaching dogmatic atheism “almost universally” misrepresent it. Its attack was social, directed at sacerdotalism, and worked indirectly: through scientific exposition readers “lose their interest, rather than their belief, in mysteries”. The editors wanted many-sided discussion (the Fermes Générales article carries a promise of a contrary Régie article that, after “eight years of storm”, was never properly written). The Encyclopædists were the most ardent propagators of toleration in a France of the wheel and the persecution of Protestants; the Tolérance article reproduces Locke and Rousseau's Social Contract on atheists, while Diderot's Intolérance is a run of angry maxims. Morley judges that this plea inflamed the churchmen more than the attack on dogma, “for most ecclesiastics valued power more dearly than truth” (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-2).

Diderot's own articles

Diderot's contributions fill more than four large volumes of his collected works. Morley finds them disproportionate and digressive (“The confusion is immense”), often essays rather than entries, with tolerance and suspended judgment insinuated past the censor, as in Aius-Locutius (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-1). Much is compiled from Bayle, Brucker, Deslandes and others, with visible inconsistencies such as the Wolffian definition in Philosophy set against the social portrait in Philosopher; the Spinosa and Leibnitz articles show his vigour and his limits (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-2). The Liberty article is, for Morley, an insincere concession to authority; the political articles show a property-minded, non-democratic Montesquieuan who nonetheless anticipates Cobden on commerce (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-3). See denis-diderot and censorship-and-insinuated-doctrine-morley.

Morley's assessment

Morley rejects the view that the Encyclopædia was merely a siege-engine: that is true of one part, not the most important. Its aim was “practice, not literature but life”, the raising of society from the paralysis imposed by civil and ecclesiastical authority, and the tools built for attack were equally suited to disinterested research in a happier season. Diderot wanted man to be the common centre from which all its lines radiate, and called an encyclopædia the work of centuries, addressed to posterity; Morley hears in this “the voice of the new time” (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-2). What it failed to be, in Morley's positivist judgement, was a conception of the unity of the intellectual history of man, treating religious and other systems as aspects of intellectual effort rather than as superstition; Diderot, the one Philosopher able to grasp that, never rose above mechanical arrangement. An urgent social task excused the lack, and Morley pictures the work as a monumental ruin of a stronghold from which a band went out to strike a blow for humanity and truth (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-3).

World Machines resonances, with the notes' hedges

The notes read the Encyclopædia as a knowledge infrastructure only with care, because Morley never frames it so. Its plates and trade articles made the realm's productive workings readable and reusable, which resonates with Legibility and composable-knowledge, though Morley's frame is moral and social honour for labour, not administrative legibility (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-2). Its fiscal articles made the old regime's workings readable and thereby indictable, again a rhyme Morley does not draw (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-1). Morley's explanation of why it could not be moved to Petersburg or Berlin, that it depended on compositors, engravers, looms, engines and processes found only in France, casts it as an enterprise rooted in a place (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-4). Its many independent hands rhyme loosely with the history-machine idea of a paradigm assembled from separately developing parts, though Morley gives a roll of honour, not a theory (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-2). And Morley's own picture of its texture, disproportion and immense confusion, is a counterweight to any tidy image of it as a classification machine (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-1). The aspiration it shares with Bacon's reorganisation of the sciences is the one this wiki calls the universal-library.
