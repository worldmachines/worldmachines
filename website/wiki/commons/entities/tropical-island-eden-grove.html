The Tropical Island as Eden and Laboratory (Grove)

Commons · Entities · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Grove's tropical island as a double object: an Edenic and Utopian screen for European fantasy, and a bounded microcosm small enough to be mentally circumscribed, where the ecological costs of colonisation became visible within a lifetime and so could be diagnosed, legislated and, later, generalised.

Source: grove-green-imperialism. Grove treats the island, with the garden, as one of two “symbolic (or even totemic) forms”: “an easily conceived allegory of a whole world,” manageable in scale and offering “the possibility of redemption” (grove-green-imperialism-introduction). The book's paradox is that the Edenic island discourse reached full flower just as the plantation islands were visibly degrading (grove-green-imperialism-introduction).

Eden displaced outward

As Mediterranean landscapes degraded, the literary imagination moved Eden-like abundance to the Hesperides and then to India, a template Grove later sees reattached to St Helena and Mauritius (grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s1-early-conservation). Columbus's “obstinate conviction” of an earthly paradise beyond the Atlantic is, for Grove, a motive and not mere rhetoric. The Tempest is the discourse's most self-aware text, holding an island that is by turns “everything advantageous to life” and lacking “means to live.” Chapbooks and poets then fixed a stock vocabulary of perpetual spring and Promised Land onto real islands (grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s3-discourse-of-islands). To exhausted sailors St Helena was paradise because it could water and revictual a fleet, and in Mandelslo's account man's presence improves its fruitfulness, the inverse of the story that follows. Huguenot refugees then separated the earthly paradise from the Fall, admiring Rodriguez's “wilderness... undisturbed by man” (grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s4-st-helena-mauritius-insular).

The bounded microcosm

An island was “knowable in terms of the ease with which the island could be mentally circumscribed” and offered fewer risks than a continent (grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s2-island-environments-1400-1660). That boundedness made it diagnostic. Metropolitan pressure built gradually, but “ecological pressures at the colonial periphery were felt far more rapidly and catastrophically.” Barbados went from first English settlement (1627) to near-total deforestation (1665) in one generation. Grove extends the point beyond Europe: “the significance of the island situation in stimulating conservationist thinking was a common factor in most early conservation thinking,” including Tokugawa Japan (grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s6-ecological-pressures). St Helena's paradise degraded within the lifetimes of single observers (grove-green-imperialism-ch03-s1-st-helena-1). On French Mauritius “the perceptually strictly finite nature of the island's resources” produced regulation decades before any climatic theory (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s1-french-mauritius). Even the Cape settlement, walled in by hedges, became “an administrative island,” and its conservation regime ended once the frontier opened new timber in 1712 (grove-green-imperialism-ch03-s4-het-paradijs).

A literary lineage that became policy

Godwin's The Man in the Moone (1638) used St Helena to dramatise “the limits of the earth itself” (grove-green-imperialism-ch03-s1-st-helena-1). The robinsonnade runs from thomas-more and more-utopia through Neville, Vairasse, Leguat and Defoe, whose Crusoe setting was modelled on a Tobago real-estate tract, to jean-jacques-rousseau (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s7-defoe-rousseau-commerson, grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s8-crusoe-rousseau). Grove's claim is that environmentalism was born from a marriage of physiocracy and this French Utopian obsession with islands: “for an island to be a Utopia,” the laws of nature had to be obeyed. Commerson renamed Tahiti “Utopia,” and his praise of Tahitian tree-planting reappears in Mauritius's ordinance a year later (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s9-commerson-utopian-science). Poivre told the colonists they lived in a “physical and social paradise” (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s5-poivre-programmes). Bernardin de Saint-Pierre took the island as his “motto,” with a hydrological frontispiece of Samos (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s10-bernardin).

Where the Eden broke

On inhabited islands the discourse curdled. In the Ceded Islands, Grove writes, the noble savage died “a violent metaphorical death” in war with the black Caribs (grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s2-soame-jenyns). Mauritius itself was a slave colony, “far from being a Utopia” (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s9-commerson-utopian-science).

The limit of the island

Islands forced the state to act quickly; continents did not. On the continent “the strength of any environmentalist message had to rely, instead, on the power of ideas and the institutional influence of scientists,” which is why diffusion took half a century longer (grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s1-banks-professionalisation). Islands proved the mechanism locally but could not certify it as a law until Humboldt (grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s6-humboldtian-diffusion). Their evidence outlived them: Forster read Table Mountain as “an island” where processes could be watched (grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s2-forsters-resolution), and Darwin carried Beatson's St Helena Tracts on the Beagle and called the island “a little world, within itself” (grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s5-burchell-beatson, grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s7-wider-perspective).

World Machines resonances, with their hedges

The notes repeatedly read the island as a bounded test bed where a system's feedback surfaces fast. They add that the legibility here is perceptual and pre-administrative, an island easy to hold in the mind, not the cadastral legibility of Legibility and legibility-as-machine-core (grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s2-island-environments-1400-1660). One note reads the island discourse as making islands thinkable as measurable before any state acted on them, a precondition in the sense of legibility-as-precondition-not-cause, and marks that as its own inference (grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s4-st-helena-mauritius-insular). Grove's own vocabulary is risk: rates of change mattered once they could be “noticed, quantified and then worried about” (grove-green-imperialism-conclusion).
