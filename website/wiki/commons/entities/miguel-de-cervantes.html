Miguel de Cervantes Saavedra (1547–1616)

Commons · Entities · updated 23 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Miguel de Cervantes Saavedra (1547-1616) - descendant of real Castilian knights-errant, wounded at Lepanto, five years a captive in Algiers, failed dramatist and tax collector, who gathered Don Quixote incidentally, treated it as a stepchild, and finished it only when a forged sequel forced him to kill his hero.

Read via: John Ormsby's prefatory biography and critical essay to his 1885 translation of cervantes-don-quixote, plus Cervantes's own prefaces to both Parts.

The evidentiary problem

Ormsby's biography opens by dramatising an absence: "Four generations had laughed over 'Don Quixote' before it occurred to anyone to ask" who wrote it, and by then "all traces of the personality of Cervantes had ... disappeared" (cervantes-don-quixote-front-cervantes-life-1). The essay's method is therefore archaeological — separating a small residue of document from a much larger accretion of conjecture that has hardened into pseudo-fact. It is worth reading as a case study in how a canonical figure's actual life dissolves into legend the moment record-keeping fails him.

Descent from real chivalry

The biography spends unusual energy establishing that the Cervantes family were authentic Castilian knights-errant — landed gentry who fought the Moors, held castles, took their name from a castle called Cervatos — before the house "tapered away" like a pyramid into the poverty of his own household. This is not genealogy for its own sake: Ormsby uses it to license the novel. A man descended from real chivalry, watching his house dwindle to nothing, had personal rather than merely literary standing to judge "the sham chivalry of the romances." The same passage places his boyhood at a hinge — "The old chivalrous Spain had passed away. The new Spain was the mightiest power the world had seen since the Roman Empire" — with Ferdinand and Ximenez gutting the free constitutions of Castile and Aragon while the printing press flooded the country with chivalric romance. An old legitimating order dying while its debased cultural residue saturates the market is the exact soil the novel grows in.

Lepanto and Algiers

Feverish and told to stay below at Lepanto, he came up and fought anyway, "saying he preferred death in the service of God and the King to health," taking three gunshot wounds and permanently losing the use of his left hand. Five years' captivity in Algiers followed, marked by repeated escape plots in which he insisted, under threat of impalement and two thousand blows of the stick, that he alone had planned everything — sparing his fellow captives, who deposed on oath that in him they "found father and mother" (cervantes-don-quixote-front-cervantes-life-1).

Both experiences re-enter the fiction directly. The Captive's tale at the inn — service under the Duke of Alva, Lepanto, capture, the baño, the ransom economy, the renegade go-between, the escape with Zoraida — is the novel's most explicit autobiographical insertion, and it is staged, pointedly, as a "true story" offered in rebuttal to the romances (cervantes-don-quixote-p1-38-40, cervantes-don-quixote-p1-41-42). And when Avellaneda mocked the maimed hand, Cervantes answered that he would rather have his share in that battle again than be whole and absent from it — wounds legible as honour to those who know how to read them (cervantes-don-quixote-transition-part2-dedication-preface).

The book he did not mean to write

The later biography is a string of failures: a stalled military career after the crippled hand, an unprofitable first novel (the Galatea), three unsuccessful years as a dramatist, tax-collecting for the crown in Granada, and a debtor's prison term (cervantes-don-quixote-front-cervantes-life-2). Ormsby's claim is that the novel's celebrated texture — inn life, wayside characters, hostelry art, reapers listening to a romance read aloud — was gathered incidentally, on those humiliating collection journeys, before it was ever organised into a book. Cervantes's real ambition was the stage: to be "the Aeschylus of Spain." He wrote and rewrote Don Quixote "by fits and starts," never corrected the printer's errors, called himself "more a stepfather than a father" to it, and treated it as a libro de entretenimiento.

He also reviewed his own earlier romance inside the fiction: the curate, sorting the library for burning, judges that the Galatea "presents us with something but brings nothing to a conclusion: we must wait for the Second Part it promises" (cervantes-don-quixote-p1-06-09).

Two prefaces, two attitudes to authority

The Part I Preface stages Cervantes's anxiety about publishing without the era's scholarly ornaments, then lets a visiting friend supply a satirical recipe for faking them — marginal Latin tags, an author-catalogue copied wholesale "from A to Z" — on the explicit ground that none of it needs to be true, only to "give a surprising look of authority to your book," since "no one will trouble himself to verify" it. The same friend then states the novel's aim without irony: it is "from beginning to end, an attack upon the books of chivalry." The unit closes on a straight, self-abasing dedication to the Duke of Béjar, sitting in unresolved tension with 250 lines mocking exactly that kind of performed deference (cervantes-don-quixote-front-preface-commendatory-dedication).

The Part II dedication makes the economics explicit: an imaginary Chinese emperor offers a college chair and no travel money, and Cervantes turns him down for the Count of Lemos, who actually pays — "Emperor for Emperor and Monarch for Monarch, I have at Naples the great Count of Lemos." Literary survival under absolutism runs through personal protection, not markets or institutions (cervantes-don-quixote-transition-part2-dedication-preface).

Avellaneda, and the ending as property enforcement

The 1614 spurious "Second Volume" published under the name Alonso Fernández de Avellaneda is, in Ormsby's reading, the reason the real Part II exists: robbed of the leisure to meander, Cervantes "set himself to finish off his task and protect Don Quixote in the only way he could, by killing him" (cervantes-don-quixote-front-cervantes-life-2). Cervantes answers the insult with two folk parables about how much easier it is to break a thing than to make one — the madman who inflates dogs ("Do your worships think, now, that it is an easy thing to blow up a dog?"), the madman with the stone slab who takes his beating and never drops another — and vows to leave Don Quixote "dead and buried, so that no one may dare to bring forward any further evidence against him."

He then prosecutes the quarrel inside the novel: an inn conversation about the counterfeit reroutes the plot from Saragossa to Barcelona (cervantes-don-quixote-p2-57-59), and Don Álvaro Tarfe, a character borrowed out of Avellaneda, is made to swear before a village magistrate that this Quixote is not the one he knew (cervantes-don-quixote-p2-71-74). It is an early and vivid case of a named author fighting to keep a text legible against its own runaway copies — the condition print-culture-and-the-individual-author describes from the other side.

Ormsby's verdicts

Two closing arguments in the biography are worth keeping. Against the consensus that his was an unhappy life, Ormsby insists on fortitude rather than temperament: the same "indomitable spirit that kept him from despair in the bagnios of Algiers" carried him through failed plays and poverty, leaving him at sixty-eight "full of life and hope and plans for the future as a boy of eighteen" — endurance as a practice rather than a disposition, adjacent to Conatus though Ormsby has no such vocabulary. And against the charge that Spain neglected him, he argues that hostility from romance-readers, the Lope circle and the culto poets is simply what a book that "turned their favourite reading into ridicule" should expect, closing with Si monumentum quoeris, circumspice over an inadequate Madrid bronze: the book in every bookseller's shop is the monument.

Ormsby's critical essay adds the compositional portrait — Sancho added only after a landlord's offhand remark, the found-manuscript frame bolted on partway through, Part I's hero flat and borrowed where Part II's is discreto and individual (cervantes-don-quixote-front-essay-don-quixote). Ormsby's own translator's creed, stated in the introduction, is the same virtue read back onto Cervantes's prose: plainness, no archaism, no comic inflation, "unsmiling gravity" preserved at all costs (cervantes-don-quixote-front-introduction).
