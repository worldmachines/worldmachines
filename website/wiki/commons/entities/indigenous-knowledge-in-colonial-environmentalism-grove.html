Indigenous Knowledge in Colonial Environmentalism (Grove)

Commons · Entities · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Grove's claim that non-European knowledge structured, rather than decorated, colonial environmental science and policy: Malayali and Ezhava botany behind the Hortus Malabaricus and Linnaeus, Chinese and Indian land-use knowledge behind Poivre, indigenous testimony behind Indian desiccation arguments, and the Sind amirs' reserves as the working model for colonial forestry, each with Grove's own qualifications.

Source: grove-green-imperialism. From the Introduction on, Grove counts “the diffusion of Indian environmental philosophy into Western epistemology” among the things the Anglo-American story leaves out (grove-green-imperialism-introduction). The Conclusion says that “the significance of initiatives taken by local actors on the basis of local and indigenous knowledge cannot be overestimated” (grove-green-imperialism-conclusion). The claim runs through botany, land use, climate evidence and forest administration.

Botany: texts organised on non-European precepts

Garcia da Orta's Coloquios (Goa, 1563) and van Reede's Hortus Malabaricus are, for Grove, “profoundly indigenous texts,” compilations of Middle Eastern and South Asian ethnobotany “organised on essentially non-European precepts.” Almost all later European texts on South Asian botany “retain [their] essential indigenous structure” (grove-green-imperialism-ch02-s1-south-west-india). Van Reede found his Brahmin consultants' botany “merely academic,” dependent on restating old texts. Within two years he shifted to Malayali sources, above all the Ezhava physician Itti Achuden and toddy-tapper-caste tree climbers, who selected, named and explained the roughly 780 species. Leiden's editors preserved the Ezhava sequence against their own instincts, and Linnaeus, Adanson, Jussieu and later Roxburgh and Hooker built on it. Since Achuden's papers are lost, the printed Hortus is “the only faithful textual record” of that knowledge (grove-green-imperialism-ch02-s2-low-caste-epistemologies). Grove adds that the Portuguese networks were “marginal extensions of an existing Indian Ocean system of plant transfer.”

Land use and policy

  • The Cape. Indigenous constructions of nature “underlay almost the whole structure” of the Dutch response to the tropics (grove-green-imperialism-ch03-s2-dutch-cape-mauritius). Van Reede's Malabar training made him object when Cape woodmen called indigenous trees by European names (grove-green-imperialism-ch03-s4-het-paradijs).
  • Poivre. His views were “strongly affected by an acquaintance with the Indian, Zoroastrian and Chinese systems of knowledge” (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s1-french-mauritius). Rejecting Buffon's degeneracy theory let him treat Chinese, Cochin-Chinese and Malabari irrigation and tree-planting as technically authoritative (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s4-poivre-origins).
  • India. Hindu, Muslim, Zoroastrian and Ezhava concepts were “more important to the evolution of company environmental policy than any set of ideas imported from outside India,” with Humboldtian ideology itself mediated through them (grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s1-company-forests-1). Grove traces Humboldt's holism to Hindu monism transmitted through Herder (grove-green-imperialism-introduction, grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s6-humboldtian-diffusion). Roxburgh's teak plantations depended on indigenous skills and owed nothing to German forestry (grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s2-roxburgh-1). Wallich invoked “the judicious Rajahs of Rampore and Rohilcund,” and Sleeman came to tree-planting through Sadi's poetry and the Hindu kalpa briksha (grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s2-roxburgh-2).

Evidence the surgeons could not supply themselves

Roxburgh reconstructed the 1685–7 drought from the testimony of “the Rajah of Pittenpore's family Brahmen” (grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s2-roxburgh-1). A February 1846 letter reporting Konkani belief that stream flow follows deforestation, and Parsi observation of declining Nilgiri rainfall, gave Gibson the regional-climate evidence his own science could not verify. The note flags this as indigenous rather than European in origin (grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s3-lobbying).

Models, not legitimation

Grove's strongest claim is about forest administration: “the critical influence of pre-colonial forest practices in providing models (rather than just a legitimation) for early management methods in colonial state forestry has not been properly appreciated.” The Amirs of Sind reafforested over a million acres into shikargah reserves. Napier administered the existing system directly after annexation, and until about 1850 it was “the only working example of formalised state forest reservation” available to the Company. Even Brandis wrote that “we owe the maintenance of forests in Sindh and of the rukhs in the Punjab entirely to the actions taken by the former rulers.” Before 1865 Bombay conservancy officers were named according to indigenous usage (grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s6-dalhousie). On St Vincent the black Caribs argued their land claim through a 1700 boundary line, and Grove corrects the commissioner who called this “the short memories of a savage people”: the social memory was “long rather than 'short'” (grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s3-carib-resistance).

Grove's own qualifications

  • Caste matters. It is specifically low-caste, practically trained knowledge that carries the evidentiary weight, not “Indian knowledge” in general. Brahminical teaching sat uneasily with the empirical stance (grove-green-imperialism-ch02-s2-low-caste-epistemologies). The note on this chapter says the caveat should travel whenever the book's indigenous-knowledge claims are generalised (grove-green-imperialism-ch02-s1-south-west-india).
  • Appropriation. Indigenous knowledge kept flowing into colonial floras but “frequently turned to more mercantile uses” (grove-green-imperialism-ch02-s3-botanical-networks).
  • No golden age. Grove attacks the “Merrie India” picture as Orientalist (grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s1-company-forests-1), and the amirs' system was “run a great deal more tightly and unsympathetically to small farmers” than early colonial rule (grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s6-dalhousie); see conservation-as-control-grove.

World Machines resonances, with their hedges

The notes read the Hortus as knowledge fixed into durable print, touching legibility-as-machine-core, but add that this is scientific and commercial rather than fiscal legibility, and that Grove's interest is whose knowledge survives the fixing (grove-green-imperialism-ch02-s2-low-caste-epistemologies). The Sind case shows a colonial legibility apparatus absorbing an existing indigenous one rather than building from nothing (grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s6-dalhousie). The pre-existing Indian Ocean plant-transfer system fits archaic-globalization-bayly's point that older Afro-Asian networks did work Europeans later claimed.
