Industrious Revolutions (Bayly)

Commons · Entities · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Bayly's generalization of Jan de Vries's “industrious revolution” into a worldwide, pre-industrial motor of change in household labour, consumption and status ambition, found from south China to Massachusetts, paired with a last “great domestication” of forest and steppe.

Jan de Vries coined “industrious revolution” for a rise, from roughly 1650, in the labour, consumption and status ambition of “middling people”. bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world expands the idea into a general, pre-industrial motor of change “from south China to Massachusetts”. In Bayly's version it is driven as much by circulating “discourse” about how ruling classes lived as by material scarcity (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-intro-thematic-problems-of-history). He uses the plural and treats it as one of the book's deepest drivers, so the page carries his suffix.

What it is

  • Demand-side, not a revolution in production. Families bought in goods and services, which generated new time discipline and new sociability. Bayly's paradigm case is “the invention of breakfast”: coffee, tea, sugar and disposable crockery. An industrious revolution does not reliably predict industrialization. It appears without early factory production in the Low Countries, Germany, coastal North America, Mexico and coastal Brazil (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch02-old-regime-industrious-revolutions).
  • Not a European monopoly. In China, mandarin taste spread down the social scale, luxury consumption became “objectified”, and women were drawn into artisanal work. In Japan, dress and swordsmanship mandated for samurai boosted metallurgy. In India, Iran and the Ottoman lands, revisionist evidence (including Parthasarathi's wage comparisons for South Asian weavers) undercuts the old “tribute economy” model (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch02-old-regime-industrious-revolutions).
  • Destabilizing. Bayly holds these uneven intensifications, not industrial capital as such, responsible for the “many localized conflicts spun out of control across the world between 1720 and 1820”, which forced states into a defensive “elephantiasis” (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-intro-thematic-problems-of-history).

Its companion: the great domestication

From about 1650, and faster after 1770, pioneer peasants, herders turned settlers, and expanding states pushed cultivation into forest, steppe and pampas, from Siberia to Buenos Aires to New Zealand. The drivers were population recovery after plague retreated, new American food crops, and states' fiscal appetite for taxable, settled subjects. The herdsmen and forest-dwellers who were made sedentary became the coolies and indentured labour of the nineteenth-century commercial economy (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch02-old-regime-industrious-revolutions).

Why Europe's version lasted

The tropical consumables at the heart of the Atlantic industrious revolution rested on Caribbean slavery, which generated “value added” that Chinese, Arab and African merchants could not match. Bayly locates Europe's advantage in social and state context that gave its industrious and later industrial revolutions more cumulative “staying power”, not in essential economic superiority (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch02-old-regime-industrious-revolutions). Japan's stability (primogeniture, rooted leadership) later let it plan industrialization on top of a pre-existing industrious revolution (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch02-european-competitive-advantage-activist-state).

Later appearances in the book

  • The third revolution. After 1780 a worldwide rise of polite, commercial middle classes “more closely represented” an accelerated industrious revolution than a revolution driven by factories. Examples run from Hamburg's revival and the clubs and libraries of Calcutta, Bombay, Penang and Batavia to the middlebrow classical china of josiah-wedgwood (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch03-ideological-origins-third-revolution).
  • Industrialization demoted. Economic historians downgraded the Industrial Revolution to an “industrial evolution” partly by way of de Vries's concept. Bayly pushes back that the change was real, only slow to reveal itself (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch05-industrialization).
  • The Crystal Palace. Europeans, Britons and Americans came to believe they had made a once-and-for-all step up into the modern age. Bayly insists this rested on earlier industrious revolutions shared with Asia (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch04-civil-war-and-convergence).
  • Among the motors. The conclusion names the rise and decline of industrious revolutions, the great domestication and expanding seaborne commerce as the deeper drivers before about 1850. Industrious revolutions here include disciplined daily life, timekeeping, and a global wave of ascetic “methodism”: Pietism, neo-Catholicism, Krio Anglicanism, Chinese New Sect Buddhism and the Faraizi movement (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch13-liberalism-crisis-and-motors-of-change).

World Machines resonance, with its hedge

The concept supports the world-machines insistence on multi-centred, interacting origins rather than a single prime mover. Bayly's register, though, stays comparative-economic. Industrious revolution and great domestication are pre-machine processes on the scale of households and land, not the state-legibility apparatus WMP treats as the core of the modernity-machine (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch02-old-regime-industrious-revolutions).
