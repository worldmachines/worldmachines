Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778)

Commons · Entities · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778) is the originator of modern Kulturkritik, whose rhetoric of nature versus culture — articulated through the figures of the noble savage and the English garden — established the oxymoronic template for critiquing modernity through modernity's own tools.

Biographical Context

  • Born Geneva; closest European approximation of his own "noble savage" ideal was the Swiss peasant
  • Wrote Discourse on the Sciences and Arts (First Discourse, 1750) and Discourse on the Origin of Inequality (Second Discourse, 1755) — the founding texts of Kulturkritik
  • Julie, or the New Heloise (1761): novel featuring the Elysium garden (Clarens)
  • Emile (1762): novel of education — Emile turned through artifice into a "savage made to inhabit cities"
  • Reveries of a Solitary Walker (posthumous): the St. Pierre Island episode as English garden experience
  • Social Contract (1762): political culmination

Intellectual Position (per Kempf)

Rousseau's Kulturkritik begins with a rhetorical modification: he rephrases the Academy of Dijon's question to include "or corrupt," turning a binary into a confrontation. This sets up the oppositional structure his work will inhabit.

Nature ("l'homme sauvage" = the bête) vs. Culture ("l'homme civil" = the bourgeois). The key antithetical claim: "The Savage lives within himself; the sociable man, always outside of himself" (CW3, p. 66).

What he actually means by "noble savage": not the solitary primitive beast, but the inhabitant of nascent society — "the happiest and most durable epoch" (CW3, p. 48) — "at equal distances from the stupidity of the brutes and the fatal enlightenment of Civil man." The closest existing approximation: the Swiss peasant.

The Rhetorical Structure

Rousseau maintains two mutually exclusive points of view simultaneously — like "standing between two mirrors." This enables:

  1. Self-projection: the bête as utopia, the bourgeois as dystopia
  2. Self-reflection: foreignizing culture (wound) and familiarizing nature (noble) — the reciprocal mirroring that makes critical self-reflection possible

The bête is explicitly admitted to be a construct: "the brute no longer exists, ... perhaps never existed [and] probably never will exist" (CW3, p. 13). Its function is purely rhetorical — to maximize the contrast needed for comprehensive critique of the bourgeois.

The Homeopathic Paradox

"Healing us from reason through reason." Rousseau's own English garden experiences use the tools of culture (magnifying glass, Linnaeus's Systema naturae, the botanizer's trained eye) to return to a nature that was supposedly prior to culture. His solutions:

  • The St. Pierre Island experience (Reveries): natural happiness experienced by a man equipped with scientific tools and cultural sensitivity
  • The Elysium garden (Julie): Julie admits she created only "the illusion of natural wilderness" — "Nature did it all, but under my direction"
  • The citoyen: Rousseau's urban ideal — achieves natural inner harmony through unnatural means
  • Emile: turned into "a savage made to inhabit cities" through elaborate artifice

Influence and Lineage

  • Precursor: The frontispiece of the Discourse on Inequality (the "noble savage" returning to nature) was drawn from a travel account, showing the "imperial eyes" problem built into the concept from the start
  • Schiller: Acknowledges Rousseau's Kulturkritik initiated processes culminating in the French Revolution; positions his own work as "an answer to the French Revolution gone awry"
  • Goethe: Writing to his sister in 1767: "Don't laugh about this seemingly foolish philosophy, the seemingly paradoxical pronouncements, they are the most splendid truths"
  • Kempf's reassessment: Contrary to scholarship that reads Kulturkritik as straightforward binary opposition, Rousseau's rhetorical strategy posits a reciprocity of nature and culture rather than a simple binary — what looks like opposition is actually a site of convergence and divergence simultaneously

Key Texts (referenced in Kempf)

The Confessions as the model of nineteenth-century autobiography (Gay)

In gay-bourgeois-experience-v4, Peter Gay reads a different facet of Rousseau from the Kulturkritik reading above: not nature against culture, but the founder of modern confession. The Confessions opens "I have conceived an enterprise that has no model whatever and that... will have no imitator," replacing Augustine's "Great art Thou, O Lord" with the first person singular and divine judgment with the verdict of Posterity. Gay reads this as the decisive break that makes it "a secular document for a secularizing age," continuous with Franklin and Gibbon rather than Augustine. Victorian autobiographers could not escape its gravity even as they recoiled from its "unsavory incidents." George Sand called her own book a "history of my life (not confessions)," Shelley called Rousseau's disclosures "falsehoods" and De Quincey found them "revolting," yet each measured his or her stance against his. Rousseau "found himself immensely interesting," and, Gay notes drily, "the Victorians proved him right." In Gay's genealogy michel-de-montaigne is the rare precursor who had gone as far, with almost no successors until Rousseau (gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-ch2-in-rousseaus-shadow).

Candor as exposure, set against Goethe's craft

Gay's comparison of the Confessions with johann-wolfgang-von-goethe's Dichtung und Wahrheit sets Rousseau, who "treated discretion as the enemy" and wrote at a headlong pace from flashes of memory, against Goethe's "canny indiscretion" and checking of documents. Gay refuses a contemporary reviewer's verdict in Rousseau's favor: "Each was honest and devious in its own way; each at once constructed, and sincerely groped for, its author's past" (gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-ch2-between-probes-and-poses). An unpublished fragment of the Confessions also supplies the genre's founding account of "double consciousness," painting "doubly the state of my soul," at the moment of the event and the moment of writing. Gay notes that "this did not trouble him," though it troubled his Victorian successors (gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-ch2-second-thoughts).

Other appearances in The Naked Heart

La Nouvelle Héloïse is one of the texts of the eighteenth-century "storm of sensibility" that prepared music's promotion to the century's organ of inward feeling (gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-art-of-listening-1). Gay credits Rousseau as the source of the French romantics' conviction that "everything is at bottom linked to politics" (gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-ch1-self-in-politics). Wordsworth denounced his "paradoxical reveries," while Shelley later praised his "exertions" alongside Locke and Voltaire, evidence that the romantics could not agree about the Enlightenment they rejected (gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-ch1-imagination-unleashed). Rousseau also stands, with Richardson and Goethe, in the epistolary-novel tradition that fed the romantic confessional letter (gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-ch6-spirit-of-the-letter-1).

Grove: Rousseau and the first conservation programme

Richard Grove's Green Imperialism (grove-green-imperialism) reads Rousseau as an environmental influence, not only a Kulturkritiker. He pushes back against LaFreniere's description of Rousseau as merely a “pre-environmentalist” and argues that Rousseau was tied more directly than recognised to an actual, practical conservation programme (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s7-defoe-rousseau-commerson).

The island as the place of nature. Grove traces Rousseau's attachment to Robinson Crusoe to a Calvinist inheritance: the “citizen of Geneva” bred in a self-styled New Jerusalem identified with Crusoe's earthly Utopia. In La Nouvelle Héloïse, Saint-Preux moves from Geneva to the garden at Etange, then to the Valais, and finally to Tinian and Juan Fernandez. Grove argues that the “wildness” of Anson's islands fed forward into Commerson's project (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s8-crusoe-rousseau). On the Ile de Saint-Pierre Rousseau took “the entire island as my botanical garden,” a private, pre-institutional version of the colonial garden networks.

The erosion parable. In the Reveries Rousseau describes the smaller island in the lake of Bienne being stripped of earth to repair storm damage to the larger: “the substance of the weak is always employed to the profit of the powerful.” Grove calls this “precisely the phenomenon which had concerned Poivre.” The difference is that Poivre turned the intuition into a “moral economy of the state,” an option “not available to Rousseau in view of his prior criticism of the state” (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s8-crusoe-rousseau). This sits close to the homeopathic paradox above: here the cure for nature's abuse runs through the state apparatus Rousseau distrusted.

The translators. Commerson's 1769 letter to the Mercure turned Rousseau's “vague and book-learned perorations” on tropical islands into elite fashion (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s7-defoe-rousseau-commerson). His description of Tahiti draws on Rousseau's state-of-nature vocabulary and cites him directly (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s9-commerson-utopian-science). Bernardin de Saint-Pierre, Rousseau's intimate, drew his attachment to the island and the robinsonnade from that closeness (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s10-bernardin). Grove reads Rousseau and Linnaeus as sharing an ambition for a total ordering of the world's organisms (grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s1-banks-professionalisation).

Limits Grove sets. Poivre himself rejected both Buffon's degeneracy theory and the vogue for an idealised primitive, so his fit with Rousseau is partial (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s4-poivre-origins). Grove also declines to reduce Alexander Anderson's St Vincent environmentalism to a borrowing from Rousseau, insisting on its “distinctively empirical and botanical consciousness” (grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s5-anderson-critique). His general verdict is that early environmentalism fused Rousseauist language with Newtonian science rather than setting them against each other (grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s11-revolution-global-context). The noble-savage reading on this page has a harder colonial counterpart in Grove's Caribbean, where the discourse did not survive contact with inhabited islands (tropical-island-eden-grove).

Douglass: Rousseau against Hobbes and 'Hobbism'

Robin Douglass's Rousseau and Hobbes (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes) reads Rousseau not as a Kulturkritiker or a confessional writer but as a political philosopher in running argument with thomas-hobbes, and with the French and Genevan caricature of him (see hobbism-douglass). Douglass's thesis is that Rousseau's anti-Hobbesian polemic, above all in the Discours sur l'inégalité, was aimed as much at Hobbes's natural-law critics (Pufendorf, Barbeyrac, Burlamaqui) and at the doux commerce theorists as at Hobbes. Rousseau took all of them to rest the social order on a Hobbesian picture of miserable natural man, and he exposed them as more Hobbesian than they would admit (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-hobbes-and-rousseau, douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-engagement-in-context). Douglass refuses both the "closet Hobbesian" Rousseau of Strauss and his successors and a simply anti-Hobbesian one. In his view Rousseau shared Hobbes's diagnosis of politics and opposed his solutions (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-oppositions-and-affinities). On the evidence, Rousseau most probably knew only De cive, in Sorbière's translation, plus commonplaces about Hobbes (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-methodological-problems).

Natural goodness as harmony, and a secularized Fall (Douglass)

Douglass gives a reading of natural goodness that differs from the rhetorical "noble savage" above. For him natural goodness means the absence of contradiction, within man and between man and his conditions. The golden age of small societies is a normative model of that harmony, not a destination (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-harmony-contradiction-hobbesian-moment). Rousseau's principle took shape where the luxury debate met natural law. Separating amour de soi-même from amour-propre, and placing pity before amour-propre, historicizes the passions Hobbes had called natural (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-natural-goodness-golden-age; see amour-propre-douglass). The war of all against all appears in the Discours only late, as the product of inequality, and serves to expose Hobbesian justifications of society as a trick of the rich on the poor (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-rousseaus-critique-reappraised). Douglass reads the whole genealogy as a secularized Augustinian Fall in which Original Sin becomes entrenched inequality and luxury (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-neutralizing-amour-propre). Nature stays a regulative standard throughout, including in Du contrat social, against Straussian readings that have Rousseau abandon it (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-law-nature-denaturing).

Free will as the hinge of Rousseau's politics (Douglass)

Against Masters and the readings that detach the Savoyard vicar's profession of faith from Rousseau's own views, Douglass holds that Rousseau was a dualist who affirmed free will, and that his political thought "makes little sense" without it (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-nature-free-will-passions). Free will grounds the distinction between moral and physical force. Force yields only necessity, never obligation, so freedom is an inalienable gift of nature alongside life (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-free-will-moral-nature, douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-free-will-slavery-obligation). Douglass's free will is thin, though: the conscious power to resist, acquiesce in, or choose among inclinations, not Kantian self-legislation. That is why it is compatible with the heavy will-formation of Emile and of republican education (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-free-will-and-virtue).

The general will and sovereignty inverted (Douglass)

On Douglass's reading Du contrat social sets out to place law above man, a problem Rousseau himself compared to squaring the circle (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-sovereignty-and-law-opening). Rousseau keeps Hobbes's absolute, rights-free sovereignty and body-politic vocabulary, but makes sovereignty inalienable, unrepresentable and indivisible, lodged in the people's general will. The sovereign is "absolute in power but limited in the form of its actions" (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-sovereignty-inverted; see sovereignty-inverted-douglass). The general will is each citizen's own will as citizen, so obedience to law is dependence on things, not men. Douglass takes a middle position between pluralistic and total-unity readings of the private/general will conflict (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-freedom-preserved). The concept itself took shape against Diderot's universal general will in "Droit naturel" (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-diderot-encyclopedie, douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-state-of-nature-to-political-society). In the civil-religion chapter Rousseau praises Hobbes as the only Christian author who saw both "the evil and the remedy", though he turns Hobbes's negative unity into a cultivated love of fatherland (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-unity-civil-religion).

Ordering the passions: love of fatherland against fear (Douglass)

For Douglass the second half of Rousseau's project is "making men what one needs them to be" (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-ordering-the-passions-opening). Reason judges and orders the passions but cannot master them, so politics must speak to the heart (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-reason-and-the-passions). Public education generalizes amour de soi-même into love of fatherland and turns amour-propre's positive branch, pride, toward civic glory (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-love-of-fatherland). Hobbes also ordered the passions for unity, but through fear, which Rousseau thought could only restrain evil, not inspire good (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-hobbes-and-fear, douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-of-love-and-fear; see love-and-fear-douglass). Douglass's closing verdict limits the vision. The republic suits only naturally good peoples not yet corrupted by commerce, luxury and inequality, so it was never a live option for large commercial states. Its critique of commercial society, however, stayed pointed enough to demand adam-smith's reply (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-republics-naturally-good). A hedged reading of this programme against the WMP legibility frame is at making-a-people-one-douglass.

Rousseau in Wulf's Jena (Magnificent Rebels)

Rousseau is a background presence in Andrea Wulf's wulf-magnificent-rebels, appearing in three roles.

The general will. Recounting the Revolution's first principles, Wulf notes that the 'general will' was based on Rousseau (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-1). August Wilhelm Schlegel's Athenaeum fragment carried it into language, which should be 'republicanised through the power of the general will' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch09-sublime-impertinence-2).

Emile and fatherhood. Wulf portrays Goethe and Schiller as doting, non-punitive fathers who let their sons 'roam freely in mind and in body', and ties this explicitly to Emile and the view that children are good by nature (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch04-intellectual-friction-2).

Women. In Wulf's account of women's legal subordination, Rousseau, 'progressive on childhood', nonetheless wanted women 'completely submissive'; the note reads this as equal faculties but unequal roles, the foil against which Caroline Schlegel and Caroline von Humboldt took control of their own lives (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch06-splendid-circle-2).

Morley's Diderot and the Encyclopaedists (Vol. I): Rousseau beside Diderot

John Morley, who had written a separate volume on Rousseau, gives him a running part in morley-diderot-v1 as Diderot's friend, his rival, and in Morley's reading the starting-point of a reaction inside the Enlightenment. Morley's judgements are his own, those of a Victorian liberal-positivist.

Friendship and Vincennes. Rousseau brought Diderot and Condillac together when all three were penniless in Paris (morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-1). Morley quotes Rousseau's own account of visiting the imprisoned Diderot at Vincennes in 1749 and conceiving his essay against civilisation on the walk there. He weighs the story that Diderot suggested the paradox that the arts and sciences corrupted manners, rates it not improbable, cites Michelet's tribute, but finds the evidence “not decisive”, and thinks it odd that Diderot, promoting the arts and sciences, should have instigated the attack (morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-3). In temperament Morley places Diderot nearer to Rousseau than to Voltaire or Fontenelle, as a man for whom truth was a means of doing good (morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-2).

The rupture. Morley treats the 1756 retreat, the hostility between Grimm and Rousseau, and Diderot's anguished letters, with their mixture of pity and revulsion. His verdict is the same whichever side's story one takes: Diderot was not the real offender, and sentimentalism readily turns into “indulgence to oneself” in place of “upright and manly judgment about others”; Rousseau, he notes, uses the same language about his own heart. Morley also compares Rousseau's refusal of a pension with Voltaire's and D'Alembert's acceptance of Frederick's (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-4). Rousseau is Morley's authority for calling Madame Diderot a shrew (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-1), and Morley sets Diderot's sentimentalism against Rousseau's misanthropic kind (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-2).

A reaction inside the illumination. Morley reads the Letter to D'Alembert on Stage Plays (1758) not as a private quarrel but as the opening of a fundamental divergence within the school: reason subordinated to emotion, industry, science and many-sided ingenuity displaced by dreamy indolence, and the suppression of opinions by the secular arm revived in theistic dress. In his judgement Rousseau's idea prevailed because it fitted the political traditions of France, and he closes with Michelet's image of Danton and Robespierre, both born in 1759, as incarnations of the two principles (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-4). The notes set this beside the counter-enlightenment with the hedge that Morley's frame is moral and political, not a theory of rival world machines.

In the Encyclopædia. Rousseau was among its famous contributors; its Tolérance article oddly reproduces the Social Contract on atheists (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-2); and Morley traces the Legislator article's enthusiasm for Lycurgus, China and Peru to Rousseau's “political sentimentalism” (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-3). He also says the New Heloise owed its fascination to the loosened moral and literary climate he describes around Diderot's 1748 tale (morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-3). The Preface places the Rousseau volume as part of Morley's series on the literary preparation of the Revolution (morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary).

Volume II: Rousseau in Morley's second volume

This section draws on Volume II of Morley's study (morley-diderot-v2). Morley's judgments remain those of a Victorian positivist.

The last quarrel. Diderot's Essay on the Reigns of Claudius and Nero, in its 1782 edition, is so full of replies to Rousseau's partisans, prompted by the prospect of the Confessions, that Morley doubts whether it is an apology for Seneca or a vindication of Diderot. His explanation is psychological: Diderot was indifferent to attacks on his opinions but sensitive to innuendoes against his private benevolence and loyalty. He adds, as a possible further motive, the harm Rousseau's sentimentalism had done to “sound and positive thinking”, and here calls Rousseau the “reactionary philosopher” of the century. The attack was, for Morley, perhaps Diderot's only breach of his own rule of charitable reading (morley-diderot-v2-ch08-closing-years-1).

Sentiment and its rivals. Morley sees a shared cult of the belle âme behind Richardson and the New Heloïse, which adds passion and wild landscape, and conjectures that Diderot's silence on the novel came from contempt for Rousseau's private perversity (morley-diderot-v2-ch02-romance-1). He makes Rousseau's triumph the cost of Helvétius's discredit: “the democracy of sentimentalism” displaced “the democracy of utility”, and the Sovereignty of the People was a sterile rendering of Helvétius's truth (morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-2; see democracy-of-utility-morley). L'Esprit, which Rousseau found detestable, spurred the Savoyard Vicar's Profession of Faith (morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1). The Social Contract was concentrated but dreary to many, where Raynal was popular and concrete (morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-2).

Exceptions and echoes. Morley infers that Wolmar in the New Heloïse is Rousseau's version of Holbach, and credits Rousseau with showing honestly that atheists can live upright lives (morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-1). Rousseau was the “significant exception” among the philosophers who kept silent on Catherine's Partition of Poland (morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-1), and the one insurgent Morley exempts from the general suppression of authorial vanity, as “a reactionist” (morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-1). Jacobi's defence of feeling, part of the reaction Hemsterhuys led, borrowed from Rousseau (morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-2). The parable of the young Mexican in the Conversation with the Maréchale was attributed to Rousseau, who only echoed it in spirit in the Savoyard Vicar (morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-2). Morley says the Supplement to Bougainville touched Diderot “almost as deeply as if he had been Rousseau”, while crediting Diderot with more scepticism; the notes connect this to the noble-savage idea (morley-diderot-v2-ch01-other-dialogues-2).
