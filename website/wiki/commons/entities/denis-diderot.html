Denis Diderot

Commons · Entities · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Denis Diderot (1713–1784), son of a Langres cutler, editor of the Encyclopædia and the one real thinker among the great illuminators in John Morley's account: a sceptic turned materialist who introduced the doctrine of relativity into France, a theorist of the drama, author of Rameau's Nephew, and a generous, dispersive genius better at talk than at books.

This page draws on Volume I (Preface, Chapters I–VIII) of john-morley's study diderot-and-the-encyclopaedists; Volume II material is added in later sections. Morley's judgements are attributed to him; Diderot's words are given as Morley quotes or summarises them.

Langres, family and schooling

Diderot was born at Langres in 1713, of a cutlery-making family. Morley quotes Diderot's own explanation of the Langres character by its violent weather, a head “like the weathercock”, quick in desire and idea but “cumbrous in speech”, and endorses it (“This was thoroughly true”), reading Diderot's versatility, impetuosity and clumsy expression as inheritances. The father in his grinder's apron weeping at his son's school prizes, a Diogenes-like sister and a devout abbé brother are drawn largely in Diderot's own words; in his account religion is the “mischievous art of stifling nature”. Like many eighteenth-century reformers he was a pupil of the Jesuits, who hoped to recruit him. He refused law and medicine, and his father handled his wish to run away tenderly (morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-1).

Paris poverty (1734–1744)

For ten years Diderot lived by tutoring and hack work in Paris garrets: pupils who paid in shirts and chairs, a tutorship quit because “What I want is not to live better, but to avoid dying”, and the Shrove Tuesday swoon from hunger after which he vowed never to refuse help to a living creature. Morley says no oath was more faithfully kept (morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-1). He taught himself English with an Anglo-Latin dictionary, translated for the booksellers (Stanyan's History of Greece) and wrote sermons for the Portuguese colonies, finding serenity in a line of Homer or a problem in geometry (morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-2). Morley reads the Shaftesbury annotations and early pieces as showing Montaigne, Pascal, Augustine, the classics, English literature and above all Bacon, whose ideas he calls “the direct source of the great undertaking of Diderot's life” (morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-2). Morley uses these years to define the new social type of the man of letters; see men-of-letters-as-spiritual-power-morley.

Marriage and household

Penniless, Diderot pressed to marry a seamstress, Antoinette Champion; his father at Langres treated him as a madman, he fell ill, and the two women nursed him into the marriage of 1743. Morley's verdict is that “destiny was hostile”: a dutiful, pious wife whose goodness, in his conjecture, took forms that exasperated a restless man, followed by Diderot's connections with Madame de Puisieux and Mademoiselle Voland. Morley declines to assign blame and argues that it would be as impertinent to charge these irregularities to the new opinions as to charge the vices of Dubois or Louis XV to the old ones (morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-2). In the encyclopædic years the household is a sour one; Morley, following Rousseau, calls Madame Diderot a shrew, while saying blame cannot be exactly divided (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-1).

Early writings: from scepticism toward materialism

After a 1745 version of Shaftesbury's Inquiry concerning Virtue and Merit came the Philosophical Thoughts (1746), burnt by the parliament of Paris, Diderot's first taste of authority's hand (morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-1). The book opens with an apology for strong passions (“Sober passions produce only the commonplace”), which Morley takes as the key-note of Diderot's whole character. It makes scepticism the touchstone, answers Pascal, and attacks revelation on evidential and moral grounds while remaining, Morley stresses, “distinctly theistic”. It also gives the atheist's dice argument more force than the apologist had. The Sufficiency of Natural Religion (1747) holds that revelation adds no moral precept and no intelligible new truth; Morley finds Diderot “content with Shaftesbury's position”, and traces Shaftesbury's lasting mark on his ethics and his criticism of art (morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-2). An early passage quotes physics against atheism, the world being “no longer a god; it is a machine with its cords, its pulleys, its springs, its weights”, which Morley treats as a halting-place, not yet consistent materialism (morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-1).

The Sceptic's Walk was seized by a police agent before publication. Morley judges the allegory poor but values its dialogue, in which an atheist argues that an observer who knows a tooth or two of one wheel of an “unknown machine” cannot infer its maker; Morley cannot say how far Diderot himself went, but detects a leaning toward an eternal, self-moving matter. He condemns the obscene tale of 1748 (aesthetically, he insists), says Diderot soon repented, and sets La Religieuse apart as a serious pathological study. Diderot's reflections on women, quoted at length, mix what a modern reader will find essentialising with strong sympathy for women's subjection under the civil laws; Morley credits him with realism and pity, not a programme (morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-3).

The Letter on the Blind and Vincennes (1749–1751)

The Letter on the Blind (1749), prompted by Réaumur's operation on a blind girl, which Diderot was refused leave to attend, was in Morley's judgement the first effective introduction into France of the relativity of knowledge, used as a weapon against an establishment resting on anthropomorphism and the absolute. Its deathbed speech for the blind geometer Saunderson attacks final causes and conjectures that only self-sustaining combinations of matter survived. Diderot's reply to Voltaire disavowed atheism, though Morley suspects the disavowal and quotes the line that belief or disbelief in God is “not important at all” (morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-2; see relativity-against-catholic-metaphysics-morley). Morley credits the Letter's suggestion of a tactile written language for the deaf-blind, later realised by Dr. Howe of Boston, and infers from weekly dinners that Diderot's talk helped move Condillac toward a pure sensationalism (morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-2).

In July 1749 Diderot was arrested under a lettre de cachet and imprisoned at Vincennes. Interrogated by the police lieutenant Berryer, he denied authorship of the Letter and the Philosophical Thoughts under oath, which Morley calls “a sorrowful picture” of an apostle's plight. Through Madame du Châtelet, a kinswoman of the governor, whom Voltaire set to write, he was moved from the dungeon to the château on parole; Rousseau visited him; pressure from the booksellers with a stake in the Encyclopædia secured his release (morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-3). The Letter on the Deaf and Dumb (1751) proposes to study the natural order of ideas through gesture and to “decompose” a man into his senses; Morley clears Diderot of borrowing from Condillac's Statue and calls the gesture section crude by later standards (ibid.).

The Encyclopædia years

Diderot turned a stalled bookseller's translation of Chambers into the encyclopedie, recruited jean-le-rond-dalembert for mathematics and physics, and after D'Alembert's retirement carried the work on alone for seven years, refusing Voltaire's advice to finish it abroad. He saw papers and plates seized in 1752, discovered that Le Breton had secretly mutilated the final volumes, and completed the text in 1765, with the plates in 1772. Morley gives his pay, after Grimm, as about three thousand livres a year, sixty thousand in all (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-3, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-4). He worked at looms and had machines taken apart in order to describe the trades (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-2); he read proofs ten hours a day for a month, wore out his eyes over plates and once fell asleep over a candle and burned half his papers (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-1).

As contributor he filled more than four large volumes of the collected works. Morley finds the result vast, uneven and disproportionate, full of essays rather than dictionary entries, with tolerance and suspended judgment insinuated past the censor. Much was compiled at second hand, legitimately in Morley's view so long as the writer passed it through his own mind; the articles on Spinosa and Leibnitz show his vigour and the limits of his metaphysical engagement. The Liberty article contradicts a 1756 letter to Landois denying liberty outright, and Morley calls such mystifications odious. In the political articles Diderot is, for Morley, a non-democratic, property-minded Montesquieuan who distrusts the multitude's judgement while wishing for its dignity, and who anticipates Cobden on commerce (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-2, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-3; see censorship-and-insinuated-doctrine-morley).

Sophie Voland, Grandval and the letters

Once settled to his “huge task”, Morley says, Diderot became “a very orderly person”. His letters to Mademoiselle Voland, whose replies were routed through Damilaville's revenue office, are Morley's main window on his daily life; Morley vouches for their authenticity on stylistic grounds while noting a murky transmission through Jeudy-Dugour, the Hermitage library and Grimm. The Regrets on My Old Dressing-gown turns a gift of scarlet luxury into a fable of things forcing a room, and its owner, into consistency with themselves. From 1759 Diderot spent autumns at Holbach's country house at Grandval, a round of work, dinner, long walks and talk with Grimm, Galiani, Marmontel and Helvétius, where he played Socrates and Panurge; Morley says that if one could spend a day in that decade, one might spend it there (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-1). The letters record dark meditations, a casuistry of feeling, Holbach's sour report on England, an eyewitness account of a colporteur branded and sent to the galleys over a banned book, and Galiani's fable of the nightingale, the cuckoo and the ass on genius and method (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-2), along with the company's dismissal of optimism through the joke of Leibnitz's pyramid (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-3).

Friends, money and patronage

Morley catalogues Diderot's friendship as service without expectation of return: to the bad poet Le Monnier, to the Jodins, to the needy Landois, to Damilaville's wife, and above all to Grimm, for whose manuscript literary correspondence he drudged (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-3, morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-4; see friedrich-melchior-grimm). His break with Rousseau, the one friend he could not hold, came out of the 1756 retreat and Grimm's hostility; Morley's verdict is that Diderot was not the real offender. Short of money, Diderot sold his library to Catherine of Russia, who kept him as its salaried custodian with a thousand-livre salary paid fifty years in advance, a compromise Morley treats as normal for the time while wishing he had kept his independence. Diderot's 1767 memorandum for Sartine defended literary property, seeing a book as “a piece of marketable ware”, and in an anecdote Morley tells he threw the bullying publisher Panckoucke out of his room (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-4).

Thought: scepticism, relativity, an ethics of feeling

Morley's Diderot praises Montaigne and Bayle as masters of ordered doubt but could not rest in doubt, because of a “vehement and positive demand for emotional certainties” (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-1). He refuses questions “shoreless and bottomless” in metaphysics (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-2). He is a “sentimentalist in the rationalistic century” who judges “not from the standards of the ethical text-books” but by feeling, never callous nor neutral (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-2). In his reply to Landois he reasons that a man is a link in a chain of events, modified from without, so the right response to wrongdoing is pity, as to a wind-storm, not anger; Morley summarises the lesson as reproach others with nothing, repent of nothing (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-3). Against public prosecutors “for the universe” he argues for fixing the eye on what is good, a trait Morley calls among the noblest (morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-2). His formula “Faire le bien, connaître le vrai” makes truth a means of doing good (morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-2), which Morley elsewhere warns can tip into measuring every conclusion by its social effect (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-3).

The stage

Diderot's theory of the drama glorified private virtue and domestic life; his plays The Natural Son and The Father of the Family failed by Morley's judgement, but Lessing thought no more philosophical mind than his had treated the theatre since Aristotle, and Morley says Diderot “invented German criticism”. The Paradox on the Player and the proposed genre sérieux complete the theory (morley-diderot-v1-ch7-the-stage-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch7-the-stage-2; see diderot-theory-of-the-drama-morley).

Character, as Morley judges it

Morley's portrait is admiring and regretful at once. Pity was Diderot's “divine gift”, practical and delicate, and he “was as good as his sentiment”; his family reckoned three-fourths of his life given to others (morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-2). He was uncramped by formal mannerisms, never made his own life the centre of the world and judged people “wholly to what they were” (morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-1). Yet he is for Morley the great example of faculty in excess of stewardship: a genius suited to talk rather than print, Socratic but a disseminator of positive ideas, who left an eminent name, fine pages and no masterpiece because he did not take himself seriously enough (morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-1). Mademoiselle de Lespinasse's remark that he pleased but did not touch her soul is quoted with approval (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-3). In the Preface Morley ranks him, after Comte, above Voltaire and Rousseau as a thinker, with a realistic, sympathetic criticism and an appeal from classic convention to common life (morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary).

Rameau's Nephew

Morley calls Rameau's Nephew Diderot's one masterpiece and tells its strange transmission: a manuscript Schiller gave Goethe in 1804, Goethe's German translation, a fraudulent French retranslation of 1821, and Brière's 1823 text from Madame de Vandeul. He rejects the single-purpose readings (satire of manners, answer to Palissot, frame for the music quarrel), calling the dialogue “all of these things, because it is none of them”, first of all a living portrait of an original, Diderot's Falstaff. Diderot, unlike Juvenal or Lucian, treats the parasite as an anatomist treats a specimen, which Morley calls “a mark of the modern spirit”; and he reads Rameau as representative of a society “immoral by formula and depraved on principle” on the road to the Revolution (morley-diderot-v1-ch8-rameaus-nephew).

Volume II: the moral dialogues

This section draws on Volume II (morley-diderot-v2). In the Conversation of a Father with his Children (1773), Diderot's father recounts nearly burning a forgotten will that would have enriched a rich bookseller at poor kinsfolk's expense; the father and the abbé defend the supremacy of law, the younger Diderot whispers that “there are no laws for the sage”, and the father replies he would not live in a town where all thought so. Morley admires the dramatic ease but thinks Diderot's irony “fails to press to the root of the matter”; in On the Inconsistency of the Public Judgment, the Desroches story, he faults the satire of Society as unable to improve anyone (morley-diderot-v2-ch01-other-dialogues-1). In the Supplement to Bougainville's Travels (written 1772, published 1796), Orou attacks European monogamy as making a free being into property. Diderot closes by insisting that bad laws must be obeyed until reformed, which Morley calls his sincerest accent, and Morley judges the attacks on constancy a moment of “elated paradox” in a writer who was the great exalter of domestic sentiment (morley-diderot-v2-ch01-other-dialogues-2).

Volume II: fiction

This section draws on Volume II. Diderot's Eloge of Richardson, written after Richardson's death in 1761, holds that “the truest history is full of falsehoods” while the novel is full of truths; Morley reads the passion for Richardson as a symptom of the passage to middle-class private life, with Diderot its “half-conscious oracle”. The Nun, written about 1760 and published 1796, began as a hoax to lure the Marquis de Croismare back to Paris; Diderot, caught in his own trap, wept over it. Morley calls him a “social destroyer by accident” and in intention “a truly scientific moralist”, and reads the book under protest (morley-diderot-v2-ch02-romance-1). Jacques le Fataliste, composed about 1773 after Diderot met Sterne at Holbach's, circulated in manuscript through Grimm's subscribers and Germany before reaching France in 1796. Morley finds it heavy and often gross, with the Madame de Pommeraye episode its one story “with meaning, purpose, and character”, while granting that it acted as “a dissolvent” of starved formalism for Goethe and Schiller (morley-diderot-v2-ch02-romance-2).

Volume II: the art critic

This section draws on Volume II. Diderot wrote nine Salons for Grimm between 1759 and 1781, missing 1773 when he was abroad. Morley finds in them “a freshness, a vivacity, a zeal, a sincerity” unique in criticism, partly because they were written privately, and makes Diderot the one writer to bring “a vivid spirit and a philosophic judgment” to Beauty in France. The atheist leader was moved to tears by a Fête-Dieu procession, which Morley takes as completing the circle of his versatility. Diderot never saw Italy, which Morley counts among the great losses of literature (morley-diderot-v2-ch03-art-1). His favourite was Greuze, whom he urged to “fais de la morale en peinture”; he never spared Boucher. His method was to study the painter's technique, as he had studied weavers and dyers for the Encyclopædia, to rewrite pictures in imagination, and to digress, which Morley calls a source of charm (morley-diderot-v2-ch03-art-2). The Essay on Painting (written 1765, published 1796) makes nature the sole source of art and blames academies and the antique for mannerism; Morley gives Goethe's rebuttal “the last word of reason and wisdom”. Morley finds the Encyclopædia essay on Beauty weak, credits the 1751 Letter on Deaf Mutes with anticipating Lessing's Laocoön, and dismisses the writing on music (morley-diderot-v2-ch03-art-3).

Volume II: the Russian journey

This section draws on Volume II. In 1773, at sixty, Diderot set out to visit Catherine II, halting at the Hague, where his letters dwell on fish and straw hats (morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-1). At St Petersburg Catherine talked with him daily, and later told Ségur his principles would make “fine books, but very bad business”. Madame Geoffrin judged him lacking in tact, and Grimm the better courtier. On the return he spent months at the Hague, where the Swedish scholar Björnstähl found him shining in conversation and preferring artisans and peasants, who could be asked about “useful and necessary things”, to courtiers. There he met Hemsterhuys and Princess Galitzin, whose “Why?” and “How?”, by her partisan biographer's account, routed his atomist proselytising. Leaving in September 1774, he calculated he had ten years left (morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-2). His Plan of a University for Russia put the sciences before Greek and Latin and deferred to the Empress on religion (morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-2). See catherine-the-great and philosophers-and-enlightened-despots-morley.

Volume II: in the books of his circle

This section draws on Volume II. Diderot ranked Helvétius's L'Esprit with Montesquieu and Buffon but objected that it missed the barrier between a man destined by nature for a function and one who brings only industry, and in 1774 wrote detailed notes against De l'Homme (morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-2). Morley thinks Holbach's System of Nature too systematic to be Diderot's, but there are “good reasons for believing” some pages were his; when rumour named him, Diderot fled Paris for Langres and Bourbonne, ready to cross the frontier (morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-1). He was popularly credited with at least a third of Raynal's History of the Indies, and Morley thinks the Polly Baker plea too close to his manner to be Raynal's own (morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-1). See baron-dholbach, claude-adrien-helvetius and guillaume-thomas-raynal.

Volume II: last years and death

This section draws on Volume II. Back from Russia, Diderot closed his notes on physiology by calling philosophy “an apprenticeship for death” and the coffin “a bed of rest”. His Essay on the Reigns of Claudius and Nero defends Seneca as a case where morality's general rules condemn but common sense acquits; Morley reads it as typical of Diderot's judging each act by its circumstances, and notes that the 1782 edition became a long attack on Rousseau, prompted by the prospect of the Confessions (morley-diderot-v2-ch08-closing-years-1). Diderot and Voltaire met only in 1778, after twenty-seven years. Romilly, visiting in 1781, found him ostentatiously atheist and furious at French tyranny. In his last illness he refused the curé of Saint Sulpice's suggested slight retractation as “an impudent lie”. His last recorded aphorism was that “the first step towards philosophy is incredulity”, and he died at table on 30 July 1784 after eating an apricot. Morley defends him against the charge of fomenting the Terror with the “entrails of the priest” couplet, a dramatic line published only in 1795, and regrets that Naigeon, who inherited his papers, could have been a Boswell and was instead a “fanatical atheist” (morley-diderot-v2-ch08-closing-years-2).

Volume II: the late philosophical works

This section draws on Volume II's Conclusion. Morley counts the Thoughts on the Interpretation of Nature (1754) the most important of Diderot's works not yet discussed: Baconian aphorisms reconciling scientists with many instruments and few ideas and philosophers with many ideas and few instruments, predicting that within a century Europe would not have three great geometers, rejecting final causes, holding that “it is utility that circumscribes all”, and asking whether nature has produced only one act. Morley reads it through Comte (morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-1). In the three dialogues of 1769 around D'Alembert's Dream, Diderot “figures definitely as a materialist”: there is “only one substance in the world”, and the thinking man is an instrument with sensibility and memory. He never meant them for the public and thought he had burned the only copy; they appeared in 1830. The Conversation with the Maréchale gives the parable of the young Mexican, on sincere error. In the Falconet correspondence (1765–67) Diderot defends the hope of posterity as a secular afterlife: “Posterity is for the philosopher what the other world is for the devout!” (morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-2). Volume II's Appendix gives Morley's translation of Rameau's Nephew; see rameaus-nephew.
