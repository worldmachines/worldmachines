Disease Regimes, Slavery and Extractive States (Mann)

Commons · Entities · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mann's argument that malaria and yellow fever, carried by the Columbian Exchange, strengthened the economic case for African slavery through differential immunity, produced absentee 'extractive states' where Europeans could not settle, and acted as unplanned military forces from Yorktown to Haiti, without single-handedly causing any of these outcomes.

Mann's argument in mann-1493 that malaria and yellow fever, carried across the Atlantic by the columbian-exchange-mann, tilted colonial labor toward enslaved Africans and produced 'extractive states' where Europeans could not survive. He calls malaria 'a historical force that deformed cultures' and 'an insistent nudge that pushed societies to answer questions in ways that today seem cruel and reprehensible' (mann-1493-ch03-about-face). His recurrent caveat is that disease strengthened these outcomes; it did not single-handedly cause them.

The biology

Plasmodium falciparum needs about 72 F and P. vivax about 59 F, so falciparum thrived in Africa and vivax in northern Europe. Falciparum came to America from Africa and vivax from Europe, 'a difference with historic consequences'. Draining the English fens under Elizabeth I created a vivax belt, from which many emigrants to Virginia came (mann-1493-ch03-extractive-states-seasoning). Yellow fever, a mild childhood illness in Africa, killed about 43 to 59 percent of victims in the episodes McNeill compiled and left survivors immune; plantation clearing and sugar pots made the Caribbean 'a lethal environment for non-immunes' (mann-1493-ch03-yellow-jack-war-mosquitoes). Duffy-negativity gives most West and Central Africans immunity to vivax, and sickle-cell and childhood exposure give partial resistance to falciparum (mann-1493-ch03-no-distempers-villa-plasmodia).

From servants to slaves

Mann poses the puzzle through adam-smith's argument that free labor 'comes cheaper in the end', and through prices: a prime-age African slave cost about 25 pounds against about 10 for a servant's contract. Yet Virginia's enslaved population went from about three thousand to more than sixteen thousand between 1680 and 1700. He treats profit, cheap land and post-civil-war labor scarcity as each partly true and each insufficient, and turns to the Scottish colony at Darien, wrecked mainly by disease (mann-1493-ch03-about-face). Philip Curtin's data suggest Englishmen were perhaps three to ten times likelier to die in their first year in falciparum and yellow-fever zones, which made a slave economically preferable at up to three times a European's price (mann-1493-ch03-no-distempers-villa-plasmodia). Mann's inversion of racial-theory logic: 'Their immunity became a wellspring for their enslavement' (mann-1493-ch03-no-distempers-villa-plasmodia).

The limit Mann sets. Slavery existed without the parasite, in Massachusetts and Argentina. The claim is comparative: falciparum zones became slave societies (Virginia, Brazil) where others remained societies with slaves. Planters did not knowingly exploit African immunity; competitive selection among planters did the work (mann-1493-ch03-no-distempers-villa-plasmodia). His more speculative extensions, such as the 'Villa Plasmodia' plantation house as anti-mosquito design and a native slave-trading boundary that roughly matches the later slave/free line, he flags as possible, or as 'speculative, but not, it seems to me, unreasonable' (mann-1493-ch03-no-distempers-villa-plasmodia).

Seasoning and settlement

Virginians expected a 'seasoning' of prolonged illness. Deaths fell from 20 or 30 percent around 1650 to 10 percent or lower around 1670 as colonists learned to avoid marshes and dusk (mann-1493-ch03-extractive-states-seasoning). Following Darrett and Anita Rutman, unreliable labor in malarial places penalized small farmers and rewarded big planters, pushing toward 'exaggerated economic polarization' (mann-1493-ch03-no-distempers-villa-plasmodia).

Extractive states

Mann reports Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson: where settlers could not survive they built extractive states rather than stable, populous colonies. He adds his own chain from ecological introduction to economic exchange to persistent political consequence, and then qualifies it as not the whole explanation (mann-1493-ch03-extractive-states-seasoning). His cases:

  • São Tomé against Madeira. Madeira lacked the mosquito vectors and São Tomé swarmed with them, 'a bit like a natural scientific experiment'. After Brazilian sugar crashed both markets, Madeira's resident settlers pivoted to wine and freed most slaves, while São Tomé's absentee owners left 'zombie enterprises'. Mann calls São Tomé the progenitor of the extractive state; the comparison is illustrative, not controlled (mann-1493-ch08-bad-beginnings).
  • The Guianas and Haiti. Absentee owners had no interest in schools, hospitals, legal codes or parliaments, and Guyana became independent in 1966 with three foreign companies holding 80 percent of export earnings (mann-1493-ch03-yellow-jack-war-mosquitoes). Suriname's absentee planters had 'little interest in creating the institutions that underlie a productive society', and Haiti was 'a true extractive state' (mann-1493-ch09-capitulations).

Mosquitoes as military actors

Following McNeill, malaria disabled armies entering the malarial South: Cornwallis's troops at Yorktown, and Union troops, where Mann's hedged view is that malaria 'likely delayed' Union victory (mann-1493-ch03-yellow-jack-war-mosquitoes). The same diseases turned against slavery. Yellow fever and malaria destroyed British and French armies in Haiti, and Mann concludes that the diseases that promoted African slavery helped Africans destroy it, so that the Louisiana Purchase is owed 'indirectly to maroons' (mann-1493-ch09-capitulations). In Panama, about two soldiers had to be imported to field one, which made anti-maroon campaigns prohibitively expensive (mann-1493-ch09-in-the-isthmus). Malaria also finished the 1878 Madeira railroad expedition that opens Mann's rubber chapter (mann-1493-ch07-bather-in-the-bubbly).

World Machines resonances, with their hedges

  • history-machine. A disease environment nobody planned or could read, shaping labor regimes and foundational political events, is the notes' repeated rhyme. The mosquito is a contingency, not a slow-assembling infrastructure, and Mann offers one causal thread, not a model (mann-1493-ch03-about-face, mann-1493-ch03-yellow-jack-war-mosquitoes).
  • legibility-as-machine-core. The immunity gradient shaped institutions without anyone seeing it; the notes read this as illegible process set against legibility projects, loosely (mann-1493-ch03-no-distempers-villa-plasmodia).
  • decay-sequence. São Tomé's plantation structure persisting after its function lapsed loosely rhymes with institutions outliving their purpose; Mann makes no such claim (mann-1493-ch08-bad-beginnings).
  • Deep infrastructure. The note on Guyana calls this 'institutional path dependence rather than a machine lifecycle', while observing that it rhymes with WMP interest in how deep infrastructure shapes centuries of outcomes (mann-1493-ch03-yellow-jack-war-mosquitoes).
