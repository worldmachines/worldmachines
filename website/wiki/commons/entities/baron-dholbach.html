Holbach

Commons · Entities · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Holbach, host of the Encyclopædists' table and author of the System of Nature (1770), which John Morley reads as a dogmatic naturalism and thorough atheism whose fusion of revolt against God with revolt against kings made it less philosophy than the literature of a political revolution.

This page draws on Volume II of john-morley's diderot-and-the-encyclopaedists (morley-diderot-v2), where Holbach is chiefly the author of the System of Nature. Morley's verdicts are marked as his; Holbach's doctrines are given as Morley expounds them.

The man and his table

Morley's Holbach is benevolent, learned and hospitable, the maître d'hôtel of philosophy, whose house hosted Hume, Wilkes, Shelburne, Garrick, Franklin and Priestley. Morley infers that Rousseau's Wolmar in the New Heloïse is Rousseau's version of Holbach. Julie laments a husband who sees in nature only “a fortuitous combination, the product of blind force”; Morley calls this an Ignoratio Elenchi, but credits Rousseau with honestly showing that atheists can live upright lives (morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-1). Sterne met Diderot at Holbach's dinners on his 1762 visit to Paris (morley-diderot-v2-ch02-romance-2), and Raynal cross-examined the guests at Holbach's table (morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-1). Holbach explained Helvétius's anglophilia as a reaction to persecution (morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1).

The System of Nature: publication and alarm

The book appeared in 1770 under the name of Mirabaud, a dead secretary of the Academy. Morley's verdict is that it is too systematic to be Diderot's, though there are “good reasons for believing” some pages were his. When the rumour of his authorship spread, Diderot fled Paris for Langres and Bourbonne, ready to cross the frontier; he praised the book as a philosophy “all of one piece”. For Morley it “gathered up all the scattered explosives of the criticism of the century into one thundering engine of revolt and destruction”, showing a public that had enjoyed fifty years of intellectual delight the “unfamiliar sky” of no God, matter in spontaneous movement and mortal souls. Contemporaries answered variously. Galiani called the author the Abbé Terrai of metaphysics; the young Goethe found the book grey and corpse-like; voltaire wrote a refutation and made sure the King heard of it; Frederick of Prussia composed a defence of theism (morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-1).

Doctrine, as Morley expounds it

Naturalism and necessity. Nature is the whole; motion flows from the essence of matter and is eternal; all phenomena are necessary, from each molecule in a dust-storm to each agent in a revolution; order is necessity seen as succession, and Chance is only “the limitation of man's knowledge” (morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-2).

Soul, free will and punishment. The soul is an “occult force” invented to cover ignorance, and medicine, not theology, holds the key to morals. Morley judges the demolition of free will one of the book's most effective parts: the man who throws himself from a window and the man who is thrown differ only in where the impulse begins. Praise, blame, reward and punishment become forces acting on motives, and the good man is a machine whose springs yield good results. The soul does not survive the body, a broken clock cannot strike, an argument Morley finds to be Lucretius's (morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-2; see necessity-and-punishment-morley).

Ethics. Against the charge of egoism, Morley holds that Holbach's real base is “the happiness of the greatest number”: virtue is placing one's interest in what accords with others' interest, and “no man can be happy alone”. He reads the book as, in substance, “a protest against ascetic and quietist ideals” (“action is the true element of the human mind”) (morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-3; see democracy-of-utility-morley).

The attack on theism. Part II condemns “theism, in its purest as in its impurest shape”. It repudiates any a priori basis for belief, against Descartes, Clarke, Malebranche and Newton, and answers the charge that atheists deny a moving force: they perceive “matter, essentially active and mobile” and decline to attach that force to a being outside the whole. It ends with a long antithesis of Nature and Religion. Morley says that if Nature means the Encyclopædists and Religion the French Catholic Church of that moment, the antitheses are “a tolerably fair account” (morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-3).

Politics

Morley holds that the book's more immediate significance was its direct attack on government, unlike Voltaire's abstention from politics and Rousseau's vagueness. Holbach indicts sovereigns, calls society a state of war between ruler and ruled, and describes whole nations labouring for “a handful of insensates”. In Morley's reading, revolt against the impotence of society and revolt against the omnipotence of God made a “firm coalition” that ended twenty-four years later, when Hébert was guillotined as an atheist (morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-1, morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-2; see speculative-to-social-attack-morley).

Morley's verdicts

Holbach is “less a seeker than an expounder; less a philosopher than a preacher” (morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-2). He shares the defect of all materialistic speculation, disregard of historic opinion: he gives reasons for changing convictions without asking what in human character the old ones were rooted in (morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-1). He fuses distinct questions under one “basis of morals” and ignores the moral gains each generation inherits, which Morley thinks inconsistent with his own doctrine of necessity. No attack on theology is more “elaborate, trenchant, and unflinching”, yet its tone is not truly philosophic, because it never tries to understand the needs behind theology. The book is “not so much philosophy as the literature of a political revolution” (morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-3). In the Conclusion Morley places Maupertuis's Law of Thrift on the road leading to the System of Nature (morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-1), and in the chapter on Helvétius he names Holbach as the bearer of a dogma of matter and motion, one of the “substitutes” for theology (morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1).

The propagandist

Holbach followed the System with a popular abridgment, Good Sense, and a flood of tracts inciting readers to intellectual revolt. He died in spring 1789. Morley imagines what he would have seen five years later: Notre Dame consecrated to Reason and a procession whose hero was an ass crowned with a mitre (morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-3).

World Machines resonances, with the notes' hedges

Holbach's necessitarian, self-moving matter fits enlightenment-monism and has a kinship with baruch-spinoza, though Holbach's matter is not a substance with attributes and Morley does not draw the Spinoza link (morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-2). The circle's output outrunning its patrons' tolerance, and the tracts preceding the Revolution, are read against the history-machine, with the hedge that Morley frames these as a vivid ironic contrast, not a theory (morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-1, morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-3).
