The Columbian Exchange (Mann)

Commons · Entities · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
The Columbian Exchange as Mann uses and extends Alfred Crosby's concept: globalization as a biological event after 1492, with an economic twin in the Manila silver link, a human wing of enslaved Africans and migrating Asians, lasting disease regimes, feedback into China, and traffic that still runs both ways.

Alfred Crosby's term, from Ecological Imperialism, for the transfer of plants, animals and microbes between the hemispheres after 1492. Charles C. Mann adopts it as the organizing idea of mann-1493 and extends it. The suffix marks Mann's usage; the concept is Crosby's and broader than one book.

Crosby's core, as Mann reports it

Europe's long-run edge was 'biological, not technological'. Crosby conceded better troops and weapons, but held that ships carrying species let Europeans remake much of the Americas, Asia and, to a lesser extent, Africa into ecological versions of Europe. The Exchange was 'neither fully controlled nor understood by its participants' (mann-1493-prologue). Mann calls it 'arguably the most important event since the death of the dinosaurs', a hedge that is his own, and notes that Crosby's once-controversial claim now has growing support (mann-1493-ch01-seams-of-pangaea).

Mann's extensions

  • Globalization as a biological event. Colón's ships re-stitched the seams of Pangaea. Long-distance trade was old, but nothing before joined both hemispheres at a scale able to disrupt societies on opposite sides of the planet (mann-1493-ch01-seams-of-pangaea).
  • An economic twin. Legazpi and Urdaneta were 'to economics what Colón was to ecology': the Manila link of 1571 bound nearly every populous area into one exchange network (mann-1493-ch01-shiploads-of-silver). See silk-for-silver-trade-mann.
  • A human wing. Mann writes that he had 'acted as if humankind were in the director's chair', whereas Homo sapiens is also a species reshuffled by the Exchange, people 'like dice flung on a gaming table'. Drawing on the Eltis slave-voyages database, about 11.7 million captives left Africa for the Americas between 1500 and 1840 against about 3.4 million European emigrants (mann-1493-ch08-johnny-good-looking). Enslaved Africans are 'the human wing of the Columbian Exchange' (mann-1493-ch03-extractive-states-seasoning), and Asians reached New Spain on the galleons (mann-1493-ch08-shook-up-city).
  • Disease regimes, not only epidemics. Smallpox and influenza came as sudden outbursts; malaria became a permanent feature of landscapes and changed who could live where (mann-1493-ch03-extractive-states-seasoning). See disease-regimes-and-extractive-states-mann.
  • Feedback into Asia. Sweet potatoes and maize opened China's dry uplands, with deforestation, floods and a population surge. Tobacco there was part of 'an unplanned ecological invasion that shaped, for better and worse, modern China' (mann-1493-ch01-shiploads-of-silver, mann-1493-ch05-hidden-passengers).
  • Possibly climate. Mann reports Ruddiman's proposal that epidemic depopulation ended Indian burning and farming, let forests regrow and drew down carbon dioxide, contributing to the Little Ice Age. It is a hypothesis 'increasingly treated seriously', not a settled result (mann-1493-ch01-reversals-of-fortune).
  • Traffic both ways, and continuing. The Colorado potato beetle followed Spanish livestock north before reaching Europe; phylloxera went from the United States to France and Italy; the blight's second mating type reached Europe in 1981 (mann-1493-ch06-war-upon-the-beetles). Rubber's leaf blight, still held off Asia by distance, is Mann's forward scenario in which 'the cycle of the Columbian Exchange will be complete, taking away what it once gave'. He presents it as projection, not fact (mann-1493-ch07-wickham-end-of-world).

Mechanism: chains of small accidents

Mann's typical unit is a chain. On Hispaniola it runs from plantains to scale insects to fire ants, which he keeps labelled as E. O. Wilson's 'informed speculation' beside Las Casas's eyewitness account (mann-1493-ch01-seams-of-pangaea). In Virginia, English soil ballast swapped for tobacco may have carried earthworms (mann-1493-ch02-lowly-creatures-strange-land). Livestock carried buffalo bur north, and the beetle followed, needing only one small mutation to jump to the potato (mann-1493-ch06-war-upon-the-beetles). A fertilizer shipping line probably carried the potato blight (mann-1493-ch06-modern-famine-lazy-beds). Wickham, who smuggled Hevea seeds, is by contrast 'a conscious human agent of the Columbian Exchange', yet even there the world-historical effect is out of proportion to his competence or intent (mann-1493-ch07-wickham-end-of-world).

Mann's verdict, hedged

Mann follows Warren Dean in holding that 'the huge benefits of moving species outweigh the huge harms', and concedes that 'the balance can be closer than free-exchange advocates tend to admit' (mann-1493-ch07-wickham-end-of-world). He borrows Fernando Ortiz's 'transculturation' (borrowed things are transformed by the borrower) and calls constant connection our 'home condition' (mann-1493-prologue). Families adapted and remade the Exchange rather than merely suffering it: a Filipino garden of American-origin maize, squashes and peppers is his closing image (mann-1493-ch10-on-the-boat).

World Machines resonances, with their hedges

  • Legibility. A world-shaping process that no participant could read or steer is the notes' most frequent rhyme (mann-1493-prologue, mann-1493-ch05-hidden-passengers). Mann is not a machine theorist and does not frame it so.
  • history-machine. The Exchange as a paradigm assembling silently rather than by great-man agency fits partially. The notes add that it is ecological rather than institutional, and that Mann still centres one man's voyages (mann-1493-ch01-seams-of-pangaea, mann-1493-ch01-to-the-lighthouse).
  • homogenocene-mann. The era the Exchange produced, in Mann's naming.
