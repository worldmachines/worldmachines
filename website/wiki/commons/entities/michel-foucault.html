Michel Foucault (1926–1984)

Commons · Entities · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
French philosopher (1926–1984) whose analytics of power/knowledge, genealogy of disciplinary institutions, and concept of biopolitics have been foundational for critical theory; Bryant's gravity framework extends and partially critiques Foucault by adding the non-discursive, material-infrastructural dimension of power.

Biographical

French philosopher and historian of ideas. Professor at the Collège de France (1970–1984), chair in "History of Systems of Thought." His work spans epistemology, psychiatry, penology, sexuality, and political rationality.

Core Concepts

Power/Knowledge: Power and knowledge are not independent — regimes of truth (what counts as knowledge) are produced within and serve to sustain specific power arrangements. There is no knowledge outside power, and power always operates through knowledge-claims.

Genealogy: Foucault's method for tracing how present institutions and practices emerged through historically contingent struggles, rather than through teleological development or rational progress. Genealogy reveals contingency where necessity is assumed.

Discipline: Discipline and Punish (1975) traces the emergence of disciplinary power — a form of power that operates not through public spectacle and punishment but through the continuous surveillance and normalization of individuals. Paradigm institution: the Panopticon prison (Bentham). Operates through spatial organization, timetables, examinations, and hierarchical surveillance.

Biopolitics: From the late 1970s lectures at the Collège de France (Society Must Be Defended; The Birth of Biopolitics), Foucault analyzed the emergence of power directed not at individual bodies but at populations — the management of birth rates, life expectancy, health, and productivity at the level of the species.

The Dispositif: The "apparatus" or "device" — the heterogeneous ensemble of discourses, institutions, architectural arrangements, regulations, administrative measures, and scientific statements that form a network of power/knowledge at a given historical moment.

Relation to Bryant's Gravity Framework

Bryant cites Foucault as a representative of the discursive analytics of power that his gravity concept extends. In Onto-Cartography Ch. 7, Bryant writes:

"The concept of gravity proposed here includes all of the discursivist and practice-based elements of concepts of power proposed by theorists such as Foucault and Bourdieu, while also helping us to recognize the power that things themselves exercise — by virtue of what they are, not by virtue of how we symbolize or represent them." (p. 194)

Bryant does not reject Foucault. His critique is one of scope: Foucauldian power analytics are excellent at capturing how discourse, norm, and subject-formation operate, but they systematically underweight the plane of content — the material, non-discursive, non-human agencies that organize social arrangements regardless of representation.

Significantly, Foucault's later "material turn" (especially the architectural analysis of the Panopticon, the spatial geography of the clinic, and the analysis of specific techniques like the examination) already gestures toward what Bryant theorizes more systematically. The gap is between a primarily discursive analytics and a flat ontology in which material and discursive agencies are equally first-class.

Sathe's Use in "We Need Homes in the Delta Quadrant" (2025)

In §III, Sathe deploys Foucault's concept of heterotopias — places that operate under different logic than the dominant spatial order — to argue that counter-spatial practice is both possible and historically persistent. Heterotopias function as Sathe's answer to the question raised by Harvey's and Mbembe's critique: if place-making is always imbricated in power, what does resistant or ethical place-making look like? Foucault's heterotopias show that spaces outside the dominant map have always existed, and that their existence reveals the contingency of the dominant order. For Sathe, this motivates the turn to custodianship-as-design-ethos: the designer who creates heterotopic conditions — places with different affordances, different logics — is doing something genuinely political.

Peter Gay's Education of the Senses as foil to the repressive hypothesis

peter-gay's The Bourgeois Experience, Vol. I (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1) is read throughout its reading notes as an archival counterpart to Foucault's critique of the repressive hypothesis: both reject the picture of a Victorian bourgeoisie that simply silenced sex, but they explain the non-silence differently.

Where the evidence converges. Much of Gay's material documents exactly the proliferation of sex-talk Foucault describes: a century-long, statistically obsessive medical crusade against masturbation with hundreds of treatises and formal symposia (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-learned-ignorance-2-profession-anxiety); an international sex-education boom of societies and congresses (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-learned-ignorance-3-flight-knowledge); an escalating archive of expert treatises on whether women feel desire (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-communions-3-problematic-sex); Kirchhoff's solicitation of 120 professors' opinions on women's fitness for study, a scientia sexualis in miniature (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-offensive-women-4-sex-mind-body); Bishop Bouvier's confessional interrogation of couples, reported to Pius IX (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-pressures-2-conscience-control); and anti-obscenity crusaders whose war generated an obsessive discourse about obscenity (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-carnal-knowledge-3-readers-conflict).

Where the mechanism diverges. Gay explains this loquacity psychologically — anxiety, projection, reaction formation, “learned ignorance” as motivated unknowing — where Foucault locates it in power/knowledge and the discursive apparatus (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-learned-ignorance-2-profession-anxiety, gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-offensive-women-2-manhood-danger). And Gay places the richest talk about sex in private genres — diaries, letters, ciphers — rather than Foucault's public institutions: “there was no conspiracy and... no silence. There was circumspection” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-fortifications-4-private-experience, gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-erotic-record-2-mabel-loomis-todd). His evidence is persons (Mabel Loomis Todd's diaries, Mosher's surveyed wives) rather than discourses (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-erotic-record-1-mabel-loomis, gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-communions-2-dubious-certainty-numbers).

A disagreement the notes do not settle. The notes differ on how far Gay himself keeps a repression model. One reading has him argue there was “little real silence to begin with” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-carnal-knowledge-2-lessons-body); others find him positing a genuine repressive doctrine over a genuine private sexuality — an authentic-experience-under-ideology model that Foucault's account of productive discourse would resist (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-communions-3-problematic-sex, gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-fortifications-3-democratization-comfort) — or locating reticence in real repression and real informational deficit: “the bourgeois century repressed better than it knew” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-learned-ignorance-1-age-factitious-innocence). One note even suggests Mosher's respondents' language of “spiritual union” is itself an instance of the discourse-multiplication Foucault described, serving to license private pleasure rather than police it (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-communions-2-dubious-certainty-numbers). The notes treat Foucault as foil, not ally, precisely because the disagreement is productive. For how Gay's defenses differ from one another, see bourgeois-defenses-against-eros.
