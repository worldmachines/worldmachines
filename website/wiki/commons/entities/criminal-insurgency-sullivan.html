Criminal insurgency (Sullivan)

Commons · Entities · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
John P. Sullivan's framework, co-developed with Robert Bunker, for gangs and cartels that become war-making actors whose sole political motive is autonomous economic control of territory, pursued by hollowing out the state, and staged in three cartel phases and three gang generations.

A framework of John P. Sullivan, a security practitioner, set out in a 2009 Small Wars Journal piece reprinted as chapter 16 of gilman-deviant-globalization (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch16-sullivan-criminal-insurgencies). The typologies were co-developed with Robert Bunker in the late 1990s. The chapter is a synthesis of that earlier work, not new fieldwork, and it is largely assertive and predictive rather than empirical. The suffix marks that this is Sullivan's usage: a security analyst's term, not the volume editors'.

Definition

In “criminal enclaves” or “lawless zones” where civil governance and social bonds have eroded, Sullivan argues, some gangs and cartels evolve into criminal insurgents. What separates them from terrorists and ordinary insurgents is a single political motive, “to gain autonomous economic control over territory”, pursued by hollowing out the state and creating enclaves. They “seek profit and are not driven by ideology”, but the ungoverned zones they leave behind attract extremists and terrorists. Citing Samuel Porteous, Sullivan says transnational crime erodes civil society and sovereignty by normalizing violence and legitimizing corruption; at neighbourhood level it produces a “failed community”, the “virtual analog of a failed state”.

Three cartel phases

  1. Medellín: aggressive competitor. It challenges the Westphalian state through extreme violence.
  2. Cali: subtle co-opter. Distributed, network-like and leaderless, it works through corruption rather than violence and is harder to decapitate. Sullivan says the model spread to Mexico.
  3. Criminal state successor. It has “yet to emerge”, but would rule enclaves like warlords. Sullivan sees warning signs in Mexico and offers Ciudad del Este, at the Paraguay-Brazil-Argentina border, as the model of a “free enclave”. His description of that enclave relies on assertion about terrorist-crime overlap rather than evidence given in the chapter.

Three gang generations

Turf gangs are local and opportunistic. Market gangs are drug-centred criminal businesses. Third-generation gangs have political and mercenary aims, global reach and police infiltration. Evolution runs through politicization, internationalization and sophistication. Sullivan stresses that the overwhelming majority of gangs stay in the first or second generation, and that third-generation gangs have so far been “primarily mercenary”. The maras are his transnational example; citing Bruneau, he says they tax people and businesses where governments have “all but given up”.

Mexico and the remedy

In Sullivan's reading, Mexico's cartels have abandoned subtle co-option for open violence to secure safe havens along the plazas. Mexico's criminal insurgency could be the genesis of the third phase. His prescriptions are analytical red teaming, full-spectrum policing and cross-border co-production of intelligence.

Relation to the editors' argument

Sullivan frames these actors as threats to be countered. The volume's editors, by contrast, read deviant globalization as possibly developmental, and they argue that most deviant entrepreneurs want autonomous zones rather than the state, because taking it over would oblige them to serve “ungrateful and low-profit clients” (gilman-deviant-globalization-conclusion-1). Sullivan's “autonomous economic control over territory” is close to the editors' “autonomous zones”, but his third phase forecasts a criminal successor to the state, which the editors think most such actors avoid. The two views are set side by side in deviant-entrepreneurs-and-the-hollowed-state.

Resonances (tentative)

The note on the chapter reads the challenge from below to the state's monopoly on violence as loosely akin to the modernity-machine in decline, and the proliferation of non-state armed actors as the divergence-machine at work, perhaps in a gramsci-gap-style interregnum. These are rhymes only: Sullivan is a security practitioner, not a historian, and offers no such periodization.
