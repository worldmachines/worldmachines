Cacambo

Commons · Entities · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Cacambo, Candide's quarter-Spanish valet in Voltaire's Candide, a cheerful opportunist of many trades who guides him through Paraguay, the Oreillons and El Dorado, talks the party out of danger, and reappears as a slave in Venice.

Cacambo is Candide's footman in voltaire-candide: one-quarter Spanish, born in Tucuman, a man of many trades, and in the notes' word a "cheerful opportunist" ("If we cannot find our account in one world, we'll find it in another"). Where Pangloss explains and Martin diagnoses, Cacambo acts, mostly by talking.

Paraguay and the Oreillons

When Candide must flee Buenos Ayres, Cacambo takes charge and turns their mission around: "you were going to fight against the Jesuits of Paraguay; now let's go and fight for them". He praises the Jesuit state as "an admirable government" where "Los Padres own everything there, and the people have no money at all", "the masterpiece of justice and reason", while noting that the Jesuits fight Spain and Portugal in America and confess the same princes in Europe. The note hears this as praise with an edge, his enthusiasm itself part of the joke, and the arbour scene that follows undercuts it (voltaire-candide-ch13-14-buenos-aires-and-paraguay). After Candide kills the Baron, Cacambo disguises him in the dead man's habit; when the monkeys turn out to be lovers he explains them as "one-quarter human"; and when the Oreillons prepare to eat a Jesuit, he wins their release by arguing in their own idiom of "the law of nature" and justice (voltaire-candide-ch15-16-baron-killed-and-oreillons).

El Dorado

He concludes that "this hemisphere is not better than the other", and proposes the river route that brings them to El Dorado (voltaire-candide-ch17-arrival-in-el-dorado). It is his question about monks "to dispute, to govern, to intrigue, and to burn people" that draws the elder's "Do you take us for fools?" He shares the roving appetite that makes the pair resolve "to be no longer happy" and leave (voltaire-candide-ch18-el-dorado). At Surinam he asks Candide what optimism is, drawing the book's clearest definition, and is sent ahead with diamonds to buy Cunégonde from the Governor (voltaire-candide-ch19-surinam-and-martin).

Absence and return

He fails to appear in Venice, and Martin deflates Candide's hopes in him (voltaire-candide-ch23-24-portsmouth-paquette-giroflee, voltaire-candide-ch25-pococurante). He returns soot-faced as a slave, servant to one of the six dethroned kings at the Venice inn: "I am a slave, and my master waits for me" (voltaire-candide-ch26-six-kings). His account of the lost fortune is a ledger: two million to the Governor for permission, the rest to a pirate, everyone enslaved. Candide buys him back (voltaire-candide-ch27-voyage-to-constantinople).

On the farm

His plan for the Baron (return him to the Turkish captain and ship him to the Jesuit Father-General) is the one adopted. He is then worn down by garden labour (voltaire-candide-ch30-conclusion-garden).
