Baruch Spinoza: Stewart's Portrait

Commons · Entities · updated 23 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Matthew Stewart's biographical record of Spinoza (double exile, excommunication, chosen poverty, failed friendships of reason, a disputed deathbed) and his recovery of the political revolutionary of the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus that later reception turned into a mystical pantheist.

Extends baruch-spinoza with material from Matthew Stewart's The Courtier and the Heretic (stewart-courtier-and-heretic): the heretic's life, and the political revolutionary of the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus that later reception buried.

Stewart's Biography: The Heretic's Life

Stewart's double biography supplies the granular record behind the summary entries in baruch-spinoza.

Double exile as vantage point. The Spinoza family fled the 1492 Iberian expulsion and then the Portuguese Inquisition's pursuit of conversos before settling in Amsterdam. Stewart is blunt that the tolerance which received them was not chiefly idealistic: "Behind Dutch tolerance of their new neighbors was something perhaps more durable than the love of liberty, namely, an enlightened understanding of commercial self-interest." The 1656 cherem — one of the harshest the Portuguese-Jewish community ever issued — then made Bento a heretic to the Jews and a Jew to the Christians. Stewart's argument is that this double marginality was epistemically productive: from the extreme margins Spinoza could see "that the old God was dying and that his theocratic rule on earth was crumbling," an observation the framework would file under gramsci-gap (stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch02-bento).

The trigger was an interrogation by two supposed friends, in which he conceded that Scripture nowhere rules out a corporeal God, that spirits are "mere phantoms," and that the soul's immortality has no scriptural warrant. He refused a thousand-guilder annuity to keep quiet on the grounds that accepting it would make him a hypocrite — where his associate Juan de Prado, facing the same tribunal, recanted. Stewart's summary judgment: "he rejected the orthodoxy of his day not because he believed less, but because he believed more."

The philosophy begins as an emergency. Stewart reads the whole vocation as issuing from vanitas — not melancholy but "a dire encounter with the prospect of a descent into absolute nothingness," described by Spinoza himself in the language of a fatal illness requiring a remedy. From it come three explicit rules of living: accommodate ordinary custom; permit pleasure only so far as it preserves health; acquire money only so far as it sustains the body for contemplation. The biographical record shows him keeping them for sixteen years — gruel and watery beer, two pairs of trousers, the refused de Vries annuity, an estate too small to interest his own sister. Poverty was chosen, competent and instrumental, and lens-grinding was a trade, not a hardship (stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch04-life-of-the-mind).

The theory of friendship, and its practice. Spinoza's stated policy was to avoid "the multitudes" while cultivating a fellowship of reason in which "Man is a God to Man." Stewart shows it working easily with ordinary people — the landlord's family, the villagers — and failing almost every time he tried to induct a correspondent into the fellowship: the Blijenburgh correspondence curdles from "deeply devoted to truth" to icy dismissal within two letters; the decade-long Oldenburg friendship collapses over the Resurrection, revealing that the two had been united by personal bond and mutual misunderstanding rather than shared philosophy (stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch08-friends-of-friends).

The death, and a small mystery. Colerus's near-contemporary biography, drawn from the landlord van der Spyck, debunks the orthodox rumours of deathbed recantation or suicide. Stewart adds a layer Colerus obscured: the doctor identified only as "L.M." who attended the final hours was not Lodewijk Meyer but Georg Hermann Schuller, whom Stewart counts as "the only suspect" in the theft of the cash and silver knife left on the table — Leibniz's own alchemist go-between in Holland, who admitted scrutinising Spinoza's papers "thoroughly one by one." Leibniz never corrected the misidentification, in forty years of opportunities (stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch13-surviving-spinoza).

The Opera Posthuma reached print only because Rieuwertsz, Jarig Jelles, Schuller and unnamed collaborators transcribed and translated in secret back rooms along the Amsterdam canals while a Vatican-alerted ecumenical dragnet — priests, ministers and a rabbi sharing a boat — hunted the presses. Stewart's own contrast at the far end of the book: six state carriages honoured Spinoza's funeral; no one from the Hanoverian court attended Leibniz's.

The Political Spinoza Stewart Recovers

baruch-spinoza records the Tractatus as "foundational for modern secularism." Stewart's chapter on it recovers something sharper: Spinoza as a self-conscious political revolutionary, an image the reception history then erased.

The Masaniello self-portrait. Colerus reports a charcoal sketch by Spinoza of the Neapolitan fisherman who led a ten-day rising against Spanish rule in 1647 before being murdered and decapitated by his own followers — drawn in the rebel's iconic costume, and bearing, per the landlord van der Spyck, a deliberate resemblance to Spinoza himself. Stewart uses it to date a shift in self-conception: the cloistered metaphysician beginning to see himself as a martyr-revolutionary in a civilization-wide struggle for freedom (stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch06-hero-of-the-people).

The argument. The biblical criticism — multiple human authors, no chosen people, no real miracles, prophets as gifted rhetoricians — serves a political thesis: that "the supreme mystery of despotism...is to keep men in a state of deception," using religion's "specious title" to make men "fight for their servitude as if for salvation." Organised religion is "the relics of man's ancient bondage." From Conatus — "no virtue can be conceived prior to this one, namely, the drive to preserve oneself" — Spinoza then grounds legitimate authority in self-interest rather than divine sanction, and builds a renewable social contract (against Hobbes's one-off surrender), an inalienable freedom of conscience, and a preference for democracy. Stewart's claim is that this combination makes him "the first truly modern political philosopher."

The qualification worth keeping. Spinoza did not extend full epistemic freedom to everyone. A "good" popular religion, administered by civil rather than ecclesiastical authority, is "very salutary" even though its doctrines are at best half-truths — an explicit exoteric/esoteric split, with public belief managed by the state rather than left to the clergy. It is a striking early instance of belief itself being made a governable object, in the spirit of legibility-as-machine-core.

The paradox Stewart leaves open. A philosophy that makes self-preservation the root of all virtue nonetheless produced a man who "brazenly crossed the line that divides self-interest from the common good" — publishing under a false imprint but publishing anyway, and, after the de Witt lynching, having to be locked in by his landlord to stop him posting ultimi barbarorum near the bodies. The signet ring read Caute; Stewart's verdict is that the caution was prescription rather than description, since "it never occurred to him to get off the slopes." The question the chapter poses and does not answer — "Can a liberal be a hero?" — is the one Leibniz's competing claim to political charity is meant to test.

And then it was buried. Stewart's closing chapter tracks how Lessing's 1765 encounter with the Opera Posthuma and Novalis's "God-intoxicated man" domesticated the seventeenth century's most infamous atheist into a mystical pantheist — a persona that still "dominates the public image of Spinoza," while "The political revolutionary who sought to overthrow theological tyranny and deconstruct the very idea of spirituality has long been forgotten" (stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch18-aftermath). Anyone using the Rao or Sathe readings on baruch-spinoza should note that both depend on the political Spinoza that reception history nearly lost.
