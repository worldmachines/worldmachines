Andrea Wulf

Commons · Entities · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Andrea Wulf, narrative historian and author of The Invention of Nature and Magnificent Rebels (2022), whose group biography of the Jena Set works through letters and reported gossip, frames the story with her own biography and a thesis about free will and selfishness, and ends in a sympathetic defence of the 'Art of being Selfish'.

Andrea Wulf is the author of Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self (2022; wulf-magnificent-rebels) and, earlier, of The Invention of Nature (wulf-invention-of-nature), her book on alexander-von-humboldt. This page records only what the Magnificent Rebels reading notes support: the biography she gives of herself in the Prologue, her method and her stance.

Her own story, as she tells it

The Prologue opens with Wulf's unconventional path: refusing university, single motherhood, moving to London and becoming a writer in an acquired language. She uses it to pose the book's question (when did we come to expect the right to determine our own lives, and to take what we want?) and complicates it as she goes. Her independence, she says, 'became egotistical', and her freedom rested on middle-class privilege (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-1). The autobiography is thesis-setting. It names the dilemma between free will and selfishness that the book then pursues historically.

Method: group biography through letters and gossip

  • A coined group. 'Jena Set' is her name for the circle, and her stated justification is coincidence of time and place: they 'all came together at the same time in the same place' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-1).
  • Letters as evidence, gossip marked as gossip. Rumours are introduced with markers such as 'some alleged' and 'others suspected' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-1). Partisan correspondents are flagged: Dorothea Veit's letters are presented as one partisan's voice (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-2), and Wulf flags Caroline's own biases (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-1).
  • Hedged motives and conditional reconstruction. 'May have played a part' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch01-happy-event-1); 'Maybe... Or maybe... Whatever his reasons' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-1); a sickroom reconstructed with 'Caroline might have' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-2); 'It's unclear precisely what happened' of the Schlegel marriage (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-2).
  • Material and legal conditions. Salaries, court sizes and patronage (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch01-happy-event-1); women's position under the 1794 Prussian General State Laws (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch06-splendid-circle-2, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch13-divorce-women-sex).
  • Irony by juxtaposition. Humboldt finding one great organism in the rainforest while the Jena circle freezes into quarrels (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-1); the rooms of the circle turned into hospitals after the Battle of Jena (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch20-french-in-town-2). The notes read these as narrative devices, not causal claims.
  • Framing at the ends. Her explicit interpretation is concentrated in the Prologue and Epilogue (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-2, wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-influence-selfish).

Stance

  • Strong first-person judgements. The Jena Set is 'intellectually speaking, the most important' of a comparison class that includes the Transcendentalists, Bloomsbury and 1920s Paris (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-2); Schelling's philosophy 'became the heartbeat of Romanticism' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch11-naturphilosophie); the Jena Set, not Freud, opened the dark regions of the mind (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch08-handful-of-darkness).
  • Sympathy for Fichte, with one warning. She judges much of the contemporary charge of egotism unfair (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-2) and defends him against 'Ich-fetishism' in the Epilogue (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-influence-selfish), yet calls his 'Ich of a nation' 'a dangerous idea' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1).
  • Recovering women's work. Caroline's reviews published under August Wilhelm Schlegel's name, Caroline as the Athenaeum's de facto editor, and Dorothea's translations published under Friedrich's name (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch04-intellectual-friction-2, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch09-sublime-impertinence-1, wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1).
  • The Enlightenment. One note judges her portrait of the Enlightenment as a distancing, mechanistic age 'somewhat one-sided' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch11-naturphilosophie), though she stresses the Jena Set wanted imagination alongside reason (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-2).
  • Moral conclusion. She redefines 'selfish' historically as self-awareness and self-determination and, resting on Novalis, makes self-understanding the precondition for empathy (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-influence-selfish; see art-of-being-selfish-wulf).

Continuity with her earlier book

Magnificent Rebels points forward to Humboldt's later thought: his galvanic work in Jena 'marked the beginning of his thinking about the forces within nature', which he 'would come to describe' as a web of life (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch07-little-academy-1), and the Epilogue makes him the friend whose Jena inheritance flourished (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1). The notes treat these as Wulf's retrospective readings. Across the notes she is described as a narrative historian, not a machine theorist; every World Machines resonance recorded in them is the notes' own gloss.
