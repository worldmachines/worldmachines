Caroline Schelling (Caroline Michaelis-Böhmer-Schlegel-Schelling)

Commons · Entities · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Caroline Michaelis-Böhmer-Schlegel-Schelling as Andrea Wulf presents her in Magnificent Rebels: the revolutionary sympathiser, uncredited co-translator and editor, and hostess whom Wulf places at the centre of the Jena Set and reads as the empowered free self lived rather than theorised.

Wulf says Caroline 'stands at the centre of this inspirational story' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-1), and the Epilogue calls her 'the heart of the Jena Set' and 'the conductor of their great symphony' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1). This page follows her through wulf-magnificent-rebels, marking what is Wulf's reading, what is Caroline's own voice and what is other people's gossip.

Mainz, prison and disgrace

Caroline welcomed the Revolution in her letters ('Let the wealthy step aside, and the poor rule the world') and lived in the short-lived Mainz Republic. On 30 March 1793, after leaving Mainz, she was arrested by Prussian soldiers and imprisoned at Königstein with her daughter Auguste while secretly pregnant by a young French officer. Rumoured affairs with General Custine and Georg Forster are gossip Wulf reports as such (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-1). Wulf's backstory adds an arranged first marriage, the deaths of three children and of the illegitimate baby in foster care. Barred from several cities as a 'revolutionary whore', Caroline protested a double standard ('a mistake that would have meant nothing were she a man') and accepted August Wilhelm Schlegel's proposal as a marriage of friendship (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch04-intellectual-friction-1).

Collaborator without a byline

She scanned and shaped the verse Shakespeare with August Wilhelm, and Wulf states that dozens of the reviews and essays published under his name were hers (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch04-intellectual-friction-2, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch06-splendid-circle-2). For the Athenaeum she shortened, deleted, added and rearranged; Wulf, in her own voice, puts Caroline's thoughts 'at the heart of the friends' work and ambitions' and describes her with a river image, irrigating rather than staking a claim (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch09-sublime-impertinence-1). Her essay 'Paintings. A Conversation' is the written trace of the Dresden gallery debates (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch10-symphilosophy). She opened Friedrich Schlegel's mind to the Revolution, on his own account (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch05-novalis-in-love). See symphilosophie-wulf.

Hostess and centre

In Jena from 8 July 1796 she ran a salon; Wulf reads her French, classically draped dress as a republican signal (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch06-splendid-circle-1, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch06-splendid-circle-2). By autumn 1799 she presided over the crowded Leutragasse household; Wulf's image is that of a conductor (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch14-schlegel-clique). She drew gendered hostility: Charlotte Schiller's insults, which Wulf frames as a clash between a conventional woman and a free spirit (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch07-little-academy-2); Tieck's 'hermaphrodite' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-1); Dorothea Veit's spiteful letters (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-2); and Novalis's condemnation after Auguste's death (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-1).

Schelling, loss and divorce

Her attachment to Schelling grows through 1798-99 (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch11-naturphilosophie, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch14-schlegel-clique). She casts a hostile review of the Athenaeum as a 'universal battle of good against evil' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-1). After a 'nervous fever' she travelled with Schelling and Auguste to Bocklet, where Auguste died of mistreated dysentery on 12 July 1800 (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-2). Wulf's interpretive claim is that Caroline, without writing treatises, 'lived and breathed the new philosophical ideas', 'the empowered free self' with a core of steel (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-1). She and August Wilhelm renegotiated their marriage as friendship and collaboration ('when the world goes up in flames like a scrap of paper, works of art will be the last living sparks' are her words) (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-2). She returned to a plundered house and a feud over her piano and porcelain (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-1). The Weimar divorce took until 17 May 1803; she then married Schelling (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-2).

Napoleon and the end

She read Napoleon as 'grazing through one country after another' and said of the defeated rulers 'what can last no longer must perish' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch20-french-in-town-1, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch20-french-in-town-2). In Munich she embraced transience ('we are all just wanderers on earth') and missed Jena's open door; she died suddenly in 1809. Wulf judges that 'unlike most women' she lived in control of her own destiny; the gossip about her death (Hegel's 'the devil had come and taken her', Friedrich's 'dead to me') belongs to its speakers (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1). See also august-wilhelm-schlegel, friedrich-wilhelm-joseph-schelling and invention-of-the-self-wulf.
