Men of letters as a new spiritual power (Morley)

Commons · Entities · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
John Morley's claim that the eighteenth-century French men of letters succeeded the Jesuits as teachers and directors of society, taking over the spiritual power from the Church as a dispersive, independent order without organic doctrine, with the Encyclopædia as its provisional organisation.

This page draws on Volume I (Preface, Chapters I–VIII) of john-morley's study diderot-and-the-encyclopaedists; Volume II material is added in later sections. The concept is Morley's, in a Comtean vocabulary of spiritual and temporal power, and the judgements below are his.

The claim

Morley argues that the men of letters of eighteenth-century France were not only pupils of the Jesuits, as Diderot was, but their “immediate successors” as teachers and directors of society. What matters in the change is not the prices paid for books but the transfer of the spiritual power from ecclesiastical hands. He gives the Jesuit order credit its rationalist critics withhold, for a practical, social habit of mind, while blaming it for laxity in moral rigour (morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-1). The Church, in his opening chapter, had for centuries exercised a moral and social direction “so immeasurably more important” than physical discovery; by mid-century the old spiritual power had become an accomplice of the temporal, and the Encyclopædia was an “informal, transitory, and provisional organisation of the new spiritual power”, winning authority by a “more penetrating eye for social exigencies” (morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary).

The man of letters defined

Morley distinguishes the man of letters from the scholar, the systematic thinker and the imaginative creator. His role is dispersive and empirical: popularising detached ideas that society can assimilate, dressing them in literary forms, judging each case as it arises. Unlike the Jesuit he has no organic doctrine, tradition or discipline. Morley hedges (“We have no wish to exalt the office”) but argues that critical ages cannot be organised. Fontenelle is the first great type (morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-1).

Independence from patronage

In Morley's account Diderot and D'Alembert made the profession of letters “definite and independent”, refusing suitorship and patronage, so that “the highest moral ideas now belonged no longer to the clergy, but to the writers”. He quotes D'Alembert on men of letters living “united and almost shut up among themselves” and giving “the law to the rest of the nation in all affairs of taste and philosophy”. He goes further, in his own partisan voice: the Church eventually absorbed the philosophers' ideas, so that Voltaire, Diderot and Rousseau were “the true reformers” of the Catholic creed, an instance of what he calls “the most interesting process in the history of truth” (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-1). The notes flag this as dated and aimed at the churches of Morley's own day.

The independence was partial. Morley lists the poverty of letters across Europe, with only Voltaire, Gray and Richardson escaping because none lived by the pen (morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-1). He finds the French man of letters “worse off than his brother in England”, exposed to police informers (morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-3). And he sets Diderot's sale of his library to Catherine beside Voltaire's and D'Alembert's pensions from Frederick and Rousseau's refusal of one, wishing Diderot had kept his independence but excusing him. Diderot's defence of literary property, treating a book as “a piece of marketable ware”, points to a market rather than a patron as the ground of independence (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-4).

A rival authority, and the incumbent's response

Morley presents the clash as structural: secular knowledge displacing sacred lore, and an old spiritual power defending itself (morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary). The Church's “flagrant social incompetence”, not the force of the thinkers alone, opened the way; Locke's Essay lay unsold as late as 1725 (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-3). The plea for toleration inflamed churchmen more than the attack on dogma, Morley says, “for most ecclesiastics valued power more dearly than truth” (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-2). The band of Encyclopædists was a “single body with a common doctrine” only as a logical fiction, but Morley thinks the popular impression of a collective order practically sound (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-2).

World Machines resonances, with the notes' hedges

The notes connect this to WM concerns only loosely. Authority passing to a lay intellectual class touches intellectual-vs-revolutionary, but Morley describes a succession of functions, moral and religious, not a strategy or a machine (morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-1). The independent writer whose authority rests on knowledge rather than office can be set beside the modern knowledge-worker, with the caveat that Morley's man of letters is defined by his social and moral office, not by the conditions of cognitive work. For the Comtean current behind Morley's notion of a new spiritual power, compare manuel-prophets-of-paris.
