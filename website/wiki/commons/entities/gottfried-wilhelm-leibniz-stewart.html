Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz: Stewart's Portrait

Commons · Entities · updated 23 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Matthew Stewart's biographical record of Leibniz (war-ruined Leipzig, a refused doctorate, God's attorney in Mainz, status-hungry Paris, the Hanover court and the Harz mines, an unmourned death) and his thesis that a single anxiety about Spinoza governed Leibniz's life after their 1676 meeting.

Extends gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz with material from Matthew Stewart's The Courtier and the Heretic (stewart-courtier-and-heretic): the courtier's life, and Stewart's thesis that one anxiety about baruch-spinoza governed Leibniz's four decades after their 1676 meeting.

Stewart's Biography: The Courtier's Life

Stewart's double biography fills in the life beneath the mostly doctrinal portrait in gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz, and the life is unflattering in ways that bear on how the philosophy is read.

A child of the interregnum. Leibniz was born in Leipzig in 1646, into the wreckage of the Thirty Years War: a German population cut from roughly 21 million to 13 million, imperial authority reduced to "historical fiction," sovereignty splintered among hundreds of petty princes, the peace itself a "Peace of Exhaustion." Stewart reads the lifelong hunger for unified order — church reunion, a universal language, a single Empire of Reason — as the appetite of a man born just after the order vanished (stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch03-gottfried).

The Leipzig rejection. His doctoral application was refused. He packed his bags the next morning and set off to ransack the world for friends and influence, inaugurating a life of peripatetic, patron-dependent ambition. Stewart stages this deliberately against Spinoza's cherem: two early expulsions, two opposite responses — withdrawal into self-sufficiency versus a throwing of oneself back at society with greater desperation to succeed.

God's attorney. Under Boineburg and the Elector of Mainz, philosophy and statecraft fused. He defended a candidate for the Polish throne by quasi-geometrical proposition, and drafted Catholic Demonstrations defending transubstantiation and the trinity for a patron who needed the arguments more than he held the convictions. Crucially, he did not argue the doctrine was true — he argued the objections were fallacious. Stewart's formulation: "Philosophical arguments are the moral equivalent of legal briefs," judged by what the jury decides. Leibniz's own habit of calling doctrines "advantageous" or "useful" rather than true — including the immortality of the soul — makes the pattern explicit (stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch05-gods-attorney).

The Egypt Plan and the master-stroke habit. The scheme to divert Louis XIV from Holland into a crusade against Egypt was to secure Germany, reunify Christendom, and carry Leibniz to Paris all at once. Stewart treats it as paradigmatic of a recurring discovery of "convenient concordance between the general good and his personal ambition."

Paris, 1672–1676. Four years of chasing status and patronage while chronically short of money — the collapsed Egypt plan, a fired tutorship, a calculating machine Colbert declined to buy, a rejected Academy candidacy. Amid it he arrived at the calculus notation (the integral sign, then dx) in autumn 1675, driven by a metaphysical intuition he called "the labyrinth of the continuum." Stewart's comic index of the temperamental gap with Spinoza: "1 Leibniz Unit = 11 Spinoza Units" (stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch09-leibniz-in-love).

Hanover, and the mines. The Harz Mountains windmill scheme to pump silver mines dry and fund an Academy consumed years, ran 800 percent over budget, and earned him a reputation among the miners as "a dangerous man and evil to deal with." He also drafted genuinely modern welfare proposals — an Academy of Commerce and Languages, an insurance scheme for widows and orphans (stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch13-surviving-spinoza).

The end. Georg Ludwig, contemptuous of his "living dictionary," conditioned Leibniz's freedom to travel on a History of the House of Brunswick he never finished — effectively house arrest, met with secret trips and fabricated alibis. He lost the presidency of the Berlin Society, had his salary suspended, and was, in Stewart's summary, "reviled in London, scorned in Berlin, ignored in Vienna and Paris." He refused last rites ("Fool, what should I confess?" he scoffed. "I have stolen or taken from no one.") and died attended by a secretary and a coachman. No one from the court came to the funeral; the Berlin and Royal Societies let the death pass unremarked. Stewart's epitaph turns his own doctrine on him: the windowless monad, a man who influenced countless lives "having touched no one very deeply at all" (stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch17-leibniz-end).

Stewart's Thesis: A Career Governed by One Anxiety

Stewart's organizing claim is that a single anxiety governs Leibniz's four decades after November 1676, and that his most famous rivalries are displaced versions of it.

The secret courtship (1671–72). While denouncing the anonymous Tractatus to Thomasius, Arnauld and Spitzel, Leibniz opened a cordial clandestine correspondence with its author under an optics pretext, routed through an intermediary so no direct trail existed. Spinoza saw through it and offered to correspond "in a clandestine way." The same month Leibniz read the Tractatus, his letter to Wedderkopf followed divine omniscience to the "hard conclusion" that God is "the absolute author of all," including the sins of a Pontius Pilate — Spinoza's doctrine reached independently through Leibniz's own principle of sufficient reason. He later scrawled "I later corrected this" in the margin (stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch07-many-faces-of-leibniz).

The instant reversal. A note dated December 12, 1676 — days after the meeting — already concludes that "a God of the kind in which the pious believe" cannot coexist with Spinoza's doctrine; marginalia of the same period on Spinoza's letters to Oldenburg add, "Therefore moral philosophy is destroyed." When he obtained the Opera Posthuma through a backchannel deal with Schuller, he annotated fifteen pages of the Ethics with private contempt — "empty, pretentious," "fallacious," "obscure and abrupt" — notes never meant for other eyes, which Stewart reads as the most sincere evidence of how unsettled he remained. His out-of-nowhere assault on the long-dead Descartes turns out to be a coded proxy: "Descartes thinks in a whisper what Spinoza says at the top of his voice" (stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch13-surviving-spinoza).

Theophile and Polidore. In an unpublished 1678 dialogue Leibniz split himself into a serene victorious Theophile and Polidore, a burnt-out ex-seeker who flirts with a Spinozist world-soul before being argued down. Stewart's sharp observation: the dialogue's closing profession of faith ("I see that virtue and honor are not chimeras…"), which he calls "perhaps the most heartfelt declaration of the great philosopher's ambition," is voiced not by the idealized Theophile but by the recovering Spinozist Polidore. The vocabulary throughout — having "leaned to the side of the Spinozists" before being cured — is that of addiction and recovery.

Every rival is Spinoza. The five-hundred-page New Essays against Locke turns on Locke's hedged suggestion that "some Systems of Matter fitly disposed" might think — functionally identical, to Leibniz, to thought and extension as attributes of one substance. Newton's gravitational attraction implies matter can move without a mindlike principle, and so collapses into the same monism. Even Chinese philosophy, whose ancient li Leibniz admired as anticipating his monadology, curdles into the charge that "modern" Chinese thinkers reduce it to Spinozist brute necessity. Stewart's compression of the political program: "Stop Spinoza" (stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch15-the-haunting).

The charge returned. Leibniz's own 1714 reply to the accusation is the concession that matters: "it is precisely by means of the monads that Spinozism is destroyed," but Spinoza "would be right, if there were no Monads." Stewart's gloss: "if he is wrong, Spinoza is right." Contemporaries and successors — Andala, Lange, Lessing ("I fear that he was himself a Spinozist at heart"), Herder, Russell — pressed the charge anyway. Stewart's psychological verdict is that Leibniz "perceived the influence of his rival everywhere because he mistook the tint in his own spectacles for a certain dark aspect of the outside world."

This record cuts across both readings on gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz. It supports Lohse's shitty-castle-pattern — Leibniz knew what his system was for — but complicates Rao's reading of a premature commitment, since the December 12 note shows the commitment made in days, not arrived at over years.
