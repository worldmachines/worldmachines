El Dorado (Candide)

Commons · Entities · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
El Dorado in Voltaire's Candide: a hidden former Inca country where gold is gravel, inns are free, and there are no courts, prisons or monks, which Candide and Cacambo leave by choice and which then serves as the book's measuring stick for Europe.

El Dorado is the utopian episode of voltaire-candide (Ch. XVII–XVIII), the one country in the book where things seem to go well. This page covers it as the reading notes record it, and how it is used after the heroes leave. The notes stress that the tone is ironic throughout, so it is not a straightforward blueprint.

Getting there

Candide and Cacambo arrive from a world in which every option is dangerous: Westphalia is ravaged, Portugal means burning, the Oreillons nearly ate them. Cacambo concludes "this hemisphere is not better than the other". They starve, take an empty canoe down a river that runs under a mountain, and are wrecked into a plain walled off by mountains, "cultivated for pleasure as well as to produce the necessaries of life", with carriages drawn by large red sheep (voltaire-candide-ch17-arrival-in-el-dorado).

Gold as gravel

Children play quoits with gold, emeralds, rubies and diamonds; Candide takes them for "the king's sons" and the schoolmaster for "the tutor of the royal family", and the schoolmaster smiles and throws the returned stones down. At the inn a lavish dinner is free, inns are kept by the government, and gold is "common pebbles of our highways". The note reads value here as a local convention, with the irony aimed at the travellers' European assumptions as well as at European greed; the pair "took care, however, to gather up the gold". Candide's response is a conditional: "Possibly this is that part of the globe where everything is right, for there must certainly be some such place" (voltaire-candide-ch17-arrival-in-el-dorado).

How it works, in the elder's account

A courtier of 172 explains that this is the old Inca country, preserved because its princes forbade anyone to leave, for "the preservation of our innocence and happiness", and because rocks and precipices keep out Europeans who would "murder us all to the very last man" for its "pebbles and dirt". He reads the Spanish conquest of Peru as a result of Inca imprudence in conquering abroad. Its religion has one God, thanked but never petitioned; asked whether they have monks "to dispute, to govern, to intrigue, and to burn people who are not of the same opinion", he answers "Do you take us for fools?" The king greets visitors with a kiss on each cheek; the city has no law courts and no prisons, but a palace of sciences with three thousand mathematicians (voltaire-candide-ch18-el-dorado).

Utopia by absence

The note observes that the place is built almost entirely out of absences of European institutions: no lawsuits, prisons, monks or persecution. It reads this as satire of dogma, the Inquisition and litigiousness, and as an Enlightenment utopia in the tradition of more-utopia and thomas-more, where gold is likewise despised, though Voltaire's scene is comic where More's is argued. The noble-savage echo is faint and reversed, since these people are learned and technically capable. The science, the note says, is shown without irony, though the machine it builds helps the visitors carry away what the hosts despise (voltaire-candide-ch17-arrival-in-el-dorado, voltaire-candide-ch18-el-dorado).

Leaving paradise

After a month Candide argues that Cunégonde is absent and that "If we stay here we will be just like everyone else", whereas a few sheep of pebbles would make them richer than the kings of Europe. The narrator says the pair's fondness for roving, for making a name and boasting of travels was "so strong… that they resolved to be no longer happy". The king calls the plan "rash and silly", but since all men are free has his engineers build a hoisting machine. The note's reading: a possibly best place is left because of desire, status and love, and Candide's aside that, had Pangloss seen El Dorado, he could no longer have held Thunder-ten-tronckh the finest of edifices undermines the doctrine by comparison rather than argument (voltaire-candide-ch18-el-dorado).

Afterlife as a measuring stick

  • Of a hundred and two laden sheep, two reach Surinam: "there is nothing solid but virtue". Vanderdendur's swindle is "a trick worthy of our old world", and Candide reasons that "if everything is for the best, it is in El Dorado" (voltaire-candide-ch19-surinam-and-martin).
  • Martin's Manichaeism hands the globe to an evil being, El Dorado excepted; one red sheep floats up from the sunk pirate ship (voltaire-candide-ch20-21-at-sea-with-martin).
  • The sheep is left with the Academy of Sciences at Bordeaux, whose prize goes to a proof of why it must be red (voltaire-candide-ch22-paris-1); after the Paris arrest Candide protests that "Travellers are not treated in this manner in the country of El Dorado" (voltaire-candide-ch22-paris-2); in Venice he consoles himself, "I have found my red sheep and Pacquette" (voltaire-candide-ch23-24-portsmouth-paquette-giroflee).

Hedged resonances

The colonial frame of the elder's account resonates loosely with columbian-exchange-mann and grove-green-imperialism, though the scene is about extractive greed rather than ecological exchange (voltaire-candide-ch18-el-dorado).
