Samuel Butler

Commons · Entities · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Samuel Butler, author of Erewhon, as he presents himself in its 1872 and 1901 prefaces: the New Zealand origin of its machine chapters in 'Darwin among the Machines' (1863), the book's patchwork assembly, its link to Life and Habit, and his denial that it ridicules Darwin or has a central idea.

Samuel Butler is the author of Erewhon; or, Over the Range (butler-erewhon), first published in 1872 and revised in 1901. This page records only what his own prefaces to that book say about him and about it. Biography beyond them is not covered here.

In his own voice: the prefaces

The three prefaces are the only parts of Erewhon in which Butler speaks for himself (butler-erewhon-prefaces). The rest of the book belongs to an unnamed first-person narrator, who is himself a satirical target, or to the Erewhonians he reports. Readers should not take the narrator's opinions for Butler's (butler-erewhon-ch01-waste-lands, butler-erewhon-ch29-conclusion).

  • First edition (1872): a single line saying that Erewhon is pronounced as three short syllables.
  • Second edition (9 June 1872): Butler answers early reviewers. He denies that the book rode on the success of The Coming Race. He denies that the chapters on Machines were an attempt to reduce Darwin's theory to an absurdity, though he admits that by choosing not to explain he invited the misreading, and he hints that the book which most deserves the charge of "the specious misuse of analogy" is another, left unnamed. He insists the book does not deny responsibility for actions, calling anyone who does "an enemy who deserves no quarter", and says he added to the chapter on Malcontents to prevent the mistake. He blames the book's inconsistencies on the Erewhonians, for whom anomalies caused no "intellectual inconvenience".
  • Revised edition (7 August 1901): a genealogy of the book and a frank account of its merits and motives.

New Zealand and the machine articles

By Butler's own account the first part of Erewhon to be written was the machine material, in New Zealand's Upper Rangitata district, which also supplied the landscape of the opening chapters. "Darwin among the Machines" appeared in the Christchurch Press on 13 June 1863, signed Cellarius. A second Machines article followed, and in 1865 the material was rewritten as "The Mechanical Creation". Butler says he is better satisfied with these often-rewritten parts than with parts written only once. The second article, he says, led to the theory of Life and Habit, which he believed "quite sound" and which he put in the mouth of an Erewhonian philosopher. That is the closest the prefaces come to Butler endorsing a machine-adjacent idea, and it concerns habit and unconscious memory, not machine consciousness. Within the book, the botany professor's argument about heredity as memory (butler-erewhon-ch27-rights-of-vegetables) is the passage that best matches this strand, though there it remains an Erewhonian's argument.

Assembling and publishing the book

Further pieces (the World of the Unborn, the Musical Banks, the trial of a man for being in a consumption) were strung together into a book only in autumn 1870, at a friend's suggestion, and finished on Sundays. Chapman & Hall's reader rejected it as "a philosophical work, little likely to be popular". Butler credited its success to two reviews and to "the sound of a new voice, and of an unknown voice". In 1901 he says he would rather have cut forty or fifty pages, but copyright law pushed him to add about sixty.

His stance on what the book means

Butler's last word on the book is a caution: "There is no central idea underlying Erewhon". It has hardly any story and is not an organic whole. Together with his denial that it mocks Darwin, his hint about the misuse of analogy, and his statement that the machine material led to Life and Habit, this rules out reading book-of-the-machines as "Butler believed machines would become conscious". The prefaces leave the satire's aim deliberately open.
