Imagination And Opinion Montaigne

Commons · Entities · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne's recurring claim that imagination and opinion make much of the good and evil we suffer: imagination moves bodies and feigned ailments become real, value is what we add, difficulty and prohibition whet desire, credulity in physic grows from fear, and fear, the passion that most dethrones judgment, breeds cruelty; he limits the claim by refusing to measure the possible by his own capacity.

A thread through Montaigne's Essays (montaigne-complete-essays) holds that much of what we suffer and value is made by imagination and opinion. It runs from Book I's Of the Force of Imagination through the essays on opinion, desire and physic. The first half of Of the Relish of Good and Evil, which tests the claim against pain and death, is treated on learning-to-die-montaigne.

Imagination moves bodies

Montaigne starts from himself. He is among those “most sensible of the power of imagination”, catching coughs and pains from the sight of others. A chain of exempla follows, mostly borrowed, with a few things he saw or knows by experience. He judges it very probable that belief in visions and enchantments comes chiefly from imagination working on “vulgar and more easy souls”. What imagination causes, imagination can cure, and he suspects physicians of feeding patients' credulity so that imagination may “supply the imposture of their decoctions”. He keeps his evidence at arm's length: the tales are charged to their sources, and “Magicians are no very good authority with me” (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch20-21-force-imagination). Not to Counterfeit Being Sick collects feigned ailments that became real. He hedges that “fortune, I know not how, sometimes seems to delight in taking us at our word”, and in one case prefers a bodily cause to imagination. A borrowed letter of Seneca turns the theme inward: we do not see our own avarice and anger, as a blind woman complains that the house is dark (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch21-26-against-idleness). For the body's and will's disobedience in sex, see sex-marriage-and-the-ageing-body-montaigne.

Opinion sets the price

“Opinion is a powerful party, bold, and without measure.” The second half of Of the Relish of Good and Evil piles up people who bear or court pain because opinion makes it honourable, and draws the rule that “we only repute for value in them, not what they bring to us, but what we add to them”. Price makes the diamond and difficulty makes virtue. “Fortune does us neither good nor hurt”: she offers the matter and the seed, which the soul turns as it pleases. Montaigne tests this on his own three phases with money, including an anxious hoarding phase. He refuses the extreme cases (“I do not follow these monstrous humours”) and never denies that pain is real. His practical close is to pick whichever consolation suits one's own humour (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch40-relish-good-evil-depends-2).

Difficulty whets desire

The same principle runs through his accounts of appetite. In Book II, chapter 15, rarity and difficulty whet desire and easy possession dulls it, from veils to distant shrines. The Roman liberty of divorce kept wives, and, he says, no civil government was ever reformed by executions, though he does not know that they whet vice (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch13-15-judging-death-another). In the Virgil essay the severity of the obligation laid on women makes suitors keener (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch05-some-verses-virgil-3). Of Moderation adds the converse: penance and medicine work only by being unpleasant to the one who undergoes them, as an exile who enjoyed Lesbos was punished by being confined at home (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch29-moderation).

Physic and belief

In the essay on the resemblance of children to fathers, physicians claim every cure and disown every death. By their own rule the patient's confidence counts for more than the doctor's learning, and their drugs are prized for strangeness (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch37-resemblance-children-fathers-2). Credulity comes from fear: the fear of death and of pain makes belief pliable. In the tale of Lahontan, which Montaigne reports from its inhabitants, a physician taught a custom-governed village the names of diseases, and the villagers say they never felt the evening air to be harmful until physic named it (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch37-resemblance-children-fathers-3).

Fear and the passions that dethrone judgment

Of Fear reports the physicians' view that no passion sooner dethrones judgment. “The thing in the world I am most afraid of is fear”, and anticipated loss is worse than actual poverty or exile (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch16-18-proceeding-some-ambassadors). A passion in distress invents an object: the soul prefers a false and fantastical subject to none, as Xerxes whips the sea (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch03-05-affections-carry-themselves). Fear also breeds cruelty. The slaughter after a victory is done by hangers-on who dared not face the first danger, and tyrants kill because their faint hearts can find no other security, so that cruelty breeds fear of revenge and more cruelty (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch27-28-cowardice-mother-cruelty). Anger is the other passion he says most carries men from right judgment, so punishment belongs to calm (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch31-anger).

Limits

Montaigne does not conclude that everything is imagination. That It Is Folly to Measure Truth and Error by Our Own Capacity confesses that he once pitied believers in ghosts and witchcraft, and argues that to condemn the improbable outright limits God and nature to the bounds of one's own capacity. Of unlikely things attested by trustworthy people, “we ought at least to leave them in suspense” (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch26-folly-measure-truth-error). The notes link the claim that value is what we add, loosely, to habit-and-identity, hedging that the focus is individual valuation rather than collective custom (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch40-relish-good-evil-depends-2). They read the Lahontan tale as a partial fit with Legibility, since naming makes bodies readable to a profession, though Montaigne tells a story of corruption by learning, not a theory of administration (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch37-resemblance-children-fathers-3).
