The Eurasian Steppe

Commons · Entities · updated 23 September 2026 · source on GitHub
The 3.1-million-square-mile grassland belt from Hungary to Manchuria, read by Chaffetz as an inland sea whose forage economy set the political ceiling for every empire on its shores until Qing China and Russia closed it in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

The 3.1-million-square-mile grassland belt running from Hungary to Manchuria, and — in David Chaffetz's Raiders, Rulers, and Traders — the single geographic fact that organized Eurasian political history for three thousand years. This page collects the book's treatment of the steppe as a place with its own ecology, economics, and political grammar, rather than as a backdrop for the peoples who crossed it.

An inland sea

Chaffetz's controlling image, from chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch01-domesticated-for-milk, is explicitly maritime: the steppe is likened to the Mediterranean, a body that "determined the destiny of their shores." Its internal features are read the same way — black earth and feather grasses as the open water, oases as islands, endorheic rivers that run to no ocean. The scale argument does real work: it is why horse pastoralism was viable there at all, and why the settled civilizations on different shores (the Middle East and Iran on one side, India and China on the other) developed so differently.

A refuge before it was a highway

Everything downstream depends on an accident of habitat. Equus was hunted to extinction in its ancestral North America and in Ice Age Europe — the ten thousand slaughtered skeletons at Solutré are the book's marker for the European kill — and survived only in the "drier, colder Eurasian steppe," a refuge with "very few humans, initially," where herds could multiply undisturbed. The steppe is the horse's ark before it is anyone's road.

The multispecies economy

Steppe pastoralism is a designed system, not an arbitrary assemblage. The tradition of the "four heads" — horses, sheep, goats, cattle grazed together — puts the horse at the front as ecological vanguard, because horses are non-ruminant, picky, tall enough to see over grass, able to break snow crust to reach forage, and willing to fight predators. Two twentieth-century experiments in abolishing the horse from the mix, Soviet and post-Soviet, failed ecologically on both occasions. Mare's milk, fermented into kumis, was a staple rather than a supplement, and chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch01-domesticated-for-milk identifies its adoption toward the end of the third millennium BCE as "a major ratcheting up of human dependence on and entanglement with the horse."

Herd growth also drove the founding social split of the book's whole story. Expanding horse herds damaged cropland and competed with farmers for marginal land, pushing herders "deeper into the steppe" — "a process full of historical portent," in Chaffetz's phrase, because it isolated the herder from the farmer and made each more dependent on his own way of life.

Grass as a political ceiling

The steppe's carrying capacity is not a background condition in this book; it is repeatedly the operative cause. chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch08-genghis-khan-empire makes the case most directly: "Pasture control lay at the heart of Mongol statecraft," with envoys clearing pastures ahead of armies, generals splitting forces along separate routes to avoid overgrazing, and a live imperial debate about converting northern China wholesale to pasture. The empire's outer limit is set the same way — Hülegü halted at the gates of Syria not by generalship alone but because Syria "lacked sufficient grass and water for the Mongols' one hundred thousand horses." It is one of the few points in the book where imperial reach is checked by carrying capacity rather than by an enemy.

The inverse holds too. chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch11-the-great-game treats William Moorcroft's diary note on Kabul's cheap winter forage — clover, alfalfa, threshed chaff — as the chapter's real thesis statement: cheap feed, not martial spirit, is why Central Asia out-produced settled empires in cavalry for centuries.

Politically unconquerable, until it wasn't

The strategic lesson the settled powers drew was set early. Cyrus's campaign across the Oxus against Tomyris ends with his death and his skull turned into a drinking goblet, and the Persian conclusion — fortify the frontier and await attacks rather than chase raiders into the grass — becomes the durable posture of every settled power the book covers (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch03-engines-of-empire). Different civilizations then diverged in how they held that line: China maintained and renewed the Great Wall for a millennium, Iran let the Wall of Gorgan crumble and forgot who had built it, and India, unable to sustain steppe herds in its climate, imported horses commercially instead of importing horse-breeding populations (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch07-hunting-for-supremacy).

The steppe's own political weakness was the mirror of its strength. Loyalty there was contingent: "If they so chose, horsemen could simply saddle up and abandon their chiefs." Crimean khans were overthrown by their own followers dozens of times across three centuries. The same autonomy that made steppe peoples impossible to subjugate made steppe polities impossible to stabilize — a textbook instance of capture-resistance with its costs shown as well as its benefits, and precisely the seam Russia exploited for two hundred years (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch10-empires-strike-back).

The closing

chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch10-empires-strike-back and chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch11-the-great-game narrate the end as a sequence rather than an event: Qing extermination of the Oirats (smallpox, massacre, enslavement, more than a million dead — flagged in the notes as "perhaps the first large-scale and historically attested example of genocide," and the book's contribution to necropolitics); the 1771 Kalmyk flight, "the last great migration of a steppe nation in history," which killed roughly half those who set out; the 1881 Treaty of St Petersburg, the first formal border ever drawn across the steppe, which in Chaffetz's words buried "the political independence of the steppe peoples"; and Skobelev's destruction of the Akhal-Teke-breeding Turkmen the same year.

The twentieth century finished the job by other means. chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-epilogue records Soviet collectivization taking Kazakh livestock from thirty-six million head in 1928 to three million in 1932, and Iran's parallel campaign against the khans pursued explicitly as disarmament and forced settlement. The anthropologist Anatoly Khazanov's anecdote is the epilogue's moral center: a Soviet work brigade needing four trucks to do what one horseman and his son used to do, and the foreman's deadpan explanation — "But in those days the herd belonged to the horseman."

Afterlife

Afghanistan alone escaped the social engineering until 1978, and became the refuge for breeders fleeing Soviet Central Asia and Iran, who brought their bloodstock with them — the book's closing image of a habitat where an older way of life persisted after being extinguished everywhere else, in the sense The Thicket gives that idea. The book's last scene has foreign observers unable to follow the riders, who vanish "into the freedom of the limitless steppe."
