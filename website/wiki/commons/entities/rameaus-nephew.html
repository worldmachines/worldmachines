Rameau's Nephew

Commons · Entities · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Rameau's Nephew (Le Neveu de Rameau), Diderot's dialogue between a philosopher-narrator (I) and the parasite Rameau (He) about genius, flattery, patronage and the morality of interest, as given in John Morley's abridged English translation in the Appendix to Volume II of Diderot and the Encyclopædists.

Le Neveu de Rameau is a dialogue by denis-diderot. This page describes the work as it appears in the Appendix to Volume II of john-morley's diderot-and-the-encyclopaedists, which prints Morley's own English translation (morley-diderot-v2). Morley's account of the dialogue's history and his reading of it belong to Volume I; see the Rameau's Nephew section of denis-diderot and morley-diderot-v1.

Attribution matters throughout. The words and positions are Diderot's, in Morley's English, not Morley's argument. They belong to two characters: I (the philosopher-narrator, “Moi”) and He (the nephew of the composer Rameau, “Lui”). Morley's own voice appears only in bracketed notes and stage directions, which mark where he has omitted pages about forgotten personages and the technical musical discussion (morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-1, morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-2). The dialogue does not settle the arguments it stages, and both voices are Diderot's.

Course of the dialogue

  • Opening. The narrator muses in the Palais Royal and watches the chess-players at the Regency coffee-house. He portrays the nephew as a mixture of elevation and lowness, and defends such “originals” as “a piece of leaven” that breaks social uniformity and unmasks rogues. The talk runs from chess and the famous uncle to whether men of genius are a blessing or a curse, and ends with He's confession of envy (“So I was jealous of my uncle”) (morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-1).
  • The lost patrons and the music lesson. He explains that his patrons expelled him for showing “a little taste, a trifle of wit, a spice of reason”. He recites the soliloquy of a would-be flatterer, mimes violin and piano, mocks I's plan to educate his daughter in reason and morality, and reconstructs a music lesson as a comedy of flattery (morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-2).
  • Idioms of vocation. He offers a theory of social morality and a fantasy of what he would do if rich. He and I clash over duty and happiness, and He demonstrates his craft of flattering a gloomy patron and a vain woman of fashion (morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-3).
  • The parasite's creed. He describes the rich man's table, where hangers-on abuse Buffon, Montesquieu, Rousseau, Voltaire, D'Alembert and Diderot, links it to Palissot's play The Philosophers, explains how he reads moralists, and narrates his expulsion (morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-4).
  • Conclusion. He tells the story of the Renegade of Avignon, explains his moral deafness by “the paternal molecule”, describes how he is bringing up his son, and acts out the “pantomime” of the needy, which I generalises to all of society. He leaves for the opera (morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-5).

Themes

Genius against ordinary goodness. He argues that men of genius are “good only for one thing” and that the world is better run by moderate duty and flattery. I answers with a long-run calculus: posterity will weep at Racine, whose harm fell on few, and the same holds for voltaire and Greuze; the man of genius is a tree that stunts a few neighbours but shades all comers. When He asks why nature did not make the great also good, I suggests the whole is perhaps neither good nor ill “if it is necessary, as so many good folk suppose”, a hedge toward determinism that He declines to follow (morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-1).

Patronage and parasitism. He's patrons valued him for his vices and paid their affection in titbits, cuffs and kicks; his lesson is “the difference between what God made you, and what your protectors wanted you to be” (morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-2). He describes a tacit compact between patrons and parasites, with nature as a second public prosecutor punishing the vices the laws miss, and reverses the hierarchy of patron and dependent: the king's fool existed, the king's sage never did, and whoever keeps a fool may be his own fool's fool (morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-4).

Morality as grammar, and interest as its measure. He holds that there is “a general conscience, just as there is a general grammar”, with each calling allowed its exceptions, “idioms of vocation”, and that all ranks devour one another as species do in nature (morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-3). He claims nothing in manners is “absolutely, essentially, and universally true or false”, only what interest requires, and that he reads Molière to learn the tone of vice, not to avoid it (morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-4). I answers with a counter-ethic: the pleasure of having “succoured the unfortunate” or written “a good page”, and the claim that “it is impossible to be unhappy under the shelter of good actions”. He's reply, that honest people are unhappy and the dishonest happy, is left standing (morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-3).

Heredity, education and appetite. He explains his deaf heart by fibre, bad company and the paternal molecule, and adapts his son's education to Paris, not Lacedaemon: show him the louis. I warns that a child left with a man's passions would “strangle his father and dishonour his own mother”. A bracketed narrator passage observes that He is more candid and consistent than most, not more vicious (morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-5). The materialist idiom belongs to Diderot's circle but is placed in a cynic's mouth; the dialogue does not endorse it.

The pantomime of dependence. He's postures of the needy man (“he jumps, he crawls, he wriggles”) become, in I's generalisation, a picture of the whole hierarchy: “Whoever has need of another is indigent, and assumes a posture”, and king, minister and abbé all dance before someone. He refuses to “perch on the epicycle of Mercury” and classify humanity as Réaumur classifies flies (morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-5).

World Machines resonances, with the notes' hedges

The notes read the patron-and-client machinery, the flattery loops and the pantomime of dependence as small social machines of legitimacy by performance, and compare them loosely with the modernity-machine and world-machines language of institutional dependency (morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-2, morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-5). Each time they add the hedge that this is satire of Paris manners and one man's psychology, not an analysis of institutions. Palissot's play and the literary underworld that abuses the philosophers can be set beside the counter-enlightenment, though the dialogue does not use that frame (morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-4). The dialogue also shows the Enlightenment's satirical self-critique pulling apart an enlightenment-monism-era moral framework, a connection the notes call loose (morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-5).
