Printing Press and Information Overload (Eisenstein)

Commons · Entities · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Eisenstein's version of print-era information overload: a condition inaugurated by print's duplicative and preservative powers, marked by a century and a half of mingled enlightenment and nonsense, met by new ordering tools, and intensified rather than resolved by every later medium.

Extends printing-press-and-information-overload with elizabeth-eisenstein's historical account from eisenstein-printing-revolution. That page narrates the print case as disorientation followed by correction: a flood outruns existing filters, bad actors thrive, and new practices restore orientation. Eisenstein's evidence supports the first half at length. It complicates the second.

The book begins with an overload

Her project starts from Bridenbaugh's 1962 lament that historians suffered “collective amnesia”. She reframes the problem as recall: data “impinging on us from so many directions and with such speed” that ordering capacity was “strained to the breaking point”. It is a rate-of-publication problem, not a moral failing (eisenstein-printing-revolution-introduction).

Altered intake

Increased output changed already-literate readers first. A buyer could bring many books into one study, ending the itinerant scholar and opening an “era of intense cross referencing”. Montaigne could see more books in a few months in his tower “than earlier scholars had seen after a lifetime of travel”, and she credits that volume with part of the “conflict and diversity” he felt. Contradictions between Aristotelians and Ptolemaists, Arabists and Galenists became “more visible”, and that friction generated new combinations (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s1-wide-dissemination). Rival authorities multiplied in astronomy too: six sets of tables by the 1640s where one had sufficed, forcing choice rather than settling it (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s2-copernican-revolution).

Enlightenment and nonsense together

The same traffic that regularized classical and scientific texts spread the hermetic corpus, sibylline prophecy and pseudo-hieroglyphs. Ficino's 1463 hermetic translation was taken for a pre-Platonic text until Casaubon redated it in 1614 (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s1-wide-dissemination). When scribal secrecy dissolved and “technology went to press”, the occult backlog went with it. “For at least a century and a half confusion persisted” while a “natural history of nonsense” flourished and miracle workers proved “more adept at press agentry than at any of the older arts” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch05-permanent-renaissance-3). The note on that passage matches her “century and a half” with the ~150 years information-overload-across-eras gives. It qualifies that her case is guild secrecy giving way to a publicity market, not an internet abolishing gatekeepers.

Tools of reorientation

Overload was met by new technique, not only raw accumulation. Reference works grew until they needed “new surveys” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s4-data-collection). Conrad Gesner's 1545 Bibliotheca universalis was the first and last serious universal bibliography. Specialized bibliographies multiplied until “bibliographies of bibliographies of bibliographies” were needed. Medieval Europe had needed about ten scribes per reader, and within a century of print the ratio inverted: abundance “can be no less dispiriting than scarcity” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-2). Ann Blair's note-taking manuals and commonplace books belong here. So does a figure Eisenstein adds, the printing-house corrector (Gabriel Harvey reading for John Wolfe), whose trained intensive reading print culture newly required (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-1).

Where Eisenstein departs from the base page

  • Not resolved. Her final remarks treat overload as print's enduring by-product. “An enhanced capacity to store and retrieve, preserve and transmit, has kept pace with an enhanced capacity to create and destroy, innovate and outmode”. Chronos's “voracious appetite” is now surpassed by “a monstrous capacity to disgorge”, and new media compound the problem (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-final-remarks).
  • Preservation, not only distribution. In 2005 she calls overload a “diachronic” side effect of print's preservative capacity, compounded by continuing hand copying, and admits it “should have been given more emphasis” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-3). The same accumulation appears as an “overpopulation of Parnassus” burdening each new generation of writers (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch04-s2-men-of-letters).
  • Productive as well as disorienting. In her account cross-referencing and contradiction were engines of invention, not only hazards (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s1-wide-dissemination).
