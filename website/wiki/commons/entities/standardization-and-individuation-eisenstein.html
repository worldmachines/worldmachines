Standardization and Individuation (Eisenstein)

Commons · Entities · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Eisenstein's second feature of print culture: a relative, “sufficiently uniform” standardization of texts, images, indexes and rites whose dialectical by-products were a sharpened sense of diversity and of the idiosyncratic individual self.

elizabeth-eisenstein treats standardization as a feature of print culture in its own right, and she pairs it with its opposite. “The more standardized the type, indeed, the more compelling the sense of an idiosyncratic personal self” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s2-standardization). The suffix marks her usage. Her standardization is relative and early-modern, not the uniformity of later mechanical reproduction.

Relative, not absolute

She rejects the idea that early printed copies were identical. The operative standard is “sufficiently uniform”, enough for scattered scholars to correspond about the same page. Sarton's formula is endorsed: printing “made it possible for the first time to publish hundreds of copies that were alike and yet might be scattered everywhere” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s2-standardization). When Adrian Johns documented misprints, piracy and non-identical First Folios, she replied that he was refuting a claim she never made. Early printed products were “more standardized than were hand-copied ones”, and that comparative gain is the whole claim (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-3). The sharpest evidence is a new capacity to correct: errata sheets, and the 1631 “Wicked Bible”, a standardized error no scribe could have propagated (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s2-standardization).

Its reach

  • Letters, numbers and pictures together. Following Sarton's “double invention”, typography and engraving rendered text, number and image repeatable at once. Technical literature depended on their interplay (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch02-defining-initial-shift-1). The images of Ptolemy, Vitruvius and Galen, lost over centuries of copying, were regained once print could reproduce them exactly (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch02-defining-initial-shift-2).
  • Order as infrastructure. Full alphabetical order was still a self-conscious novelty on the eve of print: a Genoese encyclopedist explained ordering “amo” before “bibo”, and York's library was catalogued in verse. No scribal system could make an ordering rule stick across copies; print made cataloguing commercially necessary. Ramist “method” became a marketable format, and Rastell's “Tabula” gave readers their first systematic view of parliamentary history (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s3-reorganizing-texts). Its least conspicuous innovations were pagination, running heads and title pages. She keeps this modest in 2005: repeated encounters with them “probably affected the mental habits of readers”, though each element derived from scribal practice (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-1).
  • Rite. For the first time in Christian history liturgy could be made uniform across the Latin-rite world (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch06-christendom-disrupted-1).
  • Rank and face. Repeated costume images hardened class and regional types. A printed royal portrait let “an alert Frenchman... recognize and halt Louis XVI at Varennes” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s2-standardization). Model petitions made provincial demands uniform even as print extended the crown's reach (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch04-s1-reading-public).

The dialectical twins

  • Diversity. Uniformity and diversity are “two sides of the same coin”. A Seville pattern book made “Spanish” fashion visible across the Habsburg lands, and the more standardized the typical image of a town or plant, the more clearly departures could be seen. The same process made “Gothic” and “barocco” namable as deviations from a classical canon (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s2-standardization).
  • The individual. Montaigne's Essays exposed a “solitary singular self” against the ideal types other books multiplied. The informal essay solved a new rhetorical problem, addressing scattered, solitary readers rather than an assembled crowd (same note). Woodcut portraits, prone to “comedies of errors”, still attached “distinct physiognomies” to “distinct names” (Erasmus, Luther, Loyola) for the first time. Hence Burckhardt's Renaissance individual owes “much more” to printing (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch05-permanent-renaissance-2). A reading public is structurally “more atomistic and individualistic than a hearing one” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch04-s1-reading-public).
  • Credit. The Royal Society's signed and dated Transactions drew authors with the lure of fame. The Cimento's anonymous Saggi removed that incentive and helped kill the academy within a decade (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s3-galileos-trial-2).

A cost

Rationalizing an old code can embalm it. The fully restored, indexed Digest grew less relevant to living law as it grew more coherent (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s3-reorganizing-texts).

World Machines resonances, with their hedges

  • Legibility and legibility-as-machine-core. Identical maps, calendars and costume manuals read as legibility avant la lettre. legibility-as-precondition-not-cause would caution that Eisenstein's material shows the infrastructure doing divergent work: hardening stereotypes and dynastic surveillance on one side, producing Montaigne's private self on the other (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s2-standardization).
  • print-culture-and-the-individual-author. Eisenstein supplies a concrete mechanism, the standardized type's dialectical negative, for that page's general claim that print produced solitary selfhood (same note).
  • contested-uniformity-bayly. The Johns dispute has the same shape as debates over Bayly's paradox: partial, directional uniformity read by a critic as total uniformity (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-3).
  • infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness. Print's “silent publicity” gestures toward interiority built by material conditions. Her focus stays on public fame and biography rather than inward experience, so the fit is partial (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch05-permanent-renaissance-2).
