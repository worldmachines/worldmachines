The Horse Road

Commons · Entities · updated 23 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Chaffetz's name for the trans-Eurasian horse economy that the label 'Silk Road' obscures — caravans functioning as itinerant states, fairs that seeded pilgrimage sites, silk serving as currency rather than cargo, and the monopolies built to control it.

Chaffetz's proposed correction to the name "Silk Road," and — beyond the naming argument — the trans-Eurasian horse economy the name obscures: breeders, caravans, fairs, currencies, and the state monopolies built to squeeze them.

The argument about the name

chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch05-silk-road-or-horse-road states it plainly: caravan chiefs "might have called their route not the Silk Road but the Horse Road," because horses were the true strategic commodity of the time. The evidence offered is volume, price, and necessity rather than sentiment.

  • The Tang spent roughly ten percent of the state budget on imported horses.
  • Indian courts imported up to seventy thousand horses a year, at an aggregate value Chaffetz estimates at four to five billion dollars in modern terms.
  • General Li Guangli's expedition, conventionally cited as the Silk Road's founding act, was explicitly a hunt for war-horses (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch04-heavenly-horses).
  • Silk's actual role was as a standardized unit of currency, not cargo — a horse fetching eighteen to forty bolts against a slave girl's six — used because China lacked monetary silver until the sixteenth century. When silk debased, tea replaced it.

The reason the misnomer stuck is itself the interesting part, and it is an epistemics point rather than a trade point: "Silk Road" is a twentieth-century archaeological construction built from what survives in the ground. Buried silk bundles endure; horses and the trade in them do not. The label captures the system's artifacts rather than its substance — a distributed infrastructure that operated for a millennium before a much later observer imposed a tidy name on it, in the manner history-machine describes.

Who ran it

The system's founding polity is a refugee state. The Yuezhi, shattered by the Huns in 176 BCE, fled two thousand miles in two generations, and their descendants — the Kushan yabgu — federated scattered Indo-Scythian and Central Asian horse-breeding peoples into a single polity to seize control of the route, applying, as Chaffetz notes, exactly the monopoly technique the Huns had used against their own ancestors. Empire here is an imitated trade-control method, not a conquest of territory for its own sake.

Sitting at the crossroads of Chinese, Indian, and Iranian demand, the Kushans also effectively invented horse breeds. Not by design, but through market logic: buyers wanted predictable, colour-branded foals resembling famous sires, breeders obliged, and the pool of favoured stallions shrank. The consequence is measurable in DNA — reduced Y-chromosome diversity relative to earlier Scythian and Hun horse populations — and visible today in the paedomorphic beauty standards of the Akhal-Teke, Arab, and Thoroughbred. Equine aesthetics as downstream of trade economics is one of the book's sharpest moves.

Caravans as itinerant states

The horse caravan of chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch05-silk-road-or-horse-road is thousands of people and tens of thousands of animals, armed and disciplined, blurring into "a military campaign." Joseph Kessel's eyewitness description — "opulent, powerful, proud," fully armed, filling the entire road — anchors the claim. Caravans extorted additional fairs from reluctant hosts, answered banditry by growing larger and better armed, and served as "schools of war" whose graduates went on to shape the political fates of both China and India. Their peculiar economics deserve noting: the inventory is alive, so it feeds and appreciates itself even when a journey is stalled by war, giving the caravan what Chaffetz calls political and ecological sustainability. This is a quasi-sovereign institution rather than a commercial convoy.

Fairs, and what they built

Where the fairs met a strong state they were boxed in, and where they met a weak one they reshaped the landscape. In China the Great Wall doubled as customs infrastructure, mandarins inspected quality, and quantities were rationed to ministry need. In India, with no hegemonic state, they sprawled — and Chaffetz traces a striking chain from there: horse demand drained India of metallic currency, which forced the invention of an export textile industry (an "early but profound industrial revolution"), which required new Southeast Asian partners to supply silver. The fairs at Haridwar and Pushkar seeded major Hindu pilgrimage sites and the Kumbh Mela itself. Sacred geography built on the scaffolding of horse commerce.

Monopsony, monopoly, and the empire China built by accident

chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch04-heavenly-horses contains the sharpest structural episode in the trade's history. The Han ran their border horse market as a monopsony — a single buyer setting fixed terms at a handful of crossings. Modun grasped that the same chokepoint, seized from the other side, would make him the sole seller, wielding "as much power and awe as the emperor himself." The Hun empire is thus framed as an unintended consequence of China's own attempt at legible market control: the imposition of order generating the counter-formation it was meant to prevent, and leaving behind an "unwritten playbook on how to operate a steppe empire" that the Turks and Mongols later inherited.

The same chapter records the other direction of flow. What Chinese court ritual dressed up as tribute was, from the steppe side, a redistribution engine — khans who received silk and gold immediately gave it away to more distant clans to buy loyalty, since accumulated wealth was useless to people with "no place to keep it." "Far from weakening the khan's bellicosity, the gifts made him more powerful." A model of authority built on outward flow rather than stored capital, and therefore illegible to the settled state's own accounting.

Downstream dependencies

The route did not end with antiquity. Babur called India "a land of no good horses," and the Mughals stayed permanently dependent on Turki, Arab, and Iranian stock arriving via Kabul and Kandahar; losing Afghanistan in 1737 proved fatal in a way that losing revenue-rich territory was not (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch09-riding-the-whirlwind). The Portuguese Estado da India inserted itself into the same economy from the sea, arming the southern sultanates through control of the Hormuz–Goa route — Afonso de Albuquerque's line that "everyone who possesses a Persian horse can govern throughout the Deccan" is the trade used as a pure geopolitical lever by an actor with no steppe at all.

Britain inherited the dependency along with the Mughal position. Domestic supply fell as internal peace killed the profit motive for warhorse breeding, and the search for remounts from Bukhara — a market no Englishman had visited since Elizabeth I — sent Moorcroft east in 1819 against orders, opening the rivalry with Russia that became the Great Game (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch11-the-great-game).

The end

The road closed not through obsolescence alone but through borders. chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-epilogue records the collapse of the Bukhara, Turfan, and Lahore markets and of the caravan routes under twentieth-century border closures, with Central Asia going from wealthy emporium to one of the most isolated and impoverished regions on earth (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch12-last-horse-powers). The buyers vanished too — the Indian maharajas abolished in 1971, the Qing bannermen faded in Republican China — leaving Afghan khans and beys as the last patrons of a trade that had run for two thousand years.
