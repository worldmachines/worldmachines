The art of being selfish (Wulf)

Commons · Entities · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Andrea Wulf's organising tension between free will and selfishness, traced through the contemporary charges of egotism, the Jena Set's enacted self-absorption and Fichte's national Ich, and her Epilogue's sympathetic resolution that 'selfish' once meant self-awareness as the ground of empathy, a reading the notes flag as leaving the dark side underdeveloped.

Wulf frames modern life as a thin line between free will and selfishness, self-determination and narcissism, empathy and righteousness, and tests the line on her own biography first: her independence 'became egotistical' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-1). The book's core tension, in her words, is between the 'tunnel vision of individual perspective' and belief in change for the greater good (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-2). The Epilogue's final part, 'The Art of being Selfish', is her resolution (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-influence-selfish). Between the two the narrative supplies a great deal of evidence for the darker side.

What contemporaries charged

  • Critics predicted that Fichte's Ich would breed egotistical self-absorption and despotism; Wulf judges much of this unfair, while noting his precarious balance between the empowered self, narcissism and equality (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-2).
  • Schiller feared the Ich/non-Ich 'vortex' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch03-finest-minds-2), judged the whole younger generation 'too self-centred and highly strung', having 'taken Fichte's Ich-philosophy too literally' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch07-little-academy-2), and later said the Schlegels had 'sullied the concept of a self-determined Ich with their self-absorption and arrogance' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-1).
  • Goethe remarked that the friends 'chew the cud of their Ich's' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch14-schlegel-clique); Wieland had called Goethe himself 'the greatest egoist I've ever met' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch01-happy-event-1).

These are the actors' verdicts, reported by Wulf, not her thesis.

What the narrative shows

  • Fichte abandoned his fiancée because marriage would 'clip his wings' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-1), and 'seemed to forget his celebration of free will when it came to women' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch13-divorce-women-sex).
  • Novalis moulded Fichte's free Ich into a plan to die by will alone ('Death is self-conquest'), which the note reads as a personal, not political, dark side (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch08-handful-of-darkness).
  • Under attack the friends felt invincible and ignored the gravely ill Charlotte Schiller next door (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch15-new-confederation); 'People hate us - good!' treated hostility as proof of effectiveness (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-1).
  • Dorothea's phrase for the circle, the 'republic of despots', is hers, not Wulf's (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-2).
  • Friedrich advocated women's equality in print while pursuing Sophie Mereau (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-1); partisan reports make him debt-ridden and interested only in being loved, while Dorothea, who had 'truly lived Fichte's empowered Ich', became subservient (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-1).

From the self to the nation

Wulf's single explicit political warning is Fichte's 1807 'bigger Ich - the Ich of a nation', 'a dangerous idea' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1). The path to it is traced in inwardness-without-a-state-wulf.

Wulf's resolution

The Epilogue defends Fichte against 'Ich-fetishism', citing his 1794 line that only those are free who try to make everyone around them free, and treats freedom and duty as twins. It redefines 'selfish' historically as being aware of and in control of one's own being, liberated from a predestined place under monarchs. In Schelling's frame this also means recognising one's unity with nature, so that harming the planet harms oneself. Resting on Novalis, self-understanding becomes the prerequisite for understanding others, and so for empathy (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-influence-selfish).

Hedges

The note on the Epilogue calls this Wulf's sympathetic reading. She acknowledges the modern sense of selfishness and the perils of the emboldened self, but the historical dark side of egotism and nationalism belongs to earlier chapters and is only gestured at in the conclusion (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-influence-selfish). The darker reading is Berlin's, on romanticism-and-political-danger. The same note links unselfing as a related concept without developing the comparison (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-influence-selfish). See also invention-of-the-self-wulf.
