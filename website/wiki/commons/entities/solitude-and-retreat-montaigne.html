Solitude And Retreat Montaigne

Commons · Entities · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne's account of solitude as an inward condition rather than a place: the backshop of self-possession built before retirement, the three commerces of friends, women and books ending in the tower library, the vexations of a household, freedom from obligation and travel for its own sake, and the unfortified house and inner witness of conscience in the civil wars.

Montaigne's Essays (montaigne-complete-essays) return often to retreat: from office, from crowds, from obligation, and at last into the self. The early essay Of Solitude sets the terms, and Book III's Of Three Commerces and Of Vanity apply them to his own house, library and travels. The arrière-boutique quoted from another translation on michel-de-montaigne is, in Cotton's English, the backshop.

A condition of the mind, not of place

Of Solitude sets aside the old debate between the active and the contemplative life and asks what solitude is. Changing place does not cure the mind. Socrates said of a traveller that he took himself along with him. True solitude is the mind called home, and it “may be enjoyed even in populous cities and the courts of kings, though more commodiously apart”. It must be built before one withdraws, and it suits those who have already served the world and are in decline (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch38-solitude).

The backshop

We should “reserve a backshop, wholly our own and entirely free”, so that losing wife, children or goods is no new thing. They are not to be given up, since they must be had, but happiness must not depend on them. In the decay of age one may espouse nothing but oneself, so far as the ties can be undone without flaying, for “the greatest thing in the world is for a man to know that he is his own”. He rejects two false retirements. Pliny and Cicero retire only to write for fame, “a ridiculous contradiction”, reputation being “the most useless, frivolous, and false coin that passes current”. The bookish retreat costs health like any other pleasure. The essay closes on quoted advice from two philosophers, which he endorses as the precept of true philosophy: care how you talk to yourself, not how the world talks of you, and keep Cato, Phocion and Aristides as inward witnesses (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch38-solitude).

Solitude within company

Of Three Commerces places the same idea in his temperament. His solitude is “to withdraw my thoughts and affections into myself”, not to flee people, and at court the crowd throws him back on himself. Three commerces have occupied him: a few sincere and able men, beautiful women, and books. The first two depend on others; books are “much more our own”, a viaticum for the journey. The essay ends in his round tower library with its five rows of shelves, where he walks while dictating and where “I am in my kingdom”. The note links this to studiolo-and-self-cultivation, with the hedge that Montaigne describes it plainly as a retreat (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch03-three-commerces). The dedication of Of the Affection of Fathers ties the book itself to solitude: a melancholy humour bred by solitude first put the fancy of writing in his head (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch08-affection-fathers-children-1); that origin story is told on essay-as-self-study-montaigne.

The house and its vexations

Retreat did not mean ease. Of Vanity reports that he came late to governing a house and runs it by inattention; small ills prick deeper than great ones, and he is calm abroad and irritable at home. He calls public service the most honourable calling but disclaims it for himself (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch09-vanity-1).

Freedom from obligation, and travel

“I have nothing mine but myself, and yet the possession is, in part, defective and borrowed.” Montaigne fears owing favours: giving is authoritative, accepting is a kind of submission, and a favour may be paid but is never dissolved. Travel is a school in the diversity of lives. He rides long stages despite the stone, and “I only walk for the walk's sake”. He looks on all men as compatriots while loving Paris down to her warts, and holds back from Socrates' harder examples. To friends who object that he is married and old, he replies that absence refreshes both marriage and friendship. He wants a death “quiet, solitary, and all my own” (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch09-vanity-3).

Retreat in the civil wars

Retreat was also a form of defence. Civil war, he writes, makes us stand sentinels in our own houses; he has gone to bed fearing betrayal and murder, and prefers an open enemy to “an enemy in a gown” (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch09-vanity-3). He kept his house unfortified, judging that “defence allures attempt, and defiance provokes an enemy”, and describes this as withdrawing one corner of his estate from the public tempest as he withdraws another corner in his soul. The note stresses that this is self-report, not a rule for everyone (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch13-15-judging-death-another). Of Conscience begins with a civil-war encounter between enemies who carry no outward mark of language or habit, and argues that conscience “makes us betray, accuse, and fight against ourselves”, while a good conscience steadied his own pace through hazards (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch04-05-morrows-new-day).

Hedges

The notes keep this retreat moderate and social. Montaigne calls himself “all without and in sight, born for society”, and his solitude qualifies any reading of him as a hermit (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch03-three-commerces). The studiolo-and-self-cultivation link fits the tower library better than Of Solitude, where the self is cultivated by withdrawal and rehearsal rather than by writing. One note sees in the inner life a minimal echo of the project's interest in what cannot be captured, though Montaigne's concern is moral, not political (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch38-solitude).
