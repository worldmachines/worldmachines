Contested Uniformity (Bayly)

Commons · Entities · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Bayly's organizing paradox for 1780–1914: states, religions, ideologies, economies and bodily practices converged worldwide while the same connections heightened difference and antagonism, and outward uniformity grew together with inward complexity.

Contested uniformity is the organizing paradox of bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world. Between 1780 and 1914, states, religions, ideologies, economies and everyday “bodily practices” (dress, speech, diet, family relations) converged worldwide. Yet the same connections could “heighten the sense of difference, and even antagonism, between people in different societies, and especially between their elites” (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-intro-thematic-problems-of-history). The suffix marks the page as Bayly's usage: the bare phrase would be broader than his.

The claim

  • One process seen from two sides. Bayly borrows Arjun Appadurai's image of the global and the local “cannibalizing” each other, and insists the pattern long predates the globalization that followed 1945 (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-intro-thematic-problems-of-history).
  • Uniformity is not homogeneity. Elite male dress converged on the top hat and morning coat, yet Maori chiefs paired a black coat and bow tie with their tattoos, and Geronimo posed in a suit as readily as with a rifle. Convergence was uneven by class, region and gender. Even the burkah is presented as a modern innovation that let women enter public and commercial life. The convergence was also openly resisted: an Ottoman conservative denounced a hybrid-uniformed army as “a grotesque parody of Europe” (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-intro-riddle-of-modernity-and-the-body).
  • Paired with complexity. As societies converged outwardly they differentiated inwardly. Legal, medical, financial and administrative professions split off from kinship and from the old military and religious poles of authority (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-intro-riddle-of-modernity-and-the-body). The conclusion restates the pairing: uniformity and complexity grew “at the same time, and paradoxically” (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch13-uniformity-complexity-and-the-road-to-1914).
  • Similar forms, different meanings. “Formal similarity and mutual translatability still often masked significant difference in intrinsic style”. At the 1893 World Parliament of Religions, for example, traditions could be ranked formally as “religions” without erasing what a shared rite meant in each (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-intro-riddle-of-modernity-and-the-body).

Where it shows

  • Bodies. Dress, watches and clock towers, personal names, and grammars converging on English models (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-intro-riddle-of-modernity-and-the-body).
  • Cities. Grid streets and demarcated public space flattened old “urban villages”, and in doing so fostered “exclusive and sometimes antagonistic identities”. Segregation and race ideology intensified in the 1880s and 1890s, just as cities grew most alike (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch05-cities-and-urban-culture).
  • Nations. Making a nation and making its minorities were one act, and minority leaderships co-authored their own ethnic histories (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch06b-empires-to-peoples-without-states). “Growing international uniformity went alongside growing conflict between closely defined nation-states” (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch06c-new-imperialism-and-internationalism).
  • The state. The state's apparatus spread everywhere, but the reciprocal welfare bargain it enabled at home was withheld in the colonies (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch07c-state-resources-and-obligations).
  • Ideas. Popular sovereignty, positivism and socialism were narrowed or re-encoded against each locality's prior order (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch08-representation-secularism-socialism). Social Darwinism was grafted onto local hierarchies such as Hindu purity and pollution or Japanese divine descent (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch08-science-professionalization). Liberal anti-feudalism re-founded hierarchy as a ranking of races and cultures (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch08-liberalism-land-trade).
  • Religion. The same print and pilgrimage circuits that standardized orthodoxy also let heterodox movements travel and multiply (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-pilgrimage-print-nation).
  • Arts. Neoclassical and neo-Gothic state buildings are the book's “contested global uniformity” in built form (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch10-emerging-nation-arts-and-outside-west). Uniformizing pressure produced hybrids running in both directions (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch10-arts-politics-and-hybridity). Elite reading converged while popular forms proliferated beneath it (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch10-world-literature-and-conclusion).
  • Hierarchy. Elites selected hierarchies for survival because they held together relationships that were growing “more uniform yet also more complex” (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch11-historiography-and-gender).
  • Revolt. The rebels of the 1850s understood their own revolts through local cosmologies, yet the myth of a shared global struggle became a political force of its own (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch04-civil-war-and-convergence).

World Machines resonances, with their hedges

  • legibility-and-divergence and legibility-vs-complexity-the-master-tension. Contested uniformity is close kin to both. Bayly, however, keeps the two forces braided within the same bodies and institutions, where WMP treats them as opposed machines (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-intro-riddle-of-modernity-and-the-body, bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch13-uniformity-complexity-and-the-road-to-1914).
  • legibility-as-machine-core. The uniformity of bodily practices is a softer, cultural analogue of the legibility drive. Still, Bayly describes a contingent and contested convergence rather than a single machine, and he stresses that uniformity produced antagonism rather than dissolving it (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-intro-thematic-problems-of-history).
  • divergence-machine. This is only a partial fit. The Divergence Machine treats variety as a flourishing force, whereas in Bayly's cities uniform conditions generate antagonism and hierarchy, not plurality that resists capture (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch05-cities-and-urban-culture). His heterodox movements adopt legibility's idiom rather than resisting it (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-pilgrimage-print-nation).
  • legibility-vs-divergence-the-civilizational-tradeoff. A compressing force whose by-product is excess local variety is a structural rhyme in the arts, not a claim that the mechanism is the same (bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch10-arts-politics-and-hybridity).

An early-modern antecedent: Eisenstein's print

elizabeth-eisenstein's eisenstein-printing-revolution reaches a similar shape of claim for 1450–1700, from books rather than states. The batch notes draw the rhyme and hedge it.

  • Uniformity and diversity as “two sides of the same coin”. Standardized costume books and maps spread sameness. A Seville pattern book made “Spanish” fashion visible across the Habsburg lands. The same books also hardened regional and social types vivid enough to survive “on dolls, in operas, or at costume balls” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s2-standardization).
  • One technology, toleration and dogmatism. Profit-seeking printers built cosmopolitan, multi-confessional households, while sola scriptura produced both Erasmian criticism and rigid literalism. The note flags the echo of Bayly's convergence-with-antagonism two and a half centuries early (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch06-christendom-disrupted-3).
  • Contradictions left standing. Print “fixed linguistic and national divisions more permanently while creating a cosmopolitan Commonwealth of Learning”, and Eisenstein refuses to resolve the pair dialectically (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-final-remarks).
  • The same argument about the claim. When Adrian Johns disproved identical early printed copies, Eisenstein answered that her claim was relative: more standardized than hand copies. A partial, directional uniformity was misread as total and knocked down (eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-3).

Hedges. Eisenstein's vernacular standardization is described as one-way, driven by printers and crowns, not the actively contested and resisted uniformity Bayly traces (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s5-preservative-powers). Her paired term is diversity and individuality, not Bayly's inward professional complexity. See standardization-and-individuation-eisenstein.
