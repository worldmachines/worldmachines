David Hume (1711–1776)

Commons · Entities · updated 23 September 2026 · source on GitHub
David Hume (1711-1776) is read by Rao as a radical intellectual descendant of Spinoza and 'the original philosopher of the AI age' — one who set aside complex theories of causation in favor of near-pure phenomenology; Sathe also reads him as structurally predictive of how AI cognition operates, and as the figure whose stripping of metaphysics from experience prefigures AI's existential threat to knowledge-worker identity.

Location: Edinburgh, Scotland
Key works: A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40); An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748)

Biographical Context

  • The "infidel" in The Infidel and the Professor
  • Friend and intellectual collaborator of adam-smith — key figures of the Scottish Enlightenment
  • Never held a university chair (rejected twice on grounds of heterodoxy; see Rasmussen below), though he held library, diplomatic and government posts while remaining effectively an undeclared agnostic — the more diplomatic Smith ("the Professor") was entirely undeclared
  • By Hume's time, "it wasn't even a big deal to be an infidel" — a marker of how far the Spinoza tradition had traveled in two generations

Core Philosophy

Hume was the most radical empiricist of the Scottish Enlightenment. His key moves:

  • Against causation: we never observe cause-and-effect; we only observe constant conjunction. Causation is a habit of mind, not a feature of reality.
  • Phenomenological minimalism: what is real is what can be directly experienced. Grand theoretical frameworks (clockwork universes, theological orders) are fictions imposed on the log files of experience.
  • The bundle theory of self: there is no continuous "self" — only bundles of perceptions. The self is another habit of mind.

Rao's Reading

Hume is an intellectual descendant of baruch-spinoza, with gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz (as philosopher) already faded into irrelevance by Hume's time. He represents a radical continuation of the naturalist-empiricist tendency:

"Hume, famously, went much farther than most people before or since, setting aside complex theories of causation in favor of near-pure phenomenology. In some ways, he's the original philosopher of the AI age, offering the first of what has now become an endless series of 'bitter lessons' delivered by the Argument of Progress."

The "log files of reality" framing: Hume was only willing to treat observed reality as reality. "Everything else was made up human conceits." The Kronecker parallel: "God created the integers, everything else is the work of man."

When Newton (1643–1727) had proposed his theories, the temptation of the time was to believe in a deterministic clockwork universe governed by immutable divine laws. Hume resisted this temptation in a way almost no one else did.

Sathe's Reading: "The First Philosopher of the AI Age"

From the-end-of-identity-ai-plasticity-divergence-machine:

Hume went further than any contemporary in "abandoning complex theories of causation in favor of near-pure phenomenology." While others were tempted to build clockwork models, Hume was "only willing to treat the log files of reality as real."

The AI parallel: AI systems operate exactly like Hume's epistemology. They:

  • Don't care about grand narratives or identity
  • Process prompts — "the raw log files of our know-how"
  • Extract pattern from data without positing underlying causes or meanings

This makes Hume not just historically interesting but structurally predictive of how AI cognition works.

The Irony

Hume's stripping of metaphysics from experience was philosophically liberating. The same operation performed by AI on cognitive labor is existentially threatening — because it strips the last layer of the knowledge worker's legible identity.

The Hume-Smith Pairing

Where Spinoza and Leibniz were in a partially adversarial relationship, Hume and Smith were "collaborating allies who influenced each other in their campaigns on different parts of the frontier of expanding reality."

Together, they represent a "bad-cop-good-cop assault on traditional moral and natural philosophy" — the beginning of the intellectual iconoclasm that peaks with Darwin.

Darwin's Completion of the Hume Trajectory

Darwin's real accomplishment was "assaulting the secular philosophical foundations of the modernity machine" — by making divergence and variety a load-bearing feature of how reality itself operates, Darwin "put an end to the fetish for secular canonicity that marks world views before him." Literalist religion was "already down for the count by the time Hume was done with it."

The AI Connection (Rao's Frame)

Rao's claim that Hume is "the original philosopher of the AI age" connects to the contemporary "bitter lessons" of machine learning: simple empirical models trained on large amounts of data repeatedly outperform models built on human-specified rules and theories. This is the Humean lesson applied to cognition itself.

Rasmussen's The Infidel and the Professor (2017)

Dennis Rasmussen's joint biography of Hume and adam-smith (rasmussen-infidel-and-professor) agrees with the Rao and Sathe readings above on how far Hume's naturalism went. It corrects two impressions in the biographical sketch, though: Hume never held a university chair, and being "the infidel" carried real reputational and professional costs. It also brings in what the epistemology-centred readings leave out: his arguments on miracles, history as accident and commercial modernity, and a death he staged as a public argument.

The Biographical Record

Life. Born 1711 at Ninewells, into a modest Scottish laird's family; raised by a devout Presbyterian mother, an only surviving parent, with a minister uncle who conducted heresy trials. A precocious but unsatisfying university education was followed by independent study intense enough to cost him his health.

Losing faith by reading the defenses. Hume described his adolescent doubt as involuntary and cyclical — "Doubts stole in, dissipated, return'd, were again dissipated, return'd again" — and traced its source not to irreligious authors but to Locke's and Clarke's proofs of theism. Anthony Collins's quip applies: no one doubted God's existence until Clarke tried to prove it.

No university chair — twice rejected. Hume never held a university chair. He was rejected for Edinburgh's chair of Ethics and Pneumatical Philosophy in 1745 over "Scepticism, Heterodoxy & other hard Names," with even Francis Hutcheson opposing him, and passed over again for Glasgow's Chair of Logic around 1751. The rejections pushed him into tutoring the insane Marquess of Annandale and two stints as secretary to Lieutenant-General St. Clair on military and diplomatic missions — detours that gave the future historian firsthand exposure to war and diplomacy. His eventual institutional base was the keepership of the Advocates' Library in Edinburgh (the research base for the History of England), and later the secretaryship to the British ambassador in Paris and a turn as Under-Secretary of State.

Books. A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40) aimed to "march up directly to the capital or center of" the sciences — human nature itself — by "the experimental method," and "fell dead-born from the press." The Philosophical Essays (First Enquiry, 1748) contained the arguments against miracles and against design. The six-volume History of England (1754–61) was his commercial triumph and the source of most of his fortune. The Natural History of Religion appeared in the Four Dissertations (1757). Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion was withheld for roughly twenty-five years and published posthumously in 1779. He died in Edinburgh on 25 August 1776.

What It Actually Cost to Be 'the Infidel'

Rasmussen's record complicates any impression that irreligion was cheap in Hume's Scotland, or that by his time being an infidel was no great matter (Rao's line in the biographical sketch above). The costs were real, but they were reputational and professional rather than legal, and they often fell on other people.

  • Two lost chairs, both on grounds of heterodoxy (1745, c. 1751).
  • The excommunication campaign. The Kirk's Popular Party, led by George Anderson, pressed the General Assembly repeatedly between 1755 and 1757 to excommunicate Hume on six enumerated heresies — from denying an objective distinction between virtue and vice to preferring Catholicism to the Reformation. Rasmussen judges the charges a largely fair summary of Hume's actual views. The prosecution's irony is the important part: excommunication carried no civil penalty and would only have martyred him, so the exposed parties were the Moderate ministers (Blair, Robertson, Carlyle, Jardine, Home) whose reputations depended on not being seen to keep company with an avowed skeptic. Hume met the threat with a mock-encomium for "the godly, spiteful, pious, splenetic, charitable, unrelenting, meek, persecuting, Christian, inhuman, peacemaking, furious Anderson."
  • Negotiated silence. Hume asked Hugh Blair to stop discussing religion with him altogether so their friendship could proceed on other terms — "I have, long since, done with all inquiries on such subjects, and am become incapable of instruction." The Moderates' protection ran on managed conversation, not shared belief.
  • Self-censorship. He "castrated" the Treatise of its miracles and immortality material, later calling this "a Piece of Cowardice," and in 1764 advised a wavering young clergyman that "innocent Dissimulation" was the price of passing through the world. He also noted, drily, that a "Tincture of Deism" sells books and that he owed part of his reputation to it. See calibrated-candor for the pattern.
  • The counterweight. Reputation was jurisdictional. The corpus that drew excommunication motions in Edinburgh made him the idol of Paris — "I eat nothing but Ambrosia, drink nothing but Nectar" — where d'Holbach's circle teased him for being insufficiently atheist. At home, by the 1770s, the "infidel" label had softened into local legend (the fishwives who pulled him from a bog only after he recited the Lord's Prayer; the chalked "St. David Street").
  • Blind spot on the record. The chapter registers without excusing Hume's footnote in "Of National Characters" suspecting "the negroes to be naturally inferior to the whites" — a claim built on personal suspicion rather than experience, the exact failure mode his own philosophy warned against, and pointedly contrasted with adam-smith's remarks on the dignity of enslaved Africans.

Religion, History, and Commerce: Three Lines of Argument

Beyond the epistemology for which he is best known, Rasmussen foregrounds three lines of Hume's work with direct bearing on the world-machines frame.

Against miracles and against design. Both arguments run the same proportionality rule. Belief should be proportioned to evidence; a miracle by definition violates all uniform past experience, so no testimony can tip the balance. Run in reverse: an inferred cause cannot be credited with more wisdom, power, or benevolence than its observed effects display, and a world containing "innumerable disorders and evils" makes the designer hypothesis "essentially a useless hypothesis." Compare skyhooks-and-cranes on the general shape of this refusal. The Natural History of Religion (1757) went further, arguing popular religion increases rather than allays fear and that the pursuit of special divine favor actively corrodes ordinary morality.

History as accident. The History of England's organizing thesis is the "blessings of civilization" — the present beats a romanticized past, and Hume catalogues Elizabethan squalor to prove it — coupled with a flat denial that progress was designed. Civilized society emerged from "the great mixture of accident, which commonly concurs with a small ingredient of wisdom and foresight." His two sharpest applications are both about religion producing goods no one intended: the medieval Church accidentally holding a lawless society together, and Puritan fanaticism — because zealots feared God more than any king — accidentally keeping "the precious spark of liberty" alive under Tudor and Stuart absolutism, until civil war produced a balanced constitution "only from the resulting ashes." This is history assembling sideways out of unintended byproducts, the history-machine pattern, with the interregnum between a dying absolutist legitimacy and an unborn constitutional one taking the shape of a gramsci-gap.

The brief for commercial modernity. The Political Discourses (1752), especially the essay later retitled "Of Refinement in the Arts," defends commerce and luxury against civic-republican and Christian suspicion: "ages of refinement are both the happiest and most virtuous," and commerce creates the "middling rank of men" who underwrite free government. Together with his account of feudal lords dissipating their power on trinkets, this is a foundational statement of modernity-machine optimism, and adam-smith carried it nearly intact into Book 3 of The Wealth of Nations with explicit credit.

Impartiality as epistemic stance. Hume held that "the first Quality of an Historian is to be true & impartial," and was attacked by Whigs and Tories alike for it — a reception he professed to enjoy. Rasmussen presents this as a considered position rather than thick skin: the reporter of all sides, the doubter of exclusive claims, the distruster of systems.

The Death as Public Argument (1776)

Hume's dying was a public event because his irreligion was notorious: the question of whether the famous infidel would flinch drew genuine public curiosity, with the Monthly Review wondering whether "new light" is let into "the soul's dark cottage," as the poet expresses it, "through the chinks" of its ruins.

He met it deliberately. Rasmussen's framing is that Hume "was determined to die as he thought a philosopher should — cheerfully and tranquilly, without undue hopes or fears — and to do it for a public audience." Since he and adam-smith had both argued that religion answers a psychological need to reconcile people to mortality, his composure was itself the argument: this life is enough, approached rightly.

  • My Own Life (April 1776) is curated self-fashioning, not confession: ten pages emphasizing his rise from obscurity, cataloguing the poor initial reception of nearly every book he wrote, and omitting both his intimate life and every professional cost of his irreligion. A reader without prior knowledge "would scarcely realize that she was reading the autobiography of a notorious infidel."
  • The interrogations failed. Boswell's visit, Strahan's letter, and Temple's furious reaction to Boswell's report are three versions of the same ritual. Hume told Boswell that a man's morality has nothing to do with his religion and compared extinction to nonexistence before birth; Boswell recorded, against his own hopes, "a tranquillity of mind and a clearness of head which few men possess at any time." Temple's verdict — "Let him die then & be thrown into the ditch" — measures the cultural pressure riding on a different outcome.
  • The Charon joke. Unable, unlike Lucian's shades, to invent unfinished business worth pleading for, Hume tries out an excuse about reforming public opinion against superstition and is waved off: "You loitering rogue... Get into the boat this instant." Smith kept a harsher version for his private letter to Wedderburn (hoping to see "the churches shut up, and the Clergy sent about their business") and softened it for print.
  • He died on 25 August 1776, in Joseph Black's words "in such a happy composure of mind that nothing could have made it better."

This undefended serenity — persistence in being oneself, curious and sociable, up to the boundary of non-being, with no appeal to any framework beyond that — is the unassisted striving that Conatus names, held without the external judge that receding-containers describes as thinning out of Western life. Smith's Letter to Strahan then converted it into a public claim about character, and paid for it; see rasmussen-infidel-and-professor and calibrated-candor.
