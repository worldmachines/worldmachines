Nakatomi Drift (part 2 of 5)

Commons · Contributions · updated 2 August 2026 · source on GitHub
This is an essay about using the thicket to think new thoughts.

The signature practice is the dérive – usually translated as drift. The dérive, as a way to explore the city, is not simple randomness. It suspends normal purposes while remaining acutely responsive to the environment’s attractions and resistances. Debord describes it as playful and constructive, distinct from an ordinary stroll.

During a dérive, you temporarily abandon your normal reasons for moving: commuting, shopping, exercise, sightseeing, or reaching a destination. You allow features of the environment to influence your route – changes in sound, density, atmosphere, architecture, light, smell, or social activity. Debord described it as rapid movement through varied ambiances, combining openness to the terrain with active observation of its effects.

Ambiance is a useful psychogeographic concept. An ambiance is the total felt character of a place: not just architecture but sound, temperature, lighting, movement, social expectations, historical associations, and your own bodily state.

You can often detect an ambiance through transitions:

  • the moment restaurant seating gives way to loading docks;

  • the point where pedestrians abruptly disappear;

  • the shift from independent shops to blank façades;

  • the place where you begin checking behind you;

  • the boundary where children are suddenly present;

  • the street on which conversation becomes difficult because of traffic.

These transitions may reveal the city more accurately than official neighbourhood boundaries.

An intellectual dérive would therefore mean:

Temporarily abandon the normal purpose for which you entered a body of knowledge, and allow its local affordances to redirect you.

Normally, when reading a paper, you have an itinerary:

  • Understand the claim.

  • Assess the evidence.

  • Extract what is relevant.

  • Return to your project.

That is equivalent to walking through a city using turn-by-turn directions.

During an intellectual dérive, you might instead follow:

  • a term that feels oddly chosen;

  • a citation that appears peripheral;

  • an analogy the author abandons;

  • an outdated theory mentioned only to be dismissed;

  • a methodological tool into a domain where it allegedly does not belong;

  • a contradiction between the paper’s formal method and its informal imagery;

  • a strong emotional response whose source you cannot explain.

You are mapping intellectual ambiance: where thought accelerates, becomes constrained, feels inhabited, feels exposed, or seems to contain an unexplored exit. Productive getting lost is not the abandonment of orientation. It is the abandonment of a destination while preserving sensitivity.

Nakatomi Space – anything goes #

Feyerabend’s “anything goes” is commonly interpreted as “all methods are equally good.” His more interesting argument is that no universal methodological rule survives the actual history of scientific progress. Rules that appear rational in the abstract can prevent the development of alternatives needed to expose the limitations of dominant theories.

A method is a Place. It stabilizes activity and provides reliable affordances. But when the method becomes compulsory, the Place expands until it occupies the entire perceived Space. Feyerabend’s theoretical pluralism gives us permission to use alternative frameworks to disclose observations that remain invisible within the dominant framework. A supposedly wrong theory may still function as an instrument for perceiving what the accepted theory is blind to.

This is a little like Obliquity. You can use astrology, science fiction, medieval cosmology, an obsolete statistical method, a child’s misunderstanding, or a bad analogy to open a path. That does not require you to accept its truth claims. Its value may lie in the Space it discloses rather than the Place it ultimately becomes. As my AI said to me: “Feyerabend’s counterinduction punches a hole through the wall.” You have watched Die Hard, right?

Geoff Manaugh’s “Nakatomi Space” describes built environments becoming traversable through routes that their official architectural organization does not recognize: ventilation shafts, maintenance passages, unfinished floors, cavities, infrastructure, and openings created by force. The building reveals a near-infinite interior when used against its intended circulation plan.

The intellectual equivalent is very strong.

A body of literature has an official architecture:

  • disciplines;

  • canonical interpretations;

  • introductions and conclusions;

  • schools of thought;

  • chronological histories;

  • accepted applications;

  • citation networks;

  • search keywords.

But it also has infrastructure:

  • footnotes;

  • metaphors;

  • failed experiments;

  • terminological accidents;

  • historical disputes;

  • imported methods;

  • suppressed alternatives;

  • tacit assumptions;

  • strange examples;

  • concepts used instrumentally but never examined.
