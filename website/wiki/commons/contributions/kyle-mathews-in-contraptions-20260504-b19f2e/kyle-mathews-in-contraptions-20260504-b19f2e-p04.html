Primitive Accumulation: Field Notes on Pre-Contact World-Machines (part 4 of 14)

Commons · Contributions · updated 29 April 2026 · source on GitHub
Contribution essay by kyle, submitted to World Machines.

"Let me see if I understand," the drone said. "They select thirty electors by lot. Those thirty vote to reduce themselves to nine. The nine vote to select forty new electors. The forty reduce themselves to twelve. The twelve select twenty-five. The twenty-five reduce themselves to nine. The nine select forty-five. The forty-five reduce themselves to eleven. The eleven select forty-one final electors, who actually choose the Doge by a two-thirds majority."

"Correct."

"This is insane."

"It's prevented factional capture for centuries. No other republic they know of has managed that."

"It's still insane. They could achieve the same result with a properly designed randomization algorithm in—" The drone stopped itself. "They don't have randomization algorithms. They don't have algorithms. They have... whatever this is."

"They have trial and error. Centuries of it. Every step in that process exists because someone, at some point, tried to corrupt the election, and the step was added to prevent that specific corruption. It's an evolved immune system against political capture."

"So every time someone found a way to rig the system, they added another step to block that specific trick," Just Checking said slowly. "And the people trying to rig it got cleverer, so the countermeasures got cleverer."

"Exactly. They're not just guessing at random. They're learning from attacks. That's why they've converged on something this sophisticated without any formal theory of voting systems."

Gujarat, where Hindu and Muslim merchants operated side by side under legal frameworks that both found mutually acceptable if not ideal. I spent a week in Cambay, watching the rhythms of a port that served as the western anchor of the Indian Ocean system.

The merchant I spoke with most often was a Jain named Vastupal, whose family had been trading across the ocean for six generations. He was old, wealthy, and philosophical—the combination that tends to produce the most interesting conversations in any civilization.

"You are not from here," he said, during our third meeting, over cups of a sweet milk-based drink I had developed a genuine fondness for. "You are not from anywhere I know. Your coins are strange. Your accent is strange. Your questions are strange."

I had prepared cover stories for this contingency. But something in his eyes made me hesitate.

"Does it matter?" I asked.

He considered this for a long moment. "I have traded with men from every land where ships can sail. Persians, Arabs, Africans, Malays, Chinese. Some worship one god, some many, some none. Some eat meat, some do not. Some believe in souls, some in cycles of rebirth, some in nothing at all." He took a sip of his drink. "What I have learned is that men who trade together rarely fight. The goods that flow between us make us kin in ways that blood and faith do not."

"You think trade creates peace?"

"I think trade creates understanding. Peace follows, sometimes. Not always." He smiled, showing teeth stained red from betel nut. "I have also seen merchants cheat each other, kill each other, destroy each other's families over disputes about cargo weights. We are not saints. But we are connected, and connection is not nothing."

Later, walking through the evening streets with Just Checking manifesting as a faint shimmer at my shoulder, I found myself returning to the conversation.

"He almost sees it," I said. "The system. The world-machine. He's been operating inside it for sixty years, and he's starting to perceive its outlines."

"He described trade networks. He didn't describe emergent planetary coordination."

"He described trade networks creating something larger than themselves. That's the first step." I paused, watching a ship's lanterns move slowly across the dark water of the harbour. "Give them a few more centuries and they'll have the theoretical vocabulary to articulate what he intuited."

"Or they'll destroy themselves first."

"That's always the question, isn't it?"
