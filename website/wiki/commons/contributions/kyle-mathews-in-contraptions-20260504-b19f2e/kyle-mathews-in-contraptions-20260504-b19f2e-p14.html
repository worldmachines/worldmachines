Primitive Accumulation: Field Notes on Pre-Contact World-Machines (part 14 of 14)

Commons · Contributions · updated 29 April 2026 · source on GitHub
Contribution essay by kyle, submitted to World Machines.

The Legibility Machine succeeded—and its success is what's breaking it. Between 1600 and 2000, the infrastructure of enumeration enabled a population explosion from half a billion to six billion humans. It enabled literacy to spread from a tiny elite to the majority of the species. It enabled the creation of millions of organizations, corporations, NGOs, political parties, religious movements, subcultures, fandoms, online communities—each with their own interests, their own narratives, their own claims on attention. The machine that was built to make the world countable made it possible for the world to become uncountably complex. More actors than any system can integrate. More information than any narrative can absorb. More differentiation than any framework of toleration was designed to handle.

The Legibility Machine cannot absorb the load. It was designed for bounded differences, not exponential divergence.

The Divergence Machine is what they need. Coordination without consensus. The Culture's architecture. They're building it blind, without knowing what they're building or where they're going.

The reactionary movements are attempts to restore the Legibility Machine—to make the world enumerable again. This cannot work; the complexity is structural. But the attempts can delay the transition, cause unnecessary suffering, possibly trigger catastrophic failure modes.

The question is navigation. The destination exists—I live there. The passage is what's uncertain.

They have surprised us before. The abolition of slavery was not inevitable; it happened because people chose it. The articulation of universal rights was not inevitable; it emerged because people fought for it. The survival of those principles through two world wars was not inevitable; it required continuous effort.

They keep choosing. That's what I've learned, watching them. The machine doesn't run itself. They run it. And enough of them, often enough, choose to run it toward something better.

I don't know if that will be sufficient. I know it has been sufficient so far.

Recommendation: Continued observation. Revised Contact protocols on standby—not because intervention is warranted, but because the transition, if successful, may complete faster than previous models predicted.

—Sifan

[ADDENDUM: JUST CHECKING, 2025 CE]

She's right that the destination exists. She's right that they're attempting the transition we made.

What she's not saying—what she won't say, because she's Sifan and she doesn't do self-pity—is that watching is its own form of torture when you care about the outcome and can't affect it.

I've been assigned to her for seven centuries because the Minds thought she might intervene again. She hasn't. Not once. Even when watching cost her more than acting would have.

That's not detachment. That's discipline. They're different things.

The Greater Magellanic Cloud taught her that her interventions have consequences she can't predict. It didn't teach her not to care. Nothing could teach her that. She's not built for it.

So she watches. And she hopes. And she writes reports that are more analytical than she feels, because that's the job.

If they make it through—and I'm genuinely uncertain, which is unusual for me—it will be because of what they are, not because of anything we did or didn't do. That's how it should be. That's what Contact is for.

But I hope they make it. For their sake, obviously.

Also for hers.

—JC
