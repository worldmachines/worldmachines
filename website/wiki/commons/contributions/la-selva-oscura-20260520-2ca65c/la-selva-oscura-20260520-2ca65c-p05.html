La Selva Oscura (part 5 of 5)

Commons · Contributions · updated 16 May 2026 · source on GitHub
Coda at the end draws a connection between the overall piece and the worldmachines project. The overall piece is about interpreting the Remembrance of Earth's Past trilogy as about a midlife crisis for humanity (and analogizes it to Dante and my own personal experiences) - the transitional phase we are in between machines has some analogous aspects.

Cixin Liu, The Three-Body Problem, trans. Ken Liu (Tor Books, 2014). Liu names him only as Listener 1379 — the gentlest voice in the trilogy and the counter to its Hobbesian cosmology. He reappears (via sophon-mediated communication) at the end of The Dark Forest, contesting Luo Ji’s claim that love is uniquely human and offering the line about love as something worth taking risks for even in a cutthroat universe.

Cixin Liu, Death’s End, trans. Ken Liu (Tor Books, 2016), late chapters. Without further spoilers: Cheng Xin and Yifan eventually become Returners, choosing to give the matter of their pocket universe back so the cosmic cycle can re-collapse. The Returners motif is a good contemporary literary image of vanaprastha.

Hans Blumenberg, The Legitimacy of the Modern Age, Part I. Selbstbehauptung is Blumenberg’s name for the founding human posture in the face of the absolutism of reality — neither aggression nor surrender, but the act of standing upright in a world that does not arrange itself for us.

The classical Hindu four-stage system; for the scholarship and its variations across primary texts, see Patrick Olivelle, The Āśrama System: The History and Hermeneutics of a Religious Institution (Oxford, 1993). What’s described here is the canonical form most often cited in comparative work.

Norman Maclean, A River Runs Through It and Other Stories (Univ. of Chicago Press, 1976), the title novella’s penultimate sermon. The book’s wording: “And so it is those we live with and should know who elude us. But we can still love them — we can love completely without complete understanding.” The 1992 Robert Redford film keeps the line nearly intact in the final sermon — clip on YouTube: We Can Love Completely

Liu’s image has spread far beyond the trilogy. Yancey Strickler’s Dark Forest Theory of the Internet (May 2019) argued that the public web has itself become a dark forest: serious discourse retreats into private spaces — group chats, paid newsletters, dinner tables — to avoid being attacked, decontextualized, or weaponized. The Fermi-paradox community uses dark forest as a candidate solution to the puzzle of why we don’t see other civilizations. Geopolitical realists invoke it as the natural state of international relations: every state is a hunter; revealing your strategy is suicide. The recent moral panics around AI add a third register — AI as an alien intelligence we should not have summoned, the door we have already cracked open. (I argued against this register specifically in De-Dramatizing the Digital.) Each domain reads the metaphor a little differently, but the structure is the same: visibility becomes danger; the comfortable cosmology in which the world arranges itself for us has collapsed; the question is what to do once you know.
