La Selva Oscura (part 1 of 5)

Commons · Contributions · updated 16 May 2026 · source on GitHub
Coda at the end draws a connection between the overall piece and the worldmachines project. The overall piece is about interpreting the Remembrance of Earth's Past trilogy as about a midlife crisis for humanity (and analogizes it to Dante and my own personal experiences) - the transitional phase we are in between machines has some analogous aspects.

The child that was human civilization had opened the door to her home and glanced outside. The endless night terrified her so much that she shuddered against the expansive and profound darkness, and shut the door firmly.

— Cixin Liu, Death’s End1

Nel mezzo del cammin di nostra vita / mi ritrovai per una selva oscura / ché la diritta via era smarrita.

Midway upon the journey of our life / I found myself in a dark forest, / for the straightforward path had been lost.

— Dante, Inferno I2

I awoke in a dark forest with the straight way lost at some point during COVID. I can’t point to exactly when, but it was an exact point. Like Zen master Dōgen says, “Summer does not follow spring. First there is spring, then there is summer.”3 Middle age does not follow youth. First there is youth then there is middle age. Just as firewood does not become ash, but rather firewood is at the stage of firewood and ash is at the stage of ash, life does not become death, and youth does not become middle age. I know it’s been said before that one day you just wake up like that but it’s actually true.

Soon thereafter, lost in my personal dark forest,4 I got a buzz-cut COVID-do and reading glasses, and eventually found my footing with my job and my way of life. Two surprising things happened. First, I saw the road to the end of my career — I could see all the way to retirement — and it would be exactly like the way it is now, forever. The question was no longer, how do I get there? but why does this feel less real than it did when I wanted it? Second, my inner map went blank. When the interior authority to choose a path dissolves, then no path that can be taken seems right. The straightforward path is lost, and what Jung called the encounter with the unconscious5 arrives unbidden.

The Crisis of Success

The exterior path remains clear, but the solid self that used to move forward begins to flicker. The external map — the next credential, the upgraded routine, the better-optimized life, the longer vacation — continues to function. It gives you stress but there’s also a new ease now that you have seen its sameness all the way to the end. You stop making memories at the same rate because the newness diminishes, and the lack of inner conviction begins to weigh on your mind. This is the crisis of success. Paradoxically, failure is much simpler to deal with. If you fail the world gives you errands: figure it out, make a new plan, and try again. Success can be stranger because it removes the justification to change. This dissonance between a still-functioning exterior and a now murkier interior is the dark forest.

Before I begin diagnosing eras and civilizations, a confession. I tend to ascribe to the world what I am feeling myself — I don’t think I am alone in this blemish. Currently, I am obsessed with the suspicion that my judgments are motivated almost entirely by my own stage of development. “Everything is boring,” oh yeah — “I’m boring.” “How bourgeois and stagnant the 21st century is.” Unfortunately, this means “I’m bourgeois and stagnant.” I didn’t worry about whether my boredom was a sign of civilizational collapse when I was twenty. I worry about it now. But just because I project doesn’t mean I’m wrong.

The Three Beasts

Dante knew what this looked like from inside. In the Inferno, he struggles out of the dark forest and eventually finds a sunlit hill. He begins to climb out by the direct route, and is blocked by the three beasts.6 He can’t go forward. The straight path of the first half of his life is now the wrong approach for what his soul is exploring; he must find a different way. It’s at this moment that his guide and mentor appears. In this essay the role of mentor is split: Dōgen takes the stages of life, Liu takes the cosmos, vanaprastha takes the discipline of stepping back.

I wrote in Cosmological Adventure that the Hero’s Journey has shifted from being aimed at midlife (Jesus, Buddha, Dante, and so on) to one aimed at the adolescent. Western culture has virtually no mythic vocabulary left for the path of middle age. That piece named the wrong-map problem. Now we look at what happens when the map disappears completely, and what happens to a civilization when it refuses to recognize where it is in its stage of development.

The German philosopher Hans Blumenberg has a useful word for what we are dealing with: reoccupation.7 When an old answer to a persistent question loses its force, the question itself does not go away — it waits, in an empty slot, for a new answer to occupy it. Western culture’s slot for the middle of life is empty. What we call midlife boredom is partly waiting for something to occupy this space, but answer comes there none.

The Cosmos as Selva Oscura
