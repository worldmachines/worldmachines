La Selva Oscura (part 3 of 5)

Commons · Contributions · updated 16 May 2026 · source on GitHub
Coda at the end draws a connection between the overall piece and the worldmachines project. The overall piece is about interpreting the Remembrance of Earth's Past trilogy as about a midlife crisis for humanity (and analogizes it to Dante and my own personal experiences) - the transitional phase we are in between machines has some analogous aspects.

Love isn’t wrong. A single individual cannot destroy a world. If that world was doomed, then it was the result of the efforts of everyone, including those living and those who had already died.11

There are also gentler voices in the trilogy. In the first book, a Trisolaran pacifist risked his life sending the “do not answer” warning to Earth.12 This same Trisolaran appears at the end of the second book, reflecting on whether love is uniquely human and imagining tiny pockets of love springing up throughout the galaxy if the conditions could change, but alas they most likely won’t. It would have been humanity’s genuine gift to the universe.

Cheng Xin’s small vessel escaping the solar system is a contemporary image of Dante’s pilgrim: a person for whom the path has vanished, and who must persevere. She survives for a time in a pocket universe outside our cosmos. When the cosmic cycle eventually requires that the matter of her shelter be returned, she gives it up — surrendering her final refuge so the larger order can be made whole.13 She faces the absolutism of reality without mediation. In the void she is a fragile creature, but her perseverance — the willingness to live in the dark, and to give back what was never really hers — is the ultimate act of self-assertion (Selbstbehauptung).14

The Remembrance of Earth’s Past trilogy is the Inferno of the Divergence Machine: the dark forest, the lost straight path, but applied to our whole world. (I await Liu Cixin’s Purgatorio.) Reading the trilogy as a science-fiction novel about aliens is reading it as a coming-of-age story. Reading it as a midlife myth — a trial without the consolation of paradise — is what we need in our moment. Or what I need — remember I ascribe to others what I see in myself. Why? Because the optimism and sentimentality of the hero’s journey is just not going to get us where we need to go.

Many Maps, No Path

It is worth at this moment to linger on a cross-cultural counterexample, not as a recommendation but as a contrast that exposes a blind spot. Hindu tradition divides the human life into four ashramas, four life-stages, each with its own discipline: brahmacharya (the student), grihastha (the householder), vanaprastha (the forest-dweller), and sannyasa (the renunciate).15 While some texts give special importance to the householder because that stage sustains society and makes the others possible, the stages are best understood as distinct disciplines rather than a simple ladder of promotion. The student is supported by the householder. The renunciate can withdraw because the householder maintains the world they are withdrawing from. The householder makes the hero possible.

The discipline that follows the householder is vanaprastha, the forest-dweller stage. The householder, having maintained the world for decades, begins a deliberate turn outward — a loosening of the daily grip, an investment in mentoring, a willingness to disengage from the immediate so the long-term can come into view. We have, again, no English word for this stage. We have midlife crises and we have retirement, and almost no language for the deliberate, mythic work of the transition between them. I see a need for the forest-dweller in Western society, the art of stepping back without disappearing.

A mature life requires many myths, not just one. We need narratives for generativity — the quiet heroism of building institutions and planting trees whose shade we know we will never sit in. We need myths for impermanence. We need myths for living with the people whose interiority will never be fully accessible to us. I’m reminded of Norman Maclean’s father, in his last sermon in A River Runs Through It: “It is those we live with and should know who elude us. But we can still love them. We can love completely without complete understanding.”16

This kind of labor is what Blumenberg called work on myth. Myth, in his account, is not the opposite of reason but a partner to it: both are devices for making the absolutism of reality bearable. The myths that are missing from our middle age are reoccupation slots awaiting new occupants. The slots stay open until the work is done. We can look to the three forests — selva oscura, vanaprastha, dark forest — to provoke us to rededicate ourselves, find a new path, and open the door again.
