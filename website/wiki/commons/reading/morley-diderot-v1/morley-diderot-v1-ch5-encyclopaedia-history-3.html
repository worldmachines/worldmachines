The Encyclopædia under fire: De Prades, suppression, Geneva, and the 1759 decree

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley narrates the Encyclopædia's persecution from the abbé de Prades affair and the 1752 suppression through the seventh volume, D'Alembert's Geneva article, Helvétius's De l'Esprit and the 1759 revocation of the privilege, and argues that the incoherent policy of Louis XV's government illustrates a larger collision over whether political authority may direct opinion.

Morley continues his account of the obstacles Diderot faced in bringing out the Encyclopædia. He covers the abbé de Prades's Sorbonne thesis and its condemnation (January 1752), the council's decree of 7 February 1752 suppressing the first two volumes, the confiscation of Diderot's papers, the resumption of work and the third volume (1753), the steady growth of subscribers up to the seventh volume (1757), D'Alembert's article on Geneva, Helvétius's De l'Esprit (1758), and the Parlement and Council of State's action of early 1759 withdrawing the privilege. He closes with his own verdict: the authorities' vacillation shows the incoherence of Louis XV's government, and the affair is one episode in a clash between the idea that government should stay out of directing opinion and the older idea that it should regulate it.

Themes

Persecution by proxy and cabal. In Morley's telling the clergy's first attack is indirect: De Prades is condemned because he is a friend of Diderot and suspected of writing the theology articles, so as to discredit the Encyclopædia and push the government to prohibit it. Morley says the Jesuits, jealous of an enterprise in which they had no part, set "all the machinery of its ally, authority" in motion. The archbishop's pastoral referred to unnamed works, and Morley notes this only extended the book's notoriety.

Jesuit versus Jansenist, and the philosophers' break with the Jansenists. Morley gives a short political history of the two parties. The Jansenists, from being theoretical enemies of freedom, became through persecution partisans of freedom in practice; the free-thinkers, now strong enough to stand on their own principles, break with them. Diderot's retort in his own voice (as Morley quotes it) is that Jansenist disputatiousness has made more unbelievers than all of philosophy. Morley adds his own thesis that the church's "flagrant social incompetence" is what brought Liberalism into vogue.

Authority outwitted by the editor. Diderot's papers and plates were seized, but, as Grimm is quoted saying, the Jesuits forgot to take his head and his genius; the government then had to ask Diderot and D'Alembert to resume. Morley calls this one of the meannesses of decaying authority, since the decree stayed suspended over them. D'Alembert's preface to volume three (quoted at length) answers malice with the fable of the grasshoppers.

Success and conspiracy panic. Subscribers grew from barely two thousand to three thousand by volume four and about a thousand more by volume seven. Morley says the party of superstition, enraged by this success, denounced Diderot, D'Alembert, Voltaire (voltaire), Rousseau and Buffon as a conspiratorial league against society. This is Morley's report of what opponents alleged.

The Geneva article as oblique attack. Morley reads D'Alembert's praise of the Socinian Genevese pastors as a veiled reproach to French churchmen. He stresses that the church of the time was an organised corporation with tribunals, privileges and dungeons, and he cites the Calas and La Barre cases as the context. That is Morley's reading of D'Alembert's intent, and his hedge that the article hardly goes beyond what "seem the axioms of all men of sense" now is visible and is his own dated-by-hindsight judgement.

Helvétius as embarrassment. De l'Esprit provoked a general insurrection of opinion; Grimm (quoted) feared that the philosophic party would pay with new restraints. The procureur-général then bundled Helvétius, minor publications and the Encyclopædia into one information, and the Council's decree of March 1759 withdrew the 1746 privilege.

Incoherent, not simply tyrannical, government. Morley says the motive for the 1759 decree was never made plain and weighs three explanations (crown against parlements, court favour, silencing the most accessible of three oppositions). He argues the government encouraged or repressed according to momentary calculation, so that fifty compositors kept setting a forbidden book and the Jansenist journal kept appearing. He generalises: Louis XV's failure came from lack of tenacity and conviction, not mere arbitrariness. This is a Victorian liberal's thesis about the machinery of the old regime, and it loosely rhymes with the decay of an incumbent order in gramsci-gap terms, though Morley is describing a monarchy's confusion and not an interregnum.

The underlying principle. Morley's closing claim is that history's interest lies in the collision of two conceptions: the exclusion of political authority from directing opinion (absolute secularisation of government), versus the principle behind St. Bartholomew, the dragonnades and the revocation of the Edict of Nantes. He observes that both Jesuits and, when they could, Jansenists practised the latter principle.

Notable passages

Diderot's defence of De Prades (c1). In Diderot's words as Morley translates them, theologians' declamations against reason are indecent and injure religion, and such principles "are made to frighten small souls." Morley treats the piece as still instructive in his own day.

Church incompetence opens the way (c2). Morley's compact explanation of why philosophy gained power: Locke's Essay lay unsold as late as 1725, and the weakness of ecclesiastics, not the force of thinkers, made the opening.

The Geneva article and the angry orthodox (c3). The central account of the volume-seven crisis and how the article was meant to be read.

What the church actually was (c4). Morley's reminder that the Encyclopædists fought an armed corporation, not a purified modern Christianity, while crediting the few priests who rebuked the king.

Connivance and the printing that never stopped (c5). The 1759 crisis shows policy swinging with court and mistress; the work was banned and set in type at once.

The collision of two ideas (c6). Morley's thesis for why the details of a book matter: the decree is a moment in the struggle over whether authority may direct opinion.

The Encyclopædia here is chiefly a political object under pressure from an incumbent institution; the passage touches the idea of an old order defending itself against a new publishing and knowledge enterprise, but Morley never frames it as infrastructure or legibility, so those connections stay implicit.
