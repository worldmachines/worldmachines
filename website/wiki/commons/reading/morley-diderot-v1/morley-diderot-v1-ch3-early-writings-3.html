Police spies, the Sceptic's Walk, the 1748 tale, and Diderot on women

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley uses the police seizure of Diderot's Promenade du Sceptique to describe how a censorship regime kept Paris legible to its police, then judges the allegory's artistic weakness and its sceptical-atheist argument, condemns the 1748 obscene tale as a symptom of the age's moral loosening, and credits Diderot with a clear-eyed if unconstructive reflection on women.

Morley moves through four linked topics from Diderot's early writing years (c. 1747-48). He begins with the seizure of the Promenade du Sceptique by a police agent and explains, via the manageable size and sharply grouped social worlds of mid-century Paris, how the police could know the contents of an obscure author's desk; he illustrates this with Diderot's own account (to Madame Voland) of the copyist Glenat, who turned out to be a spy. He then gives a frankly low estimate of the allegory as literature while finding the middle section's sceptical-atheist dialogue interesting. He next denounces the obscene tale of 1748, treating it as a mirror of an age whose Church-bound morality had lost its intellectual footing. Finally he turns to the dissoluteness-without-contempt-for-women of the period and quotes Diderot's reflections on women's fate, crediting him with realism while noting that Diderot went no further than pity.

Themes

Censorship works through gossip, scale and informers. Morley's own explanation is that mid-century Paris (he says under 800,000 after the collapse of Law's System) was "more like a very large provincial town", divided into definite worlds (servants, beggars, lawyers, authors), and that a censorship itself breeds whispering about suspect manuscripts which "let the police into as many secrets as they choose to know". The contrast with modern metropolises is Morley's. The picture is of a state whose surveillance depends on the city being readable; this loosely rhymes with the Legibility theme in the World Machines frame, though Morley is describing a police practice and not theorising state legibility. Diderot's good nature made him easy prey, and Morley concludes that the French man of letters was "worse off than his brother in England".

The hack and the spy. Diderot's own pictures (as Morley quotes them) of the starving literary drudge, and the Glenat anecdote with Sartine laughing at the remonstrance, show patronage-by-charity turned into a surveillance channel. Morley adds, dryly, that Diderot's vow to suspect every ragged visitor was soon forgotten.

The Sceptic's Walk: weak allegory, interesting argument. Morley's verdict is harsh: Diderot lacked the agility of Voltaire (voltaire) or the sincerity of Bunyan, and the allegory is "unskilful, obvious, poor". The one section he values is a discussion among a deist, pantheist, idealist, sceptic and atheist, where the atheist (Diderot's speaker, though Morley cannot say how far Diderot himself went) attacks the design argument: the observer knows only a tooth or two of one wheel of an "unknown machine", the repeater-watch analogy fails because a watch's origin is known and the universe's is not, and insects might equally admire the gardener of an earth-heap. Morley reads this as an argument from the relativity of human knowledge, not dogmatic denial, one that turns the deist's own sceptical weapons against him. The machine image here is an epistemic limit on inference, not a systems claim. Morley also notes the story ends with the atheist's family destroyed and the suggestion that he cannot complain on his own principles, and detects a leaning toward an eternal, self-moving matter.

The 1748 tale and the moral cost of discrediting theology. Morley condemns the tale aesthetically (he insists he means aesthetically, not morally), invokes Carlyle's verdict, and says Diderot soon repented. He then offers his own two-part sociology: court corruption after Louis XIV spread to the middle class, aided by the speculation of Law, and the reaction against the Church carried a contempt for all asceticism even among pure-living men like Condorcet (nicolas-de-condorcet). His moral is positivist and pointed: morality built on theology falls with the faith. He sets La Religieuse apart as a serious pathological study, and cites Niebuhr's comparison with Petronius, adding that Diderot lacked Petronius's contempt for the vice around him. Rousseau's New Heloise (jean-jacques-rousseau) is said to owe its fascination to this literary climate.

Women: perception of a problem, not a programme. Morley says the period's licence was not accompanied by the cynical view of women, and that a dim sense had arisen that the position of women is among the foremost questions of social improvement and that the Catholic ideal no longer fitted life. Diderot's quoted reflections are a mix of what a modern reader will find essentialising (women as inwardly savage, creatures of instinct, secret league) and strongly sympathetic (the lifecycle of subjection, childbirth, neglect, and the cruelty of civil laws). Morley frames both as unbitter observation, says Diderot left practical attack to Condorcet and others, and awards him credit only for realism. Attribute the stereotypes to Diderot as quoted, and the praise and the framing to Morley.

Notable passages

The Glenat story (c1) is the best primary evidence in the unit for how informers worked and for Diderot's temperament; Morley's gloss is that it shows French authors' worse position than English. The atheist's machine passage (c2) and Morley's analysis of it (c3) matter for the history of the design argument and for the machine metaphor in eighteenth-century thought, with Morley's hedge that we cannot tell how far Diderot went. The paragraph on Diderot's mind floating in negation (c4) gives Morley's capsule of the early position, leaning toward eternal self-moving matter. The two women passages (c5, c6) preserve Diderot's text as Morley chooses to quote it and Morley's evaluation, which is dated in its easy confidence about women's inner life but is explicit that Diderot's attitude is pity, not scorn.
