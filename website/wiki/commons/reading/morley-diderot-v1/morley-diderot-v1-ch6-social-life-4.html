Diderot's Friends, Patrons and Booksellers: Rousseau's Rupture, Grimm, Catherine, the Book Trade

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley closes Chapter VI with Diderot's break with Rousseau, his drudging friendship with the self-interested Grimm, the sale of his library to Catherine of Russia, and the Paris book trade (contraband, copyright, the booksellers' quarrel with D'Alembert), then adds outside views of the philosophic circle.

Morley closes Chapter VI by gathering the loose ends of Diderot's social and business life. He first treats the break with jean-jacques-rousseau (Morley's own verdict: Diderot was not the real offender), then Grimm and the manuscript literary correspondence Diderot drudged at for him, then Diderot's money troubles and the sale of his library to Catherine of Russia. A long section on the Paris book trade (guild, hawkers, piracy, the Bayle precedent, the La Fontaine copyright affair) leads to Diderot's memorandum on copyright and his rebuke of D'Alembert over the booksellers. The chapter ends with outside views of the philosophic circle (Gibbon, Walpole, Hume), a note on the Salon, and two remarks from Duclos.

Themes

Rousseau as the one friend Diderot could not hold. Morley says Rousseau's quarrel has been dealt with elsewhere and that judgment is the same whether we take Rousseau's story or Diderot's. He sketches the 1756 retreat, the hostility between Grimm and Rousseau, and Diderot's anguished letters, with their mix of pity and revulsion. Morley's own verdict is that sentimentalism readily turns into "indulgence to oneself" in place of "upright and manly judgment about others", and that Diderot was not the real offender. Morley notes Rousseau uses the same language about his own heart.

Grimm: a cool judge and a self-interested friend. Morley's portrait is hard-edged. Grimm is sound in judgment but alert to his own interest, and the best thing known in his favour is that he won Diderot's friendship. The asymmetry is Morley's point: Diderot weeps and drudges while Grimm collects pensions and gold boxes. Morley still values the Grimm-Diderot circular letters as the "machinery by which the light of Paris was diffused among darker lands", a record he judges unmatched for any other literary epoch. This is an early paid, manuscript, subscription-based information channel, a modest piece of knowledge infrastructure (compare printing-press-and-information-overload, though here the medium is hand-copied letters).

Money, pensions and the dependence of letters. Diderot, quoted by Morley, laments that literary earnings are spread over too many years and vanish as gathered. Morley says Diderot would not have been Diderot had he saved. The sale of his library to Catherine, who kept him as its salaried custodian, is presented as ethically compromising but normal for the time. Morley compares Voltaire's and D'Alembert's pensions from Frederick and Rousseau's refusal of one. Morley wishes Diderot had kept his independence but excuses him.

The Paris book trade: regulation, contraband and economic logic. Morley describes a guild of 360 booksellers and 36 printers, confined by quarter, undercut by hawkers who turned into pirate publishers and distributors of banned books. Prohibition raised prices and demand ("Good, another edition!"). The government came to see foreign printing as an economic leak and chose to print Bayle at home. This is an instance of an incumbent control machine failing at enforcement and then bending to protect domestic industry. It fits the project's themes only loosely: it shows a censorship regime that could not make the book market illegible or sealed (cf. illegibility-as-elite-defense, which is about elites hiding things rather than failing to suppress them) and is partly about the printing trade (cf. eisenstein-printing-revolution).

Diderot on copyright and on commerce. In his 1767 memorandum for Sartine, Diderot (per Morley) takes the booksellers' side on literary property while privately preferring abolition of all restraint. Morley credits him with seeing a book as "a piece of marketable ware", against "high-flown nonsense about the dignity of letters". In the 1759 dialogue Diderot scolds D'Alembert for dealing unfairly with the booksellers, and in the Panckoucke anecdote Diderot throws a bullying publisher out. Morley reads all this as a businesslike integrity in Diderot.

Outside views of the circle. Gibbon reports the philosophers' fame. Horace Walpole (a hostile witness, quoted at length) finds them overbearing and ridiculous. Hume finds the men of letters agreeable and irreproachable in morals. Morley adds a brief note that Diderot was not comfortable in the Salons of women, quoting Madame d'Epinay's complaint, and ends on two sayings of Duclos on morals and honour.

Notable passages

Rousseau's rupture. The opening passage gives Diderot's letters on the breach and his closing line about the wound that cannot be cauterised, followed by Morley's verdict on sentimentalism and on who was in the wrong. It sets out the personal cost of the Rousseau split.

Grimm and the literary correspondence. Morley contrasts Grimm hunting pensions with Diderot labouring "like any journeyman", and then makes the larger claim about the circular letters as a channel diffusing Paris's intelligence to the northern courts.

The Empress's purchase. The library sale is Morley's detailed account of royal patronage, including the thousand-livre custodian salary paid fifty years in advance, and his comparisons with Voltaire, D'Alembert and Rousseau.

Contraband and Bayle. The hawkers' underground, the price effect of prohibition, and the minister's decision to print Bayle in France are Morley's best illustration of why censorship leaked and how economics shaped the state's response.

Copyright. Diderot's defence of literary property, as Morley reports it, links authors' livelihood to the legal status of books as commodities.

The D'Alembert dialogue. A scene of friendship and business ethics, given as Diderot's account of what D'Alembert owed the booksellers. Note it is Diderot's own telling, via Morley, so it favours Diderot.
