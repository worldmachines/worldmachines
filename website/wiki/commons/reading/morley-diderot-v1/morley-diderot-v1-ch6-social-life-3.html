Diderot in conversation and in friendship: the disputing century and the generous man

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley closes his picture of Diderot's social life with letter-extracts on conversation and annihilation (including the Leibnitz pyramid joke against optimism), a verdict on the disputatious, amiable century, and a catalogue of Diderot's unstinting friendliness to Le Monnier, Jodin and Landois, ending in Diderot's determinist philosophy of commiseration.

This unit finishes Morley's chapter on Diderot's social life by quoting from Diderot's letters (to Sophie Volland, as Morley presents them) and then judging them. First come two sketches of table talk: conversation as free association, and an evening's talk on annihilation that slides into the problem of evil and a joke against Leibnitz's optimism. Morley then generalises (his own verdict) that the age was a "disputing, arguing, rationalistic century" with Diderot as its archetype, and that its debates, though earnest, lacked the charm of Plato. The rest is an extended, admiring catalogue of Diderot's friendliness: the abbé Le Monnier, the Jodins, Landois, and the claims of Grimm on time. It ends with Diderot's own gloss on conduct, a determinism that licenses pity over blame.

Themes

Conversation as association, not logic. Diderot (as quoted) compares conversation to the dreams of a delirious patient: it hangs together through "imperceptible links", and the yellow example (gold, silk, bile, straw) shows how one shared quality carries the mind from object to object. It is a small theory of how ideas chain, offered as observation rather than doctrine.

The problem of evil and the dismissal of optimism. In the reported conversation, the old dilemma (impotence or bad will) is put as something "a child would understand", and the whole apparatus of original sin, emanations, Manichean principles, metempsychosis and optimism is dismissed as "hollow vision of a dream" offered against a plain fact. This is Diderot's report of the company's view, which Morley passes on without demurral. The Leibnitz pyramid story (gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz's most perfect world with Tarquin and Lucretia at the summit) is the sharpest instance. The usurer's daughter anecdote is the comic counterpart: damnation is something one gets used to in ten years. Father Hoop's preference for ceasing to be, and the narrator's wavering, give the mood of the scene: serious without being solemn.

A rationalistic century (Morley's verdict). Morley says almost every letter shows the age at its height of disputing, and that Diderot "was above all others the archetype and representative" of the passion for moralising and analysing. Diderot is shown arguing for the view that makes for "the dignity and worth of human nature" against cynics, and against a sixth moral sense (his answer: all is the result of experience; science is when motives are present to memory, taste or tact when they are not). Morley compares him to Socrates and Dr. Johnson as a lover of argument.

Morley's qualified judgement. He grants, with Mademoiselle de Lespinasse (quoted), that Diderot pleases but "does not touch my soul", and calls the discussion "close, stifling, and airless", lacking the "spacious beauty" and "spontaneous piety" Plato could give. Yet he insists the society was unsurpassed in amiability and mutual kindliness, and that loose morals were compatible with "an honest anxiety to serve". This is the Victorian moralist's evaluation, to be read as his, not as neutral description.

Friendship as service without return. Morley contrasts friendship as exemption from criticism with friendship as excessive criticism, and says Diderot took it as "the right, the duty, or the privilege of rendering services" without expecting gratitude. Evidence, all narrated by Morley: the pomatum advertisement, Damilaville's grieving wife, the bad poet Le Monnier (whose Encyclopædia copy Diderot sold, offering his own drawer of money; the price of nine hundred and fifty livres is given as about forty pounds), and the Jodins, whom he advised, housed at table, and rebuked frankly. Mademoiselle Jodin, Morley says, was hardly worthy of him.

Landois and a philosophy of commiseration. Landois, an unreasonable needy man of letters, asks Diderot to revise a dangerous manuscript; Diderot (quoted) answers with irritated clear-sightedness ("It is not I who am the fool") and then drifts into a soliloquy: man is a link in a chain of events, modified from without, so the right attitude is pity, not anger, as against a wind-storm. Morley summarises this as the road to wisdom: reproach others with nothing, repent of nothing. This is a compact statement of Diderot's materialist-determinist ethics as Morley reports it, and the thread to his larger case that the philosophe is positive and humane, not merely destructive.

Time owed to friends. The unit ends on Diderot spending three days on an acquaintance's discovery while Encyclopædia proofs wait at Le Breton's, accepting Grimm's maxim that time is owed first to friends and duties; Morley notes the "lavish profusion" went on regardless. It is an incidental glimpse of the editor's labour as a competing claim on a generous person.

Notable passages

The curated passages are the problem-of-evil reasoning, the Leibnitz pyramid, Morley's verdict on the argumentative century, his qualified judgement via Lespinasse, the statement of Diderot's idea of friendship, and Diderot's reply to Landois with its determinism. Little here bears on World Machines concepts directly; the nearest rhyme is the Enlightenment's confidence that argument and experience (not revelation) settle morals, set against the Leibniz-style optimism the company mocks, and the philosophe habit of treating the person as formed by circumstance, which is secular and individual where the WMP frame is institutional.
