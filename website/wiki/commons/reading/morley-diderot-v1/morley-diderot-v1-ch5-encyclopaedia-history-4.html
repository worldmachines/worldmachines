Rousseau's schism, D'Alembert's desertion, Diderot alone, and the end of the Encyclopædia

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley closes his history of the Encyclopædia: Rousseau's 1758 break marks a reaction inside the illumination, D'Alembert quits and Diderot carries on alone for seven years until Le Breton's secret mutilation of the proofs, after which Morley gives Voltaire's Versailles anecdote, the work's afterlife, and the costs and Diderot's meagre pay.

Morley carries the publishing history of the Encyclopædia to its end. He first argues that Rousseau's Letter to D'Alembert on Stage Plays (1758) was not a mere private quarrel but the opening of a reaction within the school of illumination, one that revived the old absolutist idea of authority in theistic dress. He then narrates D'Alembert's retirement and Voltaire's advice, Diderot's reply and his seven years of solitary editing, the printer Le Breton's secret mutilation of the final ten volumes, the completion in 1765 (plates 1772), and Voltaire's anecdote of the book at the king's supper. He ends with the editions and imitators that followed, and with figures for cost, subscription price and Diderot's pay.

Themes

Reaction inside the illumination (Morley's verdict). Morley treats the Rousseau episode as a fundamental divergence, not a rivalry of authors. He lists the reaction's marks as the subordination of reason to emotion, the displacement of industry, science and many-sided ingenuity by dreamy indolence, and the return of suppression of opinions by the secular arm. In his judgement Rousseau's idea prevailed because it fitted the political traditions of France, and he closes the point with Michelet's image of Danton and Robespierre, both born in 1759, as the incarnations of the two principles. This is a Victorian liberal-positivist reading, and it can be set beside counter-enlightenment and jean-jacques-rousseau, though Morley's frame is moral and political, not a theory of rival world machines.

Desertion and persistence. D'Alembert's own letters to Voltaire, as Morley quotes them, give his reasons: affronts, satires that were protected or even commanded by those in power, sermons at Versailles, and new censors. Morley adds that dissatisfaction with the booksellers' finances was another reason. voltaire first urged resistance, then urged Diderot to finish abroad. Diderot refused, in a letter Morley quotes at length. His refusal combines a point of honour (to abandon the work is to do what the persecutors want) with weariness and doubt whether the philosopher does more than the flute-player.

The Encyclopædia as an industrial and material enterprise. Morley's explanation of why Diderot would not go to Petersburg or Berlin is that the work depended on compositors, engravers, looms, engines and processes available only in France. This is his own explanatory gloss, and it casts the work as a piece of knowledge infrastructure rooted in a place (compare Legibility and composable-knowledge, loosely: Morley does not argue in those terms).

Censorship from within the trade. The Le Breton episode shows suppression carried out by the publisher, anticipating the state, not by the state. Le Breton and his foreman cut passages deemed likely to provoke the government, then burned the manuscripts and proofs. Morley's narration leans on Grimm's account and on Diderot's own anger, and the language of 'miscreants' and 'murderous work' is Morley's moral register.

Voltaire's Versailles anecdote and its correction. The Powder and Rouge scene presents the book as a practical compendium of the arts, from the pin to the gun. Morley, characteristically, corrects the anecdote by noting that Madame de Pompadour was dead before the volumes containing those articles appeared, while holding that its substance is probably true. He then lists the foreign editions and the Methodical Encyclopædia, and contrasts its lack of doctrine with the Encyclopédie Nouvelle.

Money. The costs, the subscriber price, and Diderot's estimated salary (about three thousand livres a year, sixty thousand in all, per Grimm) are set against Voltaire's army-contract profits, with Voltaire's outcry about the contractor's twenty thousand livres a day.
