From speculation to social attack: what the Encyclopædia's articles actually said (Morley, Ch. V, section II)

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley argues that the Encyclopædia's sensationalist and Baconian philosophy turned speculative illumination into a practical social attack, and illustrates this from its articles on agriculture, the gabelle and taille, privilege, the corvée, the militia, endowments, fairs, guilds and game.

Morley (a Victorian liberal-positivist writing in 1878) explains why a metaphysical agitation (can matter think? are ideas innate?) turned after mid-century into a political and social one, and then argues, by walking through specific articles, that the Encyclopædia was constructive and practical rather than a 'gospel of negation'. The unit moves from a general account of why the attack was sharper in France than in England, through his claim that empiricist philosophy tends to expel 'Magic' and heighten faith in institutions and education, to a catalogue of articles on agriculture, the gabelle, taille, privilege, corvée, militia, foundations, fairs, guilds and game. This is Morley's own reading, presented with approving quotation and paraphrase of the articles and of Arthur Young.

Themes

Why philosophy became politics. Morley's claim is that the theological persecutors of De Prades and De Maistre's assault on Bacon were 'guided by a right instinct of self-preservation': metaphysical conclusions shape civil sentiment. He adds that the attack weighed more in France than in England because physical science had advanced, and because French institutions were 'too deeply rooted in absolutism and privilege' to be reformed, while the Catholic church was identified with repression. English deism, he says, was impotent as a merely intellectual movement. D'Argenson's 1753 remark (quoted) that hatred of priests, not English philosophy, was driving France toward revolution is offered as a third-party witness; Morley adds that hope, not only hate, drove the movement. Rhymes with intellectual-vs-revolutionary and counter-enlightenment (De Maistre as the acute Catholic opponent), though Morley frames it as ideas gaining force through 'practical opportuneness' rather than as a machine theory.

Empiricism tends to a social, positive spirit. Morley's argument: Newton over Descartes, Bacon, and Locke's sensational psychology expel 'Magic' in favour of ordered cause and effect; as knowledge widens, so does recognised nescience, so attention turns to controllable forces; man becomes 'the centre of the world to himself'; and sensationalism heightens belief in the power of education and institutions. He explicitly sets aside whether the philosophy was true, and concedes the Encyclopædists' metaphysic was 'imperfectly thought out'. Diderot's line on preventing misery rather than multiplying refuges marks the new spirit. This is a Comtean-flavoured causal story, Morley's own and contestable.

Politics in the widest sense. Economic conditions and administration, Morley says, matter more to national well-being than the form of government (his clothes-versus-blood analogy). He credits the Encyclopædists with attacking colonial tyranny, the slave trade, the fiscal system, venal justice and the penal code, and with first grasping 'the honour that is owed to productive industry'. He also hedges: they were not the originators of these ideas, owing peace-ideas to Saint Pierre and political method to Montesquieu; what changed is that 'political ideas have been grasped as instruments'.

Exposure by plain statement. Articles on agriculture (a quarter of the cultivable land unbroken or abandoned, grain-trade restrictions, arbitrary imposts, drift to towns, English free exports), on the Gabelle and Taille (absurd residence-based tax rules; the Lisieux taille proportionnelle of 1718 rejected), and on Privilegé (a bourgeois buying a salt-office place to gain exemptions, burdening the poor and disgusting talent) work, Morley says, as 'a formal indictment' without rhetoric. He draws the line to the 1789 cahiers, and concludes that the Encyclopædists merely shed 'rational discussion' on grievances no conservative would now revive. Resonates with Legibility only loosely: the articles make the realm's fiscal workings readable and thereby indictable, but Morley does not frame it so.

The Encyclopædia as a centre for practical expertise. The corvée article was written by a sub-engineer of roads and bridges at Tours; Morley treats this as evidence that the work gathered active-minded specialists. Turgot's later Limousin reforms (money payment for corvée, parish-provided militia) are cited as the practice the articles anticipated or paralleled. The Foundations article (Turgot, by Morley's account) is called the most masterly discussion of endowments; Foire and Maîtrises expose regulatory mania and guild monopoly. Articles on game (Chasse) are noted as unusually direct for the pre-1757 volumes, with Arthur Young quoted on the wrath they provoke.

Notable passages

The opening (c1) states the puzzle and the claim that theologians and De Maistre instinctively saw the political stakes of psychology. The England-versus-France comparison (c2) is Morley's explanation of why deism became socially destructive in France. The passage on the tendency of empiricism (c3) is his core philosophical argument for the positive, social spirit. The 'politics in its widest sense' passage (c4) gives his catalogue of what the Encyclopædists attacked and praised. The Privilegé and taille material (c5) is his main illustration of an abuse exposed by plain statement, tied to the 1789 cahiers. The corvée and militia passage (c6) shows the Encyclopædia as a forum for practical specialists and ends in a characteristic Morley moral about civilised remarks that go unheeded.
