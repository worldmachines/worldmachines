Diderot's talents, talk and first sceptical writings

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley opens his account of Diderot's early writings by diagnosing a genius of dispersive talents who was a great talker but never produced a masterpiece, then traces his hack-work, his Shaftesbury translation and the burned Philosophical Thoughts of 1746 to the social and religious circumstances that made a rude, common-sense scepticism possible.

This opening of Chapter III has two movements. First Morley offers his own verdict on Diderot's character as a man of letters: splendid, spacious gifts without the "economy" to concentrate them, so that he left fine pages but no fine work, and a genius better suited to talk than to print (he compares him to Johnson and Coleridge, and to Socrates, with Marmontel and Grétry as witnesses). Second, Morley narrates the start of Diderot's authorship: hack translation for the booksellers (Stanyan's History of Greece), the 1745 version of Shaftesbury's Inquiry concerning Virtue and Merit, and the Philosophical Thoughts of 1746, burnt by the parliament of Paris. He then argues, in his own voice, that books are symptoms of living social movement, and explains why Shaftesbury's common-sense scepticism suited France's public mood of the time.

Themes

Genius without economy (Morley's verdict). Morley, citing La Rochefoucauld, holds that Diderot is the great example of faculty in excess of stewardship: "To be mediocre, to be sterile, to be futile, are the three fatal endings of many superbly announced potentialities." He quotes Diderot's own one regret (July 1765, finishing the Encyclopædia) about sacrificing talent to need, but then rejects the excuse: dispersiveness, not poverty, was what ruled out masterpieces. Diderot's own quoted view is that giving his time to others was no loss. Morley's regret is that he did not take himself seriously enough.

Talker, not writer. Morley judges the writings declamatory, ill-compacted and rambling, and attributes Diderot's reputation to the spoken personality that friends (Marmontel, Grétry, Morellet and Joubert as captives of his argument) describe. A friend's phrase likens him to nature "without any dominating principle, without a master and without a God." Morley calls him Socratic but unlike Socrates a disseminator of positive and constructive ideas, and adds that excellence in literature also requires self-possession.

Hack-work and Shaftesbury. Diderot's self-taught English (using an Anglo-Latin dictionary) leads to paid translation work and the Shaftesbury version. Morley hedges on the story that Madame de Puisieux's need of money prompted the Thoughts, noting that his daughter's memoir is full of blunders but that Diderot deserves the benefit of the doubt. The Thoughts, he says, are a robuster development of the sceptical side of Shaftesbury, and their burning was Diderot's first taste of authority's hand.

Books as symbols of social movement. Morley's methodological aside: histories of literature mistake a book for the cause of controversies of which it is "only a symbol"; Voltaire and Diderot did not turn free-thinkers merely because of Bacon, Hobbes and Locke. Speculative literature's force "hangs on practical opportuneness": the economic evils of monasticism, superstition, ecclesiastical factiousness and bigoted tribunals. This anticipates the book's wider thesis that the Enlightenment was a practical social attack.

Why Shaftesbury and why common sense. Morley ties Shaftesbury's essay on Enthusiasm to fanaticism (Cevennes prophets in London; Jansenist convulsionaries in Paris) and reports a contemporary diarist's inference that miracles of antiquity were doubtful, and that the Unigenitus quarrel showed how religions establish themselves. In Morley's account Diderot's early politeness to Christianity is partly ironical deference; Montaigne also influenced him; common sense (Shaftesbury via Locke, and through Hutcheson the Scottish philosophy) was the only available weapon, since historical criticism and scientific attack were not yet possible.

Early deism at the machine's edge. Diderot quotes physics against atheism (Malpighi, Newton, and others) and says the world is "no longer a god; it is a machine with its cords, its pulleys, its springs, its weights." Morley reads this as a halting-place not yet consistent materialism, though Diderot already sees the controversy passing from metaphysicians to physicists. Here the machine image is a theological argument for a designer, not a theory of historical infrastructure, so it rhymes only loosely with world-machines; it is, though, an instructive early use of the mechanism metaphor in the Enlightenment, which Morley dates as an early stage.

Notable passages

  • The verdict (c1): Morley's statement that Diderot left an eminent name yet no masterpiece, with Marmontel's line.
  • Giving his life away (c2): Diderot's own words, and Morley's reproof that he did not take himself seriously enough.
  • Talker versus writer (c3): the case that Diderot's gifts are those of conversation, with Johnson and Coleridge as parallels.
  • Books as symbols (c4): Morley's historiographical caution, close to a social-causation view of ideas; compare the later preference for distributed, silently assembling movements in history-machine, though Morley is not making a structural argument.
  • Shaftesbury and Jansenist miracles (c5): why scepticism took this form in this moment; see also scottish-enlightenment for the common-sense line, and michel-de-montaigne, voltaire for the other influences and companions Morley names.
  • Physics against atheism (c6): the machine-with-pulleys passage and Morley's assessment of it as an early stage (isaac-newton is its hero).
