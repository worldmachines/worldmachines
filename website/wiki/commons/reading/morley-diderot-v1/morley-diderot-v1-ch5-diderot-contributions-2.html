Diderot as compiler: practical arts, borrowed philosophy, Spinosa and Leibnitz

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley closes his assessment of Diderot's Encyclopædia work by arguing that much of it, from agriculture to the history-of-philosophy series, was second-hand compilation, that this produced visible inconsistencies (Philosopher versus Philosophy, the Wolffian borrowing), and that the Spinosa and Leibnitz articles show both Diderot's vigour and the limits of his metaphysical engagement.

Morley finishes his survey of Diderot's own contributions to the Encyclopædia. He first notes Diderot's diligence on the arts and trades (workshop visits, summoned workmen), then qualifies it: even on practical subjects, such as Agriculture, much was book-knowledge taken at second-hand, and the same holds for the philosophical articles, which largely reproduce Bayle, Deslandes, Brucker, and for other entries the Annual Register or Locke. Morley defends this as suited to the book's purpose, provided the writer passes the material through his own mind, but shows where Diderot failed to do so: the paired articles Philosopher and Philosophy, the latter lifted from Christian Wolff. The unit ends with the articles on Spinosa and Leibnitz, which Morley treats as characteristic of Diderot's good and bad sides.

Themes

Compilation as the legitimate method of an encyclopædia, with one condition. Morley's own verdict is that to emulate Bayle's or Brucker's laborious scholarship would have sacrificed the whole to a minor part. The one imperative is that the writer "pass the accepted material through his own mind"; Diderot, Morley says, usually did. Diderot's merit in Agriculture (drawing on Tull via Duhamel) is, in Morley's view, that he sought the newest lights and urged fresh experiment in a world of routine. Morley also notes that Diderot honestly referred readers to his sources. Relevant to the Encyclopædia as a knowledge-infrastructure project (see composable-knowledge), though Morley frames it as a matter of literary conscience, not infrastructure.

Inconsistency from mingling others' thoughts with his own. Morley reads the Philosopher article as a social, practical portrait (Diderot's words as Morley quotes them: the philosopher suspends judgment, understands what he rejects, regards civil society as a divinity on earth). He contrasts it with the Philosophy article's Wolffian definition, "the science of possibles quâ possibles", which he calls alien to the positive spirit of the Encyclopædia. The same article ends by denouncing the systematic spirit. This is Morley's thesis (a positive, social, practical Encyclopædia) applied as a test, and it shows him measuring Diderot against Baconian rather than Leibnitzian ideals.

Diderot's sobriety about metaphysics. In the Pyrrhonienne passage Diderot argues that because all in nature is bound together no one has complete knowledge, yet one must stop disputing at an evident proposition and refuse to be drawn into questions "shoreless and bottomless". Morley leaves open whether this shows incompetence or good sense, calls it characteristic, and asks why Diderot wrote on metaphysics at all.

The Spinosa article. Morley's verdict: half is Bayle, half is Diderot's own vigorous objections, with no sign he read beyond the first book of the Ethics. Diderot attacks the geometrical method and individual propositions and finally invokes common sense (there are several substances). Morley finds this "unpleasantly like an appeal to the crowd" in science, offers a charitable reconstruction of what Diderot may have meant (a conception of substance with no relation to objective experience), and regrets he did not push the objections further. Morley himself notes this treatment falls short of the modern "literary conscience", which has "turned specialist". Connects to baruch-spinoza, though no WMP concept such as conatus appears in the text.

The Leibnitz article. Mostly a table of propositions plus Fontenelle's éloge, reproduced in admiration for a genius like Diderot's in range but superior in concentration and construction. Diderot's fanciful list of candidates for the head of the human race and his "four marvellous insects" (Bayle, Descartes, Leibnitz, Newton) are reported; Morley concludes Diderot only viewed the façade admiringly. See gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz, isaac-newton.

Notable passages

  • Second-hand practical knowledge (Agriculture, Tull, Duhamel, Cobbett): Morley's balanced judgement of Diderot's real but limited merit.
  • Confusion in Philosopher / Philosophy: the Wolff incongruity.
  • The Pyrrhonienne passage: Diderot's own words on sobriety of reason.
  • The Spinosa article and Morley's critique of the common-sense appeal.
  • The Leibnitz article as admiring but uncritical digest.
