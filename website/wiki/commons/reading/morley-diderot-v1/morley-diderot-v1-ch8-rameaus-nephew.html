Rameau's Nephew: transmission, genre, and social meaning

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley introduces Rameau's Nephew by narrating its odd transmission via Schiller and Goethe, rejecting single-purpose readings of it, comparing its parasite with Juvenal's and Lucian's, and arguing that Rameau is a documentary of the moral rot of the old French society, with side notes on Palissot and the Lullist-Piccinist music quarrel.

Morley opens Chapter VIII by framing the dialogue as the product of a mind that, like Plato, Shakespeare, Pascal and Goethe, looked into the misanthropic mood without making a home of it. He then tells the strange history of the text: the manuscript Schiller gave Goethe in 1804, its disappearance, a fraudulent French retranslation from Goethe's German in 1821, and Brière's 1823 text from Madame de Vandeul, so that for decades the one masterpiece of a man who died in 1784 rested on a single disputed manuscript. He rejects the rival one-sided interpretations (satire of manners, reductio of self-interest, answer to Palissot, a frame for the music debate) and claims it is a living portrait of an original. He compares Rameau with Juvenal's Trebius and Lucian's spunger, quotes Diderot's own sketch of the real Rameau, and offers a long judgement that the dialogue is a guide to the depravity of the ancien regime that prepared the Revolution. He closes with Palissot as an eternal type and the French-versus-Italian music controversy.

Themes

The scientific attitude to character (Morley's judgement). Morley holds that Diderot, unlike Juvenal, does not simply denounce the parasite. He treats Rameau as an anatomist treats a pathological specimen, as 'your brother and mine'. Morley calls this move from praise and blame to scrutiny 'a mark of the modern spirit', while insisting it does not relieve us of the duty to shun base characters. The ancient satirists (Juvenal, Lucian, whom he calls the voltaire of the second century) turned 'intellectual scorn' on the parasite, whereas the modern eye has 'moral patience'.

Textual history as a cautionary tale. Morley narrates, with some relish, how the text moved through friedrich-schiller and johann-wolfgang-von-goethe, was hindered by German hatred of France, and was then forged and counter-forged. He doubts that Goethe's endorsement settled authenticity, since Goethe admits he translated quickly and could not compare closely. This is Morley's own evidentiary reasoning, not Diderot's.

One work, many readings, one answer. Morley dismisses the single-purpose readings and says the dialogue 'is all of these things, because it is none of them'. It is first a portrait, with Rameau as Diderot's Falstaff. Diderot, as Morley quotes him, values such originals as a leaven that restores natural individuality and unmasks rogues.

Social significance and the road to the Revolution. Morley claims that Rameau is no accidental reprobate but a representative of courtiers, financiers, merchants and shopkeepers who were 'immoral by formula and depraved on principle'. He then argues that the Jacobin flood was as indispensable to France as the barbarian flood to Rome. This is a strongly moralising, Victorian verdict; it sits near the project's interest in intellectual-vs-revolutionary ideas and a decaying order, but Morley offers a moral diagnosis rather than a structural account, and the 'purifying flame' reading is dated.

Palissot as an eternal species. Diderot's by-play against Palissot becomes, for Morley, the pattern of the literary dangler and parasite-tyrant (La Bruyere's Troilus). Palissot's comedy against the philosophers brought him vogue, then contempt; in the Terror he escaped Chaumette, which Morley credits to the magnanimity of the 'theoretical masters'.

Music as the field of passionate controversy. Morley treats the French/Italian quarrel (Lulli and the elder Rameau, the Italian company of 1752, Gluck, Piccini) as comparable in heat to theological dispute, and says Diderot, who sided against Lulli and Rameau, was acting on his general preference for the natural over the artificial. He notes Goethe's remark about the inconsistency with Diderot's taste for expressive art.

Notable passages

  • The strange life of the manuscript (c1). The Schiller-Goethe-Brière chain and Morley's scepticism about Goethe's voucher.
  • 'All of these things, because it is none of them' (c2). Morley's rejection of reductive readings.
  • Juvenal versus Diderot (c3). Rameau as the unmasked many; 'There is in every man and woman something which, if you knew it, would make you hate them.'
  • Lucian and the modern spirit (c4). The scientific attitude to character, with the ancient parasite as foil.
  • Social significance (c5). Rameau as representative of a class depraved on principle, and the Jacobin-barbarian analogy.
  • The music quarrel (c6). Pamphlet war over French versus Italian music.
