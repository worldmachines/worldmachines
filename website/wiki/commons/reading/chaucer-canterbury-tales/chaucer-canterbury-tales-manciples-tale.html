The Manciple's Prologue and Tale: The Cook's Gourd, Phoebus's Crow, and the Peril of Speech

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
The Manciple silences the drunk, swaying Cook with a trick gourd of wine, then tells the classical fable of Phoebus's speaking white crow, whose truthful report of the god's wife's infidelity gets it blackened and struck dumb — a story the Manciple turns into a long proverbial sermon on the danger of any speech, true or false.

Connects: Conatus

The unit opens with the linking Prologue: at Bob-up-and-down near Canterbury, Harry Bailly tries to rouse the Cook, who has fallen dead drunk and can barely keep his saddle. The Manciple needles the sleeping, foul-breathed Cook so mercilessly that the Host warns him he is inviting revenge — the Cook might later expose irregularities in the Manciple's own household accounts ("pinchen at thy reckonings"). Rather than risk that, the Manciple placates the Cook with a gourd of wine, a comic bribe that Harry Bailly celebrates with a mock-hymn to Bacchus for turning "earnest into game." Only then does the Manciple tell his tale: the old fable, traced by editors back through Ovid to Gower, of Phoebus's talking white crow, who reports the god's wife's adultery and is punished — blackened, silenced, and cast out — not for lying but for telling the truth. The tale ends not with narrative closure but with a long, proverb-stuffed sermon, voiced as the Manciple's mother's childhood advice, on the wisdom of keeping one's tongue still.

Themes

Drink as social anesthetic, deflecting real conflict. The Prologue stages a near-fight defused by wine: the Manciple's mockery of the Cook could easily curdle into violence or long-term enmity (the Host explicitly predicts the Cook will "bring [him] to the lure"), but a shared gourd dissolves it into farce. The Host's toast to Bacchus — who "canst turnen earnest into game" — frames alcohol as the lubricant that keeps the pilgrimage's social fabric from tearing, a small structural echo of how the frame tale as a whole manages friction between competing tellers.

Nature resists the cage. The tale's most striking digression argues, through three stacked exempla — a caged bird pining for the wild forest, a pampered cat abandoning milk and silk the instant she scents a mouse, a she-wolf who will mate with the basest wolf available — that appetite and instinct cannot be domesticated by comfort or confinement: "Lo, here hath kind her domination, / And appetite flemeth discretion." This is offered as the moral explanation for Phoebus's wife's infidelity despite being kept in gilded captivity by a devoted, brilliant, beautiful husband. The passage resonates strongly with the WMP idea of Conatus and capture-resistance — the bird's "liberty... desireth aye" regardless of how well the cage is gilded is almost a proof-text for the notion that a being's essential striving overrides any regime built to contain it.

Status launders the same act into different names. In a striking mid-tale digression, the Manciple argues that a highborn adulteress and a poor whore commit the identical act but receive different words — "lady" and "love" versus "wench" and "leman" — and extends the logic to a tyrant and a thief, noting that only superior force distinguishes a "capitain" from an "outlaw." This is a compact essay on how language ratifies power rather than describing truth, oddly embedded inside a fable ostensibly about a talking bird.

Truth-telling is punished, not falsehood. The fable's central irony is that the crow is destroyed for accuracy: it reports exactly what it witnessed, in plain and "sade" (grave, trustworthy) terms, and Phoebus's rage — arguably his own "rakel" (rash) fault — falls on the messenger once his wife is already dead by his own hand. The crow's lasting sentence (turned black, stripped of song and speech, condemned to cry only before storms) becomes an origin myth for why crows are black, but functions allegorically as a warning against unwelcome candor.

The moral coda overwhelms the story. The tale proper ends quickly with Phoebus's violence and grief; what follows is a long catalogue of proverbs on guarding the tongue, attributed to the Manciple's mother and citing Solomon, David, Seneca, and Cato's Distichs by name. This didactic excess — far longer than the fable's action — mirrors the Manciple's own conduct in the Prologue, where he avoided provoking the Cook precisely to protect himself from exposure. The tale thus reads as self-serving advice from a teller whose profession depends on not being talked about.

Notable passages

The gourd-of-wine trick in the Prologue shows the mechanism by which the pilgrimage manages its own internal frictions through comic bribery rather than confrontation. The caged-bird/cat/she-wolf sequence is the tale's philosophical core, arguing nature overmasters constraint — the clearest resonance point with WMP's framework of striving that resists capture. The crow's unadorned accusation ("For on thy bed thy wife I saw him swive") is the fable's turning point, converting fidelity to truth into a death sentence for the wife and a mutilation sentence for the crow. Phoebus's lament over his own rashness ("O rakel hand, to do so foul amiss... That unadvised smit'st the guilteless!") supplies the tale's stated moral center before the mother's proverbs even begin, indicting hasty action as much as hasty speech. Finally, the extended tongue-guarding catechism, opening "My son, think on the crow, in Godde's name," is where the Manciple converts a classical fable into a personal code of self-protective silence.
