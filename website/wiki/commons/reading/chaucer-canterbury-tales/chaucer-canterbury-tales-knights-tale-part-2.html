The Knight's Tale, Part 2: The Lists, the Tournament, and the First Mover

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
Theseus completes the lists at Athens with three temples to Venus, Mars, and Diana whose grim iconography previews the outcome, then stages a rule-bound tournament between Palamon and Arcite for Emily's hand that Arcite wins by combat but loses by a Saturn-sent accident, dying after commending Palamon to Emily, after which Theseus's First Mover oration reframes the death within a providential cosmic order and marries Palamon to Emily.

This unit completes the Knight's Tale. Theseus finishes building his mile-wide amphitheatre and its three oratories — to Venus, Mars, and Diana — each painted with iconography that forecasts the story's outcome before a single lance is broken. Palamon's hundred knights and Arcite's hundred knights arrive; Palamon prays to Venus for possession of Emily regardless of victory, Emily prays to Diana to be spared marriage altogether, and Arcite prays to Mars for victory in arms. All three prayers are answered, but incompatibly, forcing Jupiter and finally old Saturn to broker a settlement among the gods. Theseus reshapes what was to be a mortal battle into a formal, casualty-limited tournament; Arcite wins it and is granted Emily, but Saturn's infernal fire spooks his horse, he is thrown and mortally injured, and after a long death scene in which he bequeathes Emily's service to Palamon, he dies. Following civic mourning, Egeus and then Theseus deliver consolatory speeches on mutability and providential order, and the tale closes with Palamon's marriage to Emily.

Themes

Temples as machinery of fate. The three oratories are not mere decoration but operating diagrams of the forces that will determine the plot: Venus's temple depicts the sufferings and follies of desire (Idleness, Narcissus, Solomon, Croesus "caitif in servage"), Mars's temple depicts war's mechanics of betrayal, atrocity, and inevitable ruin ("the slaughter... of Julius... Of cruel Nero" foreshadowed in the stars "although at that time they were yet unborn"), and Diana's temple depicts the punishments of chastity's transgression (Actaeon, Callisto). The knights' prayers to these gods function like petitions lodged with distinct, semi-autonomous subsystems of a single cosmic apparatus — each temple runs its own logic, and the plot is essentially the friction of these logics colliding, resolved only when Saturn, the oldest and coldest power, imposes a settlement. This is close in spirit to world-machines: interlocking, impersonal infrastructures (love, war, chaste order) that individuals petition and suffer under rather than command.

Theseus's proclamation as an act of legibility. Rather than allow the promised mortal battle, Theseus unilaterally rewrites its rules: no short swords, no poleaxes, one course with the spear, capture-and-stake instead of killing, himself as judge. "He will his firste purpose modify... No man therefore, on pain of loss of life... shall send, or thither bring" any lethal weapon. This is sovereign power exercised precisely as the imposition of legible, measurable, rule-bound procedure onto what would otherwise be anarchic slaughter — a small, telling instance of the impulse legibility-as-machine-core names: converting a contest of raw force into something orderable, scored, and survivable, scaled and administered from above.

Providential order and universal decay. After Arcite's death, Egeus's consolation ("This world is but a throughfare full of woe, / And we be pilgrims, passing to and fro") and Theseus's closing First Mover oration extend the temple-logic upward into cosmology: Jupiter's "faire chain of love" binds fire, air, water, and land, but everything bound by it is subject to a decay that is not exception but design — the oak, the hard stone underfoot, the broad river, "the greate townes," all "wane and wend." "Then may ye see that all things have an end." This universalizing of decline (living things, structures, even towns wasting in due order) is a strikingly close secular-theological cousin of the decay-sequence: institutions and even oak trees pass from vigor to dissolution as a built-in feature of the chain that made them, and wisdom consists in making "a virtue of necessity" rather than resisting the order.

Love, honor, and the arbitrariness of outcome. Arcite wins the tournament fairly — Theseus declares him victor in the field — yet loses Emily to Saturn's caprice moments later, and his dying speech to Emily reverses the entire contest by naming Palamon, his rival, as most worthy of her love. The tale insists throughout that neither prowess nor virtue secures the prize; only the gods' shifting alignment (Venus weeping, Mars satisfied, Saturn adjudicating) does. Fortune, not merit, is figured as sovereign — women, the narrator notes drily, "follow all the favour of fortune."

Notable passages

The temple of Mars passage is the darkest and most vivid set-piece in the unit: a catalogue of atrocity ("The smiler with the knife under the cloak," "the sow freting the child right in the cradle") culminating in the image of Conquest sitting under a sword hung "by a subtle y-twined thread" — Damoclean iconography of power's fragility. Theseus's tournament proclamation is quoted at length because it is the clearest dramatization of a sovereign converting lethal chaos into administered spectacle. Arcite's death speech, ending in the plain, devastating "His laste word was; 'Mercy, Emily!'" and the narrator's careful agnosticism about the afterlife ("I am no divinister... Of soules find I nought in this register"), marks the tale's emotional center. Egeus's and then Theseus's philosophical orations that close the tale — moving from a single death to the fact that stone, rivers, and towns themselves wear away — universalize the tale's fatalism into full Boethian cosmology, tying the romance plot back to the temples' logic of forces larger than any individual will.
