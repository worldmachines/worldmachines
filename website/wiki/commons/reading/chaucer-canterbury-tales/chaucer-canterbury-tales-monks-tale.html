The Monk's Tale: The Catalogue of Fortune's Falls

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
The Monk, prodded by the Host's bawdy jab at clerical celibacy, delivers his catalogue of "tragedies" — a de casibus series running from Lucifer and Adam through Sampson, Hercules, Nebuchadnezzar, Zenobia, Nero, Holofernes, Antiochus, Alexander, Julius Caesar, Croesus, and on into near-contemporary falls (Pedro of Castile, Petro of Cyprus, Bernabò Visconti, Ugolino of Pisa) — each figure raised to greatness only to be broken by Fortune's wheel.

After the Tale of Melibee, the Host turns his attention to the Monk, teasing him first for his robust, unclerical physique and lamenting that celibacy has hoarded all the best breeding stock away from the lay world's wives — a coarse, comic overture before the Monk, taking the jibe "in patience," announces he will tell not one tale but "an hundred" tragedies from his "cell." What follows is not a story but a formal catalogue: seventeen exempla, from Lucifer's fall out of Heaven to the starvation of the Earl of Pisa's children, each illustrating the same mechanism — a figure raised to "great prosperity" and then, without warning, broken by the turn of Fortune's wheel. The series moves promiscuously across biblical, classical, and near-contemporary history, treating Adam, Hercules, Zenobia, Nero, Julius Caesar, and Bernabò Visconti (d. 1385, the latest event Chaucer mentions anywhere in the Tales) as instances of one undifferentiated moral pattern, and ends — as the text we have it cuts off — with the pathos of Ugolino, borrowed explicitly from Dante.

Themes

Tragedy as a defined genre, not a plot. Before beginning, the Monk pauses to define his terms: "Tragedy is to say a certain story... Of him that stood in great prosperity, / And is y-fallen out of high degree / In misery, and endeth wretchedly." This is historiography reduced to a single repeatable shape — rise, then catastrophic, causeless reversal — presented as a genre convention (six-foot verse, or prose, "in many a sundry wise") rather than as an argument. The Monk explicitly disclaims chronological order ("Though I by order telle not these things... tell them some before and some behind"), which matters: the catalogue's power comes from the sameness of the pattern repeating across radically different eras, not from any developmental sequence.

Fortune's wheel as the sole causal engine. Every fall in the sequence is attributed not to institutions, forces, or historical process but to personified Fortune's caprice: "For, certain, when that Fortune list to flee, / There may no man the course of her wheel hold." This is a great-men (and occasionally great-women) historiography in its purest form — causality collapses entirely into individual virtue, vice, and luck. It stands in instructive contrast to a world-machines reading of historical change: where WMP's Dawn-Day-Dusk cycles are driven by the silent, distributed assembly of infrastructure — a history-machine lurching into visibility — the Monk's wheel has no infrastructure at all, only a goddess turning a crank on individuals. The medieval wheel and the modern world-machine share a conviction that dominance is always temporary and reversal is structural, but they locate the mechanism at opposite scales: personal fortune versus civilizational plumbing.

Women as agents and objects of ruin. The catalogue's misogynist undertow, primed by the Host's prologue rant against celibate monks depriving wives of "engendrure," recurs inside the tragedies themselves: Sampson is destroyed because he "told his secre" to Delilah, Hercules dies from a shirt sent by his own leman Dejanira. Zenobia complicates this pattern — she is herself the tragic subject, a warrior-queen of martial and scholarly accomplishment undone not by treachery but by naked Roman conquest, her fall staged as a specific gendered inversion: the woman who once "bare the sceptre full of flow'rs" is reduced to bearing "a distaff, her cost for to quite."

Collapsing biblical, classical, and contemporary history into one register. Lucifer and Adam sit beside Hercules and Alexander, who sit beside Pedro the Cruel of Castile and Bernabò Visconti — figures from Chaucer's own century. The effect is to flatten fourteen centuries of source material (Genesis, Judges, Boethius, Boccaccio's De Casibus and De Claris Mulieribus, Petrarch, Dante, and recent Italian and Iberian political assassination) into a single moral vocabulary, where a dynastic murder in 1385 Milan is exactly as legible as the Tower of Babel.

An interrupted, borrowed ending. The tale as given here breaks off with Ugolino of Pisa, starved in his tower with his three children — and the Monk pointedly defers the fuller telling to "the greate poet of Itale, / That Dante hight," acknowledging his direct debt to Inferno XXXIII. The text stops mid-catalogue, consistent with the tradition (noted by the editor) that the Monk's tragedies were cut short by the other pilgrims' impatience, though that interruption itself falls outside this unit.

Notable passages

The Host's teasing of the Monk is the unit's liveliest frame material, an estates-satire complaint that religious celibacy has starved the world of good breeding stock — "Religion hath ta'en up all the corn / Of treading, and we borel men be shrimps" — ending on the wives'-preference punchline about counterfeit coin ("no lusheburghes... paye ye"). It sets up the irony that this worldly, well-fed Monk is about to deliver a hundred cells' worth of ascetic moralizing on pride and downfall.

The Monk's formal definition of tragedy, and the Tale's opening stanza ("For, certain, when that Fortune list to flee, / There may no man the course of her wheel hold: / Let no man trust in blind prosperity"), together state the catalogue's operating thesis before a single exemplum begins — everything that follows is an instance, not an argument.

Zenobia's reversal is the most fully rendered of the seventeen falls, and the cruellest in its gendering: from warrior-queen and scholar to a captive paraded in Aurelian's triumph, her fate summarized in the couplet turn from sceptre to distaff.

The Croesus exemplum ends with one of the catalogue's few moments of dramatic irony rather than blunt narration: his daughter Phanie correctly interprets his dream of being washed and dried atop a tree as a premonition of the gallows, a small embedded scene of prophecy ignored.

The Ugolino passage, adapted from Dante, is the emotional climax of the fragment as we have it — the starving children first begging for bread, then offering their own flesh to their father, dying one by one in his lap. It is the unit's clearest demonstration that the catalogue, however schematic its Fortune-wheel logic, can still produce genuine pathos.
