The Wife of Bath's Prologue and Tale

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
The Wife of Bath defends her five marriages and her mastery over each husband in a long autobiographical prologue, then tells an Arthurian tale in which a knight who has raped a maiden is spared only if he can answer what women most desire, and the answer — sovereignty — is what redeems him.

The unit contains both halves of the Wife of Bath's performance: the Prologue, a sprawling autobiographical apologia in which she defends serial marriage against clerical authority and recounts, in ferocious comic detail, how she dominated five husbands (the first three old and rich, the fourth a philanderer, the fifth a young Oxford clerk named Jenkin who read aloud from a book of "wicked wives" until she tore out its pages and won the marriage's governance for good); and the Tale proper, in which a knight of Arthur's court, condemned to death for raping a maiden, is given a year to discover what women most desire, learns the answer — sovereignty — from a loathly old woman who then claims him as her price, and is transformed (and the marriage saved) once he cedes her the choice and the mastery she asked for.

Themes

Experience versus authority. The Wife opens by setting her own lived experience of five marriages against "authoritative texts," and spends the first third of the Prologue re-reading Scripture (the Samaritan woman, Solomon's wives, Abraham, Jacob, Paul on virginity) to show that no verse actually forbids remarriage — only counsels virginity as one gift among others. This is a sustained argument that lived, embodied knowledge can out-argue inherited textual authority, a move that gives the whole prologue its combative, exegetical energy.

Marriage as an economy of leverage. With her first three husbands — old, rich, and already bound to her by "the statute" of marriage debt — she describes governance almost as asset management: once they had "given her all their land and treasor," she had no further need to court their love and instead extracted labour, gifts, and submission through manufactured jealousy and relentless psychological pressure. Her performative accusations ("I bare hem wrong on hand") are a deliberate technology of control, not spontaneous emotion.

Vocation as a "vessel of gold or wood." Answering the case for universal chastity, she argues that God calls people to different offices — some to virginity, some to marriage — the way a lord's household needs vessels of both gold and wood. It's a theological argument for legitimate diversity of ends rather than a single normative path, phrased almost as a proliferation-of-forms argument against one totalizing standard of virtue. divergence-machine is a loose but real resonance here: her whole rhetorical project resists a single legible, textually-authorized template for a virtuous life.

Surveillance, capture, and the fight for self-governance. The imagined jealous husband tries to lock away her body and her goods, spies on her, even (she says) could set "Argus with his hundred eyen" to guard her — and she insists none of it would work "unless I please." The climax of the Prologue is Jenkin's nightly reading from a compendium of wife-slandering exempla (Eve, Xantippe, Clytemnestra, Eryphile), an attempt to discipline her through curated textual authority; she answers by physically destroying the book and winning "by mastery all the sovereignty." The pattern — external attempts at legibility and control met by refusal and self-assertion — resonates with capture-resistance and the Spinozan Conatus that WMP tracks: a persistent drive to keep governing one's own body and property against instruments built to fix and monitor it.

Sovereignty as the Tale's answer. The knight's quest for "what women most desire" collects a catalogue of plausible answers (riches, flattery, freedom from reproof, secrecy) before landing on the true one: mastery over husband and lover alike. The old woman who supplies this answer then enacts it twice — first by claiming the knight as payment, then by making him cede the choice of her age and fidelity entirely to her — turning the folk-tale's transactional logic into a demonstration of the very thesis it proves.

Gentillesse redefined. In the wedding-night speech, the old wife dismantles inherited nobility, arguing that "gentleness cometh from God alone" and is enacted, not inherited — fire burns the same regardless of where it's kept, so lineage cannot bequeath virtue the way it bequeaths land. It's the Tale's clearest piece of independent moral argument, transposing the Prologue's anti-authoritarian method (experience and reasoned re-reading over inherited textual/social authority) onto class rather than gender.

Notable passages

The Prologue's opening move — "Husbands at the church door have I had five" through her rejection of a fixed scriptural number for marriages — sets the entire argumentative strategy: turning ambiguous authority back on itself. The "neigheboure's wife" tirade catalogues, in the mouth of the imagined jealous husband, every contradictory slander levelled at women (too poor is costly, too rich is proud, too fair invites seduction, too foul is desperate) — a compressed satire of impossible double binds. Her declaration "we will be at our large," followed by the (self-attributed) Ptolemy proverb about not envying other people's fortune, states the autonomy thesis in miniature. The book-burning scene — from Jenkin reading "all night" through her collapse, his blow, her feigned death, and his surrender of "the governance of house and land" — is the Prologue's dramatic resolution of the sovereignty argument into physical action. In the Tale, the knight's answer, "Women desire to have the sovereignty... and for to be in mast'ry him above," is the fulcrum the whole narrative was built to reach. And the old wife's gentillesse speech, arguing that nobility "is not annexed to possession" and closing on "that he is gentle that doth gentle deedes," is the Tale's independent philosophical payload, extending the Prologue's anti-authoritarian logic to the class hierarchy of medieval England itself.
