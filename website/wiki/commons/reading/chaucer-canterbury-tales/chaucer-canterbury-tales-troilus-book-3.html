Troilus and Criseyde, Book III: Pandarus's Engineered Union

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
Pandarus's carefully engineered plot succeeds: Troilus and Criseyde are brought together, first at Deiphebus's house where Troilus haltingly declares himself, then at Pandarus's rain-swept house where the lovers are secretly united in body, an episode framed by a cosmic hymn to Venus, an aubade cursing the coming day, and Troilus's Boethian song praising Love as the force that binds the universe.

Book III opens with a proem-hymn to Venus as the binding force of the cosmos, then narrates the two set-pieces Pandarus has been building toward since Book II: Troilus's tongue-tied declaration of love to Criseyde at Deiphebus's house, and — after Pandarus extracts a pledge of secrecy and delivers a long, anxious meditation on his own compromised role as go-between — the actual physical union of the lovers at a contrived supper during a providential rainstorm. The book closes with the lovers' aubade cursing the coming day, Troilus's song in praise of Love as cosmic order, and a portrait of Troilus transfigured into the perfect knight by the ennobling power of love.

Themes

Love as cosmic infrastructure. The book frames its human drama inside a philosophical claim: Venus/Love is the force that "holds realm and house in unity," keeps discordant elements bound, constrains the greedy sea within limits, and gives the Sun and Moon their offices. Troilus's own Second Song makes this explicit — Love is what "knitteth law and company" and prevents the world from flying "asunder." This is medieval Boethian concordia discors, not the WMP framework proper, but the image of Love as an unseen, world-ordering machinery that keeps chaos at bay while remaining invisible in its ordinary operation has a family resemblance to world-machines as deep, naturalized infrastructure — worth noting lightly rather than forcing the equivalence.

Pandarus as social engineer, and secrecy as protective architecture. Pandarus repeatedly names his own role in explicitly instrumental terms — "through mine engine" (arts, contrivance), being "such a mean as make women unto men to come" — and immediately worries about exposure: if it were "wist" that he had engineered the affair, "all the people would upon it cry" and Criseyde would be "fordone." The entire second half of the book stages the construction of a small, opaque, portal-bordered space (Pandarus's house, the hidden "stew," the secret passage "through a gutter, by a privy went") defended against a legible, judging public ("the name yet of her, among the people... is hallow'd"). The narrator underscores this with "Now all is well, for all this world is blind, / In this mattere." This engineered privacy-against-publicity has a genuine resonance with capture-resistance and the thicket concept — an intimate order protected by terroir and opacity from a world that would destroy it if it became visible — though the text's own stakes are reputation and courtly shame, not the Liveness-era politics WMP is ultimately tracking.

The comedy and vulnerability of desire. Troilus's rehearsed speech collapses the moment Criseyde appears — "wax'd neither quick nor dead," his prepared lesson "through his wit y-run" — and his declaration reduces to a stammered "Mercy, mercy, my dear heart!" This deflation of the courtly lover's rhetorical mastery by actual social terror is one of the poem's running ironies, softened by Criseyde's own shrewd, unhurried management of the scene ("Advising her, and hied not too fast").

The aubade and the enmity of Day. Once the lovers are physically united, the poem pivots to the dawn-song tradition: Day is cursed as an "accuser," an "envious" spy prying into every "bow'r," while Night is begged to linger as it did for Alcmena and Jove. This is a set-piece within a long lyric tradition (compare the Provençal/Old French alba) rather than a WMP-specific idea, but its logic — legitimacy and intimacy can only exist while illegible to a watching order, and dissolve the instant that order's light reaches them — rhymes with the same capture/opacity theme above.

Love's ennobling effect. The book closes by cataloguing how love transforms Troilus into an ideal knight: bravest after Hector, generous, humble, scornful of "wretchedness," free of "pride, envy, ire, and avarice." This is the poem's explicit didactic payoff for the elaborate machinery of the plot — desire, once secured, is presented as civilizing rather than corrupting, at least for the moment the book leaves them in.

Notable passages

The proem's hymn to Venus establishes love as the hidden "cover'd quality" behind all seemingly inexplicable affinities ("why this fish, not that, comes to the weir"), setting up the whole book as an anatomy of one such affinity. The Deiphebus-house scene captures Troilus's collapse into inarticulate confession. Pandarus's self-accounting — "for thee I am become... such a mean as make women unto men to come" — is the clearest statement in the poem of his own instrumental, ethically compromised agency, and of the reputational stakes riding on secrecy. The consummation stanza ("Now be ye caught, now here is but we twain") and Criseyde's reply mark the plot's hinge. The aubade ("O cruel Day! accuser of the joy") and the Second Song ("Love, that of Earth and Sea hath governance!") bracket the physical union with, respectively, a curse against exposure and a cosmic justification of the very bond that exposure would destroy.
