The Friar's Tale

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
In the Friar's Tale, a corrupt ecclesiastical summoner who extorts money from sinners under threat of church prosecution swears brotherhood with a devil disguised as a fellow bailiff, learns from him that a curse only takes effect if meant in earnest, and is dragged bodily to hell when an old widow he tries to swindle damns him with genuine sincerity.

The Friar, needled by the Wife of Bath's incursion into clerkly territory and already trading glares with the Summoner riding beside him, takes his revenge in narrative form: a tale of a crooked ecclesiastical summoner who runs a bribery racket under an archdeacon's court, falls in with a yeoman on the road who turns out to be a devil, and is carried off to hell at the tale's end after trying to extort an old widow who curses him — and means it. The tale is one half of a two-part quarrel with the Summoner's Tale that follows, both bitter satires on the venality of the medieval Church's lower bureaucracy.

Themes

Ecclesiastical jurisdiction as an apparatus of surveillance and extraction. The tale opens by cataloguing the archdeacon's dominion — fornication, witchcraft, bawdry, defamation, adultery, tithing shortfalls, usury, simony — a court whose real business is turning moral infractions into fines ("pecunial pain"). The summoner beneath him runs a network of informants ("bawdes," "approvers") who feed him blackmail material, letting him manufacture false summonses and pocket bribes his master never sees. This is a small, ugly machine of legibility avant la lettre: sin rendered visible, catalogued, and monetized by a jurisdictional apparatus whose stated purpose (correction of souls) is a pretext for private extraction. Readers attuned to world-machines framing may note the resonance with legibility-as-machine-core — an institution that scales control not by improving virtue but by making transgression countable and billable.

Sworn brotherhood between thief and fiend. The summoner and the devil-in-disguise recognize each other as professional equals almost instantly — both work by "purchase," taking whatever they can get regardless of desert — and formalize this kinship with an oath of sworn brethren, complete with a pact to split whatever either one gains. The horror of the tale is not that the devil deceives the summoner but that the devil is simply honest about a predation the summoner already practices; the oath of brotherhood is comic precisely because it is sincere on both sides.

A brief demonology: agency, permission, and testing. The devil's disquisition on his own nature — that fiends have no fixed shape, that they act only by God's leave (as with Job), that they sometimes afflict the body alone, sometimes soul and body, and that even this affliction can serve "his salvation" though it was never the fiend's intent — is a compressed statement of orthodox scholastic demonology, delivered with startling nonchalance by the devil himself. It reframes temptation as a structural feature of a moral test, not an autonomous evil loose in the world.

Intent as the hinge of causation. The tale's real thesis, dramatized twice, is that a curse or a gift of one's goods to the devil only takes effect if truly meant. The carter who curses his stuck horses to hell ("The devil have all, horses, and cart, and hay") is safe, because — as the devil explains — "The churl spake one thing, but he thought another." The widow who curses the summoner to hell along with her prized pan means every word, and so the devil takes both, and him with them. Language here functions less as declaration than as an index of will — a distinctly pre-modern theory of speech-act efficacy in which sincerity, not utterance, is the operative cause.

Notable passages

The prologue's opening skirmish sets the terms of the whole enterprise: the Friar's needling aside that "of a Sompnour may no good be said" is less throat-clearing than thesis statement, previewing the tale's animating contempt before the Host has to intervene to keep the peace between Friar and Summoner.

The description of the summoner's bribery ring — bawds as informants, feigned mandements, wenches let go while their lovers are "pilled" — lays out the tale's real subject: not any single sin, but a bureaucracy of moral policing that has been captured entirely for private profit, closing on the flat verdict that the summoner is "A thief, and eke a Sompnour, and a bawd."

The devil's speech beginning "For sometimes we be Godde's instruments" is the tale's theological center of gravity, situating demonic action within a providential order (Job invoked by name) even as it insists a fiend has no power "withoute him" — God's permission is the leash.

The cart-in-the-mud episode, where the carter's damning of his own horses turns out to be empty of intent ("the churl spake one thing, but he thought another"), is the tale's proof-of-concept for its governing rule about curses, staged as comedy before the same rule is played straight for tragedy.

The closing exchange with the widow Mabery — extortion demanded down to the last pan, met with a curse she means with her whole heart — delivers the tale's payoff: "Body and soul, he with the devil went," the summoner's own instrument of jurisdiction turned, at last, against him.
