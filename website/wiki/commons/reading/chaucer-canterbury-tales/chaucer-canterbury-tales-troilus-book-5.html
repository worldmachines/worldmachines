Troilus and Criseyde, Book Five: The Exchange, the Betrayal, and the Death of Troilus

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
In the final book of Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde, Criseyde is exchanged to the Greek camp and gradually succumbs to Diomede's courtship, abandoning Troilus, who—devastated by proof of her infidelity—dies in battle and ascends to the eighth sphere to look down in contempt on the vanity of earthly love, as the poet closes by renouncing pagan love for Christian devotion.

This unit is the fifth and final book of Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde: Criseyde is escorted from Troy to her father Calchas among the Greeks under a ten-day truce; Troilus, desolate, wanders the emptied city reliving their courtship; Diomede methodically courts Criseyde and within weeks wins her — first her company, then her horse, brooch, and sleeve-pendant, finally (the narrator concedes with visible reluctance) her heart; Troilus, having found his own brooch on Diomede's captured coat-armour, learns the truth beyond doubt; he dies at Achilles' hand, and his spirit ascends through the spheres to look down on "this wretched world" with the serene contempt of the dead, before the poet's envoi turns from pagan tragedy to Christian devotion.

Themes

Fate closes over will before the book even begins. The opening stanza announces the ending in advance — Jove's disposition, the Fates' committee, Lachesis's thread — so that everything Troilus and Criseyde do afterward happens inside a verdict already rendered. The narrator's own footnoted aside about the Dominican/Franciscan predestination-versus-free-will controversy of Chaucer's day (note 76) is not incidental: the poem stages the same unresolved tension it declines to adjudicate, letting Troilus act as though choice matters while the frame insists it does not.

Betrayal is rendered as attrition, not villainy. Diomede's seduction is a patient tactical operation — he takes her bridle, calculates that pressing "of love" too soon would be "aboute naught," opens with sympathy and camaraderie, and only escalates once she is isolated, fatherless-in-spirit, and afraid. Criseyde's own reply is a masterclass in non-commitment ("I say not therefore that I will you love; / Nor say not nay") — she is not converted so much as worn down across the ten days that never end. The narrator's tally of what she surrenders — the horse Troilus once won, the brooch, the sleeve-pendant, and at last, hedged behind "Men say, I n'ot" (I know not), her heart — reads as an inventory of erosion rather than a single dramatic fall.

Troilus's grief is staged as ritual and liturgy before it is staged as fact. Long before he has proof, he performs mourning: dictating funeral instructions to Pandarus, apostrophizing Criseyde's shuttered palace as a desecrated shrine, narrating a walking tour of the city as a museum of lost happiness. The brooch discovered in Diomede's captured coat-armour only confirms what the ritual grief had already rehearsed — his interior collapse precedes and outpaces the external evidence.

The ending repudiates its own subject. Once Troilus is killed and his spirit rises through the spheres, the poem abruptly inverts its values: he "laughed right at the woe" of those weeping for him and "damned all our works" that chase "blinde lust." The envoi then explicitly renounces "olde clerkes'" pagan gods and the whole apparatus of courtly love the preceding four books spent building, redirecting the reader to Christ. It is a Boethian contemptus mundi turn grafted onto a completed romance, and the poem is aware of the seam — it apologizes to "every gentle woman" for Criseyde's guilt and asks pardon in advance.

Notable passages

The book opens by handing the ending to the Fates before the action starts, collapsing suspense into inevitability from the first stanza ("Approache gan the fatal destiny... Till Lachesis his thread no longer twine"). Troilus's apostrophe to Criseyde's shuttered house — "O palace desolate! / O house of houses, whilom beste hight!" — turns architecture into elegy, the empty windows standing in for the absent woman before any betrayal has occurred. Diomede's wooing speech is a small case study in seduction-as-argument: he minimizes the Trojans' prospects, flatters her face, and closes with calculated modesty ("Yea, lever than be a lord of Greekes twelve!"). Criseyde's answer to him is the poem's most exact rendering of a mind refusing to decide ("I say not therefore that I will you love; / Nor say not nay"), immediately undercut by her private prayer to see Troy again. The inventory of gifts to Diomede — horse, brooch, sleeve — culminating in the narrator's own reluctance to state plainly that she gave him her heart ("Men say, I n'ot, that she gave him her heart"), stages betrayal as something the poem itself resists narrating cleanly. And the death sequence, in which Troilus's soul rises to the eighth sphere and looks back down in amused contempt at earthly mourning, is the hinge on which the entire romance's value system flips into its Christian coda.

The unit's fatalism — an outcome fixed before the individuals inside it can act — and Troilus's doomed insistence on persisting in hope against mounting evidence resonate loosely with Conatus: the striving to endure that the poem's own cosmology (Fate, then the Boethian turn) is built to override.
