The Assembly of Fowls (The Parliament of Fowls)

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
A dreamer, having spent a day absorbed in Cicero's account of the Dream of Scipio, is led by the elder Scipio through a garden and Temple of Venus to Nature's parliament of birds on Saint Valentine's Day, where three tercel eagles plead in courtly rhetoric for a peerless formel eagle, the lower birds erupt into comic squabbling over the dispute, and Nature resolves the impasse by granting the formel a year's respite and the free choice of her own mate.

A first-person dreamer, worn out from a day spent reading Cicero's Somnium Scipionis in Macrobius's commentary, is visited in sleep by the elder Africanus, who leads him through twin inscribed gates into a park, a flowering garden, and a Temple of Venus thronged with allegorical figures of desire, before delivering him to Dame Nature's hillside court. There, on Saint Valentine's Day, every bird in creation has assembled by rank to choose a mate; the poem's real business is the case of a matchless formel eagle, courted in high courtly style by three tercel eagles, whose protracted, unresolvable pleading is interrupted by the impatient, lower-ranked birds — whose parody of legal deliberation is the poem's comic engine. Nature ultimately declines to adjudicate the tercels' rival claims of merit and instead cedes the decision to the formel herself, who asks for, and receives, a year's deferral before choosing freely. The dream ends with a French-refrained roundel and the dreamer waking to return, unsatisfied, to his books.

Themes

Books as the ground of experience, not its substitute. The poem opens with the famous paradox — "The life so short, the craft so long to learn" — of a narrator who knows Love only through report ("For all be that I know not Love indeed"), and who insists that "out of olde bookes... cometh all this new science that men lear." The entire vision is explicitly caused by reading: Scipio's dream, transmitted through Cicero and Macrobius, generates the narrator's own dream of Africanus, layering textual authority inside textual authority before the poem ever reaches its ostensible subject, the mating of birds.

The twin gates as a moral fork with no clear determinant. The garden's entrance bears two inscriptions — one promising "the blissful place / Of hearte's heal," the other warning of "the mortal strokes of the spear" — closely paralleling (per the poem's own commentary tradition) Dante's gate of Hell. The dreamer is paralyzed between them "[r]ight as betwixten adamantes two," unable to choose, until Africanus simply shoves him through. Love's promise and its danger are presented as structurally inseparable, decidable only from outside the dreamer's own judgment.

Estate and hierarchy as the organizing principle of the assembly. Nature, "the vicar of th'Almighty Lord," seats the birds by rank — birds of prey highest, then seed-fowl, with waterfowl "lowest in the dale" — before any debate begins, and rules that the "royal tercel," as "the wise and worthy... true as steel," must speak first by right of degree. The poem stages courtship as a legibly ranked social order laid over a supposedly natural mating instinct — an assembly whose form (precedence, procedure, the presiding judge) is closer to a court of law than to birdsong. Insofar as the text renders desire itself as something to be classified, seated, and processed through station before it can be voiced, the scene brushes against the ordering impulse that WMP names legibility-as-machine-core — though this is a medieval estates-satire logic, not the bureaucratic apparatus of the modernity-machine proper, and the resonance should not be overread.

Courtly rhetoric versus comic demotic irruption. The three tercels plead in the high style of fin'amor — "Do what her list, to do me live or sterve," "For in my heart is carven ev'ry vein" — declaring themselves ready to die or be torn apart by the other birds should they prove false. This register is deliberately punctured by the lower birds' impatience and mockery: the goose's brisk "let him love another," the duck's "Who can a reason find, or wit, in that?," and the tercelet's furious class-inflected rebuke — "Out of the dunghill came that word aright" — followed by the cuckoo's and merlin's exchange of insults over parenting and gluttony. The poem's comedy lives in this collision of registers: elevated love-longing judged, and deflated, by birds with no patience for it.

Deferred, self-authorizing female choice. Despite the elaborate ranking of the tercels' worth, Nature ultimately refuses to decide among them — "it may not here discussed be / Who loves her best" — and instead rules that the formel "herself shall have her election... Him that she chooseth, he shall her have as swith." The formel, in turn, refuses immediate choice altogether, asking Nature for a year's respite "to advise me" and declaring flatly that she will not yet "serve Venus, nor Cupide... by no manner way." The poem's resolution is a non-resolution: hierarchy determines who speaks first, but not who is chosen, and the chosen party postpones the choice entirely.

Notable passages

The opening proem sets Love as an authority known only secondhand, through books, framing everything that follows as an act of reading refracted through dream. The twin-gated garden entrance stages an undecidable moral fork the dreamer cannot resolve on his own judgment. The lead tercel's declaration to the formel is the poem's fullest specimen of courtly-love rhetoric, offered with total self-abnegation ("Do what her list, to do me live or sterve"). The squabble among tercelet, duck, cuckoo, and merlin punctures that rhetoric with class-inflected mockery, turning the high court of love into a barnyard argument. Finally, Nature's ruling and the formel's request for a year's delay resolve the case not by settling merit but by relocating the decision entirely into the beloved's own, deferred will.
