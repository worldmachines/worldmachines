The Reeve's Prologue and Tale: Age, Grudge, and the Miller Beguiled

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
Stung by the Miller's tale of a cuckolded carpenter, the aging Reeve Oswald delivers a bitter prologue on desire outliving the body, then tells a fabliau in which two Cambridge clerks avenge a thieving miller's grain-fraud by sleeping with his wife and daughter in one chaotic, dark-room night, leaving him beaten, cuckolded, and still out his stolen meal.

This unit contains the Prologue to the Reeve's Tale and the tale itself. Osewold the Reeve, a former carpenter, is the only pilgrim who takes offense at the Miller's preceding story of a cuckolded carpenter; he answers first with a long, sour meditation on old age — the body's decline paired with the persistence of appetite and grudge — and then with a fabliau expressly designed to "quite" (get even with) the Miller. At Trompington near Cambridge, the proud, thieving miller Simkin habitually cheats the Soler Hall college of its grain. Two headstrong young clerks, John and Alein, come to watch their own corn milled so he cannot rob them, but Simkin frees their horse, steals extra meal while they chase it, and puts them up for the night in his own chamber alongside his wife, infant, and marriageable daughter. In the dark, Alein creeps in with the daughter, and John — moving the baby's cradle to disorient the wife — ends up in her bed. A fight breaks out when Alein boasts of his conquest to the man he mistakes for John but is actually the miller; in the darkness and confusion the miller's own wife strikes her husband on the head with a staff meant for a clerk. The clerks escape with their grain and a cake baked from the stolen flour, and the tale closes with the flat moral that a cheat gets cheated in turn.

Themes

Age as decline of capacity, not of will. The Reeve's prologue is less throat-clearing than the tale's real argument in miniature: the body rots ("Till we be rotten, can we not be ripe") while desire persists undiminished, comically and grimly, past the point of use — "For though our might be gone, / Our will desireth folly ever-in-one." The image of the leek (white root, green top) and the four "gledes" of age (boasting, lying, anger, covetousness) frame old age not as peace but as appetite stranded without a body to enact it. This is a striking literary anticipation of Conatus — Spinoza's striving to persist and expand even as the material substrate that would let it act falls away; the Reeve gives that abstraction a face and a grudge.

"Quiting" as the tale-telling logic of the frame narrative. The Reeve is explicit that his story is retaliation, not entertainment for its own sake: "by your leave, I shall him quite anon," and he ends the tale itself declaring "Thus have I quit the Miller in my tale." The mote-and-beam accusation ("He can well in mine eye see a stalk, / But in his own he cannot see a balk") turns the frame's craft-rivalry (carpenter vs. miller) into the engine of the plot itself — the tale a story is allowed to tell is shaped by what tale was told before it.

Craft rivalry and social satire. Simkin the miller is drawn as an armed, blustering bully — dagger, sword, poniard, whittle-knife — whose trade gives him license to steal with impunity from the Cambridge scholars who are nominally his social betters. The clerks' Northern dialect and youthful overconfidence ("hardily they durste lay their neck") set up their humiliation as much as the miller's own outrage sets up his. The tale's satire cuts in both directions: at rustic criminality dressed up in a proud wife and pretensions to "blood," and at clerkly overconfidence undone by simple trickery.

Fabliau mechanics: darkness, mistaken identity, poetic symmetry. The plot's engine is the cradle moved to the wrong bed, a night too dark to see faces, and a wife who "knew the estres better than this John" yet still strikes the wrong skull. The tale's closing proverb — "A guiler shall himself beguiled be" — states the fabliau's iron law plainly: the miller who tricked others with false weights is himself tricked in the dark, doubly (wife and daughter both), and paid back exactly in the coin of deception he dealt out.

Notable passages

The prologue's core turn is the passage from "We olde men, I dread, so fare we" through "Yet in our ashes cold does fire reek" — the clearest statement of will surviving the body's collapse, and the emotional key to why an old man tells a young man's bawdy revenge story. Immediately after, the Reeve states his motive outright ("This drunken miller hath y-told us here / How that beguiled was a carpentere... I shall him quite anon") through the mote-and-beam line, making the tale's retaliatory logic explicit before it even begins.

Simkin's introduction ("A miller was there dwelling many a day" through "His name was hoten deinous Simekin") establishes the armed, brawling thief whose comeuppance the tale exists to deliver — proud, well-armed, canny enough to out-argue a warden but undone by two clerks he underestimates. The night's central mechanism turns on John quietly relocating the cradle ("And up he rose, and softely he went / Unto the cradle" through the wife creeping "fair and well... in by the clerk"), the single small action that converts a bawdy escapade into a full farce of mistaken beds. The fight scene — from the wife's terrified "Awake, Simon, the fiend is on me fall" to her own blow landing "on the pilled skull" of her husband — delivers the physical comedy of blind, panicked misrecognition that the whole plot has been building toward. The tale's coda ("Thus is the proude miller well y-beat" through "A guiler shall himself beguiled be") states the fabliau's moral flatly, closing the retaliatory loop the Reeve announced in his prologue.
