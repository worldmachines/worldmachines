The Man of Law's Prologue and Tale: Constance Adrift

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
In his Prologue the Man of Law debates the theft of time, the miseries of poverty, and his own literary debts before telling of Constance, the Roman emperor's daughter, who is twice married into foreign courts and twice set adrift on a rudderless ship by the schemes of two murderous, order-defending mothers-in-law, her serial survival offered throughout as proof that Christian providence, not the stars or Fortune, governs human affairs.

The Host, having calculated the hour from the sun's angle and the length of shadows, chides the pilgrims for wasting time and calls on the Man of Law to deliver his promised tale. The Man of Law agrees, but first surveys Chaucer's own catalogue of works about faithful and wronged women, pointedly refusing to retell the incestuous stories of Canace and Apollonius of Tyre (a jab widely read as aimed at Chaucer's contemporary Gower, who had told both). The Tale proper opens with a long moralizing meditation on the shame of poverty, then narrates the legend of Constance: married off to the Sultan of Syria after he converts for her sake, she alone survives a massacre engineered by her Saracen mother-in-law and is cast to sea in a rudderless ship; she washes up in Northumberland, converts her hosts, is falsely accused of murder by a spurned suitor and vindicated by a disembodied voice and instant divine judgement; she marries King Alla, only to be exiled again by a forged letter scheme run by his mother Donegild; she drifts for years, is rescued (unknowingly) by her own Roman kinsman, and is eventually reunited with husband, father, and — after Alla's early death — lives out her life piously in Rome.

Themes

Time as a measured, legible resource. The Prologue opens not with poetry but with calculation: the Host reads the hour off the sun's degree of elevation and the ratio of a shadow's length to the body that casts it, arriving at a precise "ten of the clock." This is a small, vivid instance of the pre-mechanical drive to render time quantifiable and actionable — the Host then moralizes that lost time, unlike lost goods, never returns. Written during exactly the Dawn window of the modernity-machine (1200–1600), the passage is a miniature of legibility-as-machine-core: measurement in service of discipline, centuries before clocks and timetables made it infrastructural.

Editorial curation as a moral act. The Man of Law's refusal to retell the stories of Canace (incest) and Apollonius (rape) — "Of all such cursed stories I say, Fy" — is presented as deliberate, not accidental ("of full avisement"). The passage doubles as literary criticism (an implicit rebuke of Gower) and as a statement that a teller's curation of material carries ethical weight; what is excluded from the canon matters as much as what is included.

Poverty, then providence, as the tale's twin frames. The Tale is bracketed by borrowed moralizing (from Pope Innocent's De Contemptu Mundi) on the humiliations of indigence, before pivoting to a heroine whose entire arc is a proof-text for providence overriding both material want and cosmic determinism. The narrator repeatedly interrupts the narrative to ask rhetorically who preserved Daniel, Jonah, and Mary of Egypt, answering each time: no one but Christ. Constance's rudderless drifting becomes an argument by accumulation — natural law and human malice are answered by miracle.

Old orders killing rather than yielding. Two structurally identical antagonists — the Sultaness and Donegild — destroy the marriages that would supplant their authority rather than negotiate a transition. The Sultaness vows "Life shall rather out of my body start, / Than Mahomet's law go out of mine heart" and orchestrates a massacre to preserve the old faith against her son's conversion; Donegild forges letters rather than accept a foreign daughter-in-law. Both stage bloody, morbid resistance to a change already underway rather than accommodate it — a pattern that lightly echoes the gramsci-gap: the old legitimacy lashing out because it cannot yet accept its own supersession.

Astrology interrogated, not simply invoked. The narrator's apostrophe to the "firste moving cruel Firmament" blames the stars — Mars's position, the moon's unhappy course — for dooming the Sultan's marriage, then pivots to blame human failure to read them: "Was there no philosopher in all thy town? / Is no time bet than other in such case?" Astrology here functions as a rival legibility system to providence, one the poem treats as real but insufficiently heeded — a system for reading fate that the emperor's court failed to consult.

Women as diplomatic currency and endurance as heroism. Constance is twice exchanged in dynastic marriages she never chooses, addressed directly by the narrator as a case study in gendered powerlessness ("Women are born to thraldom and penance"). Her heroism is entirely passive-endurative: patience, prayer, and silence in the face of exile, slander, and attempted rape, with agency reserved for Christ, Mary, and providence rather than for her.

Notable passages

The Host's opening tirade on time ("Lordings... Lose no time, as farforth as ye may... Let us not moulde thus in idleness") sets the frame-narrative's urgency and its Senecan source. The Man of Law's catalogue-refusal of Canace and Apollonius stages his curatorial ethics and the Gower subtext. The poverty exordium ("O scatheful harm, condition of poverty") supplies the Tale's Boethian/Innocentian moral ballast before the narrative proper begins. The Sultaness's council speech is the clearest statement of an old order's violent self-preservation. The astrological apostrophe to the Firmament stages the poem's determinism-versus-providence argument at its most explicit. The Daniel/Jonah/Mary-of-Egypt catalogue, delivered while Constance drifts unprovisioned at sea, is the tale's most concentrated statement of its providential thesis.
