The Cook's Prologue and the Unfinished Tale of Perkin Revellour

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
The Cook, Roger of Ware, banters with the Host and Reeve before telling of Perkin Revellour, a dancing, dice-mad London apprentice whose riotous living finally gets him dismissed and packed off to live with a comrade and his prostitute wife — at which point the tale, notoriously, breaks off unfinished.

This unit contains the Prologue to the Cook's Tale and the tale itself, which Chaucer left as a fragment of some sixty lines. Roger the Cook, delighted by the Reeve's story of the miller outwitted, volunteers "a little jape" of his own about city life; the Host needles him good-naturedly about his own shady trade (stale pasties, flies in the shop) before granting him leave to speak. Roger then begins the story of Perkin Revellour, a victualler's apprentice more devoted to dancing, dicing, and love-making than to his master's till, whose incorrigible "riot" eventually costs him his apprenticeship — and the text stops mid-sentence as he is shipped off to lodge with a fellow reveller whose wife works as a prostitute "for countenance."

Themes

Comic frame as social mirror. The Prologue continues the pilgrimage's running game of tales answering tales — the Reeve's story of a miller humiliated prompts the Cook's gleeful endorsement ("I pray to God to give me sorrow and care / If ever... / Heard I a miller better set a-work") — but the Host immediately turns the joke back on Roger himself, cataloguing his own tricks of the trade (recycled pies, parsley-dressed stubble-goose, flies loose in the shop). The exchange stages the same leveling irony that runs through the whole Canterbury Tales: no teller is exempt from the mockery he deals out, and "sooth play [is] quad play" — true jests cut both ways, as Roger himself concedes with some unease ("Be thou not wroth, else we departe here").

Apprenticeship as a legible, breakable contract. Perkin's story is built entirely around the formal apparatus of urban guild economy — the shop, the master's "chaffare" (merchandise/accounts), the apprentice's "paper" (his certificate of completed service), and the final "quittance" that dissolves the bond. This is a small, concrete instance of the kind of institutional legibility world-machines describes as characteristic of ordered, recordable social relations: a life's labor made visible and revocable through documents and ledgers. Perkin's riotous appetite for dance, dice, and "paramour" is precisely what escapes and corrodes that ledger ("That found his master well in his chaffare, / For oftentime he found his box full bare") — an ungovernable surplus of appetite that the disciplined shop-economy cannot metabolize, faintly resonant with the untimely as a fugitive vitality that clock- and contract-bound labor cannot fully capture.

The moralized proverb as narrative hinge. The master's decision to dismiss Perkin is framed not as personal grievance but as proverbial wisdom — "Better is rotten apple out of hoard, / Than that it should rot all the remenant" — casting the apprentice's expulsion as a matter of institutional self-preservation rather than individual punishment. Roger narrates his protagonist's ruin with evident affection even as the moral machinery condemns him; "jolly" is repeated even as Perkin is undone, and the tale's sympathies remain oddly warm toward the delinquent.

Unfinishedness as textual event. The manuscript tradition itself makes the fragment meaningful: the tale halts abruptly after Perkin is packed off to a comrade whose wife "held for countenance / A shop, and swived for her sustenance" — the material getting frankly sexual and sordid just as the text cuts out. The transcriber's note reports that Chaucer is thought to have abandoned the tale for its indecency, the same impulse behind his deathbed regret for having written so much "ribaldry." The absence is not scribal accident but authorial retreat, a rare visible instance of self-censorship shaping a canonical text's survival.

Notable passages

The Cook's boisterous approval of the Reeve's tale, and his eagerness to tell "a little jape that fell in our city," sets the comic register before the Host turns the joke back on him with a inventory of his own shop's dubious wares — pasties "twice hot and twice cold," parsley-dressed stubble-goose, flies loose among the food. Roger's uneasy acceptance of the joke ("sooth play quad play") frames the whole exchange as a reminder that mockery on this pilgrimage always circles back on the teller. The tale proper introduces Perkin Revellour in a burst of physical, appetitive description — "Galliard he was, as goldfinch in the shaw," full of "love and paramour... as is the honeycomb of honey sweet" — establishing him as a figure of pure appetite before the narrative pivots to his master's proverb-backed decision to cast him out, "with sorrow and mischance," the last coherent action before the fragment breaks off.
