The Prologue to the Legend of Good Women

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
In a May dream-vision, Chaucer is arraigned by the God of Love for writing Troilus and Criseyde and translating the Romance of the Rose as slanders against women, and is pardoned only through the intercession of Alcestis, who imposes as penance the writing of a legendary of good women.

This is the 579-line dream-vision prologue that Chaucer prefixed to his (unfinished) collection of classical and legendary heroines. It opens with a meditation on why readers should trust old books they cannot verify by experience, moves into the narrator's devotional cult of the daisy, and then stages a courtroom scene: the God of Love, arriving with a train of nineteen faithful women, accuses the dreamer of heresy against Love for having written Troilus and Criseyde and translated the Romance of the Rose — works that taught men to distrust women. The queen at Love's side, Alcestis, intervenes with an extended plea for due process and royal mercy, catalogues the dreamer's other (more pious) works in his defense, and wins his pardon. Love then assigns the penance that gives the whole project its reason for being: the dreamer must spend the rest of his career writing a Legend of Good Women, beginning with Cleopatra, and must present the finished book to the queen (Anne of Bohemia) at Eltham or Sheen. Only at the very end does the dreamer learn that his advocate is Alcestis herself, the wife who died in her husband's place and was rescued from hell by Hercules — and that she, transformed into the daisy, is the flower he has been worshipping all along.

Themes

The authority of old books versus the authority of the eye. The poem opens not with the dream but with an epistemological defense of reading: no one now living has been to heaven or hell, yet "God forbid but that men should believe / Well more thing than men have seen with eye." Since eyewitness proof is unavailable for most of what matters — "holiness," "regnes," "victories," "love," "hate" — books become the sole surviving "key" to memory. This is a deliberately loaded opening for a poem about to put the narrator's own books on trial: the same textual authority that justifies belief in old auctores is what indicts him for having faithfully translated one of them (the Rose) and rendered another (Troilus) too persuasively.

The daisy cult as a private, almost devotional, love-service. Long before the God of Love appears, the narrator describes a near-liturgical daily observance of the daisy — rising before dawn to watch it open, mourning when it closes at dusk, declaring himself its servant "as I have wit or might." This section imitates and gently parodies the French marguerite tradition (the Flower/Leaf courtly game), but its sincerity of address ("Be ye my guide, and lady sovereign") sets up the poem's late twist: the flower is Alcestis, so the narrator's private devotion has unknowingly been service to the very advocate who will save him.

Literary self-defense as courtroom drama. The God of Love's indictment is a remarkably direct piece of authorial self-consciousness: Chaucer stages himself being charged, by name-adjacent accusation, with the real critical complaint against his own corpus — that Troilus and Criseyde "maketh men to women less to trust" and that translating the Rose is "an heresy against my law." Alcestis's defense is a virtuosic bibliography-as-alibi, listing the House of Fame, the Death of Blanche the Duchess, the Parliament of Fowls, the Love of Palamon and Arcite (the proto-Knight's-Tale), the translation of Boethius, and the Life of Saint Cecile — effectively Chaucer curating his own canon within the fiction.

Alcestis's mirror-for-princes argument. The core of her plea is not sentimental but political: a god or king who condemns "without answer of word," who acts as the tyrants of Lombardy act, forfeits the distinction between rightful lordship and tyranny. She invokes due process ("it is no mastery for a lord / To damn a man, without answer of word"), the lion's disdain for retaliating against a fly, and the maxim that a true king must protect "poor and rich" alike since his lieges are "his treasure, and his gold in coffer." This is a fully worked-out mini-treatise on legitimate authority embedded inside a love-poem, and it does the actual work of winning the pardon — Love yields to Alcestis's argument about the nature of rule, not merely to her charm.

The penance as the poem's own origin myth. The prologue ends by generating the text it introduces: Love's sentence — "Thou shalt, while that thou livest, year by year, / The moste partie of thy time spend / In making of a glorious Legend / Of Goode Women" — is simultaneously narrative resolution and literal commission, tied to a real dedicatee (Queen Anne) and real palaces (Eltham, Sheen). The fiction of judgment collapses into an actual literary assignment, and the ballad naming displaced heroines (Cleopatra, Dido, Thisbe, Phyllis, Hypsipyle, Ariadne, Hypermnestra) previews the very legends the collection will go on to tell.

Notable passages

The opening defense of "olde bookes" as the only surviving vessel of things unwitnessed frames everything that follows — the dreamer will shortly be judged by the standard of textual fidelity he here defends. The daisy-devotion passage supplies the poem's emotional register before the trial turns adversarial, and its confession of insufficiency ("Alas! that I had English, rhyme or prose, / Sufficient this flow'r to praise aright!") is echoed structurally by Love's later complaint that the dreamer "cannot well endite." The ballad "Hide, Absolon, thy gilte tresses clear" stages a roll call of legendary figures (Esther, Penelope, Cleopatra, Thisbe, Hypsipyle) each displaced by "my lady" — a preview of the collection's cast presented as a court compliment before it becomes a work assignment. The God of Love's direct accusation — "art thereto nothing able," "my foe," guilty of "heresy against my law" — is the poem's central indictment scene, naming Troilus and Criseyde and the Rose translation as the offenses. Alcestis's mirror-for-princes speech, running from the claim that a natural lord must treat his lieges as treasure through the image of the lion who disdains to "wreak him on a fly," is the poem's most substantial piece of political reasoning and the actual engine of the pardon. Finally, Love's sentence — commissioning "a glorious Legend / Of Goode Women" and instructing that it be delivered to the queen "at Eltham, or at Sheen" — is the hinge where fiction becomes commission, giving the whole prologue its retrospective purpose.
