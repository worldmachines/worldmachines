The Clerk's Tale: Griselda's Patience and Chaucer's Ironic Envoy

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
The Clerk retells Petrarch's Latin version of the Griselda story (via Boccaccio): a young marquis marries a peasant girl on condition of her total, unquestioning obedience, subjects her for years to escalating staged cruelties — the pretended murder of both her children, a faked papal annulment, and public repudiation — before revealing it was all a test of her constancy, only for Chaucer's own closing envoy to mockingly reverse the tale's ostensible moral by urging wives never to imitate her.

Asked by the Host to leave off scholarly "terms" and "figures" and tell something plain and merry, the Clerk of Oxford offers a tale he says he learned from Petrarch himself: the story of Walter, Marquis of Saluzzo, and Griselda, the peasant girl he marries, torments, and ultimately vindicates. Walter is a young lord who lives entirely for the pleasure of the present — hawking, hunting — and refuses to marry, until his anxious people, fearing a succession crisis, beg him to take a wife. He agrees, but only on his own terms: he will choose in secret, and his people must swear in advance never to question his choice and to honor whomever he selects "as she an emperore's daughter were." He picks Griselda, the poorest man's daughter in a nearby hamlet, extracts from her — before the wedding — an oath of absolute, unconditional obedience, and marries her. Over the following years, having found her flawlessly loyal, Walter is seized by an unmotivated compulsion to "tempt" her further: he has their infant daughter and later their infant son secretly taken away (letting her believe they are to be killed), and finally stages a forged papal annulment and a fake remarriage, forcing Griselda to surrender her clothes and return to her father's cottage in her shift. Only at the moment of the counterfeit second wedding does Walter reveal that the "bride" is their own grown daughter and the boy her brother, restoring Griselda to her place. The Clerk closes with Petrarch's stated moral — not that wives should copy Griselda's humility ("it were importable though they would") but that all people should be as constant under whatever adversity God sends as she was under Walter's — before Chaucer appends his own comic "L'Envoy," which buries Griselda's patience with her and instructs wives to do the opposite of everything she did.

Themes

Absolute power dressed as private virtue. Walter's authority over Griselda is domestic in form (a husband's claim on a wife's obedience) but exercised with the arbitrariness of sovereign rule: he tests, conceals, forges documents, and stages atrocities purely to satisfy his own curiosity about the limits of her submission, all while his subjects — and eventually Griselda herself — take the fictions at face value. The tale repeatedly notes that Walter knows the tests are needless ("He had assayed her anough before... what needed it / Her for to tempt, and always more and more?") and is condemned in the narrator's own aside ("I say that evil it sit / T'assay a wife when that it is no need"), which keeps the poem from simply endorsing what it depicts.

Obedience as a pre-negotiated, unconditional contract. Before the marriage even occurs, Walter extracts Griselda's oath in front of her father — she must never say "Nay" to his "Yea," in word, deed, or "frowning countenance" — and she assents without knowing what it will cost. The whole tale operates as a demonstration of what happens when that contract is executed literally and completely: Griselda experiences the loss of her children and her status without ever retracting the terms she agreed to in ignorance.

The people as a fickle chorus. The commons who once begged Walter to marry, and who later murmur against Griselda's low birth and cheer his cruelty as prudent statecraft, are rebuked directly by the narrator as "stormy people, unsad and ev'r untrue... like the moon so waxe ye and wane" — an aside that reads as a small, self-contained meditation on the volatility of popular legitimacy, propped up by rumor rather than judgment.

Chaucer's irony against his own moral. The Clerk explicitly says the story is not meant as a template for wives ("it were importable though they would") but as an allegory of patient endurance under God's testing, addressed to "every wight." Chaucer then appends an envoy in a completely different register — brassy, comic, addressed to "archiwives" — that inverts the lesson point for point, telling wives to be "eager as a tiger," to answer "at the countertail," and never to let "humility" nail their tongues. The gap between the sanctioned allegorical reading and the poem's own closing voice is the tale's most interesting move: it refuses to let the model of Griselda stand unqualified even as it finishes solemnly praising her.

Notable passages

The Host's prologue demand — plain speech, no preaching, no falling asleep — frames the tale as popular entertainment even though its source is explicitly high literary (Petrarch, "the laureate poet"), a tension between register and content that recurs in the envoy. Walter's introduction ("I blame him thus, that he consider'd not / In time coming what might him betide") establishes the flaw — living wholly in the present, refusing succession — that both motivates the marriage and licenses his subsequent testing of the wife his people forced him to take.

The oath scene ("I say this, be ye ready with good heart / To all my lust... And never ye to grudge, night nor day") is the tale's load-bearing contract: Griselda's total, advance consent to whatever Walter wills, sworn before she can know what it entails, and never revoked even when it costs her both children and her marriage.

Griselda's farewell to her infant daughter, believing the sergeant has come to kill the child ("Farewell, my child, I shall thee never see... For this night shalt thou dien for my sake"), is the tale's emotional center, patience performed at the moment of maximal, deliberately inflicted loss, without protest or resistance.

Her speech at the public stripping, restoring her wedding ring and clothes and asking only for a smock to cover her body ("Naked out of my father's house... I came, and naked I must turn again"), explicitly echoes Job's language of bare arrival and departure, casting Walter's domestic tyranny in the register of divine trial — the frame the Clerk's closing moral then makes literal.

The envoy ("Griseld' is dead, and eke her patience... Let no humility your tongues nail") is where Chaucer's own voice breaks from the Clerk's solemn allegory into open comedy, instructing wives to seize "the governail" rather than emulate the model just spent four thousand lines establishing — an ironic coda that keeps the tale from resolving into a simple endorsement of suffering obedience. Read against capture-resistance and Conatus, Griselda's story is a portrait of a striving self almost perfectly captured by an arbitrary sovereign will, with her endurance legible as virtue only within Walter's frame; the envoy's refusal to let that frame stand as the final word — a comic surplus the "official" moral cannot fully govern — has a family resemblance to Akomolafe's untimely, the residue that escapes disciplinary closure even after the authorized reading has been delivered.
