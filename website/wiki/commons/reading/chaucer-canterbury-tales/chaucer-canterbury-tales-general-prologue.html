The General Prologue to The Canterbury Tales

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
Chaucer's General Prologue frames a spring pilgrimage to Canterbury as the occasion for a storytelling contest, then catalogues the roughly thirty pilgrims — knight, squire, prioress, monk, friar, merchant, clerk, lawyer, franklin, guildsmen, cook, shipman, doctor, wife of Bath, parson, ploughman, miller, manciple, reeve, summoner, and pardoner — as a cross-section of English estates ranging from idealized virtue to open venality.

The unit is the actual General Prologue: April's renewal stirs "longe folk" to pilgrimage, the narrator falls in with twenty-nine other pilgrims at the Tabard Inn in Southwark, and he resolves to describe each of them by "condition," "array," and "degree" before the journey begins. What follows is a portrait gallery — one of the great taxonomies of medieval English society — followed by the Host's proposal that each pilgrim tell two tales each way to Canterbury, with a free supper as prize, which becomes the structural device for the entire Tales. The unit ends with the company agreeing to the game and the Knight drawing the lot to go first.

Themes

Spring, compulsion, and pilgrimage as a natural drive. The Prologue opens not with piety but with a naturalistic account of April: rain penetrating March's drought, sap rising, small birds singing all night "so pricketh them nature in their corages." Human pilgrimage is presented as continuous with this seasonal stirring — desire dressed as devotion, itself a small satirical move that colors everything after.

Estates satire as social taxonomy. The Prologue systematically works down a social hierarchy — nobility (Knight, Squire), gentry service (Yeoman), regular and secular clergy (Prioress, Monk, Friar, Parson), merchant and professional classes (Merchant, Clerk, Sergeant of Law, Franklin, Doctor), guild tradesmen, laborers (Ploughman, Miller, Reeve), and ecclesiastical functionaries (Summoner, Pardoner) — giving each a stock of concrete, often materially specific detail (clothing, tools, food, physical tics) that individualizes the type without abandoning its representative function. This is the poem's engine: not moralizing from outside but letting each pilgrim's own praised attributes indict him.

Idealized figures bracket the satire. The Knight ("a very perfect gentle knight"), the Parson, and the Ploughman are presented with unqualified admiration — a working feudal-Christian ideal of chivalry, pastoral care, and honest labor performed "withouten hire." Their sincerity throws into relief the self-interest of the clerics between them.

Institutional decay among the clergy. The Monk, Friar, and Pardoner form an escalating sequence of religious offices whose original function has been hollowed into performance and extraction. The Monk openly discards the monastic rule ("This ilke text held he not worth an oyster") in favor of hunting and fine dining. The Friar monetizes confession and absolution, judging penitents' sincerity by their willingness to pay ("Full sweetely heard he confession... There as he wist to have a good pittance"). The Pardoner peddles fabricated relics — pig bones as saints' bones, a pillowcase as Our Lady's veil — to extract more money from a poor parson's parish than the parson himself collects in months. This is close to the World Machines decay-sequence: a religious institution moving from Functional (genuine cure of souls) through Performative (Friar's theatrical absolution) to purely Ornamental and finally near-Absent function (Pardoner's relics are visibly fraudulent props, kept only because they still extract value), even as the outward forms — confession, blessing, relic, sermon — are maintained.

The frame device and narratorial modesty. The unit closes by installing the story-contest that structures the whole work: the Host's proposal that each pilgrim tell tales both ways to Canterbury, judged for "best sentence and most solace." The narrator's disclaimer — that he must "rehearse... every word" a speaker used, however "rudely," on pain of telling a false tale — is a proto-realist manifesto, an early defense of reported vulgarity as fidelity rather than indecorum.

Notable passages

The opening (April showers, Zephyrus, the sleepless small birds, "specially, from every shire's end... to Canterbury they wend") establishes pilgrimage as natural compulsion rather than pure piety, and sets the frame of a single storyteller collecting a company "by aventure y-fall in fellowship."

The Knight's portrait is the poem's unqualified positive pole: a career of crusading warfare across Christendom and Heatheness rendered not as bloodlust but as the sum of "chivalry, / Truth and honour, freedom and courtesy," closing on the flat verdict "He was a very perfect gentle knight" — a benchmark the later clerical portraits are implicitly measured against and found wanting.

The Monk passage stages a direct clash between institutional rule and worldly appetite: he dismisses Augustine's demand for labor and enclosure ("This ilke text held he not worth an oyster") to become "a prickasour aright," his greyhounds and fine sleeves signaling exactly the abandonment of vocation the rule was meant to prevent.

The Friar passage exposes confession-for-hire as a market transaction dressed as sacrament: absolution's "pleasantness" is calibrated to the penitent's payment, and the closing couplet — "Therefore instead of weeping and prayeres, / Men must give silver to the poore freres" — states the racket almost as doctrine.

The Pardoner's relic-fraud passage is the satire's hardest edge: pig bones sold as saintly remains, deployed specifically against "a poore parson dwelling upon lond," extracting in a day more than the parson earns in two months — corruption preying on legitimate, humble religion rather than merely coexisting with it.

The Host's proposal ("That each of you, to shorten with your way... shall tellen tales tway") is the structural hinge quote: it is the literal contract that generates the entire Canterbury Tales, turning a devotional journey into a competitive anthology and setting up every tale that follows as a move in that game.
