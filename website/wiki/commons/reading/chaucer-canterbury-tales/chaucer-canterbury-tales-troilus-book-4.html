Troilus and Criseyde, Book IV: The Exchange of Cressida and the Lovers' Parting

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
The Trojan parliament votes to trade Cressida for the captured Antenor over Hector's objection, driving Troilus to near-suicidal grief, prompting Pandarus's cynical counsel to simply love another and a long Boethian excursus on predestination versus free will, before the lovers meet by night, exchange desperate vows, and Cressida overrules Troilus's plan to flee together, promising instead to return within ten nights.

This unit covers Book IV of Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde: a Trojan skirmish leaves Antenor a Greek prisoner; Calchas begs the Greeks to trade him for his daughter Cressida; the Trojan parliament, over Hector's lone objection, grants the exchange. Troilus, present but silent, is undone — he rages alone in his chamber, is talked at (not comforted) by Pandarus, and sinks into a long philosophical brooding on whether his loss was fated or free before Pandarus arranges a final night together. Cressida faints, is nearly mistaken for dead, and the lovers spend the night trading vows, oaths, and competing plans — Troilus wants to elope; Cressida, more calculating, insists she can engineer her own return to Troy within days. The book ends on their anguished parting.

Themes

Institutional exchange versus individual love. The book opens with a bureaucratic transaction — a prisoner swap brokered through petition and parliamentary vote — that treats Cressida as fungible currency for Antenor. Hector's objection ("we use here no women for to sell") names the logic explicitly and is overruled by mob acclamation. The poem stages private conatus — Troilus's passionate, particular attachment — being processed and dissolved by a collective, transactional mechanism indifferent to it; the tale's tragedy is seeded here, in an act of impersonal exchange-logic that faintly echoes how systems of legibility subordinate the particular to the tradeable Conatus.

Grief as dissolution of self. Troilus's response is rendered in violent physical and natural imagery — leaves stripped from a winter tree, a wild bull pierced by a dart — figuring grief as something that unmakes personhood before it unmakes plans. His monologues call on Death and dismiss his own body as a "woeful nest" for a soul that has already fled toward Cressida.

Cynicism versus fidelity as competing counsels. Pandarus's advice — that women are interchangeable ("if one can sing, another can well dance"), that new love drives out old "by process of nature" — is presented as well-meant but hollow; Troilus's rebuttal insists that what he feels cannot be reasoned away and that betraying Cressida to save himself would make him "a fiend." The debate rehearses two irreconcilable ethics of love: pragmatic replaceability against singular devotion.

Predestination and free choice. Waiting alone in a temple, Troilus works through a full Boethian argument (echoing Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy, Book V) on whether divine foreknowledge makes all outcomes necessary, concluding he cannot resolve "whose opinion I may hold." The passage is notable as sustained scholastic argumentation dropped into a romance — Troilus's personal catastrophe becomes an occasion for interrogating whether any human choice, including Cressida's or the parliament's, was ever really open. It is a striking premonition of a tension WMP's own forecasting method tries to hold apart rather than resolve — the predetermined versus the genuinely uncertain — though here the poem leaves the binary unresolved rather than staked.

Oaths as dramatic irony. Cressida's night-time vows — swearing by Juno, Atropos, and the river Simois that she will never prove false — are elaborate and absolute, made poignant (for readers who know how the story ends) by their very extravagance. Her practical counter-proposal to Troilus's elopement plan — a stratagem to lure her father back to Troy within days — reveals her as the more calculating, forward-planning of the two lovers, in contrast to Troilus's fatalistic passivity.

Notable passages

The parliament scene (c1) stages the exchange as institutional violence dressed as procedure — Hector's refusal drowned out by a crowd that values Antenor's military utility over Cressida's person. Troilus's grief monologue (c2) is the emotional pivot, his address to his own "weary ghost" marking the point where love becomes indistinguishable from a wish for annihilation. Pandarus's consolation (c3) gives the poem's clearest statement of love as fungible and time-bound, a position the narrative itself treats skeptically. The predestination excursus (c4) is the unit's intellectual centerpiece, importing formal philosophical argument into the romance to frame Troilus's suffering as possibly inevitable. Cressida's oaths (c5) and the lovers' final exchange (c6) close the book on a note of vowed constancy shadowed by the reader's foreknowledge of its collapse — Cressida's brisk, practical "fare now well, for time is that ye rise" undercutting the absolutism of her earlier swearing.
