The Knight's Tale (Part One): Theseus, Fortune, and the Rivalry of Palamon and Arcite

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
Duke Theseus of Athens conquers Thebes to avenge grieving widows, takes the cousins Palamon and Arcite captive, and must arbitrate when their sworn brotherhood shatters into rivalry over his sister-in-law Emily, resolving the feud not with more bloodshed but with a decreed hundred-a-side tournament a year hence.

This unit covers the first half of the Knight's Tale: Duke Theseus of Athens, fresh from conquering the Amazons and marrying Hippolyta, is intercepted by a company of grieving widows whose husbands died besieging Thebes and whose bodies the tyrant Creon refuses burial. Theseus marches on Thebes, kills Creon, and among the dead finds two wounded Theban cousins of royal blood, Palamon and Arcite, whom he imprisons for life without ransom. Years into their captivity, both glimpse Emily, Hippolyta's sister, from their tower window and fall instantly and violently in love, which destroys the sworn brotherhood between them. Arcite is freed but banished on pain of death; Palamon remains imprisoned. Arcite, unable to bear separation from Emily, sneaks back into Athens disguised as a squire named Philostrate and rises in Theseus's household; Palamon eventually escapes and the two collide by chance in a grove, where Theseus discovers them dueling. Rather than execute them as their own confessions warrant, Theseus — moved by the pleading of the queen, Emily, and the court ladies — converts the private, lawless duel into a formal, ruled institution: a tournament a year hence, each cousin to arrive with a hundred knights, with Emily going to the victor.

Themes

Conquest tempered by clemency. Theseus enters the tale as pure chivalric power — "greater was there none under the sun" — but the widows' lament reframes conquest as an occasion for mercy rather than mere domination. His oath to avenge them, his restoration of the slain husbands' bodies for proper obsequies, and later his forgiveness of Palamon and Arcite's capital crimes all establish a governing pattern: might justified and completed by pity. The tale repeatedly stages violence being absorbed into ritual and judgment rather than left as raw force.

Fortune's wheel and impersonal Destiny. The widow invokes "Fortune, and her false wheel, / That none estate ensureth to be wele" to explain her fall from queen to captive. Later the narrator generalizes this into a full Boethian digression on "The Destiny, minister general" that executes God's foreordained purveyance, and into Arcite's drunk-man simile — "We wot never what thing we pray for here" — where human desire is systematically misaligned with human good. This is less psychology than cosmology: the lovers' sufferings are instances of a governing structure indifferent to individual will, a theme that gives the romance its philosophical undertow.

Love as affliction, not choice. Both cousins experience desire as a wound suffered through the eye into the heart, described in the vocabulary of sickness and madness rather than courtship — Arcite's decline is explicitly likened to melancholic mania rather than ordinary love-longing. Crucially, love here overrides even sworn brotherhood: Arcite's claim that "Love is a greater lawe, by my pan, / Than may be giv'n to any earthly man" and that "positive law, and such decree, / Is broke alway for love in each degree" sets an involuntary, almost natural-law drive above the deliberately constructed obligations of oath and kinship — legibly codified bonds (a sworn pact between blood-brothers) dissolved by an irrepressible force neither man consented to.

Formal disputation embedded in romance. The narrator halts the story to pose a scholastic question directly to the reader — "Who lieth the worse, Arcite or Palamon?" — weighing the freedom of exile against the sight of the beloved from prison. This move exposes the tale's kinship with academic debate poetry: the romance plot is also an occasion for symmetrical philosophical balancing, refusing to resolve the paradox itself and instead handing it to the audience.

Taming feud into legible institution. The climax of this half is not a killing but a jurisdictional act: finding the cousins fighting an unsanctioned duel "withoute judge or other officer," Theseus could execute both on their own confessions, but instead converts their unruled violence into a scheduled, witnessed, hundred-a-side tournament with a fixed date, place, and victor's prize. This is the tale's most striking institutional gesture — private vendetta re-cast as legible, ruled contest, chaos given form and a deadline. It resonates with world-machines' account of legibility-as-machine-core: sovereign power here does not abolish the feud's violence but renders it measurable, dated, and administratively contained.

Notable passages

The widow's plea (c1) sets the tale's moral register from the start: conquest is legitimated by the mercy it performs, and Fortune's instability is named outright as the condition of all worldly "estate." Palamon's confession that he was "hurt right now thorough mine eye" (c2) establishes love-at-first-sight as physical wound rather than decision, a pattern repeated more violently in Arcite. Arcite's claim that love outranks "positive law, and such decree" (c3) is the tale's clearest statement of desire as a force above constructed obligation, the very force that breaks the cousins' sworn brotherhood. The narrator's direct question to the audience (c4) turns the romance momentarily into disputation, refusing to adjudicate whose suffering is worse. The Destiny digression (c5) universalizes the widow's Fortune-talk into a full providential framework governing "our appetites here." Finally, Theseus's decree (c6) resolves the plot's central crisis by legislating the duel into a tournament — the tale's key move from lawless private violence to legible public contest.
