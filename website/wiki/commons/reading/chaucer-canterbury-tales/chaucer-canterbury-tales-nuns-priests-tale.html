Chanticleer and the Fox: The Nun's Priest's Tale

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
A dreaming rooster's fear of a fox becomes the occasion for a mock-heroic beast fable that mounts a full scholastic debate on dream-prophecy and free will only to let vanity and flattery, not philosophy, decide the plot.

After the Host cuts off the Monk's parade of tragedies as tedious "heaviness," he demands something merry instead, and turns to the Nun's Priest ("Sir John"), who obliges with the beast-fable of Chanticleer the rooster, his favorite hen Partelote, and the colfox who nearly eats him by flattery. The tale escalates from a widow's humble farmyard into an elaborate mock-heroic set piece — a dream, a marital debate on the meaning of dreams, a digression on predestination, a Troy-and-Carthage-scaled abduction, and a reversal engineered by the very rhetorical trick (flattery) that caused the disaster — before closing with an explicit instruction to "take the fruit, and let the chaff be still," and a bawdy epilogue from the Host.

Themes

Comedy is chosen against tragedy, deliberately. The Prologue is not throat-clearing; it sets genre terms. The Knight and Host both reject the Monk's tragic mode ("Such talking is not worth a butterfly, / For therein is there no sport nor game") and demand "gladsome" matter instead. Everything that follows — the mock-epic elevation, the comic reversal, the deflating epilogue — is the tale fulfilling that explicit generic contract, which makes its later brush with genuine tragic apparatus (Troy, Carthage, Rome) a kind of controlled parody of the very mode just banished.

Barnyard life is dressed in epic and courtly language. Chanticleer is not merely a rooster but a walking heraldic and horological marvel — his comb "embattell'd... as it were a castle wall," his crowing "sickerer... than a clock, or an abbay horloge." This mock-heroic inflation is the tale's chief comic engine, but it also does real work: by borrowing the register of the tragedies just rejected, the narrator can stage a genuine fall (abduction, near-death, communal lament rivaling the sack of Troy) without ever leaving comedy's frame.

The dream-debate restages a live scholastic dispute. Partelote offers a thoroughly naturalistic, humoral theory of dreams (excess choler and melancholy, cured with laxatives and herbs, backed by Cato) while Chanticleer answers with a battery of auctores — Cicero, Macrobius, Daniel, Joseph, Croesus, Andromache — arguing dreams are prophetic. Both positions are given real intellectual weight; the irony is that the dream is proven literally true and yet nothing about the outcome turns on which theory anyone believed.

The narrator raises the necessity/free-will crux and explicitly refuses to settle it. Mid-tale the narrator interrupts himself to invoke Augustine, Boethius, and Bradwardine on divine foreknowledge versus free choice — then declines: "I will not have to do of such mattere." The binary (predestination vs. free will) is held open, its charge unresolved, and handed back to the reader as unfinished business rather than as payload — a move worth flagging as a possible resonance with the untimely/idowu-function pattern of holding a tension without discharging it, though Chaucer's motive here is comic evasion as much as philosophical humility.

Flattery, not fate or philosophy, actually springs and then releases the trap. For all the erudite apparatus about dream-prophecy, what catches Chanticleer is vanity: the fox praises his singing and urges him to "counterfeit" his father with eyes shut, and "ravish'd with his flattery," he is seized mid-crow. The escape works by the identical mechanism reversed — Chanticleer flatters the fox into opening his mouth to boast. The tale's real subject is rhetoric's power over judgment, which quietly outranks the elaborate dream-theology as the operative cause of events.

The moral is offered, and is conspicuously smaller than the tale that earned it. "Take the fruit, and let the chaff be still" instructs the reader in exactly how much allegorizing to do, citing Paul's letter to Timothy as license — but the stated lesson (beware flattery, beware unwise counsel, hold your peace) is modest next to the tale's sprawling erudition, itself part of the joke about interpretive appetite. The Host's earthy, admiring epilogue about the priest's physique deflates any residual solemnity, returning the pilgrimage frame to comic register.

Notable passages

The Prologue exchange establishes the tale's comic mandate against the Monk's tragedies — the Host's blunt dismissal ("Such talking is not worth a butterfly") is the hinge the whole tale swings on. The mock-heroic portrait of Chanticleer (castle-wall comb, clock-precise crowing) shows the tale's central stylistic joke in miniature. Partelote's humoral dream-theory and Chanticleer's counter-marshaling of authorities stage the debate as a genuine scholastic set piece rather than a throwaway squabble. The predestination digression is the tale's most striking formal move: a real philosophical crux raised and then explicitly parked. The flattery-capture sequence is the plot's actual turning point, foregrounding rhetoric over fate or piety as the operative force. And the closing moral instruction ("take the fruit, and let the chaff be still") makes explicit the interpretive contract the fable has been running on throughout.
