The Merchant's Tale: The Walled Garden and the Counterfeited Key

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
In this fabliau, a cynical Merchant tells of old January, who marries young May, imprisons her behind a walled garden he alone can unlock, goes blind from jealousy's own working, and is out-argued by his wife (with Proserpina's coaching) when a counterfeited key lets Damian cuckold him in a pear tree in plain sight.

The Merchant, introduced by a bitter prologue about his own two-month-old marriage to "the worste that may be," tells of January, a sixty-year-old Lombard knight who resolves to marry a girl barely a third his age. What follows is an elaborate, deliberately overblown encomium to wedlock — equal parts Genesis, Solomon, Seneca, and secular exempla (Rebecca, Judith, Esther) — undercut at every turn by January's actual motives, which are appetite and property. His friends Placebo (a flatterer who agrees with everything) and Justinus (a married skeptic who has "wept many a tear" over his own wife) stage a mock counsel-scene that decides nothing, since January has already chosen. He marries May, a squire named Damian falls sick with love for her, and January — now literally as well as figuratively unable to see clearly — builds a walled garden to which he alone keeps the silver key, so he can "pay his wife's debt" in seclusion. He then goes blind, becomes pathologically jealous, and keeps a hand on May at all times. May makes a wax impression of the key, has Damian counterfeit it, and arranges an assignation in a pear tree — witnessed and adjudicated by the King and Queen of Faerie, Pluto and Proserpina, who are having their own marital argument about women's truthfulness. Pluto restores January's sight at the exact moment of the affair; Proserpina, on cue, supplies May with an answer that reframes what he saw as a misperception of newly restored sight. January believes her.

Themes

Marriage as property transaction dressed as sacrament. The tale's opening praise-of-wives passage ("A wife is Godde's gifte verily") explicitly ranks a wife alongside "handes, rentes, pasture" and other durable possessions that, unlike "giftes of fortune," won't "passen as a shadow on the wall." January wants an heir, a body, and total discretion over both; the theological language (Adam and Eve, "paradise terrestre") is a thin veneer over acquisition. Justinus's counter-speech, urging that a man "ought him right well advise... to whom I give my body" and that finding a wife's true qualities "asketh leisure to inquere," reframes marriage as due diligence on an opaque asset — the comic irony being that no amount of inquiry can make another person's interior life legible in advance.

The garden and the key as an engine of control. January's walled garden, to which he "would suffer no wight to bear the key, / Save he himself," is the tale's central image: an attempt to build a fully surveilled, single-key space around his wife's body, collapsing the difference between marriage and enclosure. This reads as a small, domestic instance of what World Machines calls the drive toward legibility-as-machine-core — the impulse to render a person governable by controlling access and visibility. The scheme fails precisely at its point of maximal confidence: blindness (literalizing his already-failed legibility) and a counterfeited key (May and Damian reproducing the very instrument of control to defeat it) together dramatize how a system built on a single point of surveillance is brittle to duplication it cannot detect — a fabliau-scale case of capture-resistance enacted by the ostensibly captured party.

Perception versus interpretation. The tale's real climax is not the tryst but the argument afterward. May's defense — that a man newly restored to sight cannot yet trust it, "Till that your sight establish'd be a while, / There may full many a sighte you beguile" — turns January's own recovered eyesight against the evidentiary weight of eyesight itself. Pluto and Proserpina's prior debate over Solomon's misogyny (Pluto citing scripture against women, Proserpina supplying the resourceful lie in advance: "though they be in any guilt y-take, / With face bold they shall themselves excuse") frames the entire ending as rigged: the interpretive frame is handed to May before the fact makes any difference. Legibility (January literally seeing) is defeated not by concealment but by contesting what seeing means — evidence without an agreed interpretive frame resolves nothing.

Continuity with the marriage debate. Justinus explicitly refers the company onward — "The Wife of Bath, if ye have understand, / Of marriage, which ye have now in hand, / Declared hath full well in little space" — tying this tale into the Canterbury Tales' running quarrel over sovereignty in marriage, seeding the Merchant's cynicism as one more data point (against Griselda's patience, invoked in his own prologue) in that larger argument.

Notable passages

The Merchant's prologue frames the whole tale as one aggrieved man's testimony, not neutral narration — his wife is worse than a fiend, and marriage is a "snare." The mock-panegyric to wives that follows must be read through that frame: praise weaponized as satire. Justinus's counsel scene punctures Placebo's sycophancy with genuinely practical (if self-pitying) advice about the unknowability of a prospective spouse. The garden-and-key passage is the tale's mechanical heart — control made concrete as architecture and a single physical key — and its undoing (the counterfeited key, the blindness, the pear tree) plays out precisely at that mechanism's weakest point. The Pluto–Proserpina scene, structurally a fairy-court echo of the human marriage below it, supplies the meta-commentary: the gods argue about whether women lie, and then rig the outcome so that the argument is settled experimentally, in May's favor, before January even opens his eyes. The closing exchange about newly-restored sight is the tale's cleverest sleight, converting the strongest possible evidence (eyewitness testimony) into the weakest (an untrained faculty), and leaving January — and the reader — with no stable ground from which to adjudicate what happened.
