The Flower and the Leaf

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
In a dream-vision spring allegory (likely a later imitation rather than Chaucer's own work), a sleepless gentlewoman watches two courtly companies — knights and ladies of the Leaf, who are scorched by sun and battered by storm yet endure, and knights and ladies of the Flower, whose beauty withers at the first heat and rain — and learns from a lady in white that the Leaf signifies enduring virtue and perseverance in honour, while the Flower signifies fleeting pleasure and idle beauty.

This unit opens with a substantial editorial headnote — reproducing commentary from Speght and from the editor Bell — before giving the poem itself entire. The headnote's most important disclosure, flagged by the transcriber, is that modern scholarship doubts Chaucer wrote this poem at all; it is now generally read as a later imitation working in his manner. The poem proper is a dream-vision: a sleepless gentlewoman rises before dawn, follows an overgrown path to a hidden arbour walled in by sycamore and eglantine, and from concealment watches two allegorical processions enter a meadow — a company "in white," crowned with laurel and agnus castus, and a company "in green," crowned with flowers, each attended by knights who joust, dance, and pay reverence to a tree or a tuft of flowers respectively. At noon a storm destroys the flowers and drenches both companies, but the Leaf's company, sheltered by the laurel, emerges unscathed and moves to relieve the suffering Flower's company. Afterward, a lady in white explains to the narrator (now addressed as "daughter") the emblematic scheme: the Leaf's followers are the perseverant — the Nine Worthies, the Knights of the Round Table, the Douze Pers, the Knights of the Garter — rewarded with laurel because laurel endures every season; the Flower's followers are those devoted to idleness and pleasure, rewarded with blossoms that cannot survive a single storm.

Themes

Durability versus fragility as the poem's entire moral architecture. Every image in the poem is organized around a single opposition worked out through natural symbol: the leaf (laurel, oak, woodbind) that withstands "frostes keen" against the flower that is "lost... within a little space." The poem does not moralize abstractly until the very end — it stages the destruction literally, letting the noonday sun and the ensuing storm do the argumentative work before the lady in white supplies the gloss. This is allegory built to be experienced by the reader before it is explained, which is part of why the editorial headnote feels compelled to supply Speght's blunt paraphrase up front.

Chivalric virtue coded as seasonal endurance, not martial glory alone. The lady in white's taxonomy of the Leaf's company — Worthies, Round Table knights, Douze Pers, Garter knights — makes clear that laurel here signifies persistence in honourable action ("without feintise or sloth, / From well to better in all manner thing") rather than any single triumph. The poem explicitly subordinates achievement to constancy: a knight's laurel is a reward for a durable disposition, structurally opposed to Flora's retinue, who "loved idleness, / And not delighted in no business."

Constancy in love mapped onto the same axis. The woodbind-crowned ladies are glossed as those "never... To love untrue in word, in thought, nor deed" who would rather have their hearts torn than "flit" — collapsing romantic fidelity and martial/moral perseverance into the same emblematic logic that organizes the whole poem. Virtue of every kind is rendered as weather-resistance.

The concealed observer as a structuring device. The narrator watches the entire pageant from inside a hedged arbour "as thick... as a castle wall," able to see out while remaining unseen — a small but suggestive image of an interior sheltered by opacity from a field of spectacle and contest, not unlike a The Thicket-style enclosure defended by its own terroir even as history's pageant marches past its border.

Notable passages

The headnote's own interpretive key — quoting Speght and then Bell on the unglossed emblems of the medlar-tree, goldfinch, and nightingale — front-loads the poem's meaning before a line of verse appears, which shapes how a reader receives everything that follows; it's included here because it is the passage that actually states the allegory's terms plainly, terms the poem itself withholds until its final movement.

The arbour description ("shapen... as is a pretty parlour," hedged "as thick... as a castle wall") establishes the narrator's vantage — hidden, safe, capable of seeing without being seen — before anything allegorical begins; this frame is what makes the whole pageant legible as pageant, watched rather than lived.

The storm sequence is the poem's dramatic center: the flowers scorch, the ladies in green are burnt, the wind fells every flower in the mead, and then hail and rain leave both companies drenched — while the laurel-sheltered company in white escapes unharmed and turns at once to relieve their suffering rivals. This is the allegory's thesis rendered as weather rather than argument.

The lady in white's direct exposition ("For knights should ever be persevering... Be put to no such occupation") is the poem's explicit moral payload, converting the pageant's imagery into a stated doctrine of endurance-as-virtue versus fragility-as-vice.

The closing envoi ("O little book! thou art so uncunning... How dar'st thou put thyself in press... for dread?") is a conventional modesty topos, but it is also the moment the poem gestures at its own uncertain authorship and reception — an irony sharpened by the fact that the poem itself is no longer confidently attributed to the poet under whose name it has travelled for centuries.
