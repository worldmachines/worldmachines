The Cuckoo and the Nightingale

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
A dreamer overhears a debate between a scornful Cuckoo and a devoted Nightingale over whether Love is a blessing or a curse, sides with the Nightingale by stoning the Cuckoo away, and the birds resolve to refer the quarrel to a parliament to be held under a maple at Woodstock the morrow after Saint Valentine's Day.

A first-person dreamer, sleepless with love-longing on the third night of May, wanders to a flowering lawn by a brook and slips into a doze — "not all asleep, nor fully waking" — in which he understands bird-speech. He hears the Cuckoo sing before the Nightingale (an evil omen), then overhears the two birds fall into open quarrel: the Cuckoo mocking Love as a tyrant that brings only sickness, madness, and ruin, the Nightingale insisting Love is the well-spring of every gentle virtue. The dreamer, unable to stay neutral, throws a stone that drives the Cuckoo off; the grateful Nightingale vows to be his singer all that May, warns him against the Cuckoo's "leasing" (falsehood), and then rallies all the birds of the dale to demand justice on the Cuckoo. Rather than settle the matter themselves, the birds agree to convene a formal parliament — the eagle presiding as lord, with peers "of record" — to be held the morrow after Saint Valentine's Day under a maple before Queen Philippa's window at Woodstock. The Nightingale sings once more and the dreamer wakes. An envoy ("The Author to His Book" and "L'Envoy to the Author's Lady") follows, in which the poet apologizes to his lady for the poem's supposed rudeness and unworthiness. A transcriber's note flags that modern scholars doubt Chaucer wrote this piece, and the editorial headnote frames it explicitly as a hinge text leading into "The Assembly of Fowls," since the appeal to a bird-parliament sets up the later, fuller parliamentary debate on love.

Themes

The debate form stages love as a genuinely two-sided question rather than settling it by fiat. The Cuckoo is not a straw man: her indictment of Love — "For love is in young folk but rage, / And in old folk a great dotage" — piles up a real catalogue of love's costs ("disease and heaviness, / Sorrow and care, and many a great sickness, / Despite, debate, anger, envy... Pride, mischief, povert', and woodness"). The Nightingale answers not by refuting the costs but by asserting a competing catalogue of benefits — gladness, honour, gentleness, worship, trust, courtesy, "Dread of shame for to do amiss." The poem lets both speeches run at full rhetorical strength before the dreamer intervenes, which is what makes the debate poem more than propaganda for courtly love.

The dreamer is not an impartial judge — he is a partisan who breaks the frame. Convention would have the dream simply record the debate, but here the human dreamer physically intervenes, hunting the Cuckoo off with a thrown stone. This collapses the distance between observer and participant: the poem's judgment on the Cuckoo is enacted, not merely argued, before any formal verdict is reached — which is exactly why a parliament is then needed to make the judgment legitimate rather than merely personal.

Formal, procedural resolution is deferred rather than achieved by rhetoric. Neither bird persuades the other; the quarrel is instead escalated into an institutional process — a parliament with the eagle as lord and "other peers that been of record," summoning the absent Cuckoo to answer, to be convened at a fixed calendar point (the morrow after Saint Valentine's Day) and a fixed place (under the maple at Woodstock, before the Queen's window). The poem thus ends not in resolution but in a promissory structure for one — precisely the device the headnote says links this poem forward to "The Assembly of Fowls," where that fuller parliamentary debate plays out.

Seasonal love-sickness as physiological fact, insisted on by the narrator's own testimony. Before the debate even begins, the dreamer grounds the whole poem in bodily experience of May-sickness — "I am so shaken with the fevers white... Both hot and cold, an access ev'ry day" — asserting he speaks "of feeling truely," which pre-loads the reader to side with the Nightingale's position before the Cuckoo ever speaks.

Notable passages

The poem opens with an extended, almost liturgical invocation of the God of Love's arbitrary omnipotence ("He can make of lowe heartes high, / And of high low, and like for to die"), establishing Love as a capricious sovereign power before any debate begins — the invocation that the editorial note points out recurs nearly verbatim in the Knight's Tale, in Theseus's speech on the love-follies of Palamon and Arcite.

The dreamer's fall into trance and the ill-omened birdsong order — Cuckoo before Nightingale — is rendered with careful attention to the liminal state itself ("Not all asleep, nor fully waking"), setting up the poem's premise that the dreamer can understand bird-language only inside this threshold state.

The Nightingale's central defense of Love catalogues courtly virtue as Love's direct product — gladness, honour, gentleness, trust, courtesy — closing on the claim that Love's true servant would rather die than be shamed, the clearest statement of the poem's courtly-love thesis.

The Cuckoo's rebuttal is the poem's most vivid counter-voice, reducing love to "rage" in youth and "dotage" in age and listing its costs as a litany of social and psychological harms, ending in "woodness" (madness) — giving real weight to the anti-love position the Nightingale must overcome.

The stoning scene is the poem's turning point, where debate becomes action, and the Nightingale's grateful vow to be the dreamer's "singer" all May converts the abstract quarrel into a personal bond between bird and dreamer.

The close, where the birds by "one assent" agree to refer judgment to a parliament under the eagle's authority at a fixed place and date, is the passage the headnote singles out as the poem's hinge into "The Assembly of Fowls," turning a private dream-debate into the seed of a formal, quasi-legal convocation.
