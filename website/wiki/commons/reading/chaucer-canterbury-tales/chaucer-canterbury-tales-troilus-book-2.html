Troilus and Criseyde, Book II: Pandarus's Persuasion

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
Pandarus visits his niece Cressida, argues and maneuvers her from resistance to receptive interest in Troilus, and by the book's end has engineered — through a dream, a letter campaign, and a staged dinner at Deiphobus's house — the private meeting at which Troilus must finally speak his love.

This unit is D. Laing Purves's prose-and-verse digest of the Second Book of Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde. After a proem in which the poet turns from the "tempestuous matter of despair" toward hope and defends the plausibility of a love brokered by go-between rather than direct courtship, the action follows Pandarus as he calls on his niece Cressida, steers a conversation about the Siege of Thebes and the heroism of Hector's brother around to Troilus, and gradually extracts from her a promise to "make Troilus good cheer in honour." A providential glimpse of Troilus riding home in triumph, a garden interlude with Antigone's love-song, and a dream of an eagle exchanging hearts with her all work on Cressida's mind before she talks herself, stanza by stanza, into permitting the courtship. The second half of the book tracks Pandarus's stage-management: coaching Troilus on how to write a convincing (not over-written) love letter, delivering it, extracting a guarded reply, and finally contriving — through a false story of danger from Polyphete — a pretext dinner at Deiphobus's house that empties Troilus's chamber of witnesses and leaves Pandarus free to bring Cressida in alone.

Themes

Love as social engineering. The book is less about feeling than about the machinery that produces the occasion for feeling. Pandarus never simply reports Troilus's love; he choreographs proximity — a ride-by under the window, a letter timed to catch her alone, a dinner invitation built on a fabricated pretext of protection, a chamber conveniently vacated by Helen and Deiphobus's absorption in "documents relating to the public weal." The entire apparatus of the Deiphobus dinner is a constructed pretext-space: a curated, portal-bordered occasion (invitation, protection, business talk) that opens onto an interior, private encounter Pandarus alone controls the entrance to — a structure worth flagging against The Thicket's notion of portal-bordered Places sheltering an interior Space, though here the shelter is built for courtship rather than resistance.

Rhetoric of persuasion, twice performed. Pandarus persuades Cressida with a barrage of proverbs, threats of suicide, and appeals to the ravages of age ("elde daunteth danger at the last"); then he coaches Troilus on persuading Cressida by letter, and the advice is explicitly about craft: don't write "dignely," don't overuse one figure the way a harper who plays "ay one string" dulls his audience, don't mix incongruous registers the way a painter who gives a pike an ass's feet and an ape's head produces only "a jape." The poem is self-conscious about the difference between authentic-seeming and merely correct rhetoric — a naturalism that has to be manufactured.

Self-persuasion and the calculus of independence. Cressida's long interior monologue is the book's most striking passage: rather than simply yielding to Pandarus, she reasons her way to consent through a sequence of self-interested arguments — social advantage, safety in not offending a king's son, the harmlessness of being loved, her own worth, and above all her prized status as "mine owen woman, well at ease... untied in lusty leas." This is a portrait of a self actively calculating how to preserve its own standing and autonomy while making room for desire — a small-scale instance of a self's Conatus-like striving to persist and expand asserting itself even inside a plot designed by someone else. The cloud that crosses her bright thoughts (fear of lost liberty, of "wicked tongues") and her recovery ("He that nothing undertakes / Nothing achieveth") dramatize the cost-benefit logic beneath the sentiment.

Fortune, omen, and the unstoppable. The narrative frames the courtship as fated rather than chosen: Pandarus consults planetary alignment ("knew in good plight was the Moon") before setting out; Troilus's ride home is glossed "men say may not disturbed be / What shall betiden of necessity"; and Cressida's eagle dream — a bird tearing out her heart and replacing it, painlessly, with another — supplies a supernatural ratification of the exchange about to happen between her and Troilus. The proem's own defense of Pandarus's mediation ("For ev'ry wighte that to Rome went / Held not one path... Each country hath its laws") extends this logic of inevitability into a relativist defense of custom: love has no single correct form.

Notable passages

The proem's relativist apology ("Each country hath its laws") sets up the whole book's premise that a love conducted through a third party is not thereby suspect. The description of Troilus riding home wounded and triumphant ("It was a heaven on him for to see") is the visual turning point that starts Cressida's private reasoning. That reasoning crests in her soliloquy on being "mine owen woman," the clearest statement in the unit of a self negotiating desire against independence. The eagle dream supplies the poem's supernatural seal on the exchange of hearts about to be brokered by human stratagem. And Pandarus's coaching on the love letter — warning against a harper who plays "ay one string" or a painter who gives a pike an ass's feet — makes explicit the poem's interest in the crafted, rhetorical nature of seemingly spontaneous passion.
