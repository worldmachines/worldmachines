The Franklin's Tale: Troth, Mastery, and the Contest of Gentillesse

Commons · The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems · updated 27 July 2026 · source on GitHub
In Chaucer's Franklin's Tale, a knight and his wife found their marriage on mutual freedom rather than mastery, and when the wife's rash promise to a lovesick squire is made good by an astrologer's illusion, three men in turn outdo one another in acts of unrewarded generosity, ending in an open question about which was 'most free.'

After the Franklin praises the Squire's eloquence and is gently rebuked by the Host for standing on gentility, he offers a Breton lay he disclaims any rhetorical skill in telling. The tale that follows concerns Arviragus, a Breton knight, and Dorigen, the lady he wins and marries on the express condition that he will never exercise 'mastery' over her — though he keeps the outward 'name of sovereignety' for the sake of appearances. While Arviragus is away in England seeking honour, Dorigen pines, and in her grief walks the cliffs above the sea, cursing the 'grisly rockes blake' that threaten her husband's return as a senseless blot on divine providence. At a garden party a squire, Aurelius, who has loved her silently for two years, finally declares himself; Dorigen, meaning only to refuse him with an impossible condition, promises to love him if he clears every rock from the coast of Brittany. Aurelius, driven half-mad, engages a clerk of Orleans skilled in 'magic natural' who, through elaborate astronomical calculation rather than any supernatural power, produces the illusion that the rocks have vanished. Dorigen, bound by her word, resolves to die rather than break her troth or her chastity, rehearsing a long catalogue of classical women who killed themselves rather than be dishonoured. But when Arviragus returns and hears the story, he orders her — weeping — to keep her promise anyway, because 'Truth is the highest thing that man may keep.' Meeting her on the way, Aurelius is so moved by the couple's mutual gentility that he releases her from her vow; the clerk-magician, hearing the whole chain of generosity, in turn forgives Aurelius his enormous fee. The tale closes not with a moral but with a direct question to the pilgrims: which of the three men was 'most free' — the most generous?

Themes

Marriage as a pact against mastery. The tale opens its actual plot by establishing an unusual marital contract: Arviragus swears never to exert mastery over Dorigen, retaining only the empty title of sovereignty 'for shame of his degree,' while Dorigen swears to be a humble, true wife. The narrator generalizes this into an argument about love as such — 'Love will not be constrain'd by mastery' — and about patience as the precondition of any durable partnership between people who are, by nature, both 'of kind' desirous of liberty. The tale frames this arrangement paradoxically: Dorigen is at once 'servant in love, and lord in marriage,' and the narrator insists this is not servitude but the highest form of lordship, since Arviragus possesses both wife and love freely given.

The rashly-binding word. Dorigen's promise to Aurelius is made 'in play,' as a joke predicated on an impossibility — she is certain the rocks can never actually be removed. The tale's engine is that a vow, once spoken, binds regardless of the speaker's intent or the listener's presumed bad faith; when the impossible is made to appear true by artifice, the letter of the promise outranks its spirit. Arviragus's verdict — she must keep her word even though it destroys their happiness and even though the 'miracle' is a mere trick of astrology — turns 'Truth is the highest thing that man may keep' into the tale's explicit moral center, prior to and overriding personal cost.

Gentillesse as a competitive, cascading virtue. The tale's real climax is not the removal of the rocks but the chain of renunciations that follows: Arviragus surrenders his own comfort to his wife's truth; Aurelius, moved by that surrender, releases Dorigen from her vow and asks nothing in return; the clerk, hearing of Aurelius's release, forgives an enormous debt in turn. Each release is explicitly framed as an act of 'franchise' and 'gentleness' that a person of lower estate (squire, or even a hired magician) performs as nobly as, or better than, a knight — nobility of spirit outrunning nobility of birth. The Franklin ends by throwing this contest open to the pilgrims as a formal 'demande d'amour,' refusing to adjudicate it himself.

Magic as calculation, not sorcery. The clerk's illusion is worked not through supernatural aid but through technical apparatus — Toledan astronomical tables, the moon's twenty-eight mansions, 'centres,' 'arguments,' 'proportional convenients,' the position of Alnath in the sphere of Aries — a naturalistic, almost bureaucratic machinery of measurement deployed to engineer an appearance. The narrator is careful to frame this as 'folly' and 'illusion' rather than real power, noting that 'holy church's faith... suffreth none illusion to grieve,' which lets the plot's central impossibility be resolved by craft and trickery rather than the marvellous.

Notable passages

The marriage-contract passage (c1) states the tale's premise about mutual liberty in the plainest terms available to Chaucer, generalizing Arviragus and Dorigen's private arrangement into a claim about love itself. Dorigen's cliffside complaint (c2) is the tale's one moment of genuine theological unease, questioning why a 'perfect wise God and stable' would create rocks that serve 'no good' and only destroy — a complaint the narrator pointedly leaves unanswered ('to clerks leave I all disputation'), rather than resolving it within the tale's comic frame. The rash promise itself (c3) shows Dorigen setting an impossible condition in what she takes to be safety, the exact hinge on which the plot's crisis turns. Aurelius's renunciation (c4) and the clerk's answering renunciation (c5) are the tale's true climax, each explicitly measured against and answering the other in the vocabulary of 'gentleness' and 'franchise.' The closing question (c6) refuses to supply a verdict, converting the whole tale into an open casuistical puzzle for the pilgrim audience — and, by extension, the reader.
