Mabel Loomis Todd's Diaries: An Erotic Record of Bourgeois Courtship

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Peter Gay introduces Mabel Loomis Todd's uncommonly candid diaries as the book's central evidentiary source, tracing her adolescent sexual awakening and premarital intimacy with David Todd to show erotic energy coexisting with, rather than negated by, orthodox bourgeois propriety and class snobbery.

This opening section introduces Mabel Loomis Todd — diarist, poet, painter, and wife of the astronomer David Peck Todd — as Gay's central informant, because her exhaustive diaries and journals candidly record a courtship and marriage saturated with erotic feeling, undercutting the stereotype of the sexually anaesthetic Victorian bourgeois woman. The unit follows her from adolescent sexual awakening through her engagement, showing conventional class markers (family pride, snobbery about "vulgar" money, disdain for "common" Americans) coexisting with candid eroticism and extensive premarital intimacy, and closes on her wedding day and her mother's naive letter praising her "purity."

Themes

An unrepressed source as evidentiary coup. Gay opens by justifying his method: because "a culture that takes exceptional pains to keep its private concerns private" leaves few explicit records of erotic life, a diarist as "leisurely and uninhibited" as Todd is a gift he can only "quote at length." This is Gay's brief against the michel-foucault-associated "repressive hypothesis" mounted from a primary source rather than theory: he does not argue Victorians were unrepressed in the abstract, he produces a document showing one respectable, propertied woman recording erotic experience in granular, almost scientific detail — the retrospective diary entry reconstructing the exact morning her daughter was conceived functions as proof of concept for the whole project.

Typicality and exception held together. Gay is careful not to make Todd a freak: her diary-keeping, her novel-reading, her piano lessons, her fantasies of social success are "typical of her class and age." What is exceptional is only the "introspective candor and fine lucidity" with which she records herself — a difference of documentation, not of underlying experience. This is the chapter's argumentative hinge: if one very ordinary bourgeois woman turns out to be this erotically alive in private, the private lives of the inarticulate majority likely resembled hers.

Snobbery as the load-bearing half of her respectability. Todd's moral certainties "yielded to the pressure of her experience," but her social certainties never did. Her scorn for "vulgar" self-made men, women who "sparkled in diamonds," and "commonplace Georgetown society," and her pride in "good blood" and a family coat of arms, are offered by Gay as the stable structure inside which her erotic flexibility could occur without threatening her sense of self. Bourgeois identity here looks like Gay's "family of desires and anxieties": class anxiety is the fixed axis, sexual conduct the negotiable one.

Impulse versus defense. Gay repeatedly narrates Todd catching herself — wondering "if I am a flirt," insisting she keeps her "circle of personal magnetism" to herself, worrying over a "slight passage at arms" — only to talk herself back into propriety. These are small case studies in what Gay elsewhere calls bourgeois defenses: not the suppression of desire but its constant, effortful management alongside its expression.

The engagement as a licensed erotic zone. The unit's comparative material (Julia Ward and Samuel Howe, Elias Nason and Mira Bigelow) generalizes Todd's premarital intimacy with David Todd into a class pattern: betrothal, for the nineteenth-century bourgeoisie, was tacitly understood as a period for "exploring and being explored," bounded by a felt "line" between ordinary friendship and the "one particular kind of affection" owed a fiancé. Official doctrine condemned "promiscuous hugging and kissing"; actual practice made room for exactly that, once a ring made it exclusive.

Elastic innocence. The chapter closes on Todd's wedding day, "at least technically a virgin," and her mother's gushing, oblivious letter about the "rapt expression of purity" on her face. Gay's dry gloss — innocence was "a relatively elastic thing" — states in miniature the book's thesis about the gap between official bourgeois ideology and lived practice: appearance policed and preserved even as its substance was privately renegotiated.

Notable passages

  • The opening apologia for quoting Todd at length ties documentary scarcity to the culture's insistence on privacy — the methodological premise for the whole book.
  • The retrospective diary entry reconstructing her daughter's conception is Gay's single best piece of evidence that erotic experience was recorded, not merely lived, inside a "commonplace" bourgeois marriage.
  • Her rush of class disdain for "vulgarest" American travelers and diamond-sparkling women shows how much of her self-regard rested on breeding rather than money — the stable structure against which her sexual candor is set.
  • Her self-interrogation over being "a flirt," resolved by disowning the label while admitting she was "very fond of gentlemen," dramatizes the impulse/defense conflict Gay reads into her adolescence.
  • The comparative material on other engaged couples (Julia Ward and Howe, Nason and Bigelow) generalizes her premarital intimacy into a recognized, if unspoken, bourgeois convention of the engagement period.
  • Her mother's letter praising her "purity" days after a courtship full of erotic experimentation delivers Gay's closing irony about how elastic bourgeois "innocence" actually was.
