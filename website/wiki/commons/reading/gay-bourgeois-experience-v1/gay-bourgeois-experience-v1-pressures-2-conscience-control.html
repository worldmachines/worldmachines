From Conscience to Control

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Bourgeois parents met pregnancy, childbirth, and child death with religious submission through mid-century, but by the century's end secular contraception and abortion had displaced providential resignation as the dominant response to fertility.

This section (Chapter 3, part 2 of Education of the Senses) traces the nineteenth-century bourgeois family's arc from passive, providence-accepting fertility and child mourning toward active, secular control over reproduction, using private letters and diaries — above all William Ewart Gladstone's — as evidence, and closing with contraception and abortion statistics that chart conscience losing ground to control.

Themes

Passive maternity slowly gives way to a will toward control. Gay opens by likening motherhood's prospect to "an English sky" — glorious, threatening, always changing — and describes decades of middle-class couples accepting "frequent pregnancies with marked passivity," their perils included, because "the bourgeois conscience... found it hard to grasp that continuous child-bearing might not be a woman's highest duty." Only gradually did wives develop "fantasies of flight from home" or press husbands toward continence or birth control — the chapter's title move from conscience to control in miniature.

Christian stoicism cushions grief without abolishing it. A run of family documents — Mary Anna Gibbs writing of her sister Maggie's death after childbirth, the Lymans' letters and diary on the death of their daughter Hattie, M. Carey Thomas's childhood dictations, Edward Tallmadge Root's diary on his brother Willie — show belief in a beneficent God and heaven doing real emotional work ("Asleep in Jesus blessed sleep... From which none ever wake to weep"). Gay is careful to insist this defense was not numbing: "Their capacity for mourning was scarcely dampened by its frequency."

Gladstone's diaries as the fullest specimen of the bourgeois conscience. Gay uses William Ewart Gladstone — whose "punitive evangelical superego" he traces to his mother's obsessive piety — as an extended case: his theologizing of Catherine Gladstone's labors (childbirth as woman's "peculiar curse" and "peculiar promise," her pain read as purifying discipline she "was ready to go through... again"), his formulaic submission at the death of his infant nephew and sister-in-law Lavinia, and, most tellingly, his near-breakdown of pious language when his own daughters Agnes and Jessy nearly die — a live specimen of ambivalence, the conflict between wanting a child to live and being required to will only "Thy will be done."

Doctrine and private practice visibly diverge over contraception and abortion. Gladstone himself will not discuss birth control even in the abstract, calling the 1873 Mill-memorial controversy merely "painful" — a small act of the reticence and privatization that let practice run ahead of what could be said aloud. Meanwhile Bishop Bouvier's 1849 report to Pius IX describes French confessants who want small families and "cannot morally abstain from the conjugal act," and American medical societies (Buffalo, 1859; the New York Medico-Legal Society, 1872) and Dr. H. S. Pomeroy document "educated and refined ladies" and "highly respectable people" seeking abortions — evidence that the official theology of procreative sex alone had stopped governing bourgeois bedrooms well before anyone said so in public.

The chapter's closing claim: control wins. Gay counterpoints the Gladstones' eight children in fourteen years against the Freuds' six in eight, and cites Freud's own 1898 observation of "Malthusian tendencies... gaining increasing ground in our middle class," to stage "the great nineteenth-century struggle between conscience and control" as one that control, by the century's end, had substantially won — "a triumph of secular will over religious compliance."

Notable passages

  • The opening "English sky" paragraph sets the ambivalent emotional register (hope and dread at once) that the whole unit tracks from passivity to agency.
  • Joseph Lyman's diary line on his daughter Hattie's death — recorded flatly alongside the fact that he was, at that moment, writing for the newspaper — is a stark instance of grief kept private and terse rather than performed.
  • Gladstone's diary during Agnes's illness ("My heart is hard & unquiet & not willing to give back my child... it rebels") is the clearest textual evidence of ambivalence: love resisting the very submission his faith demanded of him.
  • Bouvier's report to Pius IX, with its confessional interrogation of couples "on the way they employ the rights of marriage," half-resonates with michel-foucault's account of confession as a discourse-producing technology of sex — though where Foucault reads confession as generative of discourse on sex as such, Gay reads it more directly as documentary evidence that practice had already outrun doctrine.
  • Dr. Pomeroy's 1888 testimony that his real "trouble" comes from "highly respectable people" rather than the poor inverts the expected class geography of vice, and is Gay's clearest single piece of evidence for the book's larger argument that respectability concealed rather than eliminated desire.
  • The closing paragraph's Gladstone/Freud contrast and its "conscience and control" formula give the unit its thesis; the slow, distributed accretion of medical-society reports and diary evidence before that thesis can be stated aloud faintly echoes how a history-machine assembles out of scattered private material long before it is named — though Gay's is a historian's synthesis, not an emergent institutional paradigm, and the resonance should not be pressed further than that.
