A Profession of Anxiety: The Medical Crusade Against Masturbation

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Nineteenth-century physicians, clergy, and educators sustained a century-long, statistically obsessive campaign against masturbation as Peter Gay's paradigm case of "learned ignorance" — a defensive, motivated unknowing rooted less in missing facts than in physicians' own professional powerlessness and their inability, as bourgeois husbands and fathers, to defy their culture's moral consensus.

This chapter section traces the roughly 150-year medical, clerical, and pedagogical campaign against masturbation — from the anonymous Onania (before 1710) and Samuel Tissot's De l'onanisme (1758) through Benjamin Rush, Maudsley, Calhoun, Deslandes, Rohleder, and dozens of lesser "crusaders," down to its dissolution around Freud's 1912 Vienna symposium on Onanie — and then turns from chronicle to diagnosis, arguing that the panic's real motor was not ignorance of fact but a defensive, self-protecting learned ignorance, itself driven by two layers of physicians' anxiety: professional (their real helplessness before disease, masked by an authoritative facade patients demanded) and bourgeois-cultural (their inability, as husbands and fathers embedded in respectable society, to stand apart from its moral consensus).

Themes

"Learned ignorance" is Gay's central diagnostic term here, and he is precise about what distinguishes it from mere unknowing: it is "far more resistant than a sheer lack of knowledge, for that may always be alleviated by pertinent new information," being instead "derived... from the desire not to know... the worst possible kind of ignorance, armored by defensiveness against correctives." Gay stresses that this was not practiced by charlatans alone — Tissot was a European Enlightenment eminence who corresponded with Voltaire and Rousseau, and Maudsley kept insisting masturbation caused insanity a quarter-century after statistical method had matured enough to show his four-case, anecdotal reasoning was worthless. The ignorance survived contact with better method because it was doing psychological work unrelated to evidence.

The campaign's own evidentiary style is a minor case study in legibility-seeking gone askew. Physicians did not simply moralize; they quantified — Dr. Meirowsky's 71 percent of German secondary-school onanists, Oskar Berger's and "Professor Joung's" flat 100 percent, Rohleder's own "nine out of every ten." Turning an intimate, invisible practice into a percentage was an attempt to render the ungovernable legible and thereby actionable, the same impulse behind statistical and cadastral technologies that WMP associates with the modernity-machine's drive to make the world "readable, measurable, orderable to scale control" — though here the object was the interior of the body rather than land or population, and the numbers, as Gay shows, were invented rather than counted, so the resonance is with the aspiration to legibility rather than its achievement.

The spermatic economy gave the anxiety its idiom. Following Tissot's claim that "the loss of one ounce of that humor is more weakening than that of forty ounces of blood," writers treated sexual expenditure as a zero-sum drain on a body pictured explicitly as "the fabric of our machine." Gay is careful to note this predates capitalism by two millennia and was not simply a bourgeois-capitalist metaphor smuggled onto the body, against readings that make too much of the colloquialism "to spend."

The remedies harden mid-century and soften again after 1900 — the shift from the Enlightenment's "vigorous exercise, soothing baths, hard mattresses" to the 1850s–1890s vogue for restraining harnesses, cauterization, infibulation, and clitoridectomy, followed by the return of "gentler methods" after 1900. Gay's own periodization here — construction, mid-century dominance and escalation, then audible cracking (Christian's 1881 dissent, Rohleder's still-alarmed but non-insanity-claiming 1899 book, Havelock Ellis's open scorn, Freud's colleagues finding the pendulum swung "too dramatically" toward exculpation by 1910) — is exactly the kind of whole-class-ideology arc (construction, dominance, audible cracking) World Machines is interested in, condensed here into a single, well-documented professional discourse rather than a civilizational one.

This chapter also reads, without naming him, as a rebuttal-in-advance of Foucault's repressive hypothesis. What the record actually shows is not silence but an explosion of talk: hundreds of reprinted treatises, case histories, testimonials from asylum superintendents and Baptist conventions, cross-citation rings (Fowler cited by Platt, Alcott cited by Fowler), formal medical symposia. Gay's own explanation is psychoanalytic — anxiety expressing itself as garrulous, punitive discourse — where michel-foucault's account treats such proliferation as a diffuse effect of power/knowledge rather than of any practitioner's inner state; the two readings share the same evidence (loquacity rather than repression) while disagreeing sharply about its motor.

Physicians could not escape being anxious twice over. Professionally, they were "privileged and powerless spectator[s] by the bedside" through most of the century, forced into a confident public face at odds with private futility, while patients demanded of them an almost parental omnipotence — "expectations of the physician's omnipotence alternated with contempt for his impotence, and they irrationally idolized or irrationally execrated him." Culturally, they were ordinary bourgeois husbands and fathers who, per Hufeland's 1836 dictum that "for no one is public opinion so important as for the physician," could not risk breaking with prevailing moral rhetoric without professional cost — which is Gay's answer to why intelligent, non-quack healers produced what he flatly calls "bad science."

The stakes, in the end, were mastery rather than morality alone. Gay's closing argument reframes the whole campaign: masturbation alarmed because it figured "a loss of mastery over the world and oneself," and the crusade was "a desperate and in the long run futile attempt to hold on to traditional ways in the face of revolutionary moral possibilities." Its defeat — "desire won out over duty" — was Pyrrhic, producing not resolution but a new, equally anxious "frantic campaign to disseminate sexual information, a symptom far more than a solution," gesturing toward the very shift from reticence to a therapeutic culture of sexual openness that later volumes track.

Notable passages

The chapter opens by naming the phenomenon — professionals as "Quixotic knights of learned ignorance" waging a campaign that "admirably condens[es] nineteenth-century middle-class anxieties" — before the long chronicle of Onania, Tissot, Rush, Calhoun, and Maudsley. Its most visceral moment is the 1894 Cleveland clitoridectomy Gay draws from Rene Spitz's survey: a seven-year-old girl, after mechanical restraints failed, is surgically cut, and afterward tells her mother, "You know there is nothing there now... so of course I could do nothing" — Gay pointedly naming the surgeon, Dr. Alvin Eyer, so the horror is not left anonymous. The analytical hinge is the paragraph defining "learned ignorance" itself as armored, motivated unknowing rather than a knowledge gap. Tissot's spermatic-economy claim supplies the pseudo-physiological engine for a century of prescription. And the closing paragraph delivers Gay's real thesis: the campaign was about mastery and control, its failure was Pyrrhic, and what followed was a new anxious symptom, not a cure.
