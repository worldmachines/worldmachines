Architects and Martyrs of Change: The Sway of the New

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Peter Gay assembles the nineteenth-century bourgeoisie's own chorus of voices proclaiming their era uniquely "new" and transitional, then grounds that ideology of change in hard demographic evidence — mass migration, urbanization, suburbanization, and the explosive rise of a clerical new middle class — whose beneficiaries were also, through constrained mobility and militarized office life, its casualties.

Opening Chapter 2 of his General Introduction, Gay argues that the nineteenth-century bourgeoisie experienced its century as an unprecedented "time of unexampled hopes and unfamiliar anxieties," and he substantiates that self-perception two ways at once: first by quoting a chorus of contemporaries — statesmen, novelists, historians, chefs, sexologists — obsessively declaring their own age uniquely "new" or "transitional," and second by piling up the demographic evidence (migration, urbanization, suburbanization, and above all the explosive growth of a new clerical middle class) that gave that rhetoric material substance. The section sets up the chapter's title: the same new middle class that built the century's transformations, Gay closes by showing, was also constrained and often ground down by them.

Themes

The cult of the new as bourgeois ideology. Gay treats the incantatory repetition of the word "new" — Burckhardt's sardonic "Everybody wants to be new, but nothing else," Holbrook Jackson's catalogue of 1890s movements ("the New Hedonism," "the New Woman"), Hermann Bahr's "irresistible thirst after the new," Escoffier's culinary "novelty by hook or by crook," Péguy's hyperbole that "the world has changed less since Jesus Christ than in the last thirty years" — not as background color but as data: a century that talked about its own novelty compulsively, across registers from statecraft to cookbooks, was performing an ideology of change as much as living one. Even psychoanalysis absorbed the habit: Freud historicized the Oedipus complex itself, reading the gap between Sophocles and Shakespeare as evidence of "the secular advance of repression in the emotional life of humanity."

Change as double-edged, not uniformly exhilarating. Gay is careful to distinguish rhetoric from experience: change in this century was "more rapid and more irresistible than in the past" but also "strikingly uneven" — scientific advance did not translate smoothly into social reform, family life was "torn by the clash of new needs and old habits." Optimists (Prince Albert, Tylor, the Saint-Simonian Heine) and pessimists (Carlyle, Metternich) drew opposite morals from the same perceived acceleration, but agreed on its premise.

Statistics as the era's proof-text for its own transformation. Gay answers rhetoric with numbers at almost every turn: immigration into the United States (150,000 in the 1820s to 5.2 million in the 1880s; 62 million total European emigrants over a century), urbanization rates (England and Wales from 21% urban in 1800 to 61% by 1890), and the sheer speed of individual city growth (Middlesbrough from 581 to 76,135 inhabitants in ninety years). This is quantification as the century's characteristic way of apprehending itself — nicely captured, within the unit, in Heine's own image of an age that has "measured the lands, weighed the forces of nature, reckoned the means of industry."

The suburb as bourgeois refuge and battleground. Suburbanization is presented as "defenses from anxiety no less than as realizations of wishes": a bourgeois retreat from urban noise (Camberwell, Wilmersdorf) that was simultaneously an object of satire and, as working-class housing and factories later encroached on the same ground, a site where "bourgeois dreams turned into nightmares." Wilkie Collins's martial metaphor for the builders of suburbia — "guerrilla regiments of the hod, the trowel, and the brick-kiln" outdoing Alexander and Napoleon as conquerors — captures how contemporaries themselves reached for the language of conquest to describe ordinary construction.

The rise of the new middle class. The chapter's demographic center of gravity is the explosion of clerical, administrative, and service occupations — typists, bookkeepers, sales personnel, civil servants — dwarfing the older commercial bourgeoisie in scale: English men in commercial occupations rose eightfold between 1851 and 1911 (91,000 to 739,000), while women clerks rose from a negligible presence to 21% of the clerking population over the same period. Comparable, if differently paced, shifts in France and Germany (where employee numbers grew nearly six times faster than the industrial workforce) show this was a pan-Western phenomenon, not an English peculiarity. Government itself was among the most voracious employers of this new class, its spending and clerical payrolls both multiplying roughly tenfold across the century.

Architects and martyrs: the new middle class as both agent and casualty of change. Gay closes the section by complicating the triumphalist reading his own statistics might invite: the clerks and officials who embodied the century's dynamism also lived "lives not wholly without hope" but frequently without real mobility — late marriages, deliberately small families, and civilian workplaces "infected by more than a touch of militarism," patterned on the authoritarian family rather than any more liberal model. The new middle class is thus simultaneously modern (unprecedented in scale, born of capitalism's newest sectors) and archaic in its internal discipline — Gay's evidence, at chapter's end, that "the bourgeoisie in the age of Freud had changed significantly from what it had been in the age of Victoria," but not simply for the better.

This dynamic — a class's culture of self-legitimation (the rhetoric of progress and the new) running ahead of, and partly papering over, the coercive realities of the institutions that same class was busy building — resonates with modernity-machine's account of legibility: the nineteenth-century bourgeoisie's compulsion to measure, count, and administer (censuses, government payrolls, urbanization statistics) is exactly the kind of infrastructure-building Gay documents here, though his frame stays psychological and class-experiential rather than institutional-systemic, and he is interested in the ideology of change itself as much as in the machinery it built.

Notable passages

  • Heine's Saint-Simonian optimism ("We have measured the lands... reckoned the means of industry") stands as the unit's clearest statement of progress-as-quantification: the future guaranteed by inventory.
  • The Burckhardt-to-Durkheim sequence assembles, in miniature, the whole discursive tic the section is built around — the reflexive, cross-genre insistence that this age, uniquely, was new.
  • The clerk statistics (91,000 to 739,000 English commercial men, 1851–1911; women clerks from negligible to 21%) are Gay's hardest evidence for the "new middle class" as a genuinely novel social formation, not just a rhetorical one.
  • Wilkie Collins's mock-martial description of suburban builders as conquering "guerrilla regiments" shows how even mundane urban development was narrated, at the time, in the century's preferred idiom of dramatic, irreversible change.
  • Bagehot's "new world of ideas... in the air" claims that invisible intellectual transformation was as consequential as the railroad or telegraph — an explicit contemporary argument for treating ideology, not just infrastructure, as historically load-bearing.
  • The closing passage on late marriage, small families, and militarized office discipline is Gay's pivot from "architects" to "martyrs": the same class whose growth exemplifies change was disciplined by distinctly unmodern, authoritarian structures.
