A Battle of Perceptions

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay lays the nineteenth century's savage anti-bourgeois caricatures (Heine, Flaubert, Marx and Engels) alongside its confident self-praise (Riehl, Mill, Brougham, Defoe's heirs), argues that the historian must credit and discount both traditions rather than side with the more eloquent prosecution, and concludes that the bourgeoisie's only stable trait was Metternich's paradox of being "at once one and many."

Gay stages the nineteenth-century argument over what the bourgeoisie was as a genuine contest between two well-developed traditions rather than a settled verdict: a hostile, increasingly canonical caricature built by Heine, Flaubert, and the Marxist critique, pitted against an older and equally confident self-congratulatory tradition running from Renaissance merchant patriciates through Defoe, Addison, and Voltaire to Victorian apologists like Riehl, James Mill, and Lord Brougham. His own move is to refuse to referee by simply picking the more eloquent side: he credits real substance in the critics' complaints about philistinism and moral defensiveness while insisting that much of the invective is recycled antiquity, critics' self-projection, or rhetorical overreach — and he closes by suggesting that the bourgeoisie's one durable, definable trait was precisely its refusal to resolve into a single thing.

Themes

The critics' canon is vivid, persuasive, and became practically canonical. Heine's tour of the 1843 Paris Salon reads bourgeois greed into every canvas, even a scourged Christ; Flaubert's "bourgeoisophobus" self-description and Marx and Engels's Communist Manifesto line about wives as "a mere instrument of production" supply the intellectual firepower behind the enduring stereotype of a bourgeoisie constitutionally incapable of love, converting "all of life into merchandise." Gay traces this indictment's genealogy back past Sturm und Drang and E. T. A. Hoffmann's Kreisler (the mad musician saner than his "philistine" neighbors) to Jesus driving the money changers from the temple — a very old anti-mercantile disgust, not a nineteenth-century discovery, that acquired new force once Flaubert, and then a "mass movement" of imitators, made contempt for the bourgeois a badge of aesthetic seriousness.

Gay's historiographical caution: take the prosecution's evidence, discount its rhetoric. The chapter's methodological center is the demand to treat "such verdicts with the utmost reserve" — some are critics' own neuroses projected outward, some are liberal culture's peculiar hospitality to its own slanderers, some are simply very old assaults repeated until they sounded self-evident. This is Gay doing what he does throughout the book: refusing to let a class's loudest enemies write its only history, while still admitting that "this furious hostility touched the realities of the time at many points" — official Salon art, censorious philistinism, and the social anxiety of parvenus clinging to safe taste were real, not invented.

"Bourgeois" curdled into an all-purpose insult even as it lost precision. Engels folds the aristocracy itself into "the bourgeoisie" for polemical convenience; Flaubert wants Salammbo to "annoy the bourgeois, that is to say, everybody." The word's very elasticity — it can mean almost anyone the speaker dislikes — is itself evidence for Gay that "bourgeois" names a perceived spirit of the age more than a demographic category.

A parallel apologetic tradition is just as old and just as confident. Riehl calls the Burger order the historic "bearer of justified social movements"; the tradition runs back through Holbein's unapologetic merchant portraits and the Medici's patronage to Defoe, Addison, and Voltaire's praise of the tolerant, peaceable trader over the "childish" aristocrat, and forward to James Mill's "glory of England" and Brougham's frank equation of "the people" with "the middle classes." Gay notes the irony that even fierce critics like Marx and Engels could not help crediting the bourgeoisie's civilizational achievement — "drawn all nations, even the most barbarian, into civilization" — inside the same Manifesto that damns it.

Self-laceration was itself a bourgeois habit, which complicates any clean verdict. Henry James observes that bourgeois-haters like Balzac's circle are almost always "fugitives from the bourgeoisie" attacking the class they fled; Arthur Symons calls the desire to "bewilder the middle classes" itself middle class. Gay extends this into a claim about bourgeois psychology proper: Toynbee's public apology to a working-class audience, Beatrice Webb's "new consciousness of sin," and Shaw's crack that the century's second half "discovered that it was the wickedest of all the centuries" show a class that turned its formidable capacity for self-scrutiny into guilt as readily as into pride — evidence, for Gay, of "some exquisite superegos."

The resolution is not a definition but an acknowledged doubleness. Gay ends by insisting there is still something real to define, and reaches for Metternich's 1820 description of "this intermediate class" as one that takes "all sorts of disguises, uniting and subdividing as occasion offers" — a polity "at once one and many." English usage settled the matter linguistically by preferring the plural "middle classes" to the singular "bourgeoisie," which Gay reads as accidentally exact: a name for coalescence-under-pressure rather than for a fixed essence.

Notable passages

Heine's Salon tour is the sharpest specimen of the caricature at its most inventive: he reads pecuniary vulgarity into a scourged Christ and grandiose history paintings alike, reducing them to "small retail stores, speculations on the stock exchange, mercantile values, bourgeois philistinism."

The "loveless bourgeois" charge is quoted at its most compressed and clinical: the claim that bourgeois marriage converts "all of experience into the cool operations of adding and subtracting," crystallized in the diagnosis "where they love they do not desire, and where they desire they cannot love" — a formula Gay explicitly flags as anticipating a Freudian split between tender and sensual currents that the bourgeoisophobes reached by instinct rather than theory.

Gay's own methodological statement — that "the historian must treat such verdicts with the utmost reserve" — is the passage that licenses everything else in the unit: it is the pivot between reporting the caricature and relativizing it.

Riehl's mid-century self-description of the Burger order as history's "bearer of justified social movements, of social reform" anchors the counter-tradition, made pointed by Gay's aside that Riehl "spoke not without satisfaction, for he counted himself a Burger" — apologetics as self-interest, just as the critique sometimes was projection.

Even the Manifesto's most quotable line of praise — that the bourgeoisie "rescued a considerable part of the population from the idiocy of rural life" — shows Marx and Engels unable to keep their own analysis fully hostile, which Gay uses as evidence that ambivalence, not univocal contempt, was the honest response even among the bourgeoisie's fiercest theorists.

Metternich's 1820 letter to Czar Alexander I, with its image of "this intermediate class" that takes "all sorts of disguises, uniting and subdividing as occasion offers," gives Gay his closing figure for a class that can be described but not pinned down — a formation held together by "coalescing under external pressure" rather than by shared essence, which resonates loosely with how modernity-machine treats a dominant order's coherence as an achieved, contested condition rather than a natural given — though Gay's frame is a class's reputation battle, not an infrastructural account of legibility or institutions.
