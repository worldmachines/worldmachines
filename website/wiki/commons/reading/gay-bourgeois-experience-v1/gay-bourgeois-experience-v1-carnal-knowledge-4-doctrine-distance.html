The Doctrine of Distance

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay names the “doctrine of distance” — the tacit rule that a nude became acceptable to bourgeois viewers in proportion to how mythologically, historically, or exotically remote it was from contemporary domestic life — and traces its workings from Ingres and Etty through Hiram Powers's Greek Slave to its rupture by Manet's contemporaneous, French, recognizable nudes.

Surveying the nineteenth century's vast public traffic in painted and sculpted nudes — from Prud'hon's ceiling allegories and Ingres's Turkish Bath to Etty's dairymaid-Venuses, Hiram Powers's Greek Slave, and Manet's scandal-provoking Olympia — Gay argues that the bourgeoisie's apparent comfort with nakedness in art rested on a specific, largely unconscious compromise he calls the doctrine of distance: a nude was permissible in proportion to how far mythology, history, religion, or exoticism removed it from the viewer's own time, place, and social world.

Themes

The doctrine of distance as a cultural defense mechanism. Gay's central analytic move is to name the tacit rule by which nineteenth-century audiences licensed themselves to look: "the more generalized and idealized the presentation of the human body in art, the more draped in elevated associations, the less likely it is to shock its viewers." Abstracting a body into a Venus, a Nymph, a Greek slave, or an Algerian odalisque let it carry erotic charge while officially carrying only aesthetic or historical meaning. This is one of Gay's recurring "defense" concepts (compare reticence and the privatization of experience elsewhere in the volume): a socially shared mechanism that manages anxiety without requiring anyone to admit what is being managed.

The doctrine is paradoxical, not merely repressive. Gay insists distance made art simultaneously less and more erotic: less, because the viewer could tell himself he was responding to a historical or literary message rather than a sexual invitation; more, because that same alibi let him leer at the nude in ways ordinary bodies could never permit. The mechanism is not simple prudish denial but a structured permission — displacement that both disguises and amplifies desire. Gay reads this as further evidence, alongside the diaries he draws on elsewhere in the volume, that respectable bourgeois were not sexually anaesthetic, only elaborately indirect.

Case studies show the compromise's range and its cynicism. Etty could be denounced by the Times for nudes that were "mere dirty flesh" and defended by Hamerton as merely pursuing his "simply artistic nature" — the same body, judged by how much cover its occasion supplied. Jean-Baptiste Clesinger's Woman Bitten by a Snake needed only a hastily added serpent to pass the Salon of 1847, prompting Gautier's dry observation that Clesinger had dared to exhibit a nude woman who was "neither a goddess, nor a nymph... but simply a woman" once the fig-leaf of a title was in place. Hiram Powers's Greek Slave is Gay's fullest illustration: Powers manufactured a scenario — a Christian captive stripped for a Turkish slave market, her piety intact — engineered so that, in his own words, no room would be "left for shame." The public obliged, treating the statue as "clothed all over with sentiment" even as it drew the largest audiences of any American sculpture of the century.

Manet breaks the treaty. Because the nudes in the Dejeuner sur l'herbe and Olympia were recognizably contemporary, French, and identifiable as real acquaintances of the painter rather than displaced into myth, critics reacted with genuine outrage rather than aesthetic tolerance. Gay reads their moral fury as inadvertently accurate criticism: the critics who accused Manet of refusing to idealize, or to distance, his naked young woman "understood his purposes better than they knew." Manet's contemporaneity, not any exceptional explicitness, is what broke the compromise — the doctrine of distance was never quite the same after Olympia. This is the chapter's clearest instance of an official convention cracking under pressure from a single insistently literal artist, decades before the wider fin-de-siecle unsettling of bourgeois certainties.

Even the fiercest moral crusaders needed the same compromise. Anthony Comstock, no friend to sensuality, still conceded that "the nude in art is not necessarily obscene, lewd or indecent" and made room for it — in a museum. Gay uses this to argue that the campaign against sensuality was self-limiting: the doctrine of distance was shared even by its ostensible opponents, which is part of why it held for so long.

The gap between visual "carnal knowledge" and actual sexual knowledge. The chapter closes on Edith Wharton's account of her mother's icy refusal, days before her wedding, to explain marital sex except by gesturing at art: "You've seen enough pictures and statues in your life. Haven't you noticed that men are made differently from women?" Gay's point is that ubiquitous nudity in respectable culture did not translate into usable knowledge for those — disproportionately women — who were denied the words to interpret it; distance protected the image from indecency at the cost of making it useless as instruction.

For World Machines, the doctrine of distance is a compact instance of how a dominant class culture manages an internal contradiction (avid interest in the body vs. an official ideology of decorum) through institutionalized workaround rather than either suppression or candor, and of how such a convention can hold for decades before a single figure (Manet) forces the contradiction into the open — a small, cultural-timescale echo of a world-machines convention cracking under its own success, though this is a decades-long shift in a visual convention, not a civilizational-infrastructure Dusk. The chapter's larger argument — that reticence and displacement, not repression, governed bourgeois sexuality, and that surface decorum concealed rather than eliminated desire — is also a clean instance of Gay's running rebuttal to michel-foucault's repressive hypothesis: where Foucault reads Victorian discourse-management as productive of sexuality through proliferating discourse, Gay reads it as a set of specific, nameable defenses (here, distance) deployed by people who already knew quite well what they wanted to look at.

Notable passages

The paragraph coining the term is the chapter's hinge: the "tacit treaty between artist and audience" that abstracts nudity from contemporary and intimate experience by lending it "alien glamor." Gay follows immediately with the paradox that distance is both prophylactic and enabling — the passage that gives the doctrine its analytic bite rather than leaving it a mere observation about taste. The Manet passage shows the doctrine failing in real time, with contemporary critics functioning, against their own intent, as accurate diagnosticians of what made Olympia intolerable. The Greek Slave passage shows the doctrine succeeding almost too well, manufacturing enough narrative cover that a fully nude figure could be received as "clothed... with sentiment." The Wharton exchange is the chapter's most human illustration of the doctrine's cost: art supplied images in abundance, but without permission to ask questions of them, the images taught nothing.
