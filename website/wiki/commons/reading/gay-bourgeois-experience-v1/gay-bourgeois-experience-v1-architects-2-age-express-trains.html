An Age of Express Trains: Change, Anxiety, and the Bourgeois Myth of Mobility

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Nineteenth-century change generated a diffuse bourgeois anxiety, diagnosed by Nietzsche, Freud, and Durkheim and voiced by witnesses from Gautier to Thackeray under the master metaphor of the express train, which coexisted with rather than contradicted the middle class's confident ideology of the self-made "architect of his own fortune."

This unit (Chapter 1, section 2 of Education of the Senses) argues that the nineteenth-century bourgeoisie's own dynamism was the source of a deep, pervasive anxiety, and that this anxiety coexisted with, rather than being cancelled by, the middle class's confident ideology of self-made mobility. Gay opens with Nietzsche, Freud, and Durkheim as the era's most acute diagnosticians of a paradox contemporaries found genuinely surprising: change for the better, not only misfortune, could generate malaise. He then assembles a chorus of contemporary witnesses — Gautier, Disraeli, Lamartine, Zola, Lady Knightley, Mill, Tocqueville, Amiel, Burckhardt, Bülow, Billroth, Thackeray — testifying to a shared sense that the century's pace had outrun anyone's capacity to master it, with the railway serving as the era's master image of accelerating, anxiety-making, faintly erotic velocity. The chapter closes by turning to the bourgeoisie's countervailing self-image: the democratized "myth of mobility," the self-made "architect of his own fortune," an Enlightenment-descended confidence that made the anxious and the buoyant two faces of the same bourgeois mood. Gay ends by signaling that the rest of the volume will pursue these tensions into Freud's "inaccessible domains of sexuality and aggression."

Themes

Change itself, not only bad news, produces anxiety. Gay's theoretical spine is the near-simultaneous discovery by Nietzsche, Freud, and Durkheim that anxiety is not simply a response to misfortune. Nietzsche traced fear to "chance, the uncertain, the sudden"; Freud generalized this into a theory of anxiety as a warning signal that mobilizes psychic defenses, and observed that the instinctual apparatus is inherently conservative, so that even desired change costs "expenditures of mental energy"; Durkheim's anomie names the disorientation that follows the collapse of "firm boundaries and recognizable rules of conduct" — a disorientation that "sudden prosperity" can produce quite as reliably as disaster. Gay treats this as the theoretical key to the whole century's affect, not a marginal curiosity.

Bourgeois anxiety was diffuse and endemic, not just a reaction to named enemies. Organized labor, radical parties, feminists, and avant-garde intellectuals sharpened bourgeois unease by the 1880s, but Gay insists the anxiety long preceded and exceeded these threats. Théophile Gautier's observation at Compiègne in 1861 — that the bourgeois "does not know too clearly just how he should behave" and "is not sure of his role" — recasts bourgeois anxiety as a crisis of class self-definition as much as a fear of external adversaries. Gay reads this as symptomatic of a century in which "the unconscious dimensions of experience assumed undeniable prominence."

The railway is the century's master metaphor for velocity and dread, with an erotic undertow. Hans von Bülow's 1874 quip calling his age "an age of express trains" (the chapter's title) crystallizes a cliché among the cultured; Gay adds that "the erotic desires and fears stimulated by the rhythmic experience of the train ride were never far beneath the surface of such epithets" — an early instance of the volume's larger argument that anxiety and eroticism were braided together beneath respectable surfaces. Thackeray's mock-elegy for the "antediluvian" pre-railway generation shows the same speed being mourned as an epochal loss even by those who benefited from it.

Religion's persistence complicates a simple secularization story. Against Marx's dictum that religion is the opium of the masses, Gay counters that religion "became once again the opium of the middle classes," particularly in Britain, the US, and Germany — a defense against, not merely a survival despite, the anxieties of change. He treats this alongside "modern cults as Christian Science and Theosophy" and "the intensifying passion for a science of society" as parallel searches for "guides through the jungle of modernity."

A countervailing ideology of hope — the myth of mobility — was the bourgeoisie's own answer to its anxiety. Gay traces a semantic shift from static estate-language ("estates," "orders," Stände) to dynamic, mobility-implying language ("classes," "movements," "interest groups") as itself a symptom and instrument of a newly fluid social order. This is embodied in the popularized figure of the self-made "architect of his own fortune" — from Enlightenment maxims (man as "master of his fate") through John Davies's 1827 address, Dickens's Mr. Micawber, and the success literature of Horatio Alger and Samuel Smiles. For the beneficiaries of this myth, Gay notes pointedly, "anomie was improbable and anxiety, if they experienced it at all, underground" — mobility ideology as a psychological defense in its own right, available mainly to the fortunate.

The chapter pivots from surface material explanation toward Freud's unconscious. Gay notes that most contemporary critics blamed "material realities" — speed, crowded cities, accelerating invention — for the age's malaise, without probing further, "until Sigmund Freud postulated the overriding influence of elemental, largely unconscious forces." This closing move sets up the rest of the volume's method: reading bourgeois respectability's public confidence against the private, unconscious material (diaries, letters) that the book will draw on.

Notable passages

The opening thesis (c1) frames the whole unit: the century's "vertiginous mobility" that made the bourgeoisie triumphant also made it, "too often," its own casualty — architects and martyrs of the same process. The Nietzsche–Freud–Durkheim passage (c2) supplies the theoretical apparatus Gay uses throughout the book: anxiety and anomie as structural products of change, not personal failings. Gautier's line about the bourgeois "not sure of his role" (c3) is the unit's sharpest image of class identity as an ongoing, anxious performance rather than a settled fact — a point with obvious bearing on how a class's self-understanding is constructed and contested while it is busy becoming dominant. The Bülow/express-train passage (c4) is the chapter's titular image, explicitly yoking speed-anxiety to eroticism. Thackeray's antediluvian conceit (c5) shows the same rupture dramatized as generational loss rather than mere unease. Finally, the "architect of his own fortune" passage (c6) gives the ideological counterweight to all the anxiety cataloged earlier — the self-made-man myth that let the fortunate metabolize a dynamic age as opportunity rather than threat.

World Machines resonances

The chapter's semantic-shift observation — that "estates," "orders," and Stände gave way to "classes," "movements," and "interest groups" — is a small, concrete instance of the drive toward legibility that modernity-machine names: renaming a social order in terms that make it countable, sortable, and trackable (a "movement" can be joined, measured, organized; an "estate" could only be inherited). The resonance is partial: Gay's legibility is social-categorical and ideological rather than administrative or cartographic, but the underlying logic — that dynamism gets its own vocabulary once it becomes normal — rhymes with the Modernity Machine's optimization for readability at scale. Separately, the chapter's closing gesture toward Freud's "inaccessible domains of sexuality and aggression" is the doorway to the volume's larger argument against the stereotype of Victorian sexual repression, for which michel-foucault's repressive-hypothesis critique is Gay's natural foil — though that argument is developed later in the volume, not within this unit itself, which stays at the level of generalized anxiety rather than sexuality specifically.
