A Note on the Sexual Symbols in Mabel Loomis Todd’s Diaries

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay decodes the private symbols Mabel Loomis Todd used in her diaries for menstruation, intercourse, and orgasm, and uses the shifting meaning of one symbol, “Sem.,” to defend his claim that she went into marriage “technically” a virgin and to expose David Todd's anxiety over semen as a finite, dangerous-to-waste resource.

In this short appendix, Gay lays out the private code Mabel Loomis Todd used in her diaries to record menstruation, sexual intercourse, and orgasm, and works through specific dated entries to argue that the code documents an active, contraceptively cautious, orgasm-conscious erotic life between Mabel and David Todd — evidence for the book’s larger argument that middle-class propriety coexisted with erotic candor in private.

Themes

A private erotic ledger. Todd recorded her body and her marriage in a running cipher: an “x” and later a dot enclosed in a circle for menstruation, “#” plus a running numeral for intercourse, and abbreviations like “f.m.” (“for me”) and “o.” that Gay reads as markers of her own orgasm. He treats the “rhythmic rise and fall” of the numbered entries themselves, not just the labels, as corroborating evidence — a philological argument built out of a pattern in a diary.

Contraception as context. Gay notes, almost in passing, that the couple relied on “a combination of the rhythm method and coitus interruptus” — the material stakes behind the record-keeping itself: pleasure pursued under a self-managed reproductive calculus, not left to chance.

The “Sem.” conundrum and the virginity question. The most interpretively fraught symbol, “Sem.,” changes meaning across the couple’s life stage: heavy petting before the 1879 wedding, marital intercourse just after it, and — during David’s later absences — masturbation, with “a”/“A” standing in for “alone.” Gay uses this shifting referent to defend an earlier claim in the main text that Mabel went into marriage “technically” a virgin, turning a note on notation into corroborating evidence for a claim about her body.

Male anxiety over the “spermatic economy.” David Todd’s request that Mabel “mark for me against yesterday something...you know in your little book” — read by Gay as a demand that she log his masturbation — exposes an internalized, culturally shared anxiety: the belief, “held generally...among medical men and ‘informed’ laymen,” that orgasm was an “expenditure” of a finite, valuable substance whose “excessive” loss carried physical and psychological danger. It is Gay’s own thesis in miniature — a defense (obsessive self-monitoring) erected over unacknowledged anxiety, inside an intimate and otherwise erotically frank marriage.

A partial resonance. The diary’s numeral-and-symbol system for tracking bodily/sexual events is a small, private echo of the record-keeping and quantification associated with modernity-machine legibility — except turned inward, kept secret rather than made visible to any authority, which is exactly the privatization of experience Gay’s book traces throughout: legibility as a personal, hidden technology rather than a state one. The appendix is also implicitly anti-michel-foucault: rather than repression producing silence, Gay reads compulsive, careful private recording as itself a sign of an active, self-aware erotic life managed by its own grammar.

Notable passages

  • The opening catalogue of symbols (x, the circled dot, #, f.m., o.) and the paired April 28–May 1, 1881 entries Gay reads as marking two consecutive nights of orgasm, treated as internally corroborating evidence.
  • The “Sem.” puzzle: David Todd’s June 30, 1882 letter asking Mabel to “mark for me...something...you know in your little book,” and the string of “Sem.” entries from courtship (1879) through the couple’s later separations that Gay uses to argue the symbol’s meaning shifted with circumstance.
  • Gay’s interpretive payoff: “Sem.” tracked petting, then intercourse, then (alone) masturbation, and David’s insistence on logging his own masturbation reflects a shared medical belief that semen was a finite resource whose “excessive” loss was dangerous.
