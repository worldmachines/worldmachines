The Mysterious Sex and the March of Women

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Nineteenth-century bourgeois men defended their eroding dominance by mythologizing woman as an eternally “mysterious sex,” even as women pressed forward—against coverture and matrimonial law, exclusion from suffrage, the professions, and the universities—an advance Gay dramatizes through the career of M. Carey Thomas.

This unit opens Chapter 2 of Education of the Senses, “Offensive Women and Defensive Men,” which diagnoses men's insistence that woman is an eternally incomprehensible “mysterious sex” as a defense mounted against a real historical fact: women's steady advance on ground men had long monopolized. Section 1, “Women on the March,” then surveys that advance and the resistance it met across law, suffrage, paid work, and the universities, closing with an extended case study of M. Carey Thomas as the embodiment of the chapter's argument.

Themes

The “mysterious sex” as a male defense mechanism. Gay traces the cliché of woman's incomprehensibility from Hume through Amiel, Alexandre Dumas fils, the American physician Nicholas Francis Cooke, and even Freud's 1926 talk of the “dark continent” of female sexuality, arguing the trope functioned less as observation than as projection—a way of managing anxiety as women's actual social position shifted. He makes the stakes explicit: men's constructions of woman “Men’s highly charged construction of the mysterious sex in art, in literature, in society,” instantiate his working definition of experience “the commanding share of subterranean feelings in the making of social postures and political ideologies.” This is a natural foil to Foucault's repressive-hypothesis critique: where Foucault reads the era's proliferating discourse about women's bodies as a technique of power, Gay reads the same clichés as symptom—evidence of a defense under strain rather than a discourse in confident command. See michel-foucault.

Legal subjection as the concrete substrate of the anxiety. Behind the mystification lay hard law: the doctrine that husband and wife are “one flesh” (with the husband speaking for the whole), the 1857 Matrimonial Causes Act's double standard for adultery, France's 1816–1884 abolition of divorce, and Prussian and Bavarian codes that let a husband “chastise her moderately” and gave him her earnings and inherited property outright. Mill's Subjection of Women (1869) catalogued the wife as “the actual bondservant of her husband”, no less bound than “slaves commonly so called”—and repair came only piecemeal, country by country, through Married Women's Property Acts (1870, 1882, 1893) and comparable Continental reforms decades apart.

The public/private gap runs through the reformers themselves. Freud, who translated Mill's Subjection of Women into German, privately told his fiancée Martha Bernays that Mill lacked the “sense of the absurd” and that, whatever “law and custom” owed women, “nature had determined woman’s destiny through beauty, charm, and sweetness”—a small, pointed instance of Gay's ambivalence thesis: an enlightened public gesture (translating the era's key feminist text) coexisting with unreconstructed private conviction.

Privatized rage in the era's private record. An unnamed thirty-three-year-old housewife's 1880 diary from Hallam, Connecticut supplies the chapter's rawest evidence of ordinary domestic exasperation—“Monotonous day,” “Chained in my cell,” “the mountains diminish into molehills.” Gay reads her laconic entries as proof that “the accepted customs of bourgeois culture, though displaying a crusty vitality, proved less than immortal”—a small, unglamorous companion to the more celebrated diary evidence (Mabel Loomis Todd) elsewhere in the book, and central to Gay's method of using private documents against public ideology.

Uneven advance across law, vote, profession, and university. Suffrage crept forward in geographically eccentric jumps (Wyoming 1890, Colorado 1893, New Zealand 1893, South Australia 1894) while the “principal countries of the Western world” waited until the 1920s, France until 1944. Professional advance was similarly lopsided: women novelists published under male pseudonyms (George Sand, George Eliot, Currer Bell), teaching absorbed women precisely because they could be paid less, and medicine admitted them only grudgingly (258 women on England's medical register by 1900; 5.6 percent of American physicians). Gay treats access to the universities—Oberlin (1833), Mount Holyoke, Cornell's “annex” Sage College, degree-less study at Cambridge and Oxford, the Sorbonne's 1880 admission to lectures—as mattering more than suffrage: “the key to the woman’s cause.”

M. Carey Thomas as case study in activity over passivity. Thomas's career—childhood fury at “the usual cant” of woman's shrine-like exaltation, exclusion from real coursework at Johns Hopkins, flight to Leipzig and then Zurich, a summa cum laude “no woman, and very few men,” had ever taken, and finally the presidency of Bryn Mawr—dramatizes “the psychological benefits of activity as against passivity.” Gay frames her as the American vanguard of a trajectory he expects bourgeois Europe to follow: “Europe is Americanizing itself.”

World Machines resonance (partial). The chapter's jagged, non-synchronous timeline—legal reform outrunning suffrage in some countries, universities outrunning both, one nation's revolution stalling while another leaps ahead—reads as a dominant class culture's assumptions entering something like a gramsci-gap: coverture and the “one flesh” doctrine are visibly discredited (Mill, Cobbe, Calm) yet still legally enforced, while no settled replacement doctrine has consolidated. This is an approximate fit, not a clean transition between world machines—it is an internal fracture within one machine's social order rather than a succession between machines. The chapter's reliance on tabulated statistics (physician percentages, women graduated by year) to track and legitimate the advance also echoes the modernity-machine's core operation of legibility—making populations countable in order to make claims about them actionable—though Gay uses the numbers to expose how little had changed rather than to celebrate administrative order.

Notable passages

c1 — Gay's definition of experience. The paragraph closing the chapter's introduction turns the “mysterious sex” material into methodological credo, naming men's charged constructions of woman as the very stuff of “experience” in Gay's technical sense: mind meeting world as unconscious dilemma meets conscious perception.

c2 — the Hallam, Connecticut diary. The anonymous housewife's terse, ironic entries (“Monotonous day,” the quotation marks around “‘The worst is over’ now”) are Gay's rawest piece of evidence that ordinary domestic contentment was often a performance laid over real exasperation.

c3 — Mill's Subjection of Women. Gay quotes Mill's 1869 indictment of coverture at length—the wife as bondservant, incapable of holding property or asserting rights over her own children—as the legal reality the mystery-woman rhetoric obscured.

c4 — Freud's private conventionality. The passage on Marianne Weber, Helene Lange, and Freud (Mill's German translator, who privately dismissed Mill's argument to Martha Bernays) shows the gap between an intellectual's public modernity and private orthodoxy about women's “destiny.”

c5 — M. Carey Thomas's origin. Thomas's childhood insistence that “Born in 1857 in Baltimore into an educated Quaker family, Carey Thomas was an eldest…” and her adult verdict that “For her, passivity was the supreme crime.” frame the extended case study Gay uses to embody the chapter's thesis in a single biography.
