Manhood in Danger: The Backlash Against Nineteenth-Century Feminism

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Nineteenth-century anti-feminist ridicule and panic in the United States, England, France, and Germany expose a widespread bourgeois male fear of women's emancipation that Gay interprets as a reaction formation shielding the era's parallel myth of female sexual anesthesia.

Gay traces the international reaction against the organized woman's movement from its 1848 Seneca Falls origins through the 1910s, arguing that the movement was itself born of insult and restraint rather than aggression, and that the ridicule, panic, and sexualized caricature it provoked in the United States, England, France, and Germany betray a deep, largely unconscious bourgeois male fear of women's advancement — a fear he ultimately reads as a psychoanalytic reaction formation, the mirror image of the era's insistence that women had no sexual appetites at all.

Themes

The "offensive" was a delayed defense, not an aggression. Gay opens by overturning the contemporaries' framing of feminism as unprovoked attack: the movement's founding act, Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott's 1848 Seneca Falls convention, followed eight years after the two women were refused seats as delegates to the 1840 World Anti-Slavery Convention in London. He is careful to note exceptions — Garrison joining the women in the gallery, Breuer's respect for "Anna O.'s" intelligence — to block any "general rule about man's fear of woman."

Ridicule was the era's default weapon, and it was everywhere. American clergymen and editorialists ("hybrid species, half man and half woman"), Punch's campaign against "Bloomerism," and Munich's Fliegende Blätter cartoons of women sprouting from flowerpots with pens and cigars all convert anxiety about feminism into sexualized mockery — the caricatured "man-woman" or "University-woman" functioning across three countries as the same displaced fear in different comic registers.

When the jokes stopped, the fear showed its full force. Gay treats Stevenson Archer's 1872 speech to the House of Representatives — warning of "a hundred thousand 'whirlwinds in petticoats'" with "talons" who could throw "the big man" onto his back — as the moment anti-feminist rhetoric drops "all pretense at humor" to reveal, in his own gloss, "the little boy concealed in the nineteenth-century man" afraid of "his powerful unpredictable mother."

France sexualized the fear most explicitly, as castration. The Goncourt brothers' contempt for women's intelligence, Zola's procession of fatal seductresses, and above all the literal castration scene in Germinal (miners' wives cutting off a grocer's penis) crystallize what Gay calls the "woman as vampire, man as victim" current of French male feeling — the most undisguised version of the anxiety running through the whole unit.

Fiction rehearsed the nightmare in order to defuse it. Sir Walter Besant's 1882 novel The Revolt of Man imagines a full reversal of gender roles — women alone educated, enfranchised, and in public office — only to restore "the old order" through a conventional marriage plot at the close, letting readers experience the fantasy of catastrophe while guaranteeing its containment.

The interpretive payoff: reaction formation links this fear to the myth of the frigid woman. Gay's closing move ties this whole unit back to the book's larger argument: denying women erotic desire "was to safeguard man's sexual adequacy," and the same unconscious machinery that produced the myth of female sexual anesthesia produced the panic here — feminism triggering "childish fears of maternal retaliation" experienced, in the last instance, as "a threat of castration." This is close kin to the "repressive hypothesis" that michel-foucault would later dispute, though Gay's account works through individual psychoanalytic defense (reaction formation, unconscious fantasy) rather than Foucault's claim of a discourse that proliferates around sex rather than silencing it; both agree the nineteenth century's official silence about female desire was doing active, productive work rather than simply repressing something.

Notable passages

The unit's spine passages, in order, are: the reframing of feminism's origin as a "delayed response to a wounding, gratuitous insult" rather than an act of aggression; the New York Herald's 1853 editorial insinuating that feminists were too "repulsive" to find husbands; Stevenson Archer's "whirlwinds in petticoats" speech, the unit's most literal image of manhood under siege; Besant's Revolt of Man, staging and then repairing a full role-reversal; the Goncourt/Zola "woman as vampire" material culminating in the Germinal castration scene; and Gay's closing paragraph naming reaction formation as the mechanism linking the fear of feminism to the denial of female sexuality elsewhere in the book. Together they move from origin story, through comic ridicule, to naked panic, to Gay's own diagnosis — the same arc as the chapter's argument.
