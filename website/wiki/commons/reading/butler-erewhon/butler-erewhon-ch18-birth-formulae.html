Birth Formulae: the contract signed by the unborn

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The narrator reports the Erewhonian belief that the unborn choose to be born and torment parents into it, and the ritual by which a newborn, then a fourteen-year-old, is made to sign a legal formula taking all blame for existing, a coerced contract defended by a Professor of Unreason as a necessary compromise.

The narrator, relaying what he learned from Mr. Nosnibor and dinner guests rather than from Arowhena, reports that the Erewhonians believe in pre-existence and that each person freely chose, in a prior state, to be born. Parents are therefore victims of the unborn, and to shift responsibility off themselves they require every child to supply a written "birth formula" indemnifying them. The chapter walks through the document's wording, the melancholy feast on the "final importunity", the proxy signature given for the crying infant, the pressured re-signing at about fourteen with the help of the Colleges of Unreason, and the narrator's exchange with a Professor of Unreason, who defends the practice as an education in compromise. The Professor's reply is voiced by an Erewhonian; the narrator is the one who objects, and he is himself a satirical figure, so the objection is not simply Butler's thesis.

Themes

Belief held as institution rather than conviction. The narrator observes that when Erewhonians profess certainty about something on which they build a system of practice, "they seldom quite believe in it", and that for them it was "a disease not to believe as they did". The mythology of the unborn works as a loophole in responsibility rather than as a creed. A likely satirical target, offered as a reading, is Victorian doctrines whose social function outran belief in them.

Consent manufactured after the fact. The formula turns parental choice into the child's guilt. The infant's cry counts as "a good sign, as showing a consciousness of guilt", a friend signs for it, and at fourteen the child is bribed and intimidated into signing for himself. This resonates loosely with contract-theory questions about tacit consent (compare thomas-hobbes and jean-jacques-rousseau), though Butler's mode is mockery of pretended consent rather than a theory of it, and the link is only a gloss.

Inconsistent standards of capacity. The most important document of a life is signed at an age when law and elders would let no one bind him to the smallest obligation because he is judged too young to know what he is about. The narrator uses this to expose the arrangement's bad faith.

Compromise as the lesson taught by concealment. The Professor of Unreason argues that language cannot render thought without "a jarring and a harshness somewhere", that affirmations rarely bear literal reading, and that the boy must learn to compromise. The narrator's counter-charge is that teachers who assert as certain what they are uncertain of damage the pupil's truth-sense. The closing line, "But they never tell this to the boy", marks the hypocrisy: the institution works only if its subject believes in it. Satirical targets plausibly include confirmation and subscription to articles of faith in Victorian schooling, offered as a hedged reading.

Legal elaboration as ritual status. Lawyers have for ages perfected the formula, and its printing on paper or parchment makes it "a test of his social position". Law, status and ritual merge, loosely rhyming with legibility as paperwork, though this is thin and not a legibility program in the Legibility sense.

Notable passages

The unborn as plaguers (c1). The foundational premise: the unborn flutter about the married and give no peace until taken under protection, so that otherwise it would be "a monstrous freedom" to impose life's chances on someone without option.

Certainty without belief (c2). The narrator's general remark on Erewhonian psychology: they stop their noses to a rat in a cherished institution.

The formula's text (c3). The unborn child, of "wanton depravity and restlessness", confesses to plaguing two contented people "with malice aforethought", and entreats pardon. This is the satire's comic centre.

Proxy signature (c4). The crying infant, the straightener who reads the formula, and the friend who signs on the child's behalf.

Signature at fourteen (c5). Half-bribery, half-intimidation, with the Colleges of Unreason as accomplices, and the contradiction with the law's own view of capacity.

The Professor's defence (c6). Compromise as education, defended with an argument from the imperfection of translation, and the closing remark that the boy is never told.

Does not fit: nothing here concerns machines directly; the chapter is a social-institutional satire that sets up the mythology of the unborn the next chapter extracts.
