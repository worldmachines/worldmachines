The narrator's return and the Erewhon Evangelisation scheme

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The narrator's rescue at sea, return to England and marriage are told in a sequence of lies and evasions, and the book closes on his prospectus for converting Erewhon by shipping its people to Queensland as labour, a scheme the satire exposes by quoting a real 1872 newspaper defence of the Polynesian labour trade and by the P.S. reappearance of Chowbok as a celebrated native missionary.

The closing chapter does no more machine philosophy. The narrator and Arowhena are picked up from the sea by an Italian guano ship, then transferred to an English wool ship, and he lies to both crews about where they came from, because he is "determined that no one should hear of Erewhon, or have the chance of getting there before me". Back in England he finds his mother dead (her death hastened by Chowbok's invented report of his drowning), his relations cool, and he earns a living writing tracts and magazine stories. He then unfolds a joint-stock plan to convert the Erewhonians: take a gunboat and steamer up the river, tempt them with Queensland wages, ship seven or eight hundred as contract labour to sugar-growers, and give them Church Catechism in the planters' households. He cites a January 1872 Times paragraph in which the Governor of Queensland defended the Polynesian labour trade. A postscript has him find Chowbok, now "the Rev. William Habakkuk", cheered at Exeter Hall as a native missionary, and end with an urgent appeal to subscribe.

Themes

The narrator is the final target of the satire. Everything in the plan is told in his own earnest voice. His moral vocabulary ("business is business", "saving souls and filling their own pockets at one and the same moment") exposes him, not Erewhon. This follows his earlier habits of piety plus acquisitiveness. It is the narrator's scheme, not Butler's thesis, and the irony runs against the narrator. Butler's own voice is absent here; this is the narrator's voice throughout. The reading that the target is Victorian missionary and commercial enterprise is a hedged inference, but the Times quotation anchors it in a real contemporary controversy.

Mirror inversion completes itself. Erewhon punished illness as crime and kept a hollow Musical Banks religion. Here the English side is shown doing something comparable: religious instruction is explicitly justified as a way to "put a stop to any uneasy feeling" about how labour was obtained. The Erewhonian absurdities have earlier been a mirror of England; this chapter turns the mirror around and lets England's own practice show up as the bigger absurdity. This is a reading, not something the text states.

Credibility, secrecy and the unreliable teller. The narrator admits to misleading Captain Gianni, lying about a balloon, a Milord and a Russian Countess, and later a Peruvian lady, and says his falsehoods would make him miserable "were I not sustained by the consolations of my religion". The frame story that vouches for Erewhon rests on a witness who confesses to lying about it. It also makes the closing scheme more suspect: he wants exclusive access to Erewhon.

Evangelism as an extractive enterprise. The plan is a limited-liability company with a prospectus, share capital and dividends, armed boats because the Erewhonians "have no gunpowder", and prisoners of war. Conversion and profit are not in tension for the narrator, and the passage about shipping converts back to Erewhon when too old to work makes the machinery of exploitation the vehicle of faith. Little here resonates with the machine material of the book (the Book of the Machines). The nearest World Machines echo is the way an enterprise, a company and its financial instruments run on people as its working parts, loosely in line with world-machines thinking about infrastructure that enrols humans; but Butler here is satirising colonial labour recruitment, not machine evolution, so the rhyme is loose. The narrator's ideal of making Erewhon legible to capital and church (statistics, catechism, contracts) also touches Legibility, though again only distantly.

Chowbok's reappearance. The postscript turns the one Erewhonian-side figure the narrator had treated as a liar and drunkard into a celebrated convert. The narrator's response is not shame but jealousy over the claimed discovery of the lost tribes, then complacent satisfaction that his own earlier baptism "had not been wholly without effect". Whether the audience's applause reflects real conversion or a market for exotic converts is left open; the satire plainly targets the fashion for native-missionary meetings.

Notable passages

The lying cover story at sea (c1) shows the narrator's willingness to "take any cue" the captain gives, and the captain's own inventions about the siege of Paris, so that the book's origin story is built from other people's misunderstandings.

The prospectus (c2, c3) lays out the company, the fifty-thousand-pound capital, the omission of the lost-tribes theory from the prospectus because "business is business", then the Queensland pitch and the cheerful anticipation of capture and contract.

The religious instruction rationale (c4) states that the catechism and psalm-singing serve to allay public unease and to comfort the shareholders.

The Times paragraph (c5) grounds the scheme in a real report of 1872 and is the book's main external anchor, in which a Governor says that religion would settle the uneasy feeling.

The Exeter Hall postscript (c6) gives the Chowbok reveal and the narrator's self-regard.
