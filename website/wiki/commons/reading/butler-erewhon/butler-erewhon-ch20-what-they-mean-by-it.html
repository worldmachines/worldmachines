The narrator on parents, schools of Unreason, and money

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The narrator comments on the Erewhonian birth-formula mythology, then drifts through parent-child estrangement, the schools of Unreason, money as the sacrament of duty, and a radical scheme to let the young flog the old, in a voice that is itself a satirical target.

The narrator closes off the unborn mythology of the preceding chapter by calling it an exaggerated, gloomy picture that no Erewhonian fully believes, and observes that Erewhonians habitually do not mean what they profess to regard as indisputable. He then turns to what the birth formula means in practice: it is seldom invoked, yet family relations in Erewhon are, he says, less happy than in Europe. The rest of the chapter is the narrator's own digressive social commentary (on compulsory affection, schooling, infanticide, wealth, and a mooted scheme of week-about rule by old and young), moving between Erewhonian practice and his plain preference for English remedies.

Themes

Professed belief versus practice. The narrator says it is "one of their peculiarities that they very often do not believe or mean things which they profess to regard as indisputable." The birth-formula theory exists to justify punishing the sick ("it is all his own doing"), but in practice habit softens parents. This is the book's recurring split between official doctrine and lived behaviour; read as satire, the likely Victorian target is conventional piety about family and respectability, though the text does not say so.

Parenthood without a talisman. The narrator argues that the word "parent" generates no affection by itself, and that Erewhon's proverbs treat "compulsory affection" as the root of deepest anguish. His own aside about his future child, offered as a "hostage," turns the criticism on himself. This is the narrator's opinion, not an Erewhonian view and not obviously Butler's thesis; note the narrator himself says he cannot see why the Erewhonians stopped short of punishing moral and intellectual disease, and that his urge to convert them is a national trait he cannot escape.

Schools of Unreason and Ydgrun. The narrator blames hypothetical education, which Ydgrun-worship (respectability) compels people to buy, for making children expensive and useless, and for a growing practice of limiting families by infanticide. He proposes apprenticeship in the workshop instead. This is the narrator's English-flavoured reform argument; Erewhonian education is the mirror of Victorian schooling and the hint of a satirical target (the universities) is a reading only. Habit as a force that softens theory has a faint rhyme with habit-and-identity, though here it is merely domestic.

Money as sacrament, reported and endorsed. Erewhonians exempt a man earning over £20,000 a year from taxation, and say that money is "the symbol of duty" and "the sacrament of having done for mankind that which mankind wanted." The narrator, who has by now been changed by Erewhon, endorses the idea and inverts a scriptural saying. Because he is a mercenary, self-satisfied narrator, the endorsement is as much the object of satire as the Erewhonian view; the chapter does not settle which.

Reverence for 'our masters'. The narrator says the irreverent-sounding passage is "conceived in a spirit of the most utter reverence" for "the things which are, which mould us and fashion us," and which "will punish us if we do not heed them." This is a moment where the unreflective deference to what has power resembles, loosely, the reverence for institutions the book mocks elsewhere; it is not machine-specific here, and should not be read as a link to the later Book of the Machines.

The week-about radicals. A party proposes alternating weeks of rule by old and young, with the young allowed corporal punishment of the old. The narrator reports it as proof of "the utter perversion of the Erewhonian mind", an irony, since he elsewhere admires Erewhonian severity on money.

Notable passages

The passage on stopping "half way" shows the narrator's own proselytizing instinct as a national character trait. The parenthood passage ends with the hostage joke. The schools-of-Unreason passage links schooling, respectability, and infanticide. The money passage is the chapter's most quotable inversion. The reverence passage sets up an ambiguity that readers should keep open instead of resolving it into a single message.
