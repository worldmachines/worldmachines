The Book of the Machines, concluded: determinism, dependence, and the extra-corporeal limb

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The Erewhonian professor's treatise on machines concludes by arguing from determinism that man and machine differ in degree rather than kind, that humans are already bound to steam and will be domesticated by machines, and that Erewhon must destroy three centuries of invention; one rival answer reframes machines as man's extra-corporeal limbs, and the narrator reports that the destroyers won.

The chapter finishes the narrator's "translation" of an Erewhonian professor's treatise (framed throughout by the narrator, who omits an "untranslatable" taxonomy of machine species and an "obscure argument" on who drives the driver). The treatise argues that machines are a new phase of life: will and spontaneity are only names for ignorance of causes, so man's difference from the machine is "one rather of degree than of kind"; machines are improving by selection toward self-regulation; humanity is already dependent on them and will end as their domesticated servants and reproductive agents; and the only sane response is to stop mechanical progress by destroying three hundred years of invention. The narrator then reports the single serious rebuttal, which says machines are man's own "extra-corporeal limbs", and a further extension of that view in which wealth equals bodily organisation. The rebutter loses, and the country destroys every invention more than 271 years old (settled after a wrangle over a mangle), after which come reactionary civil wars.

Themes

Determinism dissolves the line between man and machine. The Erewhonian writer argues that a man is "the resultant and exponent of all the forces that have been brought to bear upon him", that chance and fortune are "only words whereby we escape the admission of our own ignorance", and that the engine-driver is moved by food and warmth just as the engine is. The step that matters is the claim that regularity is therefore no proof an object lacks vitality. This is reasoning by analogy pushed to the point where it proves too much, which is arguably the satire; the narrator's tobacco-pipe anecdote at the opening, in which an Erewhonian reads a pipe's bowl-rim as a "rudimentary organ" and predicts it will become "an ornamental leaf or scroll, or even a butterfly", mocks the same method of Darwinian analogy applied to artefacts. Yet the narrator adds that on his return "my friend was right", so the parody is not simply dismissive.

Machine evolution is selection for provision against emergencies. Machines are "still in their infancy; they are mere skeletons without muscles and flesh"; self-oiling, the governor, the fly-wheel and railway buffers are "being selected for perpetuity", and the writer extrapolates "a hundred thousand years". This is a Darwinian-without-a-designer picture of artefacts, which echoes dennett-darwins-dangerous-idea and design-space loosely; but here the selecting agent is human demand rather than blind process, and the writer never says what would make machines want anything. Butler's own footnote (the narrator's, here) says he is collecting engine-drivers' talk of machines' "tricks and idiosyncrasies" "not as believing in the Erewhonian Professor's theory, but from the interest of the subject", a hedge that keeps the ambiguity open.

Mutual constitution and lock-in. The most forward-looking passage: "it is the machines which act upon man and make him man, as much as man who has acted upon and made the machines." Withdrawing steam now would mean anarchy, because population has grown on its strength. This resonates with tools-that-shape-thought, extended-mind and, less exactly, with the way world-machines are infrastructures that people cannot simply step out of; but the professor frames it as a threat of supersession by creatures, not as an infrastructure humans co-inhabit.

Domestication, with the machines needing us to reproduce. Men may become to machines what the horse and dog are to us; machines will "require our services in the reproduction and education of their young", in feeding, tending and burying them. Then the satirical turn: the slow, comfortable bondage is rationalised by the mass of men who "will acquiesce in any arrangement which gives them better food and clothing at a cheaper rate", and the professor rejects this acquiescence on grounds of pride, not evidence: he would lose "all self-respect" if an ancestor were another kind of being. His remedy is chosen as a compromise number: three hundred years of invention, with a wish for five hundred. The haggling over the date shows the argument's authority being politically negotiated; the narrator does not endorse it.

The counter-argument: machines as limbs. The rival writer says "man was a machinate mammal", that machines are "extra-corporeal limbs", and that civilisation and mechanical progress "advanced hand in hand". This is nearly the extended-mind and corporeal-incorporeal-machines intuition, and goes against the professor's us-versus-them frame. It does not fit cleanly with convivial-tools or ivan-illich, since the rebuttal is not about scale or control; and its own tail turns satirical, as the same writer's classification of men by horse-power makes wealth equal organisation ("to the rich, matter is immaterial"), reading rich men's deference-worthiness as a rung on the scale of animal life. The likely target is Victorian reverence for wealth, a reading rather than something the text states.

Outcome. The professor's side wins: this is what "led to the destruction of machinery throughout Erewhon". Compare useless-machine for halting machines by decree, though no Erewhonian here defends a machine for its own sake.

Notable passages

  • The determinism passage ("A man is the resultant and exponent...") is the premise from which everything else follows.
  • The driver-and-engine passage ends in "spontaneity is only a term for man's ignorance of the gods".
  • The degree-not-kind passage names selection-for-perpetuity of self-regulating features.
  • The dependence and domestication passage, from "The misery is that man has been blind so long already" to the machines' use of man to reproduce, is the closest thing in Erewhon to a world machine that recruits humans.
  • The close of the treatise ("The power of custom is enormous") gives the slow-capture scenario and the three-hundred-year prescription.
  • The machinate-mammal rebuttal supplies the limb picture of tools.
