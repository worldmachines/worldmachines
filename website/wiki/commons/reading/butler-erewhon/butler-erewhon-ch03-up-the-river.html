The Ascent to the Main Range

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The narrator and his Indian guide Chowbok ride up the river into the gorge, cross a saddle, find only worthless country above, and spend three weeks prospecting the glaciered main range until Chowbok deserts him as he spots a possible pass.

The narrator, a young colonist, and Chowbok, his native companion, follow the river from his master's sheep country to the mouth of a gorge, camp, force a terrible passage through the gorge, and climb out onto a saddle. Above it they find country with no sheep-run value, so the narrator turns to prospecting for gold and spends three weeks exploring every stream that leads toward the main range. All but one end at impassable glaciers. When he finally follows the last stream himself and sees a snow-covered but unglaciered saddle that seems to belong to the main range, Chowbok, who had claimed it was impassable, abandons him. The chapter is pure approach-narrative, with no Erewhonian material yet, but it sets the narrator's voice and motives.

Themes

The narrator's motive is money, not wonder. Every judgement of land is economic: the country above the gorge has "no available sheep country", and "there was no money in it whatever, unless there should be minerals". Even the wish to continue is cast as gold-washing. This is the narrator speaking, and Butler is plainly setting him up as a mercenary, self-satisfied colonist. His later schemes in Erewhon (to convert or exploit the people) are of a piece with this, so his opinions should not be read as Butler's thesis.

Sublime nature, noticed too late. The first camp is described in rapturous language, then undercut: "I call it to mind and delight in it now, but I did not notice it at the time." The narrator's reflection that we "next to never know when we are well off" turns into a darker aside that ignorance shields us from "what we truly are". It is a hint, in the narrator's own voice, of the satire to come on self-knowledge and comfortable blindness. Whether Butler endorses the aside is not something this chapter settles.

Unreliable guide, unreliable guided. Chowbok lies about the last stream, and the narrator announces he "had long ago discovered that he was a great liar". But Chowbok also has his own plan ("he had made up his mind what to do if he saw me getting too near it"), and his earlier dislike of the main range and his desertion suggest the range is feared and guarded. The narrator's condescension is itself part of the satire.

The range as threshold. Gorge, glaciers and a snow saddle build a sense of a natural barrier that discourages crossing. There is a loose rhyme with the later Erewhonian practice of closing off a dangerous technology, though nothing in this unit speaks of machines, and the resemblance is only thematic (compare useless-machine only in the sense of something deliberately kept out of reach).

Notable passages

The first camp (c1). The sublime description of the braided shingle river, the gorge and the untrodden region is a deliberately conventional picturesque set piece, which the narrator later admits he failed to appreciate.

The mirror aside (c2). The Horatian inversion ("O infortunatos...") and the line "Let us be grateful to the mirror for revealing to us our appearance only" are the nearest thing to a thesis in the chapter, and are voiced by the narrator.

Worthless country (c3). The glaciers "tumbling down the mountain sides like cataracts" are seen at once as hopeless and then as commercially empty, showing how the narrator ranks the world.

Resolve to go on (c4). The decision to follow the river up and "wash well for gold" is the narrator's pretext for the crossing, and Chowbok's concealed plan is flagged.

Desertion (c5). The discovery of the lie, the unglaciered saddle and Chowbok's flight end the chapter on a note of elation and abandonment.
