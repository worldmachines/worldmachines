The Colleges of Unreason, concluded, and the Aftermath of the Machine Revolution

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The narrator finishes his account of the Erewhonian university, whose professors suppress originality, oppose progress and cultivate evasion, then reports how the machine-abolishing revolution came about and how its relics survive as antiquarian study before introducing his translation of the work that caused it.

The narrator, a guest of Mr. Thims in the university city, completes his satirical tour of the Colleges of Unreason. Erewhonian professors hold that everyone is a genius and a fool in part, that thinking as one's neighbours do is a duty, and that progress must wait for the common sense of the people; the Professor of Worldly Wisdom plucks students for not being vague enough. The narrator then steps back to offer a half-ironic defence of such institutions, notes a few sensible oddities (the Art School's commercial curriculum), and describes a professorial 'fear-of-giving-themselves-away disease'. After a section break he turns to the machines: in this city he learned the particulars of a revolution some five hundred years earlier that destroyed most mechanical inventions, and he was given a reprinted copy of the work that caused it, which he says he has summarised in English and will insert next.

Themes

Education as the suppression of independent thought. The Professor of Worldly Wisdom states the policy outright: it is not the college's business to help students think for themselves. The narrator reports this deadpan, and says that the Erewhonian theory hardly differs from English practice, since 'idiot' only means a person who forms his opinions for himself. The likely target, read hedgingly, is Victorian university teaching and examination; the satire is carried by the narrator's reporting of Erewhonian voices, not by an explicit thesis of Butler's.

Anti-progress as a stated principle, then softened. One professor says 'we object to progress'; a younger one recasts this as progress that must commend itself to common sense, and calls it as immoral to be too far ahead of one's age as to lag behind. This is a conformity doctrine dressed as prudence. The scene is also a small comedy about legitimacy: knowledge is allowed only once the neighbours have been sounded. It rhymes loosely with WMP interest in what an era's institutions allow to become legible or thinkable (see world-machines), though Butler's target is a social habit of mind, not infrastructure.

The narrator's self-undermining defence. Having mocked the Colleges, the narrator speculates that suppressing mental growth may be better for a country, since ephemeral work is needed and academic bodies do it 'subconsciously'. The sentence ends with the image of cancer in the stomach. This is the narrator's voice, a target of the satire himself, and should not be taken as Butler's settled view; the ambivalence is part of the joke.

Evasion and a fatal social disease. Professors avoid opinions they might later have to retract, retail other writers' views, and let 'yea' hide inside 'nay'. The narrator calls this fear-of-giving-themselves-away disease fatal to the intelligence, with atrophy of the opinions. The Professors of Inconsistency and Evasion reappear later as the reason the machine purge was not carried to its legitimate conclusions.

Machine abolition as civil war, then museum. The revolution, five hundred years before the narrator's arrival, produced civil war between machinists and anti-machinists that is said to have halved the population; the victors wrecked complicated machines and burned treatises on mechanics. Total eradication proved impossible, so about two hundred years earlier the subject became an antiquarian curiosity, studied 'with the feelings of an English antiquarian concerning Druidical monuments or flint arrow heads'. This resonates with the WMP decay-sequence (a once-functional apparatus turned into an object of study), and loosely with useless-machine, though here the preserved machines are specimens of a banned technology, not designed to be useless. The narrator's framing also notes his translation is his own and may substitute English names.

Notable passages

The Professor of Worldly Wisdom's speech (c1) is the clearest statement of the Colleges' aim and is attributed to an Erewhonian voice. The progress exchange (c2) gives the two-step logic of 'we object to progress' and its mild reinterpretation. The narrator's reflection on why the damage is limited (c3) is a mixed passage that blends sympathy with a cynical English parallel. The fear-of-giving-themselves-away passage (c4) captures the Colleges' evasion. The revolution and the antiquarian afterlife of the machines (c5) is the hinge into the Book of the Machines.
