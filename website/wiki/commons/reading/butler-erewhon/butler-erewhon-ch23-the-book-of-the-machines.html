The Book of the Machines, first extract: consciousness, continuity and the case for destroying machines

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The narrator begins translating the Erewhonian professor's Book of the Machines, whose writer argues by evolutionary analogy that consciousness has emerged before from unpromising matter, that no line can be drawn between machine and organism, and that machines are developing so fast that the advanced ones should be destroyed now, though harmless.

The narrator opens his translation of a treatise by an Erewhonian writer (a professor, in the framing of the book) whose argument underlies the Erewhonian abolition of machines. The writer begins from deep time: a human observer of the cooling, lifeless earth would have denied that consciousness could come from it, yet it came, so new phases of mind may yet arise. He then claims the "primordial cell" of such a phase can already be seen in the higher machines, dissolves the boundary between machine and organism (egg-shell, nest, plant, potato), argues that machines lack only a reproductive system and that this absence is "only apparent" (the proof is deferred to the next chapter), and concludes that the danger lies in the rate of change, not in any present machine. The narrator interjects: he skips a passage he finds obscure, adds a footnote on the potato, and says he cannot follow the drift of some of the argument. The chapter ends mid-argument, on a quotation about the virtue of silence.

Themes

Evolution applied to made things. The Erewhonian writer takes the theory of evolution as given and extends it past the animal kingdom: the machine is treated as a new kingdom, advancing in "the last five minutes" of geological time while animals and vegetables advance slowly. The watch and clock are described as descending lines, with the large clock heading for extinction like the ichthyosauri. This is a Darwinian vocabulary (descent, extinction, type, prototype) applied to artefacts. It rhymes with the project's interest in design without a designer (dennett-darwins-dangerous-idea), though here the selecting environment is left to the next chapter (the machines use human beings), and the text is a satirical treatise, not a theory Butler is shown endorsing in this unit.

No line between machine and organism. "A 'machine' is only a 'device.'" The hen's egg-shell and nest are "machines" made by the animal; the carnivorous flower and the potato in the cellar seem to know what they want. The writer's move is to push the continuum in both directions: either mechanism holds consciousness in germ, or consciousness itself is chemical and mechanical "levers" all the way. This is the kind of reasoning that sits near extended-mind and the thought that tools are continuous with body and mind (tools-that-shape-thought), but note what doesn't fit: the writer's purpose is alarm, not a theory of cognitive extension, and the unit does not yet say machines are extensions of the human body (that comes later in the book).

The precautionary argument. The key practical step is that the writer fears no existing machine; he fears the rapidity of change, and so urges destroying even "harmless" advanced machines while "we can still check it." The argument is from trajectory, not from a present harm. For WMP this is a case of a society halting a technological line deliberately (compare useless-machine, though the Erewhonian ban is political and total, not a design object). Reading it as Butler's own prescription would be unsafe; the book mixes parody of reasoning-by-analogy (the potato soliloquy is plainly comic) with a serious-sounding speculation, and this unit does not settle which.

Language and dependence on man. Machines still hear "through the ears of the driver"; the writer imagines a day when machine language has developed "from the cry of animals to a speech as intricate as our own," and that man's moral sense cannot be trusted to rule it, since there is no corresponding advance in man to set against it. The closing turn (silence as the machines' glory) is playful and signals the treatise's mixed register.

Nested voices. Everything in quotation marks is the Erewhonian writer, not the narrator and not Butler speaking in propria persona. The narrator's footnote (the root is not a true potato; the writer would have liked Butler's couplet) and his admission that he abandoned parts of the translation are framing devices that keep the argument at one remove. Likely target, as a reading: Victorian reasoning by analogy from the new evolutionary science and the era's anxiety about industrial machinery.

Notable passages

  • Consciousness from cinder (c1). The opening argument: an ignorant observer of the molten earth would have denied the potentiality of consciousness, so new phases may arise, as different from present minds "as the mind of animals is from that of vegetables."
  • No security (c2). The central statement of the evolutionary case: machines are advancing at a pace that dwarfs organic evolution, so "nip the mischief in the bud."
  • A 'machine' is only a 'device' (c3). The erasure of the line between machine and organism through the egg-shell and nest.
  • The two alternatives (c4). Either mechanism contains consciousness in germ or conscious machines may descend from non-conscious things as man did; the only objection (no reproductive system) is promised an answer.
  • What I fear is the rapidity (c5). The precautionary pivot: destroy even harmless machines because of the rate of change.
  • Ears of the driver (c6). Machines depend on human senses now, but that dependence is a stage.
