Three months in the Cold Harbour jail: the missing machines and the crime of illness

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In his first months of confinement in Cold Harbour the narrator guesses wrongly why the Erewhonians have so little machinery, learns the language from a teacher and the jailor's daughter Yram, discovers that illness is a crime, and is told the Government will send him to the capital and that a kleptomaniac-embezzler merchant has 'recovered' under the straighteners.

The narrator, penniless and imprisoned, reflects on the room of preserved broken machinery and the magistrate's displeasure at his watch, and reasons (wrongly, as he later admits) that the Erewhonians must have exhausted their coal and iron. He is fed and befriended by Yram, the jailor's daughter, and taught the language by a self-important instructor. Over three months he plays fives, makes a flute and a tobacco substitute, sings English songs, and is visited by townsfolk. Two episodes expose the inverted values of the country: his Sunday observance is read as periodic sulkiness, and a cold earns him an angry rebuke from Yram because 'illness of any sort was considered in Erewhon to be highly criminal and immoral'. The chapter closes with the Government's conditions for sending him to the metropolis and the interpreter's description of a merchant host who has 'recovered' from embezzlement.

Themes

The narrator's wrong explanation of the machines. The narrator observes that the people are 'about as far advanced as Europeans of the twelfth or thirteenth century' yet once had 'the fullest knowledge of our own most recent inventions'. He sees that the preservation and ticketing of the broken machines shows this is not ignorance, and settles on resource exhaustion. The narrator flags his own error in advance ('I afterwards found how entirely mistaken it was'), so the chapter plants the question that the later Book of the Machines answers, and does so by showing the narrator as a confident but unreliable inferencer. This resonates loosely with useless-machine and with deliberate halts to technological development, though here it is only a puzzle posed, not yet an argument; the actual reason is not given in this chapter.

Illness as crime, crime as illness. Yram's anger over a cold, the two men condemned to hard labour for ill health in the next yard, and the merchant who has 'suffered terribly from' a long word 'much longer than kleptomania' together set up the mirror-inversion of Victorian attitudes: bodily misfortune is punished, while embezzlement is a malady cured by 'the straighteners'. A likely satirical target (a reading, not stated in the text) is the moralising of sickness and the medicalising of vice. The narrator does not yet understand it and declines to detail it ('I propose, therefore, to make no mention of what passed between us on this occasion').

The narrator as satirical object. He enjoys playing the picture of 'abject misery, and, like all pictures, rather untruthful', eats his dinner when Yram's back is turned, makes the most of his cold in hope of being cossetted, and keeps the Sabbath by refusing to sing on Sundays while having forgotten the hymn words. These are the narrator's vanities and mild hypocrisies, which Butler lets the reader see; they are not Butler's thesis. The Erewhonians' reading of his Sabbath observance as sulkiness, with advice that he 'ought to see some one', is a small foretaste of the straighteners.

Customs observed through partial comprehension. The narrator learns the language, in his own joke, from Yram as his 'real dictionary and grammar', and understands the explanation about his watch only as 'a very heinous offence, almost as bad (at least, so I thought I understood him) as having typhus fever'. Information reaches the reader filtered through his misunderstandings, a device that keeps the satire deniable. The Government's physical conditions for his release (light hair, blue eyes, fresh complexion, no suspicion of ill health) show the same body-as-moral-test logic at the state level.

Notable passages

The machinery puzzle. The narrator's chain of reasoning, ending in the coal-and-iron theory, is the chapter's link to World Machines themes: a society that once had advanced machines but now preserves them as relics. It resonates with useless-machine only in the sense of machines kept without being used; Butler's own explanation lies in later chapters, and this unit contains no machine theory.

The cold. The most important passage for the satire: Yram's indignation, the narrator's incomprehension, and the sentence that illness is criminal and immoral and punishable by imprisonment.

The Sabbath misreading. A short comic scene in which the narrator's religious observance is diagnosed as periodic sulkiness, showing the Erewhonians as tolerant but with a framework in which low spirits are a condition to be treated.

The wall and the groans. The narrator realises the coughing next door comes from prisoners condemned for ill health, a quiet moment of unnoticed cruelty set beside his own comfort.

The embezzling merchant. The closing line pairs a guest's crime with a medical vocabulary of recovery; the interpreter's praise ('a really wonderful recovery') makes the inversion explicit.
