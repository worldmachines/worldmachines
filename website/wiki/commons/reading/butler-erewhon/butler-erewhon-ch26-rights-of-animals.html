The Erewhonian prophet and the prohibition on killing animals

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The narrator reports how an Erewhonian prophet, claiming sole communion with an unseen power, argued from the abandonment of cannibalism to a ban on killing animals for food, and how disciples, loophole-hunting and a later pestilence-driven reaction turned the rule into evasion, hypocrisy and one student's suicide.

The narrator opens by saying the Erewhonians are "easily led by the nose" and ready to sacrifice common sense to a philosopher's logic, then recounts ("so far as I could collect it from the best authorities") a prophet who, some 2,500 years back, reasoned that because killing and eating relatives had been abandoned with good results, killing and eating animals should be abandoned too. The prophet speaks through the authority of an "unseen power" that confirms every conclusion. After his death his disciples tighten the rules (eggs, milk), the populace evades them with ever more absurd legal fictions (animals that "commit suicide", die "in self-defence", or are "warranted still-born"), a pestilence provokes a harsher reaction two centuries later, and the chapter ends on the narrator's reflection about national conscience and the story of a young man whose guilt over eating beefsteak, prescribed by his doctor, ends in suicide.

Themes

Logic from a first principle, ratified by private revelation. The prophet "wanted to reduce right and wrong to rules" and put matters "on a logical basis, which people whose time is money are content to accept on no basis at all." His argument is an analogy chain (fellow-men, therefore fellow-creatures) closed off by the claim that the unseen power called the conclusion "irrefragable", so dissent is simply referred back to the one channel only he can consult. The narrator frames this as the Erewhonian habit of offering up common sense "at the shrine of logic"; it is the same weakness the Book of the Machines chapters exploit. Butler's likely target is Victorian moral reformers and clerical authority, though that is a reading, not something the text states. It sits slightly against the machine chapters, where the reasoning is also pushed to a drastic policy but is more ambiguous.

Prohibition breeds evasion and corrupts the law. Disciples extend the rule (eggs as potential chickens, milk as robbing calves); the populace answers with inspectors' labels, dogs as loophole, and judges ruling that self-defence makes an attacker's death "natural". The rule-set becomes a legibility apparatus (inspectors, certificates) that produces fraudulent documents rather than behaviour change. This faintly rhymes with Legibility and the cost of imposing a tidy scheme on a living practice, though nothing here is about states mapping populations; it is a satire of rules-lawyering, not of statecraft.

Doctrinaire reform versus common sense, with conscience as the casualty. The narrator says the real damage was not going without meat but "the jar which undue prohibition gave to the consciences" of the many. The line "though conscience as a rule boons, it can also bane" and the closing voices of Common Sense and Duty personify the split. The narrator's own framing is moralising and complacent, so it should not be read as Butler's settled view; Butler may equally be mocking the narrator's tidy reassurance.

Hypocrisy and mimicry. Students model themselves on one another's supposed abstinence while most secretly eat meat ("They, eat a beefsteak? Never."), a small portrait of a social norm held up by mutual pretence, which anticipates the Ydgrun-style respectability satire the book develops elsewhere. The chapter is also a utopian-mirror device in the manner of more-utopia: an invented society's institutions reflecting England's, here its enthusiasms and food taboos, though the chapter offers no positive programme.

Cost of the unseen power. Authority rests on an invisible source that cannot be checked, and the text notes that it is also what makes the prohibition last two centuries. This has no direct World Machines counterpart; the nearest loose echo is how an infrastructure of belief outlives its justification, but the text does not describe machines at all.

Notable passages

  • The prophet's argument (c1): the cannibalism-to-vegetarianism analogy, the claim that differences between humans and animals are "few and secondary", the carve-out for animals that died naturally, were deformed or "have committed suicide", and the vegetable clause ("all those that will let you eat them with impunity"). The humour is already in the loopholes the prophet himself supplies.
  • The disciples' extensions (c2): eggs and milk, the inspector's label "Laid not less than three months".
  • Evasion by the populace (c3): the epidemic of animal suicide among sheep and cattle that "would scent out a butcher's knife".
  • The cost to conscience (c4): the narrator's verdict that a nation's conscience woken by a respectable old gentleman "who has an unseen power up his sleeve will pave hell with a vengeance".
  • The young man (c5): the doctor's advice, the secret steak, Common Sense versus Duty, and a suicide the narrator reports flatly. The anecdote also shows that illness is not yet a crime in this early period, as the narrator notes in a parenthesis.

The prophet's rules are Erewhonian-reported and filtered through the narrator, who says he was "told" and collected the story from authorities; Butler himself does not speak in this unit.
