The balloon escape from Erewhon

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The narrator, threatened with prosecution for owning a watch and attempting to reintroduce machinery, deceives the King and Queen into building him a balloon and escapes with Arowhena, surviving a delirious flight and a sinking into the sea before an Italian ship rescues them.

The narrator, still translating the Erewhonian treatises on the machines, learns from a cashier of the Musical Banks that he is to be prosecuted for "measles" (in effect, as he reports it, for owning a watch and trying to reintroduce machinery). He turns the Queen's curiosity and the King's uxoriousness to his purpose: he tells them he will ascend in a balloon to interview the drought-causing air god, secretly intending to ride the steady upper wind out of the country with Arowhena hidden in the car. After a garden rendezvous with Arowhena and a week of preparation, they lift off at the moment her absence is being discovered. A dream-like flight over cloud and mountain ends with the balloon sinking into the sea; they throw out everything, books included, and are rescued from the water by an Italian vessel.

Themes

The satire of illness-as-crime closes on the narrator himself. The Erewhonian cashier (an informant from the Musical Banks) explains that measles is chosen as the charge because a jury might be moved by extenuating circumstances for typhus or small-pox, but a conviction is likely for "a disease which could be sufficiently punished in a person of my age." The mirror logic of earlier chapters, where misfortune is prosecuted, is applied as pure expedience; the stated pretext (measles) and real motive (the watch, machinery) are openly separated. This reads, hedged, as Butler's target being legal and moral hypocrisy that dresses one offence in the clothes of another. The prosecution also ties the escape back to the machine prohibition: the one machine he is charged over is a timepiece.

The narrator is not a hero of principle. His deceit rests on a lie that borders, as he admits, on "the idolatrous," yet he claims the lie "will probably lead to the conversion of the whole country." This is the narrator's own self-satisfied piety and opportunism, which the satire targets; it is not Butler's thesis. The Queen's eagerness to see "so strange a sight" and the King's belief that the balloon will collapse show Erewhonian rulers governed by curiosity and spousal indulgence rather than doctrine.

A machine ban that tolerates a loophole. The narrator stresses that a balloon needs "no complicated machinery," only silk, a car, ropes and gas that antiquarians can teach workmen to make. The Erewhonian prohibition (see the Book of the Machines chapters) thus yields to a simple artefact that carries him out of the society that banned machines. This sits loosely beside WMP interest in deliberately halted technology (useless-machine is a distant cousin at best), but nothing here argues that the ban is wise or foolish; the unit only shows its porousness. The foreman who "threw himself heart and soul into the matter" also hints that Erewhonian craftsmen are not hostile to machinery in practice.

Time, the watch, and bodily substitutes. In the fog the narrator laments his lost watch and counts his own pulse to prove that time "was there" and that they were not "gone adrift into the timelessness of eternity." A faintly extended-mind/tools-that-shape-thought resonance: the instrument had become a prop for orientation, and without it he falls back on the body as clock. This is a gloss; Butler offers it as a moment of personal dread, not an argument about tools. The dream of locomotives hissing, followed by real hissing, adds an echo of machines pursuing him out of the country, though the text does not say what the noise was.

Escape as threshold passage. The St. Gothard tunnel simile frames the rescue as a sudden pivot from the extreme of the terrible to a smiling valley. The unit ends the Erewhon narrative proper by an abrupt deus ex machina rescue, in keeping with the narrator's claim that this is the part of his story "in no other have I endeavoured to adhere more conscientiously to facts" (a claim the reader is invited to doubt).

Notable passages

Why measles (c1). The informant's explanation of the charge is the clearest remaining statement of the Erewhonian legal inversion, now used against the narrator.

The plan and the plea to credulity (c2). The narrator's insistence on factual accuracy about the balloon is a typical piece of Butler's mock-realist narration.

The air-god pretext (c3). The drought, the proposed interview with the air god, and the self-congratulating hope of conversion show the narrator's deceit and complacency at once.

The watch and the pulse (c4). The most psychologically interesting moment: time as a thing to be proved.

The sinking (c5). Ballast, provisions and finally books thrown away while the manuscripts are clung to; the pair smile "with a ghastly hopefulness."

The Gothard simile and rescue (c6). The relief arrives as an Italian ship, ending the narrator's passage out of Erewhon.
