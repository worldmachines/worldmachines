The Wool-Shed Interrogation: Chowbok's Pantomime and the Decision to Explore

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The narrator bribes and badgers the native Chowbok (Kahabuka) with grog in the wool-shed to learn what lies beyond the main range, gets only a terrified wordless pantomime, and, fired by it, plans an expedition with Chowbok as unwitting porter.

At shearing time the narrator, a young colonist and station hand, tries to extract from the old native Chowbok (said to be really named Kahabuka) what is known of the country behind the nearer ranges and the main range. Chowbok talks freely about the nearer ranges but turns evasive about the main one. After a failed first attempt, the narrator takes him to the wool-shed at night with rum as bait and spends two hours in a contest of wills. Chowbok finally answers with a wordless, terrified performance: he builds a seat from wool bales, sits rigid in a wool-pack mantle with a fiendish face, moans like wind, holds up ten fingers, and flees. The narrator cannot interpret it, yet it fires his imagination. He resolves to explore after shearing and to take Chowbok along, concealing the true destination.

Themes

The narrator as self-satisfied, mercenary frontiersman. The narrator reports his own manipulation (grog as bribe, flattery by using the name Kahabuka, concealing the main range from Chowbok) without any sign of shame. This is the narrator's voice, not Butler's thesis, and he is a target of the satire: the colonial negotiator who treats a man's fear as a resource. Whether Butler also means the scene to mock Victorian missionary and colonial attitudes toward natives is a reading, not stated in the text, which notes only that Chowbok was "a great favourite with the missionaries".

Knowledge as traditions, not facts. What is known of the range is inherited tribal tradition, which the narrator can see exists but cannot extract. The unknown is conveyed by dread rather than information, and the unreadable pantomime is what convinces the narrator the range hides "something well worth discovering". The satire of rational inquiry is mild here: the narrator concedes he has no idea what the performance means, yet draws a confident conclusion. This is the book's first move in building Erewhon as a place defined by what cannot be said about it.

The wool-shed as mock-cathedral. The narrator compares the shed to a cathedral (aisles, nave) and says it gives him "a semblance of antiquity (precious in a new country)", though the oldest in the settlement is seven years old. It is a small, ironic seed of the later Butler pairing of institutional forms with their hollowness, though nothing in this unit develops it and it should not be pressed further.

Fit with World Machines. There is little in this short framing chapter that connects to the project's machine concepts; it is setup for the crossing of the range. Its nearest resonance is the frontier as an edge of the legible: a surveyed, wool-producing settlement bordered by a region only oral tradition and fear describe. That is the author's gloss, and the text itself is only a story of a bargain and a scare.

Notable passages

Opening and the first failed interrogation. The narrator introduces Chowbok, his motives (grog), and the shift in his manner from the nearer ranges to the main range, ending with his feigned drunkenness while being kicked.

The wool-shed as cathedral. The narrator's comparison of the shed to a cathedral, with the admission that its antiquity is an illusion.

The churning simile and Chowbok's performance. The narrator's butter-churning image for patience in interrogation, followed immediately by Chowbok's bale-and-pack ritual, whose "grotesque fiendishness" the narrator concedes approaches the sublime.

Meaning he cannot read. The narrator admits he had no conception of what Chowbok meant, but is sure it was true and awful to Chowbok, and that this is more stirring than any intelligible story.
