Illness as Crime, Crime as Illness: The Straighteners and Mr. Nosnibor

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The narrator explains Erewhon's inverted moral economy, in which illness and misfortune are punishable crimes while fraud and violence are treated as ailments by professional 'straighteners', illustrated by his host Mr. Nosnibor's prescribed flogging for embezzlement.

The unnamed narrator reports what he has learned of Erewhonian law and manners. Bodily illness before seventy is tried and punished, and so is ill luck generally, while forgery, arson and violent robbery are handled as misfortunes to be treated with sympathy by 'straighteners', a profession modelled on English doctors. The narrator offers parallels from England, Italy, Islam and the Maori, then gives a worked case: his host Mr. Nosnibor, who embezzled a widow's property, is prescribed a fine, a bread-and-milk diet and monthly floggings. The chapter closes with how straighteners are trained, by practising each vice in turn as a religious duty, and with the Erewhonian suspicion of unalloyed virtue.

Themes

Inversion as satirical mirror. The narrator reports a society that reverses the English moral map: what England treats as crime Erewhon treats as sickness, and the reverse. The reversal is exact enough to expose the English arrangement as arbitrary. Likely targets, offered as a reading, are Victorian moralism toward the poor and sick, and the medical profession's authority. The narrator's own comparisons (English 'peccant' matter, Italian 'disgrazia') function as a gloss that makes the strangeness familiar.

Misfortune as offence. The Erewhonian rule is that ill luck 'makes people uncomfortable to hear of it', so it is punished. The narrator then defends this as 'natural, but desirable for any society', where the fortunate dislike the unfortunate. This is the narrator's voice, a self-satisfied and unreliable apologist, and should not be read as Butler's thesis. The irony cuts at English attitudes toward poverty and failure ('those who are either poor or poorly').

Professional authority and its theatre. Straighteners classify disorders with names from the 'hypothetical language', and patients are reassured by 'familiarity with the long names'. The Erewhonians evade their own ill-health laws with polite fictions ('to have the socks'). The pattern is a system of managed appearances in which everyone understands and nobody may say they understand. Loosely, this rhymes with the WMP interest in institutions whose forms outlive their function (see decay-sequence, where Performative and Ornamental stages keep the ritual after the working purpose has gone), though Butler's target is moral hypocrisy, not infrastructure.

Treatment versus punishment. The narrator argues that Englishmen would hide illness if it were punished, just as the Erewhonians hide bodily ill health and openly discuss moral lapses. Mr. Nosnibor's flogging is thus not punishment but cure, and a cure that visits suffering the narrator calls 'exquisite' yet judges effective. Butler leaves the ambiguity open: the narrator approves, while the narrator's own approval (and his remark that the host's health was 'a little affected') invites the reader to doubt.

Moderation in virtue. The straighteners train by practising vice, and the Erewhonians hold that 'unalloyed virtue is not a thing to be immoderately indulged in', preferring people 'not remarkable either for vice or virtue'. This is a satire on pious earnestness; the Hogarth joke is the narrator's small clash of Victorian and Erewhonian taste.

Notable passages

The core statement of the inversion (c1). The opening paragraph sets out the trial of the ill and the hospital for the criminal, with the cheque-forger 'suffering from a severe fit of immorality' and receiving visits. Bad conduct is still deplorable but is 'held to be the result of either pre-natal or post-natal misfortune'.

Misfortune as offence (c2). The 'strange part of the story' is that the same Erewhonians who excuse moral defects will not hear a plea of misfortune where England would pity.

The straighteners (c3). The narrator introduces the profession, translated as 'one who bends back the crooked', with its quasi-surreptitious fee. The analogy to English doctors carries the satire of the clergy as well, as a reading.

The English doctor comparison (c4). The argument that Erewhonians take a flogging weekly because they are not scouted for moral lapse, just as Englishmen undergo surgery. It is the chapter's most explicit logical turn and a good example of reasoning by analogy that Butler lets stand without endorsing.

Mr. Nosnibor's prescription (c5). The concrete case: a fine to the State, bread and milk, and monthly floggings, with the swindled widow nowhere compensated because she would herself have been prosecuted in the Misplaced Confidence Court.
