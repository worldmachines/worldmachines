The Unborn: Time Walking Backwards and Birth as Felony

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The narrator reports the Erewhonian myth of the unborn, disembodied souls who must sign an affidavit, drink a memory-destroying potion and be allotted parents by lot to be born, after first hearing Erewhonian images of time as a backward-facing journey and a magistrate's lecture that birth is a felony.

The chapter opens with the narrator relaying Erewhonian figures of time: we are drawn through life backwards, or watch a panorama wind from the roller of the future to that of the past, and the future is as real as the past though unseen. The narrator then turns from "many chapters like the above" to the unborn: ghostly, deathless, bodiless souls living in an insubstantial world who arrive from unknown planets and can only leave by the route of birth, which is a kind of suicide. A would-be-born soul must swear an affidavit before a magistrate, accept conditions (a potion destroying memory and identity, a disposition drawn by lot, parents allotted by chance whom it must find and pester), and listen to a long lecture from the wiser unborn arguing that to be born is a capital felony. The lecture, an Erewhonian voice that the narrator translates, catalogues the risks of bad, silly, possessive or uncomprehending parents, the compulsion of free will, the decay of life's pleasures, and a closing counsel to take refuge in "present and immediate duty". The chapter ends with the ceremony at the courthouse and the potion that dissolves the soul into a bare vital principle with one instinct.

Themes

Time as a backward-facing predicament. The Erewhonian images (the dark corridor, the two-roller panorama, the race that knew the future and died of misery) present knowledge as always reflected from behind, with our conceit of knowing the past "poorly founded". The passage that the future is "in the loins of the past" and that "the past must alter before the future can do so" is a loose rhyme with the project's interest in history-machine style path dependence, though Butler is offering a mood and a paradox, not a theory of history. The aside that a too-prescient child "would be culled out by natural selection" is an early Darwinian joke inside the Erewhonian voice.

Birth inverted into a crime. This is the book's central inversion, applied to the origin of life itself. In the Erewhonian myth being born is a felony and a voluntary one, so that the unborn consent to a "snare" and may not later complain. Read as a hedged satire, it mirrors the book's treatment of illness as crime (adversity as culpability), and prepares for the birth formulae of later chapters, which are not in this unit. The narrator frames it as reasoning with a spendthrift, i.e. the wise unborn are cast as sober Victorian elders and the would-be-born as the unquiet and foolish.

The parent-child relation, seen from both sides. The lecture's most pointed satire targets parents who treat a child as chattel or as a thing to be cowed, and equally the burden on parents of the unborn who pester them: "It is hard upon the duckling to have been hatched by a hen, but is it not also hard upon the hen to have hatched the duckling?" It is a likely, hedged target of Victorian family authority, but Butler balances it with sympathy for the parents, so the satire is not one-sided.

Memory, identity and chance. The potion that destroys "memory and sense of identity", the dispositions drawn by lot, and the scales of the mind biased by use and by childhood tampering form a miniature theory of character as allotment plus training. This touches habit-and-identity, though here identity is erased at the threshold rather than built by habit, and the vestigial dream-memory the lecturer anticipates is met with advice to flee into immediate duty. Free will is presented not as a gift but as a compulsion: "you will have free will; that you will be obliged to have it".

Strange fate and the voices. The text nests voices: the Erewhonians, the narrator relaying them, the wiser unborn in direct speech, and a quotation from "one of their own poets" that the narrator's footnote says is a familiar myth of ours with changed names. None of these is Butler's own thesis; the narrator is a conduit, though the satire's targets plausibly include Victorian religion and domestic morality.

Notable passages

The two time images (c1) set the dark-corridor and two-roller pictures against our conceit about the past. The portrait of the unborn world (c2) gives the deathless, bodiless, complaining souls who leave by a kind of suicide. The conditions of birth (c3) are the potion, the lots and the allotted parents. The opening of the magistrate-side lecture (c4), "To be born is a felony", is the chapter's inversion at its most compact. The duckling-and-hen passage (c5) states the double burden of the parent-child relation. The closing advice and ceremony (c6) turn the lecture's last word into a counsel of duty and then show the potion that dissolves the unborn into a bare vital principle.
