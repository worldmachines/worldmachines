Butler's three prefaces: authorial disclaimers and the genesis of Erewhon

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In three prefaces (1872 first and second, 1901 revised) Butler, speaking in his own voice, disclaims any intent to ridicule Darwin or deny moral responsibility, blames the Erewhonians for the book's inconsistencies, and reconstructs how Erewhon grew from the 1863 'Darwin among the Machines' article into a patched-together book with, he says, no central idea.

These are the only passages of the book in Butler's own voice, not the narrator's. The first edition's preface is a single line fixing the pronunciation of Erewhon as three short syllables. The second-edition preface (dated June 9, 1872) answers early reception: it denies that the book rode on the success of The Coming Race, rejects the reading of the Machines chapters as an attempt to make Darwin's theory absurd, denies that the book absolves men of responsibility for their actions, jokes about a corrected Latin quotation, and blames the Erewhonians for the book's inconsistencies. The revised-edition preface (August 7, 1901) is a genealogy: the Machines material was written first, in New Zealand's Upper Rangitata district, in 1863, then rewritten in 1865, and the whole was strung together into a book only after 1870. Butler also confesses that he padded the 1901 text partly for copyright reasons, and says there is no central idea underlying the book. A bracketed footnote about chapter numbering is a Project Gutenberg transcriber's note (signed DP), not Butler's.

Themes

The machine chapters, intent, and the misuse of analogy. Butler says reviewers took the chapters on Machines as an attempt to reduce Darwin's theory to an absurdity, which he denies, though he admits he chose not to explain and so invited the misreading. He then turns the charge outward: the book that most naturally deserves to be the target of 'the specious misuse of analogy' is left unnamed, with 'the hint' left to suffice. The ambiguity is worth keeping: Butler is disclaiming ridicule of Darwin while leaving open that the machine argument is a parody of reasoning by analogy. This is a claim about the satire's aim, not an endorsement of the machine thesis; the revised preface separately says that the second Machines article led to the theory of Life and Habit, which he 'believe[s] to be quite sound' and puts in the mouth of an Erewhonian philosopher. That is the nearest the prefaces come to Butler taking a machine-adjacent idea seriously, and it concerns habit and unconscious memory, not the conscious-machine scenario. It rhymes loosely with dennett-darwins-dangerous-idea (Darwinian reasoning pushed beyond biology) and habit-and-identity, though Butler offers no argument here, only a pointer.

Responsibility and the mirror. In 1872 Butler insists he has not denied responsibility for actions, calling anyone who does so 'an enemy who deserves no quarter', and says he added to the chapter on Malcontents to prevent further mistake. This is a reading he rejects, offered about a book whose inversions (illness as crime, crime as illness) invite exactly that reading.

The Erewhonians as unreasoning interlocutors. Butler blames the book's inconsistencies on the Erewhonians, a people for whom anomalies caused no 'intellectual inconvenience' and who would accept being called life-long self-deceivers because 'it did not matter'. This is a jibe at a society (and, by the satirical mirror, a reading of Victorian England) that is untouched by argument until money or pain intervenes. It is Butler speaking, framing his fiction as reportage, which is itself part of the joke.

Genealogy of a patchwork book. The 1901 preface dates the pieces: 'Darwin among the Machines' (signed Cellarius, Christchurch Press, June 13, 1863); a second Machines article; the 1865 rewrite as 'The Mechanical Creation'; the World of the Unborn; the Musical Banks; the trial of a man for being in a consumption. Only in autumn 1870, at a friend's suggestion, were they strung into a book, finished on Sundays. Butler says the opening chapters were drawn from the Upper Rangitata district. This is a rare datable account of the machine idea being repeatedly rewritten and enlarged, which Butler says is why he is better satisfied with those parts than with parts written once.

Candour about merit and motive. Butler records the rejection by Chapman & Hall (their reader called it 'a philosophical work, little likely to be popular'), credits the book's success to two reviews and to 'the sound of a new voice, and of an unknown voice', and admits that he would cut forty or fifty pages, but copyright law forced him to add about sixty instead. He closes by saying 'There is no central idea underlying Erewhon' and that it was not an organic whole. That should temper any attempt to read a single thesis, including a machine-theoretic one, into the book; the project's resonances (world-machines-style thinking about machines as an evolving order) are the reader's gloss, not something the prefaces claim.

Notable passages

The Darwin disclaimer (c1). Butler denies that the Machines chapters laugh at Darwin, admits he provoked the misreading, and points at an unnamed book as the real example of misused analogy.

Responsibility (c2). A short, emphatic denial that the book denies moral responsibility.

The Erewhonians' indifference to anomaly (c3). The 1872 explanation of the book's inconsistencies, which doubles as a portrait of the Erewhonian mind.

Origin of the Machines (c4). The first part written, in the Upper Rangitata, and its link forward to Life and Habit and the Erewhonian philosopher.

Assembling the book (c5). The World of the Unborn, the Musical Banks, the consumption trial, and the 1870 suggestion to string them together.

No central idea (c6). Butler's own verdict: no central idea, hardly any story, not an organic whole. A caution against over-systematizing the satire.
