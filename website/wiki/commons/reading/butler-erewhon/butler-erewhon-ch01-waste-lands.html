The narrator's frame and the sheep-station frontier

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The unnamed narrator, a mercenary young Englishman, introduces his reluctant, secretive account, describes the pastoral colony and his shepherding post below the second range, and records the dreams of land, gold and exploration that push him toward the unknown mountains.

The opening chapter sets up the narrator and the ground the story will cross. An unnamed Englishman declines to explain his antecedents, says he left home to find waste crown land for sheep or cattle farming, and admits he failed to profit. He claims a "discovery" worth more than money, yet needs funds to exploit it, so he publishes his adventures while concealing the destination, the season and the starting point, for fear that richer men will anticipate him. He then describes a colony about eight or nine years open to settlers, a coast and plain filling rapidly with sheep stations up to a second range, where he was taken on as a cadet at twenty-two. His rounds, a lyrical passage on the mountain solitude, and a lamb-and-ewe vignette lead into his growing fixation on what lies behind the ranges, and the prospect of pasture, gold or glory there.

Themes

A narrator who is himself a satirical target. The narrator is not Butler's mouthpiece. He asks the reader to trust him because "truth bears its own impress" and because "no one who is himself honest will doubt" him, while openly withholding facts, and he frames his whole narrative as a fund-raising device. The reading that suggests itself is that Butler is establishing a self-interested, pious-and-mercenary witness whose later schemes (the book's own later conversion and labour-export plans lie outside this unit) will be part of the joke; the unit itself shows only the profit motive and the secrecy.

Secrecy and credibility. The narrator makes the tale's believability depend on his own honesty and on concealment: he fears being doubted if he conceals and anticipated if he tells. This is a ready-made hoax-narrator pose, hedged here as a reading, not a claim made by the text.

The frontier as expansion by capital. Settlement moves inland "one behind the other" until the front ranges halt "the tide of squatters", then experiment overturns the prudential objections. The narrator knows that "only three years ago the same cry had been raised against the country which my master's flock was now overrunning", and reasons from that precedent to the next range. Land, borrowed capital, gold and diamonds are all imagined as routes to being "a made man". This frontier logic of expansion until a natural limit is hit loosely rhymes with the extensive phase of the modernity-machine, though Butler's text is concerned with a colonial sheep economy, not a world-machine theory.

Seeing enough to feel easy. The shepherding routine is a minimal surveillance: he needs only to "see enough of them here and there to feel easy that nothing had gone wrong", recognising marked sheep and tolerating missing twenty out of a few hundred. This faintly resembles the legibility-by-sampling that Legibility names, but it is an incidental detail, not an argument in the text.

Solitude and sentiment. The lyrical passages (the sea of white mist with mountain tops "like islands", the lost lamb and the mistaken ewe) show the narrator's sentimental register, and the ewe-and-lamb scene ends in a note that both are mistaken and "part in coldness"; read generously it prefigures the book's themes of kinship and mismatch, but this is inference.

The unexplored range as threshold. The great snowy range, and what lies beyond it, is posed as unknowable ("There was no one in the whole world who had the smallest idea"), setting up the crossing that the following chapters narrate. The title's "waste lands" refers to crown land fit for pasture, not (yet) to any satirical inversion; the Erewhonian society itself does not appear in this unit.

Notable passages

The opening three paragraphs and the consolation paragraph (c1) are the most important for attribution: they define the narrator's motives and the credibility game. The colony description (c2) establishes the expansion logic and the second range as "the extreme limits of pastoral country". The daily rounds (c3) give the sheep-counting-by-eye routine. The lamb-and-ewe vignette (c4) is the main sentimental interlude. The last two curated passages (c5, c6) carry the narrator's motives for going further: borrowed capital, defiance of "every one" who says it is madness, the gold that "people always said there was no gold until it was found to be abundant", and the unreachable great range.
