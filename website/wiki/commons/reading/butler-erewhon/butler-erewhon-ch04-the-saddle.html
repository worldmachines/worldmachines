The Saddle: Crossing the First Threshold, Alone

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Abandoned by Chowbok, the narrator climbs the saddle, glimpses an easy pass onto distant plains, descends through a perilous chasm to a lonely camp, and spends the night haunted by an organ dream and a faint sound of music.

The narrator, a young English sheep-station hand, is left without his native companion Chowbok, who has fled after trying to keep him from this valley. Reading the guide’s reluctance as proof that he is on the right route, the narrator resolves to go on, climbs the snow-covered saddle, and sees that it is not the main range: beyond a roaring glacial river lies an easy pass and a glimpse of “blue and distant plains” before cloud closes over it. Nightfall and a commercial dream of sheep country push him to descend, through a dangerous rift in the rock, to a camp where he is cut off from all return. The night brings an organ dream (Handel) and a faint, wind-borne sound of music that he first fears and then explains away. The chapter is an adventure set-piece, but it is also the book’s threshold: the point at which the narrator commits to a place he cannot leave by the way he came.

Themes

The narrator’s motives are mercenary, and the satire starts here. His decision to risk his life is stated in terms of ownership: the hope of “an immense tract of available sheep country (which I was determined that I would monopolise as far as I possibly could)”. This is the narrator speaking, not Butler’s thesis; it is the first sign of the colonial, self-flattering figure the book later makes a target (reading, hedged: the Victorian emigrant-pastoralist). The same paragraph mixes “fame and perhaps fortune” with a piety that is easily invoked (“Providence was on my side”), so that his heroism and his greed are not separable.

Reading a warning as a signpost. Chowbok’s flight is interpreted as evidence that the “mysteries of the great ranges” lie ahead. The narrator’s inference is motivated reasoning: he wants the pass to exist, and the ambiguous cloud and rent in it are repeatedly resolved in his favour (“It was substantial; there had been no mistake whatsoever”). The scene shows a mind building certainty from a glimpse, which is a small foretaste of the book’s later interest in reasoning by analogy, though that connection is my gloss and nothing in the chapter states it.

Isolation and the fragility of identity. Cut off, the narrator anchors himself with blankets and a ticking watch, and says that without companionship “One begins doubting one’s own identity.” This sits loosely beside habit-and-identity, where selfhood is held in place by routine and surroundings; the chapter offers only the observation, not an argument, and the narrator’s remedy (animals, objects, prayer) is modest.

Music as the border’s signal. The organ dream, a mountainside of pipes as a “golden city” with Handel at the keyboard, and the AEolian sound that follows are the first appearance of music in a book where music is later institutionalised (the narrator is not yet aware of that). Here the sound is explained away as a dream or as Chowbok’s noise in the wool-shed; the reader, with the book’s later chapters, may read it as foreshadowing. The dream’s city-on-a-mountain also faintly echoes the utopian approach-narrative (more-utopia), where the traveller reaches an ideal place after a perilous passage; the fit is partial, since here the vision is a dream and the destination is not yet known to be anything.

Comic tone toward danger. The closing advice that the reader “remain in Europe if he can” and that exploring is “delightful to look forward to and back upon, but … not comfortable at the time” is the narrator’s retrospective, mock-prudential voice.

Notable passages

The view from the top. The staccato “A glance … Another glance …” sequence gives the first sight of the pass and the plains. It is a point-by-point reveal of a threshold, and the immediate loss of it behind cloud sets the narrator’s doubt against his desire.

The decision to proceed. The sheep-country paragraph is the most revealing about the narrator’s character and about what drives the crossing.

Solitude. The “dreadful feeling … of being cut off from all one’s kind” passage is the chapter’s one reflective generalisation.

The organ dream and the music. The dream (with the unnamed voice saying “it is Handel”) and the waking sound frame the supernatural-feeling boundary of Erewhon.
