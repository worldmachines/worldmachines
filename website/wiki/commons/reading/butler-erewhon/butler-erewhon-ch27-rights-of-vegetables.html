The Botany Professor's Reductio: Vegetables as Cousins of Animals

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
An Erewhonian botany professor, perhaps a covert meat-eater, argues from common descent and inherited germ-memory that plants are as intelligent and alive as animals, drives the meat prohibition to absurdity, and wins its repeal via an oracle, after which the narrator laments that Erewhonians still trust prophets.

The narrator resumes the history of Erewhon's meat prohibition. Laws against secret meat-eating keep being evaded and then revived by disaster or fanatic preaching. Six or seven hundred years after the old prophet, a Professor of botany appears who, unlike the prophet, claims no unseen authority but legislates just as confidently. Some Erewhonians suspect he was a secret meat-eater who wished to push the prohibition to an absurdity no Puritan could bear. The Professor argues that animals and plants share a common ancestry and so are cousins; that germs of oak and of man are indistinguishable; that development is governed by the habits of ancestral germs; and that plant adaptation is a form of intelligence. He concludes, or pretends to conclude, that eating vegetables is as sinful as eating animals, leaving only windfalls and dying leaves permissible. The country refers the dilemma to an oracle, whose rhyme sanctions killing and eating, and the meat laws are repealed. The narrator closes by remarking that the people soon fall for new prophets and faddists, and that "reason uncorrected by instinct is as bad as instinct uncorrected by reason."

Themes

Reductio ad absurdum as satire of moral absolutism. The narrator reports the view that the Professor's "monstrous proposals" were a trap: a nation that held legislation permitting killing to be sinful could only be shown otherwise by proving that "they must either sin to a certain extent, or die." The target is plausibly Victorian ethical purism and food-reform enthusiasm, though the text does not name one; the narrator himself says he suspects the Professor of insidiousness, and the attribution of motive is explicitly hedged ("Many think").

Plant intelligence defined by competence, not by interest in human affairs. The Professor's speech (an Erewhonian voice, filtered through the narrator's translation) inverts the usual test: plants are called stupid because they do not understand our business, yet we cannot do what they do ("the alchemy with which it turns mud into rose-leaves"). Intelligence is knowing how to do what one wants and doing it repeatedly; its mark is that it has become unconscious through perfection. This is a decentred, competence-first notion of mind that gestures at later talk of non-human cognition, though it is argued here by analogy and rhetoric, not experiment.

Heredity as memory and continuous personality. The core argument is that germ and parent form one chain of identity, so each generation remembers what it did "in the persons of its ancestors," and that skill becomes unconscious when perfected. This is the thesis Butler's Prefaces tie to unconscious memory, but within this unit it is only the Professor's argument. It resonates with habit-and-identity, where habit and identity are bound together, though Butler's version is a vitalist claim about literal memory, which does not map onto modern theories of habit. The shared-ancestry premise and the idea of development steered by inherited history also echo evolutionary thinking as discussed in dennett-darwins-dangerous-idea, although the Professor's view is explicitly mnemonic and intelligence-driven, much less like Darwinian design without a designer.

Scripture reread. The narrator, in his own voice, links the Professor to the Gospel's lilies of the field, and quarrels with the claim that they "toil not" and "spin not," noting there is "textile fabric in a leaf." This is the narrator's gloss, in a pious register, not Butler's thesis; note the earlier chapters' Book of the Machines is said to have been inspired by this passage.

Oracle and common sense. The oracle's verse ("Be killed or kill; / Choose which you will") resolves the impasse by acknowledging that living involves harm. The narrator calls this the country arriving at "the conclusions that common sense had long since arrived at." The Puritans' diet of apple-and-cabbage jam is a comic image of strictness defeated.

Credulity persists. The narrator ends by saying people want to believe that some know what they say they know and so save themselves "the trouble of thinking for themselves"; prophets, faddists and philosophers regain power. His closing maxim about reason and instinct is the narrator's, and it is not obviously Butler's settled position.

Notable passages

The germ argument (c1) sets the premise that plants and animals are cousins and that development follows ancestral habit. The rose speech (c2) is the satirical high point on plant competence. The "intelligence" definition (c3) states the unconscious-skill thesis; the chain-of-personality argument (c4) supplies its mechanism. The oracle (c5) closes the plot, and the final paragraph (c6) is the narrator's moral about credulity and the balance of reason and instinct.
