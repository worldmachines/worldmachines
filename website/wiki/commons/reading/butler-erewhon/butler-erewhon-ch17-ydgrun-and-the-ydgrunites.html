Ydgrun, the Real Religion of Erewhon

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The narrator reports that the Erewhonians' official religion is skin-deep while their real, unacknowledged faith is the goddess Ydgrun (respectability or custom), describes the self-governing 'high Ydgrunites', and records his own failed missionary scruples and an argument over a sect preaching a future state.

The narrator argues that the Erewhonians' professed religion, with its idols, temples and priests, is only skin-deep, and that their working faith is the worship of the goddess Ydgrun, which guides almost every action without show. He sorts her devotees into Ydgrunites, low Ydgrunites and 'high Ydgrunites', the last a class of fearless, courteous gentlemen who privately disbelieve the official gods yet conform out of kindness. He then turns to his own position. He wants to convert the Erewhonians, believes them to be the lost tribes of Israel, and finds that the words stick in his throat when he faces the high Ydgrunites. The chapter ends with the Erewhonians' defence of the old personified deities as a support for ordinary virtue, a small sect that preaches an afterlife of reward for the healthy and torture for the sick, and a dialogue in which an Erewhonian turns Hamlet's soliloquy on its head. Everything here is the narrator's report. He is himself a target of the satire, so his verdicts are not Butler's thesis.

Themes

The shadow religion behind the official one. The narrator offers three reasons for thinking the professed faith has little hold: priests complain of indifference, the gods get no show of devotion that compares with Ydgrun's, and priests are often priests of Ydgrun in practice. A reader may take Ydgrun as Butler's figure for Victorian respectability or social convention, which is a reading and not something the unit names. She is 'omnipresent and omnipotent' yet 'not an elevated conception', and she tolerates denial as long as she is 'obeyed and feared'. The narrator calls her 'beneficent and useful', so the satire cuts both ways. Convention is a crude but effective social technology, and it holds many people to conduct that a 'higher and more spiritual ideal' could not. This loosely echoes the WMP interest in what actually runs a society beneath its declared institutions (world-machines). The decay-sequence idea of a performative or ornamental official layer may fit the Musical Banks and gods, though this chapter does not describe that process.

The gentleman as an unacknowledged exception. The high Ydgrunites are courageous, generous and self-reliant, and they override Ydgrun 'with due self-reliance' because 'they are brave, and Ydgrun is not'. They keep their disbelief in the gods private so as not to give pain, and they conform until conformity is 'absolutely intolerable'. The narrator admires them and then spoils the admiration with his own pious wish to save them from 'perdition'. He also suggests that the best they could do for others is example. The tone is affectionate toward the gentleman and mocking of the narrator.

Candour versus discretion. The narrator objects that people with clear minds owe it to others to say openly what they think, so that errors can be refuted. He admits a self-interested motive: it would make his own 'future task more easy'. A sincere liberal principle and a mercenary missionary motive are woven together, which marks him as an unreliable guide.

Why the old deities are kept. The Erewhonians resist dropping the 'objective personality' of hope, justice and the like. They argue that ordinary virtue would lapse without it, and the narrator's reply (that anyone not kept straight by love and fear of seen men will not be kept straight by unseen gods) leaves the dispute unresolved, since they say they have 'ages ago' seen the divinities. Practice is managed through 'unwritten compromises (for the most part in Ydgrun's favour)'.

The sect and the inverted Hamlet. A sect rewards the healthy, handsome and strong in the afterlife and tortures the sickly, ignoring moral conduct. That is a theological continuation of the Erewhonian equation of health with virtue. The majority oppose it as immoral because it would cheapen this life. In the dialogue the Erewhonian reverses Hamlet: people stay at their posts out of hope that the fire may burn less fiercely, not out of fear of something worse after death. The narrator is again unable to answer.

Notable passages

The compass of their lives. The opening sets up the thesis that the real religion is invisible from outside but steers 'the mariner's compass of their lives'. See chunk c1.

Ydgrun's anomalous position. Her character (cruel and absurd, ashamed-of, tolerant of denial, practically useful) is the key characterization. See c2.

High Ydgrunites. The gentlemen who override the goddess because 'Ydgrun is not' brave. This is Butler's most sympathetic portrait in the chapter, softened by the narrator's missionary condescension. See c3.

Duty to speak plainly. The narrator's open-debate argument, which doubles as his own self-interest. See c4.

Why keep the gods. The Erewhonian defence of personified deities and the narrator's retort. See c5.

The sect and its critics. The healthy-saved, sick-damned afterlife and the arguments against it, which end in the Hamlet dispute. See c6.
