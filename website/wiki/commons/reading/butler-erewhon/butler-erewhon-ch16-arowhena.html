Arowhena, the King's distrust of machines, and the personified gods

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The narrator falls for Arowhena while being steered toward her sister Zulora, deflects the King's suspicion of mechanical contrivances, and reports the Erewhonians' real religion of personified, outcome-punishing deities, which Arowhena defends against his demythologizing arguments.

The narrator confesses he cannot like the Nosnibors except Arowhena, whom he loves, and he learns he is expected by custom to marry the eldest unmarried daughter, Zulora. Between the courtship thread he reports court life, including a conversation in which the King's grave reaction to his watch leads him to hide steam-engines, railroads and telegraphs and offer balloons instead (a detail he calls "pregnant with the most important consequences"). Arowhena is set on him to open a pro formâ account at the Musical Banks, and in those conversations he learns the Erewhonians' actual, unrecognised religion: open idolatry of personified qualities (justice, hope, fear, love) plus gods of physical law who punish the deed, not the motive. He argues that justice is only a mode of human thought; she answers that belief in the person is what sustains reverence for the thing, then turns his argument around on his own God. He wins by appeal to ancient books, not by reasoning, and later records that her remarks have haunted him.

Themes

The narrator as satirical target. He calls Mr. Nosnibor an embezzler, Mrs. Nosnibor worldly, and Arowhena perfection, but also admits to arguing for the pleasure of being argued with, to shrinking his own beliefs when they cost him affection, and to "the cloven hoof" he shows only after marriage. His reasoning is expedient rather than principled; his opinions should not be read as Butler's thesis.

Machines as a suspect category. The King questions the narrator about his watch and the "dangerous inventions" of his country; the narrator lies by omission about steam and telegraphs. This is the first appearance in this unit of the Erewhonian wariness of machinery, set up before the Book of the Machines. It is a prohibition seen from the outside, with no reasons yet given. It faintly echoes useless-machine and the idea of a society deliberately halting technology, though here it is only courtly unease, not an argued position.

Heredity of custom and rules about marriage. Physical excellence (light hair) outweighs other disqualifications, and a "sacred and inviolable rule" requires marrying the eldest unmarried daughter. A likely target is Victorian family and marriage custom, and the valuation of looks and money; this is a reading, not stated.

Personification and the law that ignores motive. The Erewhonian gods of time and space punish a stone and a head that occupy the same space, regardless of intent; the air-god drowns a man whether he fell in or went in to save a child. The narrator reports that they are "even as the English law, which assumes itself to be known to every one." This reads as a satire of treating physical accident as moral transgression, consistent with the book's inversion of illness and crime, and of the law's indifference to ignorance.

Reverence for the thing vs. belief in the person. Arowhena's position is that demythologizing abolishes the incentive to be just or hopeful. The narrator's counter is mild; her turnabout, that his God is likewise only man's expression of the Divine, nearly conquers him, and he retreats to scriptural authority. Both sides are staged by the narrator, so the argument is a double-edged satire on Erewhonian idolatry and on English Christian personalism; Butler's own view is not asserted here. Her argument rhymes loosely with WMP interests in how the symbols and tools a society uses shape what it can think, cf. tools-that-shape-thought, though the text is about theology, not tools.

Official vs. actual belief. The Musical Banks are the recognised institution; the personified gods are the "genuine and potent faith which existed without recognition alongside of their idol worship." Ydgrun is named here as the goddess whose dictates favour the Banks.

Notable passages

The catalogue of the open gods and the hostility to any "higher spiritual conception" is the unit's core statement of the Erewhonian religion. The law-of-occupied-space passage is the sharpest satire of motive-blind justice. Arowhena's reply, and later her demythologizing of his God, is the dramatic centre; the closing paragraph, with "godly people who have had a great knowledge of divinity, but no sense of the divine", is the narrator's retrospective turn and the one place where the satire softens into something more earnest. The watch conversation is the load-bearing hook for the machines theme.
