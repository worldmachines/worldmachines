Mahaina and the Concealment of Illness

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The narrator, lodging with the Nosnibors, watches the family dissect a visitor's claimed dipsomania, then retreats to reflect that Erewhon's punishment of bodily ill-health only breeds concealment and makes sickness unknowable.

The narrator stays with the Nosnibors; Mr. Nosnibor, recovered from his flogging, is prosperous and paid by the minute. A visitor, Mahaina, arrives complaining of her "poor dipsomania", and the ladies trade straightener recommendations and formulaic cures. After she leaves, the family debates whether she drinks at all, Zulora arguing it is a pretence to dodge calisthenics. The narrator, who has concluded that Mahaina is temperate, retires to his room and reflects on why Erewhonian severity toward ill-health produces dissimulation, and on how illness ought to be openly announced.

Themes

Inversion of illness and vice. In Erewhon, drunkenness is a mental malady for straighteners, while a weak body is a moral failing. Mahaina's claimed vice is socially safer than admitted physical weakness: Zulora says she "pretends to get tipsy in order to win a forbearance from her friends to which she is not entitled". The likely Victorian target (a hedged reading) is a culture that moralises sickness and medicalises vice, and the hypocrisy of drawing-room gossip.

Gossip as a self-undoing loop. The ladies assert charity, then undo it. Butler's narrator calls the debate a "cyclone or circular storm". Each affirmation of Mahaina's health is retracted with a "serious imputation upon her constitution", a nice picture of social judgement that cannot settle.

The narrator is a target too. His reflections are earnest, even self-satisfied: he sorts body (no initial control) from mind ("capable of being created anew"). That position is the narrator's, not necessarily Butler's, and the sharp inversion of body and mind there is itself a piece of satire on Erewhonian reasoning, as is his own assumption that his views would be received. Treat his remedial proposals (a pill and a bedroom for a headache) as sincere but limited.

Punishment breeds opacity. The unit's most legible argument: where ill-health is a crime, people become skilled at hiding it, so that "it was really impossible to say whether any one was well or ill" without months or years of acquaintance, and marriages suffer. This is a loose rhyme with the project's interest in Legibility: a regime that tries to make bodies legible by punishing deviation instead produces dissimulation and opacity. The fit is partial, since Butler's concern is social hypocrisy, not state measurement or infrastructure.

Notable passages

Mahaina's arrival. The embrace and the inquiry after her "poor dipsomania" set up the inversion: an illness is claimed with pride and consoled by good health.

Zulora's accusation. She calls it "indigestion" and says Mahaina feigns tipsiness to escape calisthenics, showing that vice can be the preferred diagnosis.

The circular storm. The half-hour debate is the unit's comic centre.

The narrator's complaint. His reflection on body and mind, and his plea for compassion, is the clearest articulation of the narrator's reformist stance.

Dissembling. The passage on painted faces and concealed infirmity states the cost of the Erewhonian system.

Headaches and medicine-chests. A small comic image: a healthy society where almost every family hides a medicine-chest.
