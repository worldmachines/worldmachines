Illness on Trial: The Personal Bereavement Court and the Consumptive's Sentence

Commons · Erewhon · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The narrator reports Erewhonian courts that try misfortune as crime (a bereaved widower, a swindled minor) and, at length, a consumptive man whose sentencing speech justifies punishing illness as a menace to respectable society, to the unborn, and against a feared medical profession.

The narrator, describing Erewhon in the first person, shows how the Erewhonian principle that misfortune is more or less criminal operates in court. He first reports two short cases in the Personal Bereavement Court: a widower convicted of losing a beloved wife (his sentence commuted to a fine of a quarter of his insurance money), and a youth swindled by his guardian, who is flogged for being young and inexperienced. He then gives, out of chronological order, the full trial of a young man charged with pulmonary consumption, which was until recently a capital offence. The trial is procedurally fair and "absolutely impartial", and the judge's long sentence, which the narrator says he copied from a newspaper report, is the chapter's centrepiece. It ends with life imprisonment at hard labour, castor oil, and the narrator's remark on the general respect for law and order.

Themes

Misfortune as crime; luck as the object of veneration. The judge in the first case says that books extenuating the guilt of misfortune will keep appearing "until it is recognised as an axiom of morality that luck is the only fit object of human veneration". Who counts as lucky is settled "by a kind of higgling and haggling of the market, and ultimately by brute force". This is the sharpest sentence in the chapter: Butler (through a satirised judge) lets the logic of desert reduce to a market and force. The likely target, read hedgingly, is Victorian moralising about poverty and sickness as signs of moral failing, and the respectability worship the book later names Ydgrun. The widower's lucky insurance windfall, and the fine as a percentage of it, make the insurance company a quiet third party.

Procedural justice around an absurd premise. The narrator stresses that the trial copies European forms (jury, plea, impartial counsel). The horror is that fairness is applied to a category error. The defence's only live argument is that the man is faking illness for an annuity; if shown, he would be treated for a "moral ailment". The inversion is exact: here illness is crime and fraud is the thing treated in hospital. The satire works through the court's courtesy and the prisoner's meek acceptance that he has had a fair trial.

Refusing causes. The judge rules out explanations of origin (unhealthy parents, childhood accident) as "curious metaphysical questions" that would blame "the tissues of the primordial cell, or on the elementary gases". He reduces the question to "are you wicked or not?" and later "it is your crime to be unfortunate". Read as satire, this parodies a legal and moral habit of ignoring causal and social origins, and a regress argument that would dissolve all responsibility. Butler's ambiguity is visible: the judge's regress is also a real problem about responsibility that the text does not settle.

Fear of the physician as institutional power. The judge defends the ban on medicine on political grounds: physicians would acquire power over households and the nation, and "universal dephysicalisation" and medicine-vendors would follow. This is a satire of an institution defending itself by suppressing the knowledge that would threaten it, and, read hedgingly, of English attitudes to the medical profession. It is also oddly like an argument about capture by a specialist class, though here it justifies suppression rather than warning of it.

The unborn as natural enemies. The judge says the unborn "are our natural enemies" who "must be quartered upon those who are least likely to corrupt them". This anticipates the book's later birth-formula material but is only touched here; the unborn are treated as a threat to be managed.

Absurd symmetry of punishment. Even acquittal would not save the prisoner, who would then be guilty of "the crime of having been maligned unjustly". The sentence closes the logical trap: there is no outcome in which the prisoner is innocent. The treatment prescribed (castor oil) is a bathos that deflates the "majestic" severity the narrator admires.

The narrator. The narrator calls the sentence "solemn, not to say majestic" and admires the "general respect for law and order". He is himself a target: his detached, approving tone mirrors the people he describes, and the reader must supply the irony. His opinions here are not Butler's thesis.

For World Machines the chapter has little direct machine content. The nearest rhyme is institutional: a legibility-style classification of the body and its failures into offences (Legibility), where the system's categories override the causes and the individual. That fit is loose; Butler is satirising moral jurisprudence, not building a model of a social machine.

Notable passages

  • The judge's luck speech (c1) states the chapter's thesis in the first, minor case: luck is venerated, and the distribution of luck is market and force.
  • The opening of the consumption sentence (c2) shows the formal, impartial register and the claim that the evidence was conclusive and the constitution "radically vicious".
  • The refusal of causes (c3) rules out parentage, accident and metaphysical regress, reducing the question to "are you wicked or not?"
  • The physician passage (c4) justifies the whole regime as a defence against medical power.
  • The "fault in you" passage (c5) contains the inversion in its purest form: "it is your crime to be unfortunate".
  • The final sentence (c6) adds hard labour for life and castor oil.
