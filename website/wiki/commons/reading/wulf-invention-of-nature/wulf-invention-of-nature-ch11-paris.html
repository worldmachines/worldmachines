Humboldt's Paris Years: Fame, Rivalry, and the Fall of Napoleon

Commons · The Invention of Nature: Alexander von Humboldt's New World · updated 22 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Between his return from South America and Napoleon's fall, Humboldt builds a frenetic Paris life of publication and correspondence, forges his closest friendship with Arago while alienating his patriotic brother Wilhelm, endures Napoleon's jealous suspicion of his rival scientific empire, and finally watches the occupying Allied armies fill the city he calls his second fatherland.

This unit covers roughly a decade of Alexander von Humboldt's life in Paris, from his triumphant return from the Americas through the completion of his lavish Vues des Cordillères, into the deepening friendship with François Arago, the strain with his brother Wilhelm over his choice to live among the 'enemy,' Napoleon's petty hostility toward him as a scientific rival, and finally the collapse of Napoleon's empire as Allied armies occupy Paris in 1814 and again after Waterloo in 1815 — with Humboldt using his standing to shield the Jardin des Plantes from military encampment both times.

Themes

Productivity as identity, and the encyclopedic project as rivalry. Humboldt works himself into exhaustion producing multiple simultaneous publications, most notably the opulent Vues des Cordillères. His correspondence network spans Jefferson, Banks, and colleagues across Europe and the Americas — an early instance of a scientist building a personal information network that rivals state apparatuses. This becomes explicit rivalry with Napoleon: Humboldt's multi-volume Voyage directly competes with the state-sponsored Description de l'Égypte, itself the product of some 200 scientists cataloguing a conquered territory. Both projects are totalizing attempts to render a newly seized or explored world legible through exhaustive plates and description — one backed by an empire's army and treasury, the other by a single indefatigable Prussian — and the text notes with clear relish that Humboldt's, lacking either, still 'achieved more.' The parallel between conquest-by-cataloguing and Humboldt's own cataloguing of the Andes is suggestive of legibility-as-machine-core, though the chapter treats it as personal rivalry rather than systemic argument.

Friendship as counterweight to displacement. Arago becomes Humboldt's 'Siamese twin' and the person to whom he can show 'fears and self-doubts' — a rare admission for a man otherwise performing tireless brilliance in salons. The friendship is turbulent (fights, sulking, reconciliation) but functions as Humboldt's real emotional anchor in a Paris where he is politically and nationally exposed. Wilhelm's anxious remark about Alexander's 'passion to be only with one person' signals how unusual and consuming the bond is.

Cosmopolitanism versus patriotic duty. Wilhelm, dutifully serving Prussia as Minister of Education and then ambassador to Vienna, is repeatedly wounded by Alexander's refusal to come home, his publishing in French, and his failure to protect the family estate at Tegel. The brothers embody two responses to national crisis: Wilhelm's institutional service versus Alexander's insistence on staying in the capital of the power devastating his own country, because that capital is where the science is. A German newspaper's public attack on Humboldt for preferring French friendship to the 'honour' of his people sharpens this into an explicit charge of national betrayal.

State suspicion of an unbendable mind. Napoleon's contempt for Humboldt — surveillance, an expulsion order, sneering condescension about botany — reads as a study in how autocratic power reacts to an intellect it cannot direct or flatter into service. Humboldt's is a rare case of scientific fame that Napoleon could neither buy nor punish out of existence, rescued only by the intervention of the treasurer of the Senate.

Collapse of an imperial order and the protected space of science. The chapter ends with the Napoleonic world machine cracking in real time: the catastrophic Russian campaign, Leipzig, the Allied occupation of Paris, exile to Elba, the Hundred Days, and Waterloo. Humboldt, watching the ideals of 1789 evaporate, occupies an awkward middle position — too French for Prussians, too Prussian to fully belong in France. Yet his standing lets him do something concrete amid the chaos: twice he intervenes with occupying Prussian commanders to keep troops out of the Jardin des Plantes, protecting Cuvier's collections and menagerie from a plundering, war-torn city. The garden functions here almost as a small defended enclave of ordered knowledge holding out against the surrounding upheaval — a faint echo of The Thicket as a curated space sustained by personal authority rather than by institutional protection, and the whole episode reads as a local, human-scale instance of the interregnum that WMP calls a gramsci-gap, where an old order's legitimacy is visibly dying and nothing has yet replaced it.

Notable passages

Napoleon's sneer about botany and Humboldt's flat verdict that the emperor 'hates me' (paragraph on their first meeting) crystallizes the state's discomfort with an intellect it cannot bend. The Description de l'Égypte comparison makes the rivalry material: two competing encyclopedic projects of a newly opened world, one funded by empire, one by a single scientist's will. The Arago passage, with its 'Siamese twins' language, is the clearest window into Humboldt's interior life offered in the whole unit. Wilhelm's complaint that his brother 'had stopped being German' captures the cost of Humboldt's cosmopolitan choice. The Haydon description of the Allied entry into Paris — Cossacks, Tartars in chainmail, Prussians filling the streets — stages the world-machine collapse visually. And the Jardin des Plantes rescue shows Humboldt converting personal fame into protection for a specific, bounded space of scientific order against an occupying army.
