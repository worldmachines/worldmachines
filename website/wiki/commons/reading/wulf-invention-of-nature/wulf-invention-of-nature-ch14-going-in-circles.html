Going in Circles: Humboldt Stalled Between Paris and Berlin

Commons · The Invention of Nature: Alexander von Humboldt's New World · updated 22 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Blocked for a decade from mounting a new expedition to India by the East India Company, the fifty-something Humboldt cycles restlessly between scientific patronage, financial ruin, and Restoration-era Paris politics until his own king finally orders him back to Berlin in 1827.

Covering roughly 1818 to 1827, this unit follows Humboldt through the most frustrating decade of his career: a man famous across Europe, showered with royal audiences and scientific admirers, yet unable to secure the one thing he wants — permission to travel to India — while his finances collapse under the cost of self-publishing his American research, his old companion Bonpland is kidnapped and imprisoned by a Paraguayan dictator, and Restoration France's political climate turns hostile enough that his own king finally orders him home to Berlin. The unit ends on the threshold of Part IV ("Influence: Spreading Ideas"), with eighteen-year-old Charles Darwin quitting medical school — an unremarked hinge on which the book's next movement turns.

Themes

Stagnation dressed as fame. Humboldt spends this decade as Europe's most celebrated scientist and its most thwarted traveler simultaneously. The Prince Regent, George Canning, and the Prussian king all offer support, but the East India Company simply refuses him entry, year after year. The contrast with his earlier "breathless activity and constant forward trajectory" in Latin America is the chapter's organizing irony, crystallized in a friend's diagnosis of his condition as a "maladie centrifuge" — a centrifugal illness, spinning without displacement. His vacillating fantasies of relocating to Mexico or Latin America (which Wilhelm dismisses as things Alexander "envisages... as being huge, and then not even half of it happens") are symptoms of the same blockage.

Self-ruin as scientific investment. The thirty-four-volume Voyage to the Equinoctial Regions of the New Continent — the most expensive privately published scientific work of its time — consumes his inheritance entirely. He lives frugally, in unfashionable clothes, in a sparsely furnished apartment, while continuing to bankroll younger scientists' careers (Caroline's complaint that "he eats dry bread, so that they can eat meat" captures the pattern). Humboldt functions as an informal, self-financed institution of science: recommending, equipping, and connecting Lyell, Liebig, Gallatin, and a stream of unnamed protégés, in a period before formalized research funding existed.

Isotherms as a legibility technology. Humboldt's 1817 essay on isothermal lines converts scattered, incomparable tables of temperature readings into a single global visual pattern — wavy belts hugging the earth — founding what he called vergleichende Klimatologie, comparative climatology. This is a clean instance of the legibility-as-machine-core move: raw, locally-embedded data rendered into a standardized, comparable, map-based form that other observers (Lyell, in this case, building Principles of Geology) can then build on. The isotherm is presented explicitly as an ancestor of a still-living scientific infrastructure — "today's scientists still use them to understand and depict climate change."

Science as a nation without borders — until it collides with power. Bonpland's arc dramatizes both the promise and the fragility of that idea. Recruited by Bolívar's revolutionaries as a form of soft-power science diplomacy ("the illustrious Franklin obtained more good in France for his country through the natural sciences than through all the diplomatic efforts"), Bonpland returns to South America to found botanic gardens and grow yerba mate — only to be violently kidnapped and imprisoned by Paraguay's dictator Francia on suspicion of "agricultural espionage." Even Bolívar's and Humboldt's combined political capital cannot free him. Science's claim to transcend borders is shown to be conditional on the tolerance of whoever holds a border's guns.

The closing of Restoration France. Humboldt's reluctant return to Berlin is driven less by royal impatience alone than by the curdling of Paris itself: ultra-royalist ascendancy, tightened censorship, a secret Académie committee purging Rousseau and Voltaire from its library, and religion reasserting its grip as church spires rise across the skyline. Humboldt's own remark that savants have become "pliant tools" trading science for "ribbons, crosses, titles and Court favours" reads as a small, personal instance of a wider machine-transition friction — a liberal, cosmopolitan scientific culture losing its footing as reactionary institutions reconsolidate.

Undimmed physical daring. Even as bureaucratic doors close on him, Humboldt's appetite for embodied risk hasn't aged out. His descent with Isambard Kingdom Brunel in a diving bell to inspect the half-built Thames Tunnel — ending in ruptured blood vessels and a nosebleed he compares to his Chimborazo ascent — shows the same experiential, first-person empiricism that defined his youth, applied now to industrial rather than natural wonders.

Notable passages

The "maladie centrifuge" paragraph is the chapter's thesis in miniature: fame without motion, restlessness with nowhere sanctioned to go. The isotherms passage is the unit's clearest resonance with the WMP legibility concept — a named, still-used method for converting local variation into comparable global pattern. Bonpland's imprisonment supplies the darkest counterweight to Humboldt's cosmopolitan science-diplomacy ideal. The king's 1826 letter ordering Humboldt's "speedy return" is the formal instrument that ends the Paris chapter of his life. And the Thames Tunnel diving-bell scene is included for its vividness and its argument-by-contrast: whatever doors politics closed on Humboldt, his body was still willing to descend into a river to measure air pressure.
