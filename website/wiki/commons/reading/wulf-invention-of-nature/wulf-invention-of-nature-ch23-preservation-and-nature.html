John Muir: Walking Into Humboldt's Cosmos

Commons · The Invention of Nature: Alexander von Humboldt's New World · updated 22 August 2026 · source on GitHub
John Muir builds his entire life's trajectory as a re-enactment of Humboldt's, walking from Indianapolis to Florida to the Sierra Nevada, discovering Yosemite's glacial origins and a mystical vision of ecological unity, and ultimately converting Humboldt's holistic science into the American preservationist movement (Yosemite National Park, the Sierra Club, and the losing fight to save Hetch Hetchy) before finally reaching South America at age seventy-three.

This unit traces John Muir's life from his 1867 departure from Indianapolis — undertaken explicitly to become "a Humboldt" — through his near-blinding accident, his thousand-mile walk south, his discovery and glacial theory of Yosemite Valley, his conversion of Humboldtian holism into a nature mysticism, and his transformation into America's leading preservationist, founder of the Sierra Club, and loser of the Hetch Hetchy fight, closing as he finally sets foot in South America in 1911, forty-four years after first trying and more than a century after Humboldt himself.

Themes

Discipleship as itinerary. Muir's journey is staged, almost liturgically, as a re-enactment of Humboldt's voyage: he opens his notebook with "John Muir, Earth-planet, Universe," reads Humboldt's descriptions of the "flooded forests of the Orinoco" while scrambling through Canadian bogs, and declares outright, "How intensely I desire to be a Humboldt." The accident that blinds him temporarily and the subsequent detour through malaria in Florida, illness in Cuba, and a weeping glimpse of rainforest from a Panama train car all read as obstacles in a hero's quest that is only redirected — to California — rather than abandoned, and is finally completed at age seventy-three.

From specimen-collecting to holism. Muir starts as a Humboldt-inspired "botanizer" nicknamed "Botany," but the walk south changes his mode of perception: he moves from collecting individual specimens to seeing "no unconnected 'fragment,'" a direct extension of Humboldt's Cosmos and Naturgemälde. In Yosemite this becomes his signature formulation — "When we try to pick out anything by itself, we find it hitched to everything else in the universe" — and structures his later political rhetoric (the valley as "fingers to the palm of a hand," not a separable "fragment").

Empirical synthesis in Humboldt's mode. Muir's glacial theory of Yosemite's formation — contradicting the state Geological Survey's cataclysmic-eruption account — is presented as Humboldtian method in practice: he "zoomed from the minute to the magnificent," counting flowers by the hundred thousand in one breath and staking a living glacier's motion in the next, uniting sensory immersion with quantitative measurement rather than choosing between them.

Nature reenchanted as religion. Where Humboldt felt nature emotionally and organically, Muir goes further into explicit theology: Yosemite becomes his "mountain temple," its rocks and plants "the words of God," and he casts himself as an "apostle" preaching nature to a "juiceless world." This is less an extension of Humboldt's science than a devotional intensification of it — a difference the note treats as consequential for what preservation later means to Muir.

Preservation splits from conservation. The chapter draws an explicit lineage and a fork: Humboldt diagnosed deforestation, Marsh (in Man and Nature) built the evidentiary case for regulated, economically-rational conservation, and Muir broke off toward preservation — protecting nature from human impact altogether, on moral rather than economic grounds. This bifurcation of a single intellectual inheritance into two competing political projects is the chapter's central argumentative move.

Activism as institution-building, and its first great defeat. Muir converts Humboldtian ideas into durable political machinery: the 1890 Yosemite National Park designation, the 1892 founding of the Sierra Club, and the national letter-writing campaign against the Hetch Hetchy dam — the first fight to be waged explicitly between "preservation and progress" on a national stage. The campaign is lost, San Francisco gets its reservoir, and Muir's "Nothing dollarable is safe, however guarded" reads as a founding trauma that nonetheless taught the preservationist movement how to organize nationally.

A world remaking itself faster than wilderness can be defended. Running underneath Muir's story is the closing of the American frontier: 15 million acres claimed annually, transcontinental telegraph and railway completed within Muir's own Yosemite years, standardized "railway time" replacing sun and moon, and Frederick Jackson Turner's 1903 declaration that the conquest of the wilderness was complete. This is the modernity-machine's legibility-as-machine-core operating at full speed — land, time, and resources rendered measurable and extractable — against which Muir's preservationism functions as a form of capture-resistance, insisting that some things must remain outside the "dollarable." Muir's decades-long, unextinguished pursuit of his Amazon dream — "It has been burning in me for half a century, and will burn forever" — also reads as a striking case of Conatus, a persistence that outlasts every practical reason to let the ambition go.

Notable passages

The opening hero-worship passage ("How intensely I desire to be a Humboldt") establishes the imitative frame for everything that follows — Muir's entire biography here is legible only against Humboldt's itinerary and self-conception. The "hitched to everything else in the universe" passage is the chapter's philosophical center, showing Muir converting Humboldtian holism into a durable, quotable formula that later underwrites his political rhetoric about Yosemite's interdependent valleys. The preservation-vs-conservation passage makes the chapter's key historiographical distinction explicit, crediting Humboldt with the diagnosis, Marsh with the argument, and Muir with turning it into a public movement. The Hetch Hetchy passage shows preservation's first national test and its defeat, articulating the stakes ("if parts of a national park could be claimed for commercial reasons, then nothing was truly protected") that still define wilderness politics. Finally, the railway-and-standard-time passage supplies the historical counter-pressure against which Muir's preservationism is fighting: an accelerating, legibility-hungry industrial machine that measures, claims, and times the whole continent.
