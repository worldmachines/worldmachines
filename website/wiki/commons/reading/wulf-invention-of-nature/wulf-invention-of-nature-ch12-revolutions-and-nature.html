Bolívar's Revolution, Written in Humboldt's Language

Commons · The Invention of Nature: Alexander von Humboldt's New World · updated 22 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Simón Bolívar's decade-plus campaign to liberate Spanish South America is narrated as inseparable from Alexander von Humboldt's influence — his statistics-laden critique of colonialism supplying revolutionary ammunition, his descriptions of Andean landscapes supplying Bolívar's rhetorical language, and his rebuttal of Buffon's degeneracy thesis supplying the continent a claim to natural and civilizational parity with Europe.

This unit follows Simón Bolívar's nearly twenty-year war to expel Spain from northern South America (1807–1822), framing the revolution as one fought as much with Humboldt's ideas as with guns. Bolívar, radicalized in Europe and stirred by his 1804–5 meetings with Humboldt, returns to Venezuela to lead a rebellion marked by catastrophic reversals (the 1812 Caracas earthquake, the civil war against Boves's "Legions of Hell," repeated exile), a bloody "War to the Death" decree, an alliance with Haiti's Pétion that ties revolution to abolition, and eventual triumph at Boyacá (1819) and the founding of Gran Colombia. Threaded through the military narrative is Humboldt's parallel role: his statistical, data-dense Political Essay on the Kingdom of New Spain becomes required reading for both Jefferson (anxious about the colonies' fate and about future economic competition) and Bolívar (who mines it for facts about his own continent and for its blistering indictment of colonial rule). The chapter closes with the transatlantic debate over Buffon's theory of American "degeneracy" and Humboldt's systematic rebuttal of it — a rebuttal Bolívar and Jefferson both draw on to argue that the New World, its peoples and its nature, are not inferior to the Old.

Themes

Nature supplies the revolution's language. Bolívar does not merely fight in the Andean landscape; he thinks and speaks in it. Storms, volcanoes, gangrene, locusts, abysses — his rhetoric of liberty is built almost entirely from natural imagery, and the text argues this is a direct inheritance from Humboldt's writing ("as if writing with Humboldt's pen"). Bolívar's prose poem "My Delirium on Chimborazo" is the culminating instance: an ascent up Humboldt's own mountain that becomes an allegory of national liberation and a vision of Time itself, with Bolívar explicitly walking in and then past Humboldt's tracks.

Legibility as ammunition. Humboldt's Political Essay — tables of silver production, agricultural yields, imports and exports, demographic and racial statistics — is less travel narrative than "a handbook of facts, hard data and numbers," so exhaustive an English translator complained it would "fatigue the attention of the reader." Yet this quantifying, cataloguing impulse (rendering the Spanish empire newly legible to itself and to outsiders) becomes the evidentiary base both for Jefferson's wary calculus about a potential rival nation and for Bolívar's own case against "the odious monopoly" of colonial extraction. The instinct to make a place legible through data here serves anti-imperial ends rather than the administrative control Scott describes elsewhere — a useful counter-case for legibility-as-machine-core.

Nature as a battlefield of civilizational rank. The unit stages the colonial-era dispute over Buffon's claim that American nature — and by extension American peoples — was smaller, weaker, more "degenerate" than Europe's, a theory Jefferson had spent decades trying to refute (moose carcasses shipped to Paris, mastodon bones sent to the Académie des Sciences) and which Humboldt demolishes from direct observation: Caribs he calls "like bronze statues of Jupiter," pre-Inca astronomical manuscripts, and languages rich enough to hold abstractions like eternity. For Bolívar, this is not academic: dismantling Buffon dismantles the ideological scaffolding of colonial contempt.

Revolution's brutal texture, not romanticized. Alongside the nature-mysticism, the chapter is blunt about atrocity — the earthquake read as divine punishment, the "War to the Death" decree condemning Spaniards en masse, Boves's 80,000 dead, and Bolívar's own contradictions (charming yet capable of ordering mass executions). Slavery's centrality to the war (the Pétion pact, the "black veil" that persists even after Bolívar's own emancipation) is treated as a hinge point rather than a footnote.

Notable passages

The epigraph poem stages Bolívar's self-conscious positioning relative to Humboldt — walking his tracks, then deliberately leaving them to make his own. The Caracas earthquake passage ("If Nature itself decides to oppose us... we will fight and force her to obey") shows nature treated as an active political antagonist, not backdrop. The Buffon-degeneracy material and Humboldt's rebuttal together frame nature as contested evidence in an argument about civilizational standing — a dispute Jefferson, Franklin, and Bolívar all fight on the same terrain. The closing quotation, "Nature... was the 'infallible teacher of men,'" states outright the chapter's organizing claim: that for Bolívar political wisdom is to be read directly off the land.
