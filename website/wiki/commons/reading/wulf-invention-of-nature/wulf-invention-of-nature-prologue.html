Prologue: The Man Who Invented Nature

Commons · The Invention of Nature: Alexander von Humboldt's New World · updated 22 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Wulf's prologue frames Alexander von Humboldt's harrowing 1802 ascent of Chimborazo as the crucible moment when he conceived of nature as one interconnected living web, then traces how thoroughly that idea (and Humboldt's own fame) has been absorbed into modern thought while his name has faded from popular memory.

Wulf opens not with Humboldt's birth but with his near-death: the 1802 climb of Chimborazo, then believed the world's tallest mountain, where at over 19,000 feet Humboldt looked down on stacked vegetation zones and arrived at a totalizing vision of the earth as a single living organism. The prologue then widens out biographically and historiographically — establishing Humboldt as a man of contradictions (aristocrat and anti-colonialist, courtier and revolutionary sympathizer), cataloguing the astonishing global reach of his name (currents, mountains, animals, minerals, even a lunar mare), and describing his 1869 centennial as a worldwide celebration on the scale of a state funeral for a still-living idea. It closes with Wulf's own methodological pilgrimage — retracing Humboldt's routes from Venezuela to Russia to Chimborazo itself — as justification for why this largely forgotten figure deserves recovery.

Themes

Nature as a connected whole, discovered at altitude. The mountain functions as both literal setting and structuring metaphor: physical extremity (bleeding feet, numb hands, thin air, a life-threatening crevasse) produces intellectual breakthrough. Humboldt's realization that vegetation zones on Chimborazo mirrored zones he had seen in Switzerland, the Arctic, and Lapland lets him see climate and life as a planetary system rather than a local curiosity — nature organized by location and climate rather than taxonomic category. This is presented as the origin of ecological thinking itself: "With this insight, he invented the web of life, the concept of nature as we know it today." This connective, systems-level way of reading the world — refusing to consider any fact in isolation — resonates with the World Machines emphasis on interlocking, distributed systems whose logic only becomes visible in aggregate, akin to a history-machine assembling silently until it lurches into view.

A visionary later occluded by his own success. Wulf's central irony is that Humboldt's ideas became so pervasive — ecosystems, isotherms, climate zones, the interconnection of species — that they turned invisible as his ideas, absorbed into common sense while his name receded from English-language memory. This is a variant of the decay-sequence logic (decay-sequence): a conceptual infrastructure so thoroughly naturalized it becomes functionally invisible, cited by Rachel Carson and James Lovelock (whose Gaia hypothesis Humboldt anticipated by over 150 years) without acknowledgment. The prologue explicitly stages Humboldt alongside Copernicus, Newton, Jefferson, and Darwin as one of the shapers of how we understand our place in the world — but the one whose name has detached from his contribution.

Instrumentation and sensation together, not measurement alone. Humboldt is drawn as a paradoxical empiricist: obsessive about instruments (barometer, cyanometer, sextant) and precise data, yet insistent that "nature had to be experienced through feelings" and that a "love of nature" mattered as much as universal laws. This dual commitment — legible quantification paired with romantic sensory apprehension — sits at a real friction point in the era's intellectual culture, faintly echoing the tension between the modernity-machine's drive toward legibility and a more variety-preserving, experiential mode of knowing that resists full standardization.

Foreshadowing of ecological/climate warning. The prologue previews Humboldt's observations at Lake Valencia, Venezuela, where he links deforestation to falling water levels and soil erosion, making him (per Wulf) the first scientist to describe human-induced climate change and to warn of impacts on "future generations." This positions the entire book's stakes: an early, largely lost warning about anthropogenic environmental damage.

Notable passages

The ridge-crawling opening ("a high narrow ridge that was in places only two inches wide") establishes physical peril as a narrative engine before any biography is given — Wulf wants readers to feel the stakes of the idea before she explains it. The Chimborazo summit passage, where fog lifts to reveal the crevasse and Humboldt calculates their altitude, is the hinge moment where observation (barometer readings, species counts) converts into philosophy ("He saw the earth as one great living organism where everything was connected"). The catalogue of contradictions ("He was a fierce critic of colonialism... yet was chamberlain to two Prussian kings") does double duty: characterizing the man and warning the reader against flattening him into either hero or hypocrite. The web-of-life passage ("In this great chain of causes and effects... no single fact can be considered in isolation") is the book's thesis statement in miniature. Finally, the 1869 centennial description — tens of thousands marching in Central Park, 80,000 gathering in Berlin despite rain — quantifies just how total Humboldt's cultural presence once was, setting up the book's recovery project against the scale of what was lost.
