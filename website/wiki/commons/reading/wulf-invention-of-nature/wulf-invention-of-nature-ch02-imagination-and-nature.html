The Jena Circle: Humboldt, Goethe, and the Fusion of Science and Imagination

Commons · The Invention of Nature: Alexander von Humboldt's New World · updated 22 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Between 1794 and 1797 in Jena and Weimar, Alexander von Humboldt's friendship with Goethe fuses Humboldt's empirical rigor with Goethe's organicist, imaginative approach to nature, and this synthesis—forged through shared dissection, electrical experiments, and immersion in Kantian philosophy—reshapes Humboldt's science into the holistic, feeling-infused method he will carry to South America.

This unit traces Alexander von Humboldt's recurring visits to Jena and Weimar between 1794 and 1797, where his friendship with Johann Wolfgang von Goethe—already Germany's most famous poet but privately a frustrated naturalist—catalyzes a mutual transformation. Goethe, becalmed after the Sturm und Drang and disillusioned by the Terror and the Napoleonic Wars, finds in Humboldt the scientific sparring partner he lacked; Humboldt, in turn, absorbs Goethe's organicist morphology and, through the Jena circle's saturation in Kant, begins to fold subjective feeling and imagination into his previously strict empiricism—the intellectual equipment he will take to South America.

Themes

Goethe's urform and the unity behind variety. Goethe's Metamorphosis of Plants argues that a single archetypal form, the leaf, underlies the endless variety of plant structures—"Behind variety was unity." He extends this to animals via an internal "formative drive" (borrowed from Blumenbach) that shapes an organism from within, distinct from the external force of environment. This is an early, informal instance of what World Machines calls a history-machine: a paradigm—Humboldtian holistic science—assembling quietly from independent pieces (Goethe's botany, Blumenbach's vitalism, Humboldt's electrical experiments, Kantian philosophy) years before it "lurches into visibility" in Humboldt's later published synthesis.

Organism against machine. Goethe explicitly rejects the Cartesian view of animals as machines: a machine's parts can be dismantled and reassembled, but an organism's parts function only in relation to the whole—"In a mechanical system the parts shaped the whole while in an organic system the whole shaped the parts." This is a direct precursor to the tension World Machines names between legibility-as-machine-core's decompose-and-measure ethos and a holistic, non-reductive way of knowing nature—though Wulf's Humboldt does not abandon measurement so much as subordinate it to a larger unity.

Kant's transcendental turn and the co-shaping of mind and nature. The Jena circle is saturated in Kant's Critique of Pure Reason: the claim that the mind's categories order nature rather than nature dictating to the mind ("We impose this order on nature, and not nature upon us"). This dissolves the strict Enlightenment split between internal and external worlds and elevates the perceiving Self—setting up Humboldt's later conviction that the external world "only existed in so far as we perceived it 'within ourselves.'" The mutual shaping of observer and observed anticipates the non-teleological co-emergence that World Machines calls Configurancy.

Electricity, vitalism, and "breathing life into it." Humboldt's animal-electricity experiments with Goethe—including the accidental discovery that his own breath's moisture, not "vital force," convulsed a frog's leg—are later shown wrong by Volta, but they hand Humboldt his real prize: the idea of nature as a single field of interacting forces, to be understood through comparison rather than abstract mathematics.

Striving as method. Goethe's Faust, written in bursts coinciding with Humboldt's visits, is explicitly modeled in part on Humboldt's "feverish unrest" and desire to "detect the inmost force / Which binds the world, and guides its course." This restless, never-satisfied drive to know—shared by author and subject alike—reads as a period articulation of what World Machines calls Conatus, the striving to persist, expand, and comprehend that resists reduction to mere data-gathering (Schiller's dismissive charge that Humboldt's work showed a "poverty of meaning" despite its wealth of measurement).

The marriage of art and science. Against Schiller's fear that science was pulling Goethe from poetry, Goethe insists on their union—translating his botanical urform into verse, his chemistry into the plot of Elective Affinities. Humboldt absorbs the lesson: "Nature must be experienced through feeling," he tells Goethe, warning that classification alone "will never get close to it." This subjective turn becomes the hinge that lets Humboldt later bridge Newtonian mechanism and Romantic poetry (Keats's complaint that Newton "destroyed all the Poetry of the rainbow").

Notable passages

The urform passage crystallizes Goethe's search for unity beneath botanical variety, a structuring idea he shares with Humboldt before either has fully articulated the holistic science both will pursue. The organism-versus-machine passage gives the clearest statement of Goethe's anti-Cartesian, anti-reductive stance. The Kant passage shows the philosophical ground—the mind ordering nature rather than reading it off passively—that the whole circle absorbs and that reshapes Humboldt's epistemology. The frog's-leg experiment passage captures the moment of scientific error (soon corrected by Volta) that nonetheless supplies Humboldt with his generative metaphor of "breathing life" into inert matter. The subjectivity passage marks the explicit turning point in Humboldt's own thinking, from pure Enlightenment empiricism toward feeling as a mode of knowledge. The closing passage—Jena giving Humboldt "new organs" with which he will "see South America"—is the unit's thesis in miniature: this intellectual apprenticeship is presented as the direct precondition for Humboldt's later fieldwork and its distinctive fusion of measurement and wonder.
