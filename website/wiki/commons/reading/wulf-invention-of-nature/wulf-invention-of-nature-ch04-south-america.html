Cumaná, Caracas, and the Discovery of Deforestation at Lake Valencia

Commons · The Invention of Nature: Alexander von Humboldt's New World · updated 22 August 2026 · source on GitHub
In Cumaná, Caracas, and the Aragua Valley, Humboldt catalogues nature obsessively, survives an earthquake that shatters his faith in the earth's stability, and at Lake Valencia becomes the first scientist to identify human-caused deforestation as a driver of climate change.

Humboldt and Bonpland spend their first months in South America (Cumaná, then Caracas, then the Aragua Valley) in a state of sensory and intellectual overload, cataloguing plants, animals, and geological features while Humboldt begins to intuit that everything in nature is interconnected. The unit turns on two ruptures: the Cumaná earthquake of November 1799, which shakes Humboldt's faith in the stability of the physical world, and his observations at Lake Valencia, where he traces falling water levels to the colonial clearing of surrounding forests — the founding insight of what Wulf calls his fathering of the environmental movement.

Themes

Collecting ideas, not specimens. From the outset Humboldt distinguishes himself from taxonomically-minded naturalists: he wants the "impression of the whole" rather than a filled-in catalogue of species. This holistic instinct — comparing Venezuelan landscapes to Italian pines, Derbyshire valleys, Franconian caverns — is the seed of his mature conviction that nature is a single connected web, not a collection of discrete facts. The chapter explicitly frames this as the idea that would organize his thinking for the rest of his life.

Slavery as a fixed backdrop. Humboldt's wonder is repeatedly interrupted by the slave market opposite his rented house in Cumaná, where Africans are oiled and paraded like "horses in a market." The text is unambiguous that this is what made Humboldt a lifelong abolitionist — a moral rupture that runs alongside, rather than being resolved by, his scientific raptures.

The earthquake as epistemic shock. The 4 November 1799 earthquake in Cumaná is staged as a personal crisis of certainty: even as Humboldt calmly times the shocks and takes instrument readings, he registers, in his own words, the destruction of "the illusion of a whole life" — an unwavering assumption that solid ground, unlike water, does not move. This is less a science anecdote than a demonstration of how directly Humboldt's empirical practice and his emotional/philosophical life were fused.

Lake Valencia and the invention of anthropogenic climate change. At Lake Valencia, Humboldt combines direct measurement (fine sands on former shorelines, comparative evaporation data), local testimony, and cross-regional memory (the Fichtel Mountains near Bayreuth) to conclude that forest clearance for agriculture — not a mythical underground drain — explains the lake's falling water. He generalizes this into an early theory of human-caused climate change with a genuinely global, forward-looking warning about the ecosystem services of forests (water retention, moisture, cooling, oxygen).

Against the "improvement" ideology of nature. Wulf sets Humboldt's view against a long Western tradition — Aristotle, Linnaeus, the Bible, Bacon, Descartes, Montesquieu, Buffon, Tocqueville, American "clearing improves the climate" boosters — in which wilderness is chaos to be subdued and cultivated land is the only beautiful land. Humboldt's insistence that forces must first be understood before they are altered ("Man can only act upon nature… by comprehending her laws") is a direct rebuttal of this dominant paradigm of nature as raw material for legible, productive order. This is close kin to the legibility-as-machine-core logic of the modernity-machine: cadastral clearing of "howling wilderness" into orderly fields is exactly the kind of state-legible simplification that destroys the complexity (in this case ecological) it cannot see — the planters' rational agriculture, Humboldt shows, undermines its own long-term productive base.

Notable passages

The unit's rhetorical center of gravity is the block quotation from Humboldt's own published work describing how deforestation dries springs and converts riverbeds into flash-flood torrents — presented by Wulf as the founding text of ecological thinking. It is bracketed by two framing claims: the earlier insight that "everything seemed somehow connected," formed impressionistically in the first weeks at Cumaná, and the later, more explicit ecological chain-reaction examples (turtle-egg oil harvesting, depleted pearl oysters) that culminate in Humboldt's own aphorism, "Everything is interaction and reciprocal." The earthquake passage functions differently — not as natural-historical observation but as a moment where Humboldt's confidence in nature's constancy is punctured, setting up the deeper argument that follows: that nature's apparent stability is itself contingent and can be broken by human action just as it can by geological accident.
