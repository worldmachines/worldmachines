Fourier's Phalanstery: The Economy of Passion and the Mask of Harlequin

Commons · The Prophets of Paris: Turgot · updated 23 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Manuel lays out Fourier's phalanstery program of uncoerced child-rearing, passional free love, abundance-neutralized inequality, and cosmic "harmonious armies," then turns to psychobiography, arguing that Fourier's self-described "mask of Harlequin" concealment strategy and his megalomaniacal fantasies of vengeance against France and Paris betray a sadistic, possibly masochistic substrate beneath his utopia of love.

This unit combines two sections of Manuel's study: "The Delights of Phalanstery," which surveys Fourier's blueprint for child-rearing without coercion, free and plural love, an economic hierarchy preserved but psychologically neutralized by universal abundance, and the cosmic "harmonious armies" that fuse labor and eros into bloodless world-engineering; and "The Mask of Harlequin," Manuel's psychobiographical turn, in which Fourier's own account of deliberately disguising his sexual and economic radicalism behind absurd cosmology is read alongside evidence of paranoia, sadism, and vengeful fantasy beneath the professed utopia of love.

Themes

Passional plurality over Stoic moderation. Fourier's psychology, dramatized in the phalanstery ball's ritual of "composite" refusal (multiple torch-offers rather than single invitations, so no individual suitor is wounded), holds that multiplicity and complexity are the source of happiness and freedom, while exclusivity breeds disaster. Manuel sets this directly against Condorcet, for whom the passions were winds that could wreck the ship of Stoic calm and had to be moderated; Fourier instead treats every passion as God-given, needing only proper social channels, never suppression — a stance that resonates with conatus, the refusal to discipline striving down toward an averaged calm.

Inequality preserved, but rendered psychologically insignificant. Fourier kept private property and inheritance, denouncing Saint-Simonian communism as "unnatural," yet argued that universal material abundance (the poorest phalanstery table outclassing the finest Romanée-Conti reserved for the richest men in civilization) makes wealth differences irrelevant to lived happiness. Manuel notes that later Fourierists, downplaying this hierarchy in favor of economic communism, may not have been as wrong about Fourier's real intent as a literal reading suggests.

Talent as an inexhaustible resource. Fourier's arithmetic hyperbole — thirty-seven million future Homers, Newtons, and Molières on a fully populated globe — makes explicit his conviction that scarcity of genius is an artifact of civilization's repression rather than of nature: a vision of radical proliferation of human capacity rather than standardized, scarce output. The ball-ritual's maxim that "the complex is always just and true, the simple always false" generalizes this into an epistemology, faintly resonant with divergence-machine logics of variety-over-legibility, though Fourier's own aim was cosmic harmony rather than resistance to standardization as such.

Eros as economic and military engine. In the "harmonious armies," labor and love are administratively fused: ranked corps of bacchantes serve armies undertaking colossal public works, and war itself becomes bloodless positional play judged like chess, with captured combatants assigned as lovers rather than casualties. Manuel treats this as Fourier's fullest fantasy of frictionless social engineering, desire itself run like a civil service, financed "as economically as possible."

The mask of Harlequin — mystification as deliberate strategy. In the manuscript "Le Sphinx sans Oedipe," Fourier claims to have smuggled his attacks on the family and on the economic order past the "philosophical cabal" by burying them beneath sensational, ridiculous cosmogony — "the pearl in the mud." Manuel is careful to flag the ambiguity: this account may be genuine strategy, or a later rationalization built to armor Fourier against the ridicule his cosmology actually earned.

Psychobiography of vengeance. Fourier's claim that France's military catastrophes from 1808 onward were providential "punishment" for having mocked his treatise — tallied with grotesque precision as "a million heads" — is, for Manuel, evidence of a sadistic substrate beneath the professed utopia of universal love. The unit closes by invoking Freud and Nietzsche to suggest the vision of total love was itself born of fantasies of hate and destruction.

Notable passages

The wine-and-luxury passage stages Fourier's core move on inequality: differences in wealth persist structurally but are drowned out by an abundance so total that "the differences existed" yet "did not matter" — hierarchy retained in form, evacuated of stakes. The thirty-seven-million-geniuses passage is Fourier at his most characteristic: numerical fantasy deployed as an argument that nature is prodigal, not miserly, with talent — civilization alone artificially rations it. The ball-ritual passage on composite refusal shows Fourier's psychology of plurality operating at the level of etiquette itself, engineering a social mechanism so that no single rejection can wound. The mask-of-Harlequin passage is the interpretive crux of the second half: Fourier's own retrospective claim that his cosmological absurdities were calculated camouflage for the family- and economy-shattering doctrines beneath — a claim Manuel neither fully credits nor dismisses. The "Opprobrium, ruin, public servitude" passage is Manuel's clearest evidence for the paranoid-sadistic reading: Fourier casts France's national catastrophes as cosmic retribution for having mocked him, phrased with a de Maistre-like relish for scourge and vengeance. The closing Freud/Nietzsche passage is Manuel's own interpretive payload, proposing that Fourier's total-love utopia and his fantasies of total annihilation are transformations of a single underlying drive — the chapter's final word on the "mask of Harlequin."
