Condorcet: Taming the Future

Commons · The Prophets of Paris: Turgot · updated 23 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Manuel portrays Condorcet as an emotionally tormented 'snow-capped volcano' whose secular Esquisse turned Turgot's providential history of progress into a self-contained statistical science of prediction — an attempt literally to 'tame the future' — and pushed that science toward the democratization of genius and the equalization of nations, ending in an unexamined Eurocentric universalism.

This unit combines Manuel's psychological portrait of the Marquis de Condorcet with his analysis of the Esquisse d'un tableau historique des progres de l'esprit humain, the clandestine last testament Condorcet wrote while hiding from Robespierre's police. The chapter moves from biography ("A Snow-Capped Volcano") through Condorcet's secularization of Turgot's theory of progress into a would-be predictive science of history ("The Mechanics of Secular History") to his conversion of progress into an intrinsically egalitarian project ("Progress in Equality"), closing on the naive, absolutist universalism that this optimism licensed.

Themes

The volcano beneath the philosophe. Manuel insists the public Condorcet — cool secretary of the Academy of Sciences, apostle of dispassionate reason — concealed a compulsively suffering, submissive romantic (D'Alembert's "snow-capped volcano"). This is not incidental color: Manuel reads Condorcet's compensatory historical optimism as psychologically continuous with his private anguish, an anxious man who "always expected catastrophe" building a doctrine of guaranteed collective happiness. The biography (Sophie de Grouchy, Mme. Suard, the flight and death in 1793) supplies the emotional register in which the Esquisse's serenity was actually composed: under the shadow of the guillotine, not from Olympian detachment.

Secularizing history into a science. Condorcet strips Turgot's theodicy of its residual theology. Where Vico, Turgot, and Herder needed a rational God to guarantee that history, like physics, obeyed law, Condorcet abandons any transcendental sanction and instead identifies history completely with the other sciences, resting on Hume's demolition of necessary causality: if physical laws are only probabilistic, history's "general and constant facts" can claim the same epistemic status. History becomes, in Condorcet's own words, "a science to foresee the progressions of the human species" — the capacity "to tame the future." This is the chapter's central move and its clearest resonance with world-machines-style psychohistory: a project to extract from the historical record extrapolable, actionable regularities rather than theological or moral lessons alone.

Utility as the engine, bookkeeping as its logic. Condorcet's account of change is relentlessly utilitarian — men adopt whatever is less costly or more pleasurable once a genius points out the profit. Even the humane turn away from slaughtering war captives is explained as "a tidy bit of commonsensical bookkeeping" once slave labor outvalued the cost of subsistence. This ledger-logic of history — converting moral and social advance into calculable debit and credit — is a small but telling instance of the accounting/measuring impulse that legibility-as-machine-core treats as constitutive of a world machine's core operation, applied here retroactively as historical explanation rather than administrative technique.

Priesthoods, system-builders, and the freezing of genius. Condorcet's crude "dialectic" recurs: fresh knowledge kindled by genius is absorbed by an entrenched caste (priests, scholastics, system-makers), ossifies into self-serving dogma the custodians can no longer even understand, and eventually cracks open again from within. This stagnation-then-rupture pattern — vital innovation curdling into stale, hollowed-out orthodoxy before the next eruption of genius — rhymes with the decay-sequence's account of infrastructure hollowing from functional to merely ornamental before collapse.

From elite genius to statistical mass. Condorcet's most consequential revision of Turgot is to convert genius from a fixed, rare endowment into a statistical proportion of an educated population: expand instruction and the supply of "productive scientists" multiplies "astronomically." Progress is relocated from the heroic individual to the social readiness to accept an innovation, and value is relocated from intensive elite discovery to its extensive diffusion among "the most numerous portion of society." This is the pivot that lets equality become, for Condorcet, not a moral add-on but the very measure of progress.

Equality as the content of progress, and its Eurocentric blind spot. Condorcet's three hopes (equality among nations, among classes within a nation, and in the biological perfection of the species) all cash out as extensions of the Franco-Atlantic "plateau" outward to the rest of humanity. Manuel is sharply critical of what this optimism concealed: an unexamined conviction that European scientific-utilitarian civilization was the sole legitimate form of knowledge, whose worldwide imposition — by "altruist white Europeans bearing science" — never needed justification. The tension Manuel flags between liberty and the tempo of progress (should freedom or organized acceleration deliver perfectibility faster?) anticipates the authoritarian-collectivist turn Saint-Simon and Comte will take.

Notable passages

The "snow-capped volcano" portrait establishes the gap between Condorcet's public rationalist mask and his private emotional turbulence — essential context for reading the Esquisse's serenity as achieved rather than given. The "tame the future" passage is the chapter's thesis statement: history reconceived as a genuinely predictive science, its findings mere "truths with a high degree of probability" rather than certainties, explicitly modeled on Newtonian calculus applied to a "sufficient number of observations." The bookkeeping passage on captive-slaves shows the mechanism Condorcet imagines actually driving change — cold utilitarian calculation, not virtue. The statistical-genius passage marks the theoretical hinge from Turgot's elitism to Condorcet's mass democratization of intellectual progress. The closing Eurocentrism passage is Manuel's own indictment, quoted at length precisely because it shows how far unqualified faith in "scientific utilitarian civilization" could extend before anyone thought to question it.
