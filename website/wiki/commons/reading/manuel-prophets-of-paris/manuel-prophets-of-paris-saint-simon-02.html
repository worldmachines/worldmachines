Saint-Simon: The Twilight of Power and the New Christianity

Commons · The Prophets of Paris: Turgot · updated 23 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Manuel analyzes Saint-Simon's late doctrine that the industrial-scientific order will dissolve the power drive by redirecting human aggression from man onto nature and will reduce government to pure administration, then traces how this technocratic vision culminated in the Nouveau Christianisme, Saint-Simon's deathbed reformulation of Christianity around a single commandment—labor for the poorest and most numerous class—meant to fill the spiritual void left by the collapsing Catholic order.

This unit covers two linked movements in Saint-Simon's late thought: first, his theory that the industrial-scientific society would extinguish the ancient "power drive" by rechanneling human aggression from domination over men to domination over nature, culminating in a functionally stateless "administered" society; second, the Nouveau Christianisme (1825), his deathbed religious testament, which recast Christianity as a single moral commandment—amelioration of the poorest and most numerous class—meant to replace a Catholic clergy he judged spiritually bankrupt.

Themes

Power as displaced aggression, not eliminated instinct. Saint-Simon does not claim men will stop wanting to dominate; he claims the object of domination will shift from other men to nature. Napoleon-style power lust is dismissed as monstrous aberration, safely quarantined from the analysis, while ordinary human ambition is redirected: "the desire to command men has slowly transformed itself into the desire to make and remake nature in accordance with our will." This sleight of hand—treating aggression as a fixed quantum of energy that can be rerouted rather than a social relation that can be abolished—is the hinge on which his entire optimism about a conflict-free industrial future turns.

A society of capacities has no basis for class conflict. Because scientific, artistic, and industrial talents are (he assumes) mutually exclusive and self-evident, and because appreciation of superior genius is "miraculously free from the baser passions,” Saint-Simon reasons that a class society organized by capacity rather than by control over men cannot generate the antagonisms of feudal or monarchical society. Manuel flags this as naive but notes its long afterlife: Marx's own faith that a proletariat which only works and never exploits cannot become an exploiting class shares this same blind spot about the realities of power.

The state reduced to nothing, or almost nothing. Saint-Simon's critique of Bourbon expenditure (pensions, sinecures, an oversized judiciary, an unnecessary army) leads him to a de facto "withering away" of government, though—Manuel notes—he never uses the phrase. Since disorder springs from poverty, idleness, and ignorance, and industry eliminates all three, even the police function dissolves into a duty "common to all citizens." What remains is pure administration on the model of a bank, canal company, or insurance society—the state as national workshop. This is legibility's logic pushed to its self-abolishing extreme: a fully rationalized, capacity-sorted social order needs no command function at all, only technical direction. legibility-as-machine-core

The New Christianity as replacement spiritual power. Saint-Simon judges the Catholic clergy to have forfeited their claim to intellectual and moral leadership by failing to keep pace with science, so they must "vanish as classes," their remnants absorbed into new Academies of Reasoning and Sentiment. He reduces Christianity to the Golden Rule and insists its "appropriate application" must evolve—by the nineteenth century, meaning organized labor for the poorest and most numerous class. Manuel is skeptical of the work's religious sincerity yet notes Saint-Simon may genuinely have come to believe himself a messianic figure by the end.

A history machine of alternating epochs. In a passage Enfantin would later read as trinitarian dogma, Saint-Simon periodizes Christian history into an age of generalization (medieval unity of doctrine, subordinating particular interests to a universal principle) followed by an age of particularization since Luther (specialization, private interest, and a corrosive "egotism" that let kings reclaim political power). His prescription is not reversion but coexistence of both tendencies—a rough analogue to WMP's picture of coexisting, contending organizing logics across an era. world-machines

The Gramsci gap made explicit. Manuel's account of the post-Napoleonic "religious and moral vacuum"—young men like Julien Sorel who had broken with revealed religion but found nothing to fill the emptiness—and Saint-Simon's own words about the Catholic system's fall being "the signal for a new belief which is going to fill with its enthusiasm the void which criticism has left in the souls of men" describe, almost exactly, the interregnum in which old legitimacy has died and the new has not yet been born. gramsci-gap

The clergy's decay from function to husk. The old spiritual power is traced through a decline from genuine intellectual authority (medieval clergy as science's sole repository) to defensive superstition to a "mere shell" awaiting dissolution—a fairly clean instance of an institution moving down toward the ornamental and the absent while its successor (the scientific-industrial elite) has already been quietly absorbing its prestige for centuries. decay-sequence

Notable passages

The opening epigraph on universal power lust ("Every man, every grouping of men... tries to replace" the ruling being atop the plateau) sets up the whole chapter's problem: how can a system built on capacity, not force, survive humanity's apparently indestructible love of dominion? Saint-Simon's answer—the redirection of "action on man" into "action on things"—is given in his own words in the passage beginning "The only useful action that man can perform is the action of man on things," which Manuel explicitly reads as anticipating both Marx and Freud.

The administrative-utopian culmination appears in the passage on governmental action being "reduced to nothing, or almost nothing": industry eliminates poverty, idleness, and ignorance, and with them the causes of disorder the state exists to police, so that "it made it possible virtually to eliminate the state."

The religious pivot is captured in two passages: the reduction of Christianity to the Golden Rule ("summed up in one sublime commandment... men should behave toward one another like brothers," defended by the monist dogma that it would be "blasphemy" to found religion on several principles), and the demand that the church "recast Christianity into a morality" centered on labor for "the poorest and most numerous classes"—the phrase that would become the watchword of the entire Saint-Simonian movement.

The unit closes on Saint-Simon's own deathbed testimony to Olinde Rodrigues—"Our last work will be the last to be understood"—forecasting that the Catholic system's inevitable fall would be "the signal for a new belief" to fill the resulting void, ending with the epigrammatic prophecy "A passing sleep followed by a perpetual awakening."
