Condorcet's Republic of Science and the Cult of Progress

Commons · The Prophets of Paris: Turgot · updated 23 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Manuel reconstructs Condorcet's late, fragmentary blueprint (from his commentary on Bacon's New Atlantis and the Esquisse) for a self-organizing, quantifying world republic of science, and traces how his doctrine of infinite, moral-absolute progress culminates in a technocratic vision of a state run by "social mathematics" and a quasi-religious cult of futurity.

This unit follows Manuel through three linked movements in Condorcet's late thought: first, the doctrine that progress is not merely desirable but a moral absolute whose acceleration is a duty and whose deceleration a "treachery against humanity"; second, the practical utopia sketched in Condorcet's fragmentary commentary on Bacon's New Atlantis — a voluntary, self-governing, internationally organized "republic of science" funded by subscription, insulated from state control, and tasked with a staggering catalogue of research projects (astronomy, meteorology, vital statistics, contraception, eugenics, artificial insemination); and third, the culmination of this program in a vision of society steered by the "calculus of probabilities" — a state that could in principle be run by mathematics "without debates." The section closes with the almost devotional final pages of the Esquisse, in which Progress becomes a substitute god offering the philosophe consolation, an Elysium of the mind, even as its author awaited the guillotine.

Themes

Progress as moral absolute, not mere description. Manuel's central claim is that Condorcet transforms progress from a hopeful generalization about history into an ethical imperative with its own logic of sin and virtue: since progress is defined in utilitarian terms, any delay in delivering its "bounty" becomes an injustice against those denied it, and the philosophe's individual duty is to maximize the historical tempo. This is a hinge moment for the idea of progress in Western thought — Manuel notes it prefigures the Left/Right tempo debates of the twentieth century ("how fast shall the tempo be accelerated and what price in liberty shall be paid"). It is also where the tension between liberty and organization first bites: Condorcet, an absolutist defender of individual rights, allows liberty to "bend" once progress itself has become the higher moral principle.

The republic of science as a legibility project avant la lettre. Against the historical backdrop of genuinely atomized, competitive, anti-systematic eighteenth-century science (prizes, academies, jealous individual genius, no coordinating body), Condorcet proposes something structurally new: a voluntary but planned, funded, and administratively continuous world organization for perpetual data collection — uniform method, dispersed observation posts, decennial reporting, automatic budget allocation to long-term projects, supermajority rules to prevent any one generation from derailing the plan. This is an engineering solution to the problem of collective epistemic action across centuries, and it strains against Condorcet's own individualist and libertarian instincts — he tries to "soften the contradiction between liberty and progress" but the apparatus itself points toward comprehensive planning and standardization. This is a clear precursor of the modernity-machine's drive toward legibility-as-machine-core: uniform method, quantified observation, a supreme coordinating body substituting for local, idiosyncratic practice.

Quantification as universal solvent. Condorcet's guiding conviction — inherited and extended from his edition of Pascal — is that there is no true boundary between the mathematical and moral kingdoms; the calculus of probabilities is the bridge that will let "moral problems be resolved as scientifically as geometric ones." Manuel shows this ambition metastasizing from a technical treatise on voting probabilities into a full "mathematique sociale": data-driven government able, in the limit, to dispense with debate and passion altogether, since "the state could be run by social mathematics." Manuel is careful to flag the historical irony — Condorcet, the great protector of individual rights against the state, arrives at the ideal of the all-knowing scientific technician as ruler, a leap that "traversed the age of middle-class parliamentarism" a full century early.

The catalogue of the pleasurable future. The "Work Projects" and "Pleasures of Foretelling" sections show Condorcet's imagination running past mere method into concrete futurology: vital statistics and medical case-studies to defeat disease; contraception decoupling pleasure from reproduction; sex-selection and artificial insemination as eugenic tools; the abolition of vast fortunes and hereditary bureaucratic castes; a society in which the only outlet left for ambitious men, once war, wealth, and rule have been foreclosed, is scientific achievement itself. Manuel treats this as one of history's great self-fulfilling prophecies — many of Condorcet's guesses (statistical demography, biometrics, social mathematics) did in fact define the next century and a half of inquiry.

Progress as religion. The unit ends where the Esquisse ends: Condorcet, writing in hiding on the eve of his death, converts the doctrine of progress into a substitute eschatology. Sympathy (bienveillance) is stretched from present humanity to all future generations, and contemplation of that future bliss becomes an "asylum" from present persecution — Progress as "the new god of the age," borrowing the old religion's "dream of future beatitude" without its apocalypse. This is the clearest statement in the unit of progress functioning as a pull attractor — a shared, world-organizing telos — that anticipates the modernity-machine's optimism before its Dusk. Condorcet's anxiety about historical catastrophe (his echo of Boulanger's deluge theory, and his hope that hieroglyph-like steles could preserve scientific knowledge "even from a general revolution of the globe") is a striking, half-buried anticipation of the impulse behind a Prime Radiant: an archive built to outlast the civilization that made it.

Notable passages

The opening paragraph on tempo ("There was a peculiar merit in accelerating the rate of progress...") establishes the moral-absolute reading of progress that organizes the whole chapter — deceleration as "a great iniquity," a "refusal to bestow happiness," ultimately "an act of treachery against humanity." The passage on scientists rushing to join the world society "out of curiosity and for the pleasure of learning new truths" (culminating in the quoted line about "the love of truth") shows how Condorcet tries to ground compulsory-feeling organization in voluntary passion. The technocratic culmination — the state potentially "run by social mathematics... without debates" — is the unit's sharpest and most quotable statement of where quantified social science tends. The image of progress as "the advance of the whole of mankind on an open plain," with the scientific elite dashing ahead and dragging the rest of humanity by sheer momentum, is Manuel's own vivid gloss on Condorcet's vanguardism. Finally, the closing Elysium passage — Progress as the new god, offering the persecuted philosophe an asylum of the mind — is where Manuel lets Condorcet's own prose (composed as he awaited arrest) carry the chapter's final, almost tragic weight.
