Saint-Simon: From Sans-Culotte Speculator to the Prophet of Organicism

Commons · The Prophets of Paris: Turgot · updated 23 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Manuel narrates Saint-Simon's picaresque life of speculation, imprisonment, and self-reinvention, then traces his shifting theory of the scientist's social role from Newtonian high priest to subordinated commodity, and his late turn from Enlightenment egalitarianism to a physiology-based organicism of three unequal, functionally specialized human types.

This unit opens Manuel's treatment of Saint-Simon with a biographical sketch of his picaresque life — aristocrat, American War veteran, revolutionary land speculator who renamed himself "Bonhomme," near-victim of the Terror, penniless pamphleteer, attempted suicide, and finally the master of a small circle of disciples including Augustin Thierry and Auguste Comte — before turning to two interlocking intellectual arcs: the rise and fall of the scientist's claim to social supremacy ("Science Dethroned"), and Saint-Simon's late abandonment of egalitarian rationalism for an organicist doctrine of natural, unequal, physiologically grounded human types.

Themes

A life built and rebuilt on speculation. Saint-Simon's career has no single throughline except restless reinvention: he speculates in confiscated church and émigré property during the Revolution, narrowly escapes the guillotine through a case of mistaken identity, burns through a fortune underwriting his own "projects for the betterment of mankind," descends into poverty and madness, attempts suicide, and only in his final years — sustained by young disciples like Olinde Rodrigues who transcribe his oral teaching — produces the doctrine for which he is remembered. Manuel frames this less as eccentricity than as the psychological substrate of prophecy: a man possessed by "the spirit of foretelling" regardless of his fitness for the role.

The scientist as floating elite, then as dethroned commodity. Saint-Simon's earliest writings, fully in Condorcet's spirit, cast scientists as a natural aristocracy — a "floating elite" holding the balance of power between propertied and propertyless classes, destined to found a Religion of Newton with a scientific priesthood replacing the discredited clergy. But bitter personal rejection by the Napoleonic scientific establishment (Laplace, Bouvard) curdles this into a theory of impersonal historical determinism, in which the individual genius is a mere function of "a law of circumstances," and finally, under the influence of Restoration liberal economists, into an image of scientists as timid sinecure-holders whose work is a commodity to be judged by industrialists. By the end of his life the scientist has become the least-favored of his "great trinity" of capacities, subordinated first to the industrialist and then to the religious moralist — the trajectory Comte would complete by banishing technical science altogether. This arc is itself a small case of a history-machine-like realization: Saint-Simon oscillates between a "great genius" theory of scientific progress and an "absolute and anonymous rhythm of scientific evolution" that eclipses the person of the scientist entirely.

The deathbed plea for religious continuity. The chapter's epigraph — Saint-Simon's dying insistence that "religion must bring itself into harmony with the progress of the sciences" rather than simply disappear — crystallizes a gramsci-gap: the old sacral legitimacy is dead, but Saint-Simon refuses to let the interregnum stand empty, demanding instead that a new spiritual authority (soon: the Nouveau Christianisme) be born to fill it.

From egalitarian atomism to organicist hierarchy. Saint-Simon shares the Enlightenment egalitarians' premise that the good society must mirror what is "natural" in man, but his reading of Cabanis and Bichat convinces him the natural is inequality, not sameness. He adapts Bichat's tripartite physiology (motor, rational, sensory) into three mutually exclusive social classes — industrialists, scientists, and artist-moralists — each occupying its rightful station by "anomaly" of natural endowment, with careers kept "open to talent" only within class. This is explicitly counterposed to the "atomist egalitarian society," which Saint-Simon likens to inert mechanical clockwork, in favor of an organic body social bound together by a "vitalist" element — the love of humanity as a literal animating fluid. Manuel notes the doctrine borrows its anti-egalitarian premise directly from the counter-revolutionary theocrats de Maistre and de Bonald, even as Saint-Simon translates it into ostensibly scientific, physiological terms — a telling instance of the tension between legibility's atomizing uniformity and a push toward organized, non-convergent variety among human types.

Notable passages

The opening biographical passage sets Saint-Simon's picaresque, Gil-Blas-like character against his eventual role as prophet — the contrast Manuel builds the whole chapter around. The Lettres d'un habitant de Genève passage on scientists as a "floating elite" shows Saint-Simon's earliest, most Condorcet-indebted theory of a scientific priesthood arbitrating class war. The furious "anarchist scientists" outburst and the Lagrange–Laplace "law of circumstances" reflection together capture his mid-career crisis: rage at scientists' indifference curdling into a proto-deterministic philosophy of scientific history in which even genius is circumstance-bound. Finally, the two passages from "From Equality to Organicism" — on men desiring "not equality but the expression of their true social natures," and on the organic society's need for a "vitalist" element against egalitarian clockwork — state the doctrine that would organize all of Saint-Simon's late system and everything the Saint-Simonians and Comte inherited from him.
