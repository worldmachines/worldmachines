Fourier's Twelve Passions: Taxonomy, Repression, and the Theodicy of Desire

Commons · The Prophets of Paris: Turgot · updated 23 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Fourier catalogues twelve innate human passions—organized like a tree into a luxurious, a group (affective), and a serial (distributive) branch under the trunk of 'unityism'—and argues that civilization's repression of these passions perverts them into malignant 'counterpassions' and manias, while a rightly engineered phalanstery could channel even sadistic and destructive drives into harmless or productive expression without sublimation.

This unit lays out the architecture of Fourier's psychology — the twelve passions organized as a tree with 'unityism' at the trunk and the luxurious, group (affective), and serial (distributive) passions as branches — and then turns to his theory of repression, arguing that civilization does not eliminate destructive impulses but merely deforms them into 'counterpassions' and manias. Manuel threads in comparisons to Freud, Nietzsche, Simmel, de Maistre, and the Christian theodicists to show both how original and how period-bound Fourier's system was.

Themes

The passions as an organic, tree-structured system. Fourier's twelve passions are not a flat list but a genealogical structure: unityism (the trunk, opposite of civilized egotism) integrates three branches — the luxurious (the five senses), the group/affective (respect, friendship, love, parenthood), and the serial/distributive (the composite, the cabalist, the butterfly). The serial passions are explicitly instrumental: they are the 'socializing mechanism' whose suppression in civilization prevents the sensory and affective passions from ever being realized, which is why Fourier, unlike the philosophes or Saint-Simonians, rehabilitates discord, intrigue, and inconstancy rather than seeking their elimination.

Passions as the only invariant foundation for morality. Having absorbed the Enlightenment's discovery of the cultural relativity of custom, Fourier cannot ground ethics in reason (buffeted by passion) or in convention (endlessly variable). Instead he grounds it in the passions themselves, which he insists are constant across every century, country, and legislator — 'nothing can vary in my theory.' This is a strikingly literal instance of treating desire as an unmovable natural force that no moral or philosophical system can suppress, closely paralleling the Spinozist Conatus this project treats as what capture-resistance regimes attempt, and fail, to statistically average away.

Repression does not destroy passion, it deforms it into counterpassion. Fourier's central psychological claim, elaborated with the 'nature driven out through the door comes back through the window' aphorism, is that repression is never actually achieved — only redirected. A suffocated passion produces a 'counterpassion... as malignant as the natural passion would have been benign': the ambitious become philosophes, the amorous become prostitutes, an unacknowledged love becomes cruelty (the Lady Strogonoff case). This 'double evil instead of double good' is the diagnostic core of his critique of civilization, and Manuel notes its likeness to (and departure from) Freudian sublimation — Fourier insists redirected passions can be channelized without any loss of vivacity, with no need for sublimation into 'higher' systems.

Manias, typology, and social engineering. Beyond the twelve base passions, Fourier posits innumerable 'manias' — rare, individually distorted expressions occurring in ratios as fine as 1 in 810 (intramania) or 1 in 100,000 (extramania). These feed his fantasy of exhaustive psychological cataloguing: 810 fundamental character types, a phalansterian 'card catalogue' matching travelers to partners, and 'horoscopes' that could forecast a child's future manias — including catching a future monster before he reaches the throne. This is Fourier's own legibility project turned toward the interior of the self: total, actuarial transparency of desire, in service of an order that (unlike the phrenologists) claims not just to read character but to engineer the circumstances that shape it.

A theodicy of destructive passions. Rather than deny that sadism, ferocity, robbery, or pederasty exist, Fourier insists none are 'vicious in the seed' — all were instituted by God for a harmonious purpose, and only civilization's suffocation renders them monstrous. The bloodthirsty man will hunt animals instead of men; the future Nero will be routed into a butcher's trade; the sadist's cruelty will be redirected into passionate friendship. 'As soon as we wish to repress a single passion we are engaged in an act of insurrection against God.' Manuel frames this as pushing the Leibnizian argument from providential design to its most literal, faintly absurd conclusion.

Notable passages

The tree-of-passions passage is the taxonomic backbone of the whole system and shows Fourier reasoning by structural analogy (zodiac signs, apostles, conic sections) rather than empirical observation. The 'invariable passions' passage is the clearest statement of his relativism-driven turn to desire as bedrock — worth reading against WMP's treatment of conatus as the drive capture regimes try and fail to average out. The repression/counterpassion mechanism and the Lady Strogonoff case history together demonstrate Fourier's most original psychological insight: that suppressed desire returns in transfigured, often violent form rather than disappearing — a proto-psychoanalytic move Manuel explicitly sets beside Freud. Finally, the theodicy of 'bloodthirsty characters' and the 'permanent orgasm' passage from the Lettre au grand juge show the outer edges of the system: a divine sanction for every passion, however monstrous in civilization, and a vision of harmony as literally unending gratification compressing subjective time itself.
