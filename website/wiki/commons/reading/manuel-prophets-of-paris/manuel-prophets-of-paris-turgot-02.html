Turgot: The Demonstration of Inevitability and Progress as Theodicy

Commons · The Prophets of Paris: Turgot · updated 23 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Manuel reconstructs Turgot's twin arguments for the inevitability of progress — an empirical proof from the past and a demonstration that regression is now structurally impossible — and then shows how this doctrine of progress functioned as a modern theodicy, converting the self-interested passions and crimes of history into unwitting instruments of a providential design.

This unit reconstructs the architecture of Turgot's doctrine of progress in two movements. The first, "The Demonstration of Inevitability," lays out why Turgot believed future relapse into barbarism or stagnation had become structurally impossible once enlightenment achieved global diffusion and knowledge began encasing itself in mathematical symbols. The second, "Progress a Theodicy," reveals the theological substrate beneath this apparently secular optimism: progress is Manuel's candidate answer to how eighteenth- and nineteenth-century thinkers solved the problem of evil once otherworldly reward and punishment had been stripped away, with vice and ambition recast as Providence's unwitting instruments. The unit closes on a human note — Turgot's habitual half-smile of resignation set against Condorcet's furious grief at his mentor's fall from power, the passing of the torch from philosophe to revolutionary.

Themes

Progress as a closed historical loop. Turgot's case for inevitability rests on retiring history's two traditional antagonists — barbarian invasion and the internal "rut" of routine — by appeal to a newly unified world civilization: knowledge is now so widely diffused that no single catastrophe or tyrant can extinguish it globally, and any nation that stagnates will be "forcibly awakened" by commerce or absorbed by a more vigorous rival. This is a strikingly totalizing, convergent vision of history — a single accelerating wheel with no exit — that resonates with the pull logic of a modernity-machine: a shared telos of Progress organizing the world toward legibility and improvement, with no genuine outside left once "no spot on earth was excluded from the illumination of science."

Intensive and extensive progress, and the civilizing mission. Turgot splits progress into a vertical axis (the accretion of scientific truth) and a horizontal one (its diffusion across peoples and territories), and treats communication technology — he tinkered personally with cheap reproduction of writing — as the practical lever accelerating both. This horizontal axis licenses a civilizing mission: Europe must draw "the whole of the world into the orbit of civilization" not out of altruism but because any uncivilized penumbra remains a standing threat to civilized peoples themselves. Manuel is explicit that this Eurocentric logic of universal enlightenment as insurance policy passes intact from Turgot through Condorcet to Saint-Simon.

Mathematics as the final refuge from historical contingency. Once knowledge is "encased in mathematical symbols," Turgot holds, it becomes immune to the vicissitudes that plague vernacular language and rhetoric — chimeras of the imagination, personal prejudice, political passion. He anticipates the mathematicization of the social sciences themselves (the coming "invasion" of moral behavior by the calculus of probabilities), a project his disciple Condorcet would take up directly. Manuel frames this as part of a French intellectual lineage running from Descartes to Valéry that treats mathematics as an ultimate haven from "the real world with its oppressive stupidity and irrationality."

Progress as theodicy. The unit's central move is Manuel's argument that progress-doctrine solves the theodicy problem: if Providence is good, why does history run through crime and devastation? Turgot's answer — echoed with variation in Vico, Herder, Kant, and Hegel — is that individual immoral passions and ambitions are the "leading strings" by which nature or Providence advances the species, without the actors' knowledge or consent. Ambition remains the personal moral responsibility of the ambitious, yet its historical effect is objectively good. This is a striving-toward-more logic reminiscent of Conatus pressed into providential service — self-interested persistence and expansion harnessed, behind the actors' backs, into a single historical direction, rather than left to proliferate on its own terms.

Reason superseding passion. Crucially, Turgot confines this validation of the passions to history's infancy: once Reason matures, the passions become superfluous and should be progressively "cast out," leaving, in the end of days, only reason. Manuel notes the latent cost of this vision — an impoverishment of the emotional and imaginative faculties — without Turgot himself registering it as a problem, since to him mankind had "only begun to fight the battle of rationality."

The progressists were not naive optimists. Manuel insists, against a popularizing caricature, that Turgot, Condorcet, Saint-Simon, and Comte were each personally acquainted with catastrophe (disgrace, the guillotine, war, class violence) and built their systems precisely as attempts to metabolize that catastrophe rather than deny it. The final scene — Turgot's unbroken half-smile of resignation versus Condorcet's raw fury at Voltaire — dramatizes the generational hinge on which the essay closes: "the day of the philosophes was drawing to a close, and the men of action were about to take over."

Notable passages

The civilizing-mission passage ("No region of the globe... could enjoy perfect tranquillity as long as savages lurked on the rim of civility") is the clearest statement of extensive progress as a totalizing, no-outside project — enlightenment must annex the whole globe or remain permanently vulnerable, a logic that reads as an early articulation of legibility's expansionary pull.

The mathematicization passage shows Turgot's hope that once moral and social knowledge is armored in "numbers and equations," it escapes both superstition and "the disputes of the marketplace where they always provoked destructive violence" — knowledge as depoliticized certainty.

The "immense army" passage, quoting Turgot directly, is the theodicy argument in its most vivid form: ambition and rage as blind squadrons whose collective movement only "the chief" (Providence/nature) comprehends — the mechanism by which subjective evil intent yields objective historical good.

Condorcet's report of Turgot's considered view — "The universe viewed in its totality... is a most glorious spectacle, witness to the wisdom which presides over it" — is the theological payoff of the whole doctrine, progress as vindication of divine design.

The closing passage, contrasting Turgot's imperturbable resignation with Condorcet's readiness to "gird himself for war" and "flung ~1imself into the revolutionary battle," marks the transition Manuel is tracking across the whole book: from Enlightenment philosophe to revolutionary actor, each in turn suffering "the fate of the philosopher engaged."
