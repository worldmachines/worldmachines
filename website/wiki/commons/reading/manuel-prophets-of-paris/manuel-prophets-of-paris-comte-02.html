Order and Progress, Moral Love and Gay Science

Commons · The Prophets of Paris: Turgot · updated 23 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Manuel dissects Auguste Comte's systematization of the philosophy of history into the law of the three states, the encyclopedic hierarchy of sciences, and a psychology of historical regression, then traces how Comte's late religion of Humanity subordinates the individual, capitalists, women, and eventually labor and science itself to a mystical cult of altruistic love centered on the Great Being.

This unit covers two consecutive sections of Manuel's chapter on Auguste Comte: "Order and Progress," which lays out Comte's systematization of Saint-Simonian philosophy of history into rigid, interlocking laws of social statics and social dynamics, and "Moral Love and Gay Science," which follows the system into its late mystical phase, where the positivist priesthood, the cult of Clothilde de Vaux, and a final utopia of loving idleness supersede the earlier intellectualist framework. Manuel's argument throughout is that Comte is the priest who succeeds the prophets — the system-builder who freezes the fluid, often contradictory historical speculations of Turgot, Condorcet, and Saint-Simon into "hard and sharp lines" and "ironclad categories," and who then, under the pressure of his own madness and his love for Clothilde, pushes that architecture toward an ecstatic religion of self-abnegation.

Themes

Comte as priest, not prophet. Manuel frames Comte's relation to his predecessors as the closing of an anarchic era: where Turgot, Condorcet, and Saint-Simon "stammered" and left their systems half-built, Comte imposes total consistency, self-consciously assuming the title "High Priest of Humanity." This is less a claim about originality than about institutional consolidation — Comte does for the philosophy of history what Aquinas did for Christian theology, converting scattered doctrine into a Summa. The framing recalls WMP's history-machine concept in an inverted register: rather than a paradigm silently assembling from distributed components until it lurches into visibility, Comte performs the consolidation as a single, self-appointed sacerdotal act — a "great man" closing down the messier, more distributed process that produced his materials.

The law of three states as totalizing legibility project. Comte's central historical machinery — the law of the three states (fetishism/theologism, metaphysicism, positivism), correlated with three modes of activity (conquest, defense, labor) and three ages of humanity (infancy, adolescence, maturity) — is an attempt to render the entire historical process measurable, classifiable, and orderable into a single hierarchy of sciences from astronomy to sociology to morality. This obsessive classificatory impulse, applied globally (even colonized peoples must pass through the same stages, merely at different rates), resonates with legibility-as-machine-core: Comte's system is a philosophical instrument for making history and society scale-legible, collapsing contingency into ironclad, universally applicable law.

Psychology of regression and the suppression of personality. Comte's most original contribution, per Manuel, is grounding the law of three states in his own lived experience of madness (1826), where he felt himself regress through metaphysics, monotheism, polytheism, to fetishism, then recover forward again — collapsing phylogeny and ontogeny into a single lived arc. This personal, embodied theory of stages is paired with an explicit demand that the individual dissolve into the "Great Being": Comte makes "Personality" an "odious word," insisting it must "merge in the totality of existence" rather than flower. This is a stark instance of what WMP calls the suppression of Conatus — Comte's system is explicitly hostile to individual striving and self-realization, subordinating it to a totalizing collective organism in a way that reads as an extreme case against which capture-resistance can be defined by contrast.

Historical relativism as apologetics for order. Comte's "absolute relativism" judges every historical event, ruler, or revolution not by fixed moral standards but by whether it served or retarded the "harmonious unity" of the developing Great Being — making liberty a virtue against feudalism but a vice against the nineteenth century, and branding revolutionary anarchists (not traditionalists like de Maistre) as history's true villains. Timing itself becomes a moral category: revolutions are only legitimate when they march in step with the "parade of order and progress"; anything premature is "untimely" in a punitive, not liberatory, sense — the opposite valence from Akomolafe's untimely, where fugitive surplus resists discipline rather than being condemned by it.

Love, asceticism, and the end of history as gaie science. The second section traces Comte's turn from Cours-era intellectualism to a religion centered on love, catalyzed by his passion for Clothilde de Vaux. Capitalists and scientists persist but are steadily subordinated to affect; sexuality itself is prophesied to wither (Comte's bowdlerized speculation about reproduction "from woman alone"); and the final utopia abolishes labor and science altogether in favor of a world of games, fêtes, and an aesthetic "gaie science" — a phrase Manuel flags as anticipating Nietzsche. The trajectory (freedom from necessity enabling pure self-expression) is explicitly likened to Marx's German Ideology utopia, underscoring how many nineteenth-century systems converge on a post-labor endpoint even from incompatible premises.

Notable passages

The opening declaration that Comte's self-naming as "High Priest of Humanity" marks the historical hinge from prophecy to priesthood sets Manuel's frame for the whole chapter. The three-ages passage ("conquest, defense, and labor... theological and military... metaphysical and feudal... positive and industrial") is Comte's most quotable compression of the law of three states into a single sociological formula. The account of his 1826 madness as the experiential ground of that law shows Manuel's insistence that Comte's system is not merely intellectual construction but autobiography raised to cosmic law. The letter to Clothilde de Vaux ("a passion at once profound and pure... the final systematization of the whole of human existence around its true universal center: love") is the hinge document for the shift from the Cours to the Politique positive. And the utopian close — labor and science dissolving into fêtes and a "gaie science" — shows how far the system travels from its origins in rigorous classification toward an almost aesthetic mysticism.
