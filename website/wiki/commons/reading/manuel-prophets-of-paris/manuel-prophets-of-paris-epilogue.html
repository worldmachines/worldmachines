Epilogue: Comparing the Prophets of Paris

Commons · The Prophets of Paris: Turgot · updated 23 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Manuel closes his study of Turgot, Condorcet, Saint-Simon, Fourier, and Comte with a comparative epilogue that weighs what united and divided the five prophets of philosophical history — their attitudes toward system-building, action versus contemplation, revolution, love and the family, religion, property and labor — and argues that their common blind spot was a refusal to confront the tragic, meaningless destructiveness of fate.

Manuel's closing chapter steps back from the five men studied in the book — Turgot, Condorcet, Saint-Simon, Fourier, Comte — to draw out what they held in common as "philosophical historians" and where they diverged: their tolerance for systematic closure, their relation between thought and action, their stance on revolution and parliamentary government, their redefinitions of love and the family, their manufacture of a religion to replace Christianity, their doctrines of property and labor, and finally a shared moral failure — an inability to look at the tragic dimension of existence squarely. The chapter is explicitly comparative and synthetic, using contrasts (above all with Hegel) to sharpen what is distinctive about the French tradition of active, committed prophecy.

Themes

From tentative essay to closed system. Manuel opens with a contrast in intellectual temperament: Turgot and Condorcet distrusted the esprit de systeme — Turgot likened a closed system to "a bier in which a cadaver is contained," a monument covering the shame of ignorance rather than dispelling it. By the nineteenth century this reticence vanished; the prophets built "mammoth structures" synthesizing past, present, and future into a single mystical unity, and their voices turned from reflective to strident and absolutist. This is a hinge point for the whole book: the eighteenth-century essayistic mode of philosophical history curdles into the nineteenth-century system as prophecy.

Action versus contemplation — the prophets were "of the dawn, not the twilight." Manuel stages his sharpest contrast against Hegel, quoting the famous passage from the Philosophy of Right — philosophy "paints its gray in gray" only after actuality has finished developing; "the owl of Minerva only begins its flight when twilight falls." Unlike Hegel's closeted retrospective philosophy, none of the Paris prophets were content merely to understand history after the fact: "action was always bound up with theory... They were of the dawn, not the twilight." Manuel even traces Marx's activist thesis on Feuerbach ("the issue is to change it") to this French lineage rather than to Hegel. This dawn/twilight opposition — construction-facing prophecy versus retrospective understanding — resonates suggestively with world-machines's own Dawn/Day/Dusk vocabulary, though the prophets use it to claim they stand at a beginning, not to describe an infrastructural cycle.

Determinism and activism coexist. Each prophet grounded historical necessity in a different agency — Providence for Turgot, nature for Condorcet and Saint-Simon, the Great Being for Comte — yet all insisted the philosopher-historian was also an actor whose opinions "regulate human society." Manuel is careful to show this determinism was never as rigid as caricature suggests: Turgot's marginalia ("truer, but not always," "depending on the circumstances") and Condorcet's own notes anticipating "oscillations" and "passing eclipses" in progress complicate the myth of naive linear perfectibility.

Revolution, government, and the abstention from politics. Only Condorcet flirted with revolutionary violence, and even he was wracked by scruple, voting against the King's execution while privately admitting doubt that sudden revolutionary triumphs could implant durable truth ("They give way to enthusiasm and not to conviction"). The nineteenth-century prophets — Saint-Simon, Fourier, Comte — treated revolution as pure pathology, a relapse into metaphysical confusion, and had equally little faith in parliamentary democracy, preferring rule by "the wise and the most competent" to majority rule.

Redefining love and the family as a religious problem. The eighteenth-century philosophes left the monogamous family basically intact; the nineteenth-century prophets made love and the family central, and mutually incompatible: Fourier's permissive multiplication of passional loves, Saint-Simonian/Comtean insistence on the pair as the nucleus of society (with Enfantin's flexible unions against Comte's eternal fidelity), and shared anxiety about reconciling love of the pair with love of humanity — a problem Manuel notes Freud would later resolve by treating libido as a finite, competitive resource.

A substitute religion to fill the post-Christian vacuum. Saint-Simon's move from "generalized science" to an explicit religion, elaborated by the Saint-Simonians into fathers, priestesses, and a Mother Church in Paris, and radicalized by Comte's Religion of Humanity and its Great Being, is presented as the group's most ambitious — and most linguistically strained — creative act: men without "the power of the word" groping for a vocabulary to name "the hitherto unfelt love of humanity." This is the closest the epilogue comes to describing a gramsci-gap: the old Christian legitimacy has died for these secular clerisies, and they labor, awkwardly and self-consciously, to construct new liturgical and institutional forms before the vacuum fills with something worse.

Property, labor, and the Marxist afterlife. Manuel traces a steady radicalization from Turgot's abolition of feudal restraints on grain and occupation, through Condorcet's demand for relative economic equality, to the Saint-Simonians' abolition of inheritance and Fourier's guaranteed minimum — all short of communism, but supplying, Manuel argues, the actual phrases Marx recombines in the Critique of the Gotha Programme's "to each according to his needs."

The absent tragic sense. The epilogue's final judgment: despite melancholy episodes (Condorcet's fugitive death clutching Horace, the botched suicides of Saint-Simon and Comte), none of the prophets confronted "the blind, meaningless destructiveness of fate." Their historical faith always supplied an escape hatch — a compensatory "blue heaven" of the future — which Manuel suggests is both their optimism's secret and its limit, reducing their humanity to mere philanthropy.

Notable passages

The Turgot epigram on systems as tombs ("a bier in which a cadaver is contained") sets the essay's opening opposition between eighteenth-century tentativeness and nineteenth-century dogmatism. The Hegel quotation and Manuel's "dawn, not twilight" rejoinder is the epilogue's structural pivot, distinguishing French activist prophecy from German retrospective philosophy. Saint-Simon's self-description of the philosopher as "an actor of the first rank in the moral world" crystallizes the determinism-plus-activism theme running through all five men. The Marx quotation from the Gotha Programme is Manuel's proof-text for the prophets' afterlife in nineteenth- and twentieth-century socialist rhetoric. And the closing passage on the absent tragic sense delivers the book's final verdict, arguing that the prophets' recourse to a redemptive future foreclosed any real reckoning with meaningless fate.
