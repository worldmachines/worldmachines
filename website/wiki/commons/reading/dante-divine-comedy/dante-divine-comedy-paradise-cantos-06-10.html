The Eagle, the Cross, and the Sun: Paradiso VI (cont.)–X

Commons · The Divine Comedy · updated 20 July 2026 · source on GitHub
In the close of Canto VI and the whole of Cantos VII through X, Justinian narrates the Roman eagle-standard's history and damns both Guelphs and Ghibellines for misusing it, Beatrice resolves the paradox of a just act justly avenged by explaining why God redeemed humanity through the Incarnation and Crucifixion, Charles Martel argues in the sphere of Venus that nature requires a diversity of human vocations that institutions perversely override, Cunizza and Folco speak before Folco denounces Rome's money-corrupted papacy, and Dante ascends into the Sun where Thomas Aquinas introduces the garland of twelve theologian-philosophers.

This unit opens mid-speech in the sphere of Mercury, where the emperor Justinian finishes recounting the millennium-long career of the Roman eagle-standard and ends with Romeo di Villanova's story of unrewarded service; moves into Canto VII, where Beatrice unfolds the scholastic logic of why God redeemed fallen humanity through the Incarnation and Crucifixion rather than by simple pardon; ascends into the sphere of Venus for Canto VIII, where the soul of Charles Martel explains that human nature requires differentiated vocations and indicts the world for forcing people into roles against their nature; continues in Canto IX with Cunizza da Romano's grim prophecies against the tyrants of the Trevisan March and Folco of Marseilles' meditation on transfigured love, Rahab's precedence in the Harrowing of Hell, and a scorching attack on a Rome corrupted by gold; and closes with the opening of Canto X, in which Dante and Beatrice rise into the Sun and Thomas Aquinas begins introducing the ring of twelve luminous theologian-philosophers.

Themes

The Roman eagle as an institution that outlives its bearers, then decays into faction. Justinian's speech is a compressed political theology: the eagle-standard passes from Aeneas through the kings, the Republic, Caesar, Titus, and Charlemagne, each bearer adding to a cumulative legitimacy that supposedly culminates in Rome's providential role as instrument of "living Justice." But by Dante's own day that inherited legitimacy has been hollowed out: Guelphs "rear" the papal lilies against the imperial ensign while Ghibellines "arrogate" the eagle to private faction, and Justinian condemns both as illegitimate claimants fighting over an emptied symbol. The passage reads as a proto account of the decay-sequence applied to a sacred-political emblem — a standard that was once straightforwardly Functional degrades into a contested Allegorical trophy, its original charge unrecoverable by either side that invokes it.

Beatrice's theology of atonement as an argument about necessity and justice. Canto VII resolves Dante's doubt — how a just deed (the Crucifixion) could rightly be "justly avenged" — by distinguishing the nature assumed (blameless, punished justly) from the person who assumed it (wronged unjustly), and by arguing that of the two possible paths to human restoration — free pardon, or self-atonement — only the Incarnation combined perfect justice with perfect bounty. It is dense scholastic argument rendered as poetry, culminating in Beatrice's claim that God's self-gift in becoming flesh was the maximal possible act of both justice and generosity at once.

Charles Martel: nature seals mortal wax with irreducible diversity, and institutions betray it. In the sphere of Venus, Charles Martel argues that a well-ordered human world depends on different people being born to different roles — "one is Solon born; / Another, Xerxes; and Melchisidec / A third" — because nature, "the seal to mortal wax," does not distinguish between households in stamping character. Providence alone accounts for the variance (Jacob unlike Esau, Quirinus born of Mars from a base father); left purely to generation, like breeds like. His closing rebuke — that the world perversely conscripts a born soldier into the priesthood and makes kings of "the fluent phrasemen" — is a critique of institutions overriding natural aptitude, a differentiation-without-teleology that echoes Configurancy's emphasis on non-teleological, locally-determined unfolding rather than imposed uniform roles.

Cunizza, Folco, and the indictment of a Church remade by money. Cunizza's speech is a set of dated, tripwire-like prophecies against Padua, Treviso, and Feltre's "godless shepherd," almost a Spine-and-Gate forecast in miniature — each doom pinned to a place and a coming event. Folco's speech pivots from his own erotic past to Rahab, first soul redeemed through Christ's harrowing of Hell, and then erupts into Dante's harshest indictment yet of institutional Rome: Florence — "plant / Of him, that on his Maker turn'd the back" — "engenders and expands the cursed flower" (the gold florin) that has turned pastors from shepherds into wolves, so that popes and cardinals study only the decretals' "stuft margins" and never journey, even in thought, to the Gospel's Nazareth. Writing at the very moment (c. 1320) the modernity-machine's legibility apparatus — coinage, ledgers, quantified value — was beginning its Dawn, Dante here registers the felt shock of a sacred order being recoded by a monetary one; the florin as corrupting agent is a strikingly literal image of quantification displacing an older order's meaning.

The Sun as a canon of sanctioned wisdom. Canto X's arrival in the Sun stages knowledge itself as a luminous, dancing constellation: Aquinas — introducing himself last among the first ring — names Albertus Magnus, Gratian, Peter Lombard, Solomon, Dionysius the Areopagite, Boethius, Isidore, Bede, Richard of St. Victor, and Sigier of Brabant in turn, assembling a canon of theology, canon law, and philosophy into a single circling "wreath." It is Dante's own curated intellectual inheritance, rendered as cosmic order rather than argument.

Notable passages

The eagle's verdict on faction (Canto VI) delivers Justinian's most pointed political judgment: having traced the standard from Pallas's death through Caesar, Titus, and Charlemagne, he turns to condemn both parties who fight over it in Dante's own Italy — Guelphs who oppose the "universal ensign" outright, and Ghibellines who claim it for private gain, with the Angevin "new-crown'd Charles" warned not to trust that Heaven will ever "truck its armour for his lilied shield."

The atonement argument (Canto VII) is Beatrice's dense theological centerpiece: she insists no unripened human eye can fathom why God chose this particular "way," then explains that of the only two possible remedies — free pardon or self-payment — God chose the one that let him give the most, becoming flesh rather than merely releasing man from debt.

Charles Martel's conclusion on vocation (Canto VIII) states plainly that different "roots" must produce different "operations," and ends by blaming the world's disorder on forcing born soldiers into the cloister and born speakers onto the throne — nature's design overridden by institutional convenience.

Folco's attack on the corrupted papacy (Canto IX) names Florence itself as the source of the "cursed flower" that has turned shepherds to wolves, and ends with a rare note of hope that Rome might yet be freed from its "adult'rous bond."

Aquinas's roll call in the Sun (Canto X) opens the ring of the wise, beginning with his own Dominican allegiance and Albertus Magnus at his side — the passage that inaugurates the entire theological canon Dante will spend two more cantos cataloguing.
