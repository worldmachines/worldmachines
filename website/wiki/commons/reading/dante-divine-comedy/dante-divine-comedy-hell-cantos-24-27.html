Thieves, Serpents, and Fraudulent Counselors

Commons · The Divine Comedy · updated 20 July 2026 · source on GitHub
Dante and Virgil climb into the seventh and eighth chasms of Malebolge, watching serpent-tormented thieves melt and swap bodies before hearing the flame-wrapped Ulysses recount his final forbidden voyage and Guido da Montefeltro recount how a pope's advance absolution damned him for fraudulent counsel.

This unit covers Cantos XXIV through XXVII of the Inferno: the descent from a broken bridge into the seventh bolgia, where thieves are tormented, incinerated, and grotesquely merged with serpents (culminating in the blasphemer Vanni Fucci and two virtuoso metamorphosis scenes), followed by the eighth bolgia, where the fraudulent counselors burn wrapped each in his own flame — Ulysses recounting his last, fatal voyage beyond the known world, and Guido da Montefeltro recounting the papal absolution-in-advance that damned him.

Themes

Effort and legacy as the poem's own ethic. Before any sinner appears, Virgil rebukes Dante's fatigue with a meta-poetic aside: fame is not won reclining under canopies, and whoever leaves no vestige of striving "leaveth such vestige of himself on earth, / As smoke in air or foam upon the wave." The passage reads almost as the poem's manifesto for its own project — climbing toward difficult truth is itself the moral labor, not merely witnessing it.

Theft punished as loss of stable form. The seventh bolgia literalizes theft as identity-theft: sinners are periodically reduced to ash and phoenix-like reborn, or — in the poem's most technically audacious set-piece — fuse bodily with serpents, trading limbs, faces, and speech until "of former shape / All trace was vanish'd." Dante explicitly claims to have out-done Ovid and Lucan, since no prior poet showed "two natures" transmuted "face to face." The thief who stole others' substance in life now cannot even keep his own.

Blasphemy, prophecy, and Florentine politics inside the poem. Vanni Fucci, caught and shamed, responds not with contrition but an obscene gesture of defiance at God, and is immediately re-bound by serpents. His confession doubles as political prophecy — Pistoia's factional ruin, Florence's coming upheaval, the storm from Valdimagra that will crush "each helpless Bianco" — grounding the infernal vision in dated, real-world Guelph-Ghibelline conflict rather than abstract allegory.

Ulysses: transgression as both heroism and fraud. Ulysses' voyage past the Pillars of Hercules — "the bound'ries not to be o'erstepp'd by man" — reads as a striving that will not be capped by inherited limits: neither son, father, nor wife "could overcome in me the zeal I had / T' explore the world, and search the ways of life." His speech to his crew ("Ye were not form'd to live the life of brutes / But virtue to pursue and knowledge high") is genuinely stirring, yet the poem places him among fraudulent counselors and drowns him within sight of a forbidden mountain — the world machine of the poem punishes exactly the kind of boundary-crossing conatus it also makes unforgettable. This tension between capture-resistant striving and the order that forecloses it is close to Conatus as WMP uses the term, though Dante's judgment on it is unambiguous: transgression here is damnation, not liberation.

Corrupted counsel and the bureaucracy of sin. Guido da Montefeltro's story turns fraud into an administrative transaction: the "chief of the new Pharisees" (Boniface VIII) offers to "absolve" a sin before it is committed, treating the keys of heaven as a ledger to be worked around rather than a moral reality — "Large promise with performance scant." A devil then out-argues the absolution on strict logical grounds: repentance and the will to sin cannot coexist, so no power can absolve the impenitent in advance. It is a small, devastating episode about legitimacy issued by an authority that no longer earns it — a corrupted attempt to make sin legible and pre-cleared that the poem's own logic refuses to honor.

Notable passages

The unit's curated quotes trace this arc: Virgil's exhortation to effort; the phoenix-simile transformation of the newly arrived thief; Vanni Fucci's blasphemous gesture and re-binding; Ulysses' declaration of restless zeal; his full "O brothers" speech and the shipwreck that ends it; and Guido da Montefeltro's account of the pope's advance absolution and the devil's rebuttal at his death.
