Through Gluttony, Avarice, and Wrath to the Gates of Dis

Commons · The Divine Comedy · updated 20 July 2026 · source on GitHub
Dante and Virgil pass through the circles of gluttony, avarice-and-prodigality, and wrath, are refused entry at the walled city of Dis until a heavenly messenger forces the gate, meet the arch-heretics Farinata degli Uberti and Cavalcante de' Cavalcanti among the burning tombs, and finally receive Virgil's systematic taxonomy of all the sins punished in the circles still to come.

This unit carries Dante and Virgil from the rain-lashed circle of the gluttonous, past Plutus and the wheeling weight-rollers of avarice and prodigality, into the marsh of the wrathful and sullen, and then to a hard narrative hinge: the demons of the city of Dis slam its gates on Virgil and only a heaven-sent messenger can force them open. Once inside, the pilgrims walk among flaming sepulchres of arch-heretics, hold two charged conversations with the dead Florentine nobleman Farinata degli Uberti and the grieving Cavalcante de' Cavalcanti, and close with Virgil's extended lecture mapping the entire remaining architecture of Hell — violence, fraud, and their subdivisions — before the canto breaks off at the threshold of Canto XII.

Themes

Florence as a damned polity. The unit is threaded with contemporary Florentine politics recast as infernal testimony. Ciacco, sunk in the gluttons' rain, delivers a coded prophecy of the city's Guelph-Ghibelline bloodletting — "the wild party from the woods" driving out its rivals, only to fall in turn — and diagnoses the root cause as a trinity of vices: "Av'rice, envy, pride, / Three fatal sparks, have set the hearts of all / On fire." Farinata later resumes the same civic argument from beyond the grave, defending his lone vote against razing Florence after Montaperti and needling Dante about his own family's failed returns from exile — a detail that also functions as a veiled prophecy of Dante's own coming banishment.

Fortune as an impersonal governing machine. Virgil's answer to "what fortune is" (Canto VII) reframes fortune not as caprice but as a divinely appointed "general minister" that redistributes "the empty vantages of life / From race to race, from one to other's blood, / Beyond prevention of man's wisest care." It is deliberately impersonal — indifferent to the praise or blame heaped on it, cycling nations up and down by a logic humans cannot audit. This is strikingly close to the WMP sense of a world machine: an infrastructural process organizing the rise and fall of polities across long spans, operating by its own rules "from us conceal'd," beyond any single actor's control.

The poem flags its own allegory. At the threshold of Dis, three Furies threaten to summon Medusa and turn Dante to stone if he looks — and the narrator breaks frame to address the reader directly: "Ye of intellect / Sound and entire, mark well the lore conceal'd / Under close texture of the mystic strain!" This is the clearest moment in the unit where the text insists it is operating on two levels at once, inviting (and daring) allegorical reading rather than literal description.

Barred at the gate, admitted by force. The city of Dis is walled, garrisoned by fallen angels, and initially impenetrable to Virgil's authority alone — a rare moment where Dante's guide is humiliated and doubts himself ("Who hath denied me these abodes of woe?"). Entry is finally compelled by a scornful, silent messenger from heaven who touches the gate with a wand. The episode marks a structural threshold in the poem: everything before is the outer Hell of incontinence, everything after is the walled, harder Hell of malice.

A legible taxonomy of sin. Canto XI is essentially an audit of the moral order still to come, delivered while the travelers pause to acclimate to the stench rising from the pit. Virgil sorts sin exhaustively: violence against neighbor, self, or God (three rounds of one circle), and fraud (worse, "because of man peculiar evil") split into simple fraud and treachery against special trust, "Point of the universe, dread seat of Dis." He closes with a scholastic digression tying usury to a double offense against Nature and Art, both said to descend "in second... from God." The whole passage reads as an act of world-ordering classification — sins measured, ranked, and assigned coordinates — which resonates with the WMP notion of legibility-as-machine-core: making a moral universe fully readable and orderable so that punishment can scale to exact desert.

Foreknowledge without presence. Farinata explains that the damned "view, as one who hath an evil sight... objects far remote," seeing the distant future clearly while remaining blind to the present and near future — their knowledge will vanish entirely "when on futurity the portals close" at Judgment. It is an inverted oracle: perfect long-range sight paired with total blindness up close, the mirror image of what any forecasting discipline would want.

Notable passages

Ciacco's prophecy (Canto VI) compresses Florentine faction violence into a diagnosis of avarice, envy, and pride as the city's animating "three fatal sparks." Virgil's speech on Fortune (Canto VII) is the unit's most abstract and machine-like passage, casting historical rise and fall as the operation of a minister acting by rules opaque to human wisdom. The Filippo Argenti scene (Canto VIII), where Dante turns on the drowning, raging spirit with open contempt — "In mourning and in woe, / Curs'd spirit! tarry thou. I know thee well" — marks a hardening of the pilgrim's moral affect compared to his earlier pity for the gluttons. The Medusa episode (Canto IX) is the unit's most self-conscious moment, an explicit instruction to read allegorically. Farinata's exchange with Dante (Canto X) fuses personal history, civic memory, and an epistemology of exile into the pilgrim's most substantial political dialogue in this unit. And Virgil's taxonomy (Canto XI) is the unit's structural payoff: a complete, legible map of the sins the poem has not yet shown, organized by force versus fraud and ranked by their offense against love and trust.
