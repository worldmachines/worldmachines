Wrath's Smoke, Sloth's Haste, and the Root of All Sin: Purgatorio XV–XIX

Commons · The Divine Comedy · updated 20 July 2026 · source on GitHub
On the terrace of wrath Dante and Virgil hear Marco Lombardo's discourse on free will and the corrupt fusion of Church and Empire, then pass through the terrace of sloth where Virgil expounds love as the root of every virtue and vice, before a dream-vision of a Siren gives way to the terrace of avarice and Pope Adrian V's confession.

This unit carries Dante and Virgil from the closing exempla of the wrath terrace, through the blinding smoke where Marco Lombardo delivers a set-piece on free will and corrupt governance, over the threshold into sloth (marked by Virgil's discourse on love as the single motive-spring of all human acts), and finally down onto the terrace of avarice, where a dream of a Siren precedes the penitents' confession, culminating in the fallen pope Adrian V's account of his own avarice. Across five cantos (the tail of XV through XIX, with XX just beginning), the poem builds a coherent moral architecture: every sin, high or low, is a malformation of love, and the poem's political anger and psychological analysis are shown to be two expressions of the same diagnosis.

Themes

Love as the single organizing motive of the moral cosmos. Virgil's discourse at the opening of the sloth terrace (Canto XVII) declares that neither Creator nor creature ever existed "without love," and that this love is "germin of each virtue" and of every act that "merits pain." Purgatory's entire structure follows from this: the terraces below (pride, envy, wrath) punish love perverted toward another's harm; sloth punishes love that is merely deficient, too slow to pursue the good; the terraces above (avarice, gluttony, lust) punish love pursued with disordered excess toward otherwise legitimate objects. This is less a list of sins than a taxonomy of misdirected striving — a single appetitive force (something close in spirit to Spinoza's Conatus, the drive to persist and expand) that produces virtue when rightly aimed and vice when captured, starved, or overshot. The poem insists the fault lies in the aiming, not in the existence of the drive itself.

Free will against determinism, and the corruption of double governance. Marco Lombardo's speech in the smoke of wrath (Canto XVI) is the unit's sharpest polemical moment. Asked why the world has gone bad, he refuses both fatalism ("if this were so, / Free choice in you were none") and any excuse that blames the stars: humans retain "light... to follow evil or good, / And of the will free power." The true cause, he argues, is bad leadership — laws unenforced, shepherds who fail to guide. This sharpens into an image of institutional collapse: Rome once had "two suns," one lighting the way of the world and one the way of God, but "One since hath quench'd the other; and the sword / Is grafted on the crook," so that Church and Empire, once separately legitimate, now corrupt each other for want of mutual restraint — "each must perforce decline to worse, unaw'd / By fear of other." The passage reads as a diagnosis of collapsed institutional separation, worth noting for anyone tracking how fused or overreaching authority structures decay.

Exemplary pedagogy: virtue and vice staged as visions. Each terrace instructs through a formal device — voices or ecstatic visions recounting historical or legendary instances of the relevant virtue and the opposite vice's punishment. In this unit, Dante's rapture on the wrath terrace shows him the stoning of a young man (an unnamed Stephen-figure) who forgives his killers even as they kill him, his eyes made "gates to heav'n"; later, entering sloth, voices recount Mary's haste to the hill country and Caesar's swift march on Ilerda as models of holy zeal, precisely because sloth's penance is enforced motion — the shades must run without rest.

The Siren and the mis-recognized object of desire. Canto XIX opens with a dream: a woman "with lips that stammer'd, eyes aslant, / Distorted feet, hands maim'd" transforms, under Dante's gaze, into the Siren who claims to have enchanted Ulysses and to leave her devotees permanently content. A holy woman tears open her robe to reveal a foul-smelling belly, and the illusion collapses. The sequence functions as a compressed allegory for the terraces still ahead (avarice, gluttony, lust): desire's object looks whole and beautiful only until truly seen, and what looks like satisfaction is corruption in disguise — a fitting threshold-vision just before the avarice terrace, where love has attached itself to something ultimately empty.

Avarice at the summit of temporal power. The unit closes with Pope Adrian V, prostrate and chained on the avarice terrace, confessing that even the papacy itself became an object of avarice for him — "the dream / And cozenage of life" — until he grasped, too late, that "no prouder height / Lur'd on the climber." Paired with Marco Lombardo's critique of the corrupted crook, this gives the political theme a personal, confessional coda: the highest office of the Church is shown from the inside as just as vulnerable to disordered love as any other.

Notable passages

The curated passages track this arc: Marco Lombardo's free-will speech and his "two suns" indictment of merged spiritual and temporal power; Virgil's foundational claim that love is the germ of every virtue and vice; the vision of the stoned youth forgiving his murderers; the Siren's dream-transformation and exposure; and Adrian V's avarice confession. Together they move from public/political diagnosis (Marco) to psychological/metaphysical theory (Virgil) to individual confession (Adrian V) — the same account of misdirected love examined at three different scales.
