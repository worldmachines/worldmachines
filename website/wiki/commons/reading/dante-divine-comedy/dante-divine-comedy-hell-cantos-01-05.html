Inferno, Cantos I–V: From the Dark Wood to the Circle of Lust

Commons · The Divine Comedy · updated 20 July 2026 · source on GitHub
Dante, lost in a dark wood and blocked by three beasts, is rescued by the shade of Virgil (sent at the intercession of Beatrice, Lucia, and Rachel) and led through the inscribed gate of Hell, past the neutral souls and Charon's crossing, into Limbo among the virtuous pagans, and finally into the second circle, where the whirlwind of the lustful carries Francesca da Rimini, whose account of the love that damned her and Paolo overwhelms him.

This unit (Cary's 19th-century blank-verse translation) opens the Inferno proper: the narrator, astray in a "gloomy wood" at the midpoint of life, is turned back from a sunlit hill by a panther, a lion, and a ravenous she-wolf, and is rescued by the shade of Virgil, who reveals he was sent by Beatrice — herself moved by Lucia and seated in the company of Rachel — to guide Dante through Hell. Cantos III–V then move the pilgrim through the inscribed gate of Hell, past the wailing neutral souls and Charon's ferry across Acheron, into the noble castle of Limbo among unbaptized virtuous pagans and poets, and down into the second circle, where the eternal whirlwind battering the lustful culminates in Dante's encounter with Francesca da Rimini, whose account of illicit love told through the romance of Lancelot leaves him fainting.

Themes

The failure of the will and the need for external rescue. Dante cannot ascend the "delightful mount" on his own strength — the she-wolf, "so bad and so accursed in her kind, / That never sated is her ravenous will," drives him back into darkness before any guide appears. Canto II underscores that even Virgil's summons is not self-initiated: it descends a chain of intercession (Beatrice moved by an unnamed heavenly lady, who was moved by Lucia, in whose urgency Rachel is present) before it reaches the human traveler at all. Salvation here is structurally relational, not solitary striving.

Hell as a legible, adjudicated order. From the gate's inscription ("Justice the founder of my fabric mov'd... Supremest wisdom, and primeval love") through Minos's mechanical sentencing — he "foldeth him around" with his tail, coiling once for each circle a soul must descend — the poem renders damnation as a precisely measured bureaucracy of sin. Every torment is calibrated to its transgression; every soul is examined, classified, and routed. This is a moral cosmos built for total readability, an instructive contrast case for how a civilization can imagine total legibility centuries before modern administrative technique — see legibility-as-machine-core.

Sympathy versus judgment. The pilgrim's compassion is not incidental — it structures his encounters. He weeps at the mere sound of the neutral souls' torment, faints at the sight of Francesca's grief, and repeatedly needs Virgil to redirect him past souls he is not meant to pity ("Speak not of them, but look, and pass them by"). The Francesca episode in particular stages this tension starkly: her rhetoric of love ("Love, that in gentle heart is quickly learnt") is seductive enough that the poem's own narrator collapses under its pull, even while the theological frame around it insists on her damnation.

Virtue without grace. Limbo's virtuous pagans and unbaptized infants are punished only by unfulfilled desire — "we live / Desiring without hope" — not by active torment. The episode draws a hard boundary between moral excellence and doctrinal admission, and Virgil's own exclusion from the beatific vision (he can guide only so far, then "a spirit worthier than I / Must lead thee") makes him simultaneously the poem's wisest voice and one of its saddest exiles.

Notable passages

The opening lines set the entire poem's frame in miniature: lost in "a gloomy wood... how savage wild / That forest," the narrator's midlife disorientation is immediately literalized as terrain. The gate of Hell's inscription is the unit's most compressed statement of theme — Hell as an eternal artifact of "Justice," "power divine," "Supremest wisdom," and "primeval love" all at once, closing on the famous "All hope abandon ye who enter here." Beatrice's own account of her descent ("I was among the tribe, / Who rest suspended...") shows the intercessory machinery of the poem's cosmos operating in the beloved's own words rather than Virgil's report of it. The Limbo passage where Virgil explains "these of sin / Were blameless" but are lost "for these defects" alone is the clearest statement of the poem's harshest doctrinal edge — virtue is necessary but insufficient. Canto V's description of the lustful as souls where "Reason by lust is sway'd," borne like starlings and cranes in "the tyrannous gust," gives the circle its governing image before Francesca ever speaks. And her narration — how she and Paolo, reading of Lancelot, were undone at the moment "of that smile we read... We read no more" — ends the canto on Dante's own collapse, "like a corpse fell to the ground," the pilgrim's compassion overwhelming his judgment for the first (and most quoted) time in the poem.
