Purgatorio X–XIV: The Terraces of Pride and Envy

Commons · The Divine Comedy · updated 20 July 2026 · source on GitHub
On the first terrace of Purgatory the pilgrim views marble carvings of humility and studies the Proud bent double under stones, converses with Omberto Aldobrandeschi and the illuminator Oderisi on the transience of earthly fame, watches Virgil's guiding angel erase a letter P from his brow, then climbs to the second terrace where the Envious sit with eyelids sewn shut by wire while voices proclaim examples of love and Guido del Duca laments the vanished noble houses of the Arno valley and Romagna.

The unit opens just past the gate of Purgatory proper and carries the pilgrim through the whole of the first terrace (Pride), the transition via the Angel of Humility, and well into the second terrace (Envy), ending as Canto XV begins (with no text yet given). Structurally each terrace repeats a triadic pattern: sculpted or spoken exempla of the opposing virtue, direct encounter with penitent souls bearing the vice's punishment, and a closing exemplum of the vice itself before the ascent to the next circle.

Themes

Art as moral pedagogy — the sculpture that out-speaks nature. The terrace wall is carved with scenes of humility (the Annunciation, David dancing before the Ark, Trajan's justice to the widow) rendered so faithfully that Dante calls it "visible speech" — a technology for making an abstract virtue legible and affectively binding on those who view it, not unlike how the Proud souls' whole penance is designed to physically re-teach a posture (bent backs, eyes on the ground) that their pride refused in life.

Pride literalized as crushing weight, contrasted with insect metamorphosis. The Proud crawl beneath stones shaped like architectural corbels, an image Dante turns into direct address to the reader: humans are "worms" destined to become the "winged insect" of angelic judgment, and pride aborts that transformation, leaving the soul "shapeless" — an embryo that never completes its metamorphosis. This is the canto's clearest statement of Purgatory's logic: punishment as arrested or corrected development, not mere retribution.

Fame as a wind that shifts its name. Oderisi da Gubbio's speech on the illuminator's art gives the unit its most historiographical passage: Cimabue eclipsed by Giotto, one Guido's poetic "letter'd prize" already snatched by another, and an unborn successor waiting to displace them both. Fame is described as literally directionless wind, no more durable across "some thousand years" than an eyelash-blink against eternity. The pattern described — a dominant figure's paradigm silently displaced by the next, visible only in retrospect — resonates with the WMP idea of a history-machine, paradigms accumulating out of sight until a new one abruptly holds the field.

Envy's punishment as forced blindness and forced communion. On the second terrace the envious sit wrapped in sackcloth, propped on one another, eyelids sewn shut with wire "as for the taming of a haggard hawk" — the sense that most served their sin (covetous sight) is the one now denied them, while their forced physical dependence on neighbors ironizes a vice built on begrudging others' good.

Guido del Duca's lament as a decay sequence. Guido's long invective — first against the moral degradation running down the Arno from Casentino swine to Arezzo curs to Florentine wolves to Pisan foxes, then against the vanished noble houses of Romagna (Lizio, Manardi, the Traversari, Anastagi, the line reduced to "bastard slips") — reads as a genealogical version of the decay-sequence: institutions and lineages that once organized courtesy and chivalric virtue, now empty of function, surviving only as names or already extinguished. Guido explicitly prefers a line's clean extinction ("Well doeth he, that bids his lineage cease") to its persistence as a hollow, corrupted vessel — an allegorical-to-absent trajectory for noble houses rather than institutions of state, but the same logic of legitimacy outliving function.

The old enemy's bait. Virgil's closing reflection — that humanity is "baited" by an adversary who "drags you eager to him" even while heaven wheels overhead with "everlasting beauties" — frames both terraces' vices (pride, envy) as failures of attention: appetite recaptured by the earth despite a countervailing pull toward the sky.

Notable passages

The Annunciation carving (Canto X) establishes the terrace's central conceit that sculpted art can "speak" more persuasively than living people — Dante insists the angel "look'd no silent image," as though sound were sculpted along with form. The Trajan-and-widow dialogue extends this into full dramatized speech carved in stone, justice and pity in argument with each other. The direct address "Christians and proud!" is the unit's thesis statement on pride as arrested metamorphosis. Oderisi's speech on Cimabue, Giotto, and the two Guidos supplies the meditation on fame's instability. The wire-sewn eyes of the envious is the unit's most visceral punishment-image, cross-referencing the vice to its organ. And Guido del Duca's roll call of extinguished Romagnol houses is the clearest sustained decay narrative in the passage, mourning institutions and courtesies that have no living heir.
