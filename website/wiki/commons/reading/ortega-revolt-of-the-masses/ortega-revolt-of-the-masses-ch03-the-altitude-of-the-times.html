The Altitude of the Times

Commons · The Revolt of the Masses · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Every epoch has a felt vital altitude independent of chronological time — some ages (echoing Jorge Manrique and Rome's decline) look back to a lost golden age, the just-passed nineteenth century felt itself at a self-satisfied "plenitude," and the present feels itself ampler than all past life yet cut loose from any inherited model, at once confident and unsure of its destiny.

Ortega develops the idea that every historical period has a felt "altitude" or vital elevation, distinct from the abstract, uniform time of the calendar: individuals and whole generations sense whether their own pulse rises to, lags behind, or exceeds "the height of the time." He surveys the different ways ages have registered that sensation — the golden-age reflex of Jorge Manrique and of declining Rome, the self-satisfied "plenitude" the nineteenth century felt in its own "modern culture," and, by contrast, his own present, which feels itself superior to every past age yet has no fixed models to stand on, an age that is at once a beginning and possibly an agony.

Themes

Vital time versus chronological time. Ortega's central distinction is between the featureless "abstract time of chronology" and "the vital time that each generation calls 'our time,'" which always sits at some elevation — higher, level, or lower than the time before it. A person out of step with that elevation feels like "a shipwrecked man who cannot keep afloat"; a person in step with it lives "fully and with joy." This is the phenomenon the whole chapter unpacks.

The golden-age reflex. Most historical periods, per Ortega, have not felt themselves superior to the past but inferior to it — imagining a fuller, richer "golden age" behind them (Manrique's "Any time gone by was better," the Greek and Roman aurea aetas, the Australian Bushmen's Alcheringa). He treats this as a symptom of diminished vitality, not a neutral historical judgment, and stages Horace's lament over degenerating generations and the demographic and military collapse of the later Roman Empire (barren women, depopulated Italy, centurion posts filled by hired Dalmatians and Barbarians) as the paradigm case.

Plenitude and its hollowness. Against the golden-age posture, some ages instead feel they have arrived — "the plenitude of the time," a long-desired goal finally reached. Ortega diagnoses the nineteenth century's faith in "modern culture" this way, and then inverts the usual reading of it: a period that is fully satisfied has, by definition, exhausted its desire, and "these centuries, so self-satisfied, so perfectly rounded-off, are dead within." He invokes Cervantes — "The road is always better than the inn" — to argue that arrival, not struggle, is the sign of expiring vitality.

The present as a beginning without ancestors. Ortega's own age, by contrast, "no longer regards itself as definitive": it makes "tabula rasa of all classicism," finds the Renaissance itself "narrow" and "corny," and — in a passage he quotes from his own earlier writing — feels that "the dead did not die in appearance only but totally," leaving "no living ghosts" and no models, norms, or precedents to work from. The image is Peter Schlemihl (garbled in this edition as "Peter' Schlehmil"), the man who lost his shadow.

Decadence redefined. Ortega reframes "decadence" as inescapably comparative and viewpoint-dependent (relative even to something as trivial as amber mouthpieces going out of fashion), and proposes the only legitimate test: whether a life, viewed from inside, prefers itself to any life that came before. By that test his own age, however anxious, is not decadent — it is the first period in memory that would refuse to trade places with any past one.

The closing formula. The chapter ends on Ortega's own deliberately paradoxical summary of "the height of our times": "superior to other times, inferior to itself; confident, but at the same time uncertain of its destiny; proud of its strengths, and at the same time fearing them."

Notable passages

The opening definition of vital altitude (c1) sets the chapter's terms and its central image — the shipwrecked man out of step with his own era's tempo. The Rome passage (c2) grounds the golden-age reflex in a concrete historical symptom-list rather than leaving it abstract. The plenitude passage (c3), with its Cervantes quotation and the "happy drone" image, is the chapter's sharpest inversion: satisfaction read as death rather than fulfillment. The "dissociation of past and present" passage (c4) is the most striking resonance with gramsci-gap in this unit — Ortega describes his own generation as cut loose from inherited authority, "models, norms, hints" exhausted and unusable, in language close to "the old is dying and the new cannot be born." The fit is not exact: Ortega is describing a felt, near-biological loss of ancestral vitality rather than an institutional legitimacy crisis, and he reads the resulting groundlessness as exhilarating ("the noisy joy of kids let loose from school") rather than as Gramsci's "morbid symptoms" — but the structural shape, an age standing after its models have wholly died and before new ones exist, is the same. The closing formula (c5) is the chapter's own one-sentence definition of its title concept and the best single anchor for the whole argument.
