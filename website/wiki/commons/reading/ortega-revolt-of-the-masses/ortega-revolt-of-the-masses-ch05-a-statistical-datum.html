A Statistical Datum

Commons · The Revolt of the Masses · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Ortega grounds the mass-man's political rise in a single demographic fact from Werner Sombart — Europe's population nearly tripled between 1800 and 1914 after twelve static centuries — arguing that this human flood outran traditional culture's capacity to educate it, producing a governing type with power but no program, and warning that continued mass rule could revert Europe to barbarism within a generation.

Continuing the diagnosis of "our time," Ortega argues that both individual and collective life are settled not by circumstance but by character — and that in Europe's collective life the dominant character is now the mass-man's. He then grounds this claim in a single demographic fact borrowed from the economist Werner Sombart: Europe's population, static under 180 million for twelve centuries, nearly tripled to 460 million between 1800 and 1914. That "vertiginous increase" flooded history with more people than traditional culture could educate, producing a type that is instructed in modern technique but not formed by its spirit — a type now ruling without any vital program, and one Ortega calls, in the same breath, capable of Europe's greatest good or its reversion to barbarism.

Themes

Character decides, not circumstance — and this holds collectively too. Ortega opens from the individual: life hands us several possible trajectories, never one, so "to live is to feel ourselves fatally obliged to exercise our liberty." It is "false to say that in life 'circumstances decide' — what actually decides is our character." He extends this directly to collective life: a society's effective form is likewise chosen by "the character of society, or what comes to the same thing, of the type of men dominant in it." In our time that type is the mass-man, and this is the hinge that licenses the rest of the chapter's argument.

From backing a minority's program to program-less rule. Under 19th-century liberal democracy the masses did not decide directly — they supported one minority's "program" over another's, and a program was, by definition, an offer aimed at the future. What Ortega diagnoses as new (looking at "the Mediterranean countries," i.e. contemporary Italy and Spain) is direct rule by the masses themselves, which is rule with no program at all: government that "exists from hand to mouth," offers no "frank vision for the future," and is reduced to "avoiding the hourly conflicts" rather than resolving them — "omnipotent and ephemeral." A polity that has shed its old future-oriented legitimacy but not replaced it with any new direction reads as a genuine, if partial, rhyme with gramsci-gap — though Ortega's frame is a psychological diagnosis of a ruling type (the mass-man "whose life lacks projects"), not the structural interregnum-between-orders that the WMP term names; the two overlap in symptom more than in mechanism.

The statistical datum. The chapter's title move: from the 6th century to 1800 — twelve centuries — Europe's population never exceeded 180 million; from 1800 to 1914, in barely a century, it rose from 180 to 460 million. Ortega reads this raw acceleration as the material cause of the masses' triumph — "heap after heap of human beings" dumped onto the historic stage faster than they could be "saturated with traditional culture" — and uses it to correct the "mirage" that this kind of population surge was a distinctly American phenomenon; Europe outgrew the United States in absolute terms across the same century.

Instructed, not educated: technique without spirit. The schools "instructed the masses in the technique of modern life" — gave them "tools to live intensely" — but could not educate them, could not inoculate them with "their great historic duties" or the "spirit" behind the instruments they were handed. The result is a psyche Ortega calls "healthier and stronger... but much more simple," a "primitive man suddenly risen during a very old civilization," ready to "take over command of the world as if the world were a paradise without trace of old footsteps."

A qualified defense of the 19th century, then a warning against its own offspring. Ortega draws three conclusions from the statistic: liberal democracy plus technical knowledge is "the highest type of public life known so far"; any better type must preserve those two principles' essence; and it would be "suicidal" to regress below them. Yet the same century that produced this "gigantic laboratory" of human flourishing also engendered, as a "constitutional defect," the mass-man in revolt — a type now endangering the very principles that made him possible. Left dominant, Ortega warns, "thirty years will suffice for our continent to revert to barbarism," technique dissipating "with the same facility with which so many trade secrets have often been lost," abundance collapsing into "real shortages, scarcity, anguished impotence." He names this outright as Rathenau's "vertical invasion of the barbarians." The warning that technique itself can dissipate and be forgotten within a generation — not through gradual ritualization but through outright loss — rhymes loosely with decay-sequence's account of infrastructure hollowing out, though Ortega's version is an abrupt intergenerational forgetting rather than the slower Functional→Absent drift the WMP term describes.

Notable passages

The chapter's philosophical hinge is the move from "circumstances decide" (false) to "our character" (true) at the individual level, then transplanted wholesale onto collective life: society's form flows from "the type of men dominant in it," and today that is the mass-man. This is followed immediately by the sharpest political contrast in the unit — masses that once merely supported a minority's forward- looking "program" now are the government, and that government, for the first time, has none: it is "omnipotent and ephemeral," unable to offer "a frank vision for the future."

The evidentiary core is the Sombart statistic itself: static population for twelve centuries, then a near-tripling in one. Ortega treats the raw number as doing real explanatory work — it is not incidental color but the chapter's namesake "datum." Paired with it is the account of what that acceleration did to the average European: instructed in technique, never educated in spirit, left a "primitive man suddenly risen during a very old civilization."

The chapter closes on its most consequential claim: an admission that liberal democracy and technical knowledge together represent civilization's best achievement so far, immediately qualified by the warning that the type this achievement bred could unmake it — "thirty years" to barbarism, technique lost like forgotten trade secrets, the whole crisis named as Rathenau's "vertical invasion of the barbarians." The final line refuses to resolve the ambivalence: the mass-man is "pure potency for the greatest good and for the greatest evil as well."
