Chapter 8 — Why the Masses Intervene in Everything, and Why They Intervene Only With Violence

Commons · The Revolt of the Masses · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
The mass-man's self-satisfied, hermetically closed soul explains why he believes he already possesses ideas without needing to justify them, and why, refusing all appeal to shared standards, his only mode of political action is violent "direct action," which Ortega calls the Magna Carta of barbarism.

Ortega explains the mass-man's psychology as a "hermetically closed" soul that never doubts its own completeness, then traces the political consequence: because the mass never submits its opinions to any external standard, its only possible mode of public action is direct violence rather than persuasion, which Ortega reads as barbarism displacing the whole achievement of civilization.

Themes

Self-satisfaction, not vanity, is the mass-man's defining trait. The mass-man "regards himself as perfect" with an unreflective, "paradisiacal" self-confidence unlike the vain select man, who at least needs others' validation and therefore never quite believes himself complete; true intelligence, by contrast, is "constantly catching himself within an inch of being a fool." Ortega borrows Anatole France's line that the fool is worse than the knave because "the knave does take a rest sometimes, the fool never."

Culture is nothing but standards one can appeal to; barbarism is their absence. Politics, law, economics, and aesthetics all require "a series of standards to which it is possible to appeal" for a dispute to be settled; without them there is, "in the strictest sense of the word, barbarism." This structural claim — that common life depends on a shared, appealable order rather than on any particular content — rhymes with legibility-as-machine-core's account of shared, orderable frameworks as what makes coordinated common life possible at all, though Ortega's "standards" are moral and intellectual (reason, discussion, justice) rather than administrative or measurable.

"Direct action" inverts civilization by making violence prima ratio instead of ultima ratio. Force has always been civilization's last resort — "the previous submission of force to methods of reason"; Syndicalism and Fascism represent, for Ortega, "a type of man who does not want to give reasons or to be right, but determined to impose his opinions," the "reason of unreason." Writing in 1930, Ortega is diagnosing the same historical moment Gramsci named with "morbid symptoms": the old order's legitimacy (appeal, discussion, standards) dying while nothing but naked imposition fills the gap. The resonance with gramsci-gap is real but partial — Ortega frames it as barbarism reasserting itself against culture, not as a passage between two world machines.

Civilization is "the will to live in common"; barbarism is disassociation. Through the root civis, all the "complications" of restriction, courtesy, and indirect method exist to make the city possible; refusing to take others into account is what makes "barbarous epochs... times of human scattering."

Liberal democracy is civilization's hardest achievement — and the mass wants to end it. Liberalism is "the right which the majority concedes to minorities," the willingness to "share existence with the enemy" even when weak — for Ortega "the noblest cry that has ever resounded in this planet," and also, he warns, "too difficult and complex to take firm root on earth." The chapter ends on the mass's "deadly hatred of all that is not itself," crushing every opposing group into a homogeneous whole. This is a close cousin of capture-resistance: liberal democracy is precisely a political architecture built to keep a majority from capturing or crushing a weaker, dissenting minority, and Ortega is describing that architecture's collapse — though his frame is majoritarian politics, not the Liveness Machine's autonomy-and-opacity sense of capture resistance.

Notable passages

The self-satisfaction passage (c1) is the psychological hinge of the whole chapter: everything that follows about the mass's refusal of standards and discussion follows from a soul that, unlike the vain man, needs no one else's confirmation to feel complete. The Anatole France aside (c2) sharpens this into a claim about intelligence: cleverness doesn't save the mass-man, because self-correction requires exactly the outward comparison his hermetism forecloses.

The "no culture" litany (c3) is Ortega's most explicit definition of culture as procedural rather than substantive — any domain (law, economics, aesthetics) counts as cultured only insofar as its disputes can be referred to a standard, and barbarism is simply that referral's absence.

The direct-action passage (c4) is the chapter's political payload: it names the specific mechanism (Syndicalism, Fascism, "direct action") by which hermetic self-satisfaction becomes a live historical event, and gives it a memorable epithet, "the Magna Carta of barbarism." The civilization-as-common-life passage (c5) supplies the positive counter-term. The closing liberalism passage (c6) is Ortega's own worked example of a standards-based, indirect political order and its vulnerability to exactly the mass psychology described earlier in the chapter.
