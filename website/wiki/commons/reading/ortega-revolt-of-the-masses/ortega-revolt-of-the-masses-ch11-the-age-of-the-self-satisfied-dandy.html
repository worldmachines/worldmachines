The Self-Satisfied Man: Spoiled Heir and the Farce of Political Destiny

Commons · The Revolt of the Masses · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Ortega restates the mass-man's three-part psychology (ease, self-satisfaction, direct action), casts him as one species of a general "heir-man" genus alongside the degenerating hereditary aristocrat and the family-spoiled fils de famille, and extends the diagnosis into interwar politics, arguing that Fascists and Communists alike secretly know liberalism is Europe's inescapable destiny and merely perform insincere, farcical opposition to it.

Ortega opens by recapping, in condensed form, the mass-man's psychological structure—an inborn sense that life is easy and unlimited, which breeds self-satisfied closure to outside judgment, which in turn licenses "direct action" in all matters—then generalizes this type into a broader genus of "heir-man" (spoiled child, degenerating hereditary aristocrat, mass-man) before pivoting, in the chapter's second half, to argue that contemporary Fascism and Communism are insincere performances staged against a liberal destiny that both camps secretly know is inescapable.

Themes

The mass-man's three-part psychology, restated as a program. Ortega recaps the chapter's throughline in a single dense paragraph: (1) an "inborn, root-impression that life is easy, plentiful, without any grave limitations" produces a sensation of power; (2) that sensation "invites him to stand up for himself as he is" and close himself to any "external court of appeal"; (3) the result is intervention in everything via "a system of 'direct action.'" Ortega frames the essay itself as only a "preliminary skirmish" and "first skirmish" against this "triumphant man," promising a future "frontal attack" the mass-man "will not suspect."

The heir-man genus: aristocrat, spoiled child, and mass-man as one species. The mass-man is named "the spoiled child of human history," defined as "the heir who behaves exclusively as a mere heir." Ortega generalizes this into a taxonomy—the "heir-man," of which "the 'aristocrat' is only one particular case, the spoiled child another, and the mass-man of our time, more fully, more radically, a third"—unified by inheriting "conditions of life which he has not created," wearing "the trappings of another existence."

Inheritance without struggle destroys authenticity—a Conatus-adjacent claim. The aristocrat, condemned to "represent the other man" rather than live his own destiny, loses "all authenticity" and becomes "pure representation or fiction." Ortega's justification is almost Spinozist: "All life is the struggle, the effort to be itself. The difficulties which I meet with in order to realize my existence are precisely what awaken and mobilize my activities, my capacities"—resistance is what makes a body, and a self, legible to itself ("If the atmosphere did not press on me, I should feel my body as something vague, flabby, and unsubstantial"). This rhymes with WMP's Conatus (striving sustained by resistance, not smoothed away by ease) and loosely with capture-resistance, though Ortega's frame is existentialist-vitalist, about an individual losing selfhood through frictionless inheritance, not a political theory of capture regimes.

Civilization's gifts arrive pre-built, and the heir cannot see the labor behind them. The 19th-century average man "finds himself surrounded by marvelous instruments, healing medicines, watchful governments, comfortable privileges" while remaining "ignorant how difficult it is to invent those medicines and those instruments and to assure their production in the future." This inherited-infrastructure blindness—benefiting from a civilizational apparatus one did not build and cannot reproduce—rhymes with WMP's world-machines frame (deep infrastructure occupied without being understood) and with the decay-sequence, where systems keep running on inertia after the understanding that built them has hollowed out; Ortega's version is narrower and moralized (a failure of gratitude and vigilance, not a structural account of institutional drift), but the shape—inheriting function while losing the knowledge that produces it—matches. (Ortega also makes a dated biogeographic aside here, that "inferior races—the pygmies, for example—have been pushed back towards the tropics by races born after them and superior in the scale of evolution"; this reflects period racial-hierarchy science and should be read as such, not adopted.)

Destiny is accepted or rejected, never discussed—and the self-satisfied man mistakes negative freedom for real freedom. Ortega distinguishes "theoretic truths," which "live as long as they are discussed," from truths "of destiny," which are "not discussed, it is either accepted or rejected." The self-satisfied man behaves like the fils de famille who thinks he can "do 'what he jolly well likes'"; in fact "we only possess a negative freedom of will, a voluntas"—one can refuse one's destiny but not escape it, only "fall a prisoner in the deeper dungeons" of it.

Liberalism as Europe's destiny—known even by those who perform against it. Ortega's sharpest political claim: "the most reactionary of Europeans knows, in the depths of his conscience," that 19th-century liberalism is "something inevitable, inexorable." This "final evidence is present equally in the European Communist as in the Fascist," whatever they profess; the insincerity that follows—"the tonic that keeps the mass-man in form"—is what Ortega calls "the joke."

The interwar political scene as comic farce—a Gramsci-gap-adjacent diagnosis. Ortega describes Europe as gripped by a "hurricane of farcicality," where "almost all the positions taken up and proclaimed are false ones,' politics is "letting things slide," and "never as now have we had these lives without substance or root." He draws the parallel to the Hellenistic Cynic (Diogenes among Aristippus's carpets)—a "parasite of civilization" who lives by denying it precisely because he is confident it will not fail, just as the Fascist needs liberty to abuse and the surrealist needs art to blaspheme. Written the same year (1930) Gramsci was formulating the interregnum where "the old is dying and the new cannot be born," this passage describes something adjacent to WMP's gramsci-gap—a legitimacy crisis producing performative, insincere "morbid symptoms"—though Ortega locates the cause in individual psychology (self-satisfaction, insincerity) rather than in institutional collapse, and treats liberalism's destiny as secure rather than genuinely up for grabs.

Notable passages

The opening recap ("To resume, the new social fact...direct action") is the chapter's most compressed statement of the mass-man's psychology, functioning as a program note before Ortega generalizes the type.

The "spoiled child of human history" passage names the heir-man taxonomy that structures the chapter's middle section, tying the mass-man explicitly to the degenerating hereditary aristocrat.

The aristocrat's loss-of-authenticity passage ("What does this bring us to?...irremediable degeneration") is the chapter's existentialist core, arguing inheritance without struggle empties out selfhood.

The negative-freedom passage ("It is not that one ought not to do just what one pleases...deeper dungeons of our destiny") states Ortega's voluntas/destiny distinction plainly.

The liberalism-as-destiny passage ("Even though it be proved...we must do what we do not feel like doing") is the chapter's political payload, claiming even Fascists and Communists secretly know liberalism cannot be escaped.

The "hurricane of farcicality" passage closes the chapter's political argument, diagnosing interwar Europe as a comic, rootless performance rather than a genuine confrontation with destiny.
