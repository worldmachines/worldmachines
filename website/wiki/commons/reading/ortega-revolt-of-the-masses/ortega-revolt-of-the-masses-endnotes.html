The Endnotes: A Second Argument in Miniature

Commons · The Revolt of the Masses · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Ortega's fifty-eight numbered endnotes to The Revolt of the Masses form a second, aphoristic argument in miniature, returning to the self-satisfaction of an age that believes itself a culmination, the mass-man defined against the excellent man, the fragility of civilizations kept alive by a handful of specialists, the mechanics of generational and historical decline, the danger of the State, and the nation as a wagered project of common life rather than a fact of blood or language.

These are Ortega's own fifty-eight numbered endnotes to The Revolt of the Masses (1930), collected here detached from the chapters they annotate. Freed from the pull of continuous argument, they range from bare citations (Horace, Aristotle, Ranke, Hegel, Halevy, Dopsch, and repeatedly his own earlier essays) to dense standalone aphorisms that sharpen or extend claims made in the body text. Read as a set rather than as isolated footnotes, they circle back on a small number of preoccupations: an age's belief that it is a culmination, the mass-man defined at the level of a single thought, the fragility of a civilization whose highest achievements are held alive by only a few specialists, the mechanics of generational and historical decline, the danger and weakness of the State, and the nation understood as a project of common life rather than a fact of blood, soil, or language.

Themes

An age judging itself golden. Several notes return to the psychology the main text calls self-satisfaction and the "altitude of the times." Roman coins under Hadrian bore legends like Italia Felix and Saeculum aureum — a "golden age" proclaiming itself on its own currency (n. 3); Hegel's pages on "periods of self-satisfaction" are cited approvingly (n. 4); and note 5 traces the word "modern" itself to this same reflex — a "consciousness of a new life, superior to the old one," an "imperative call to be at the height of one's time," so that for modern man "not to be 'modern' means to fall below the historic level." The same reflex reappears as nationalism, which Ortega calls one of the "manifestations... of the ruling 'self-satisfaction'" — the "self-conceit... of the least developed of the nations" (n. 36) — and as the false confidence bred by nineteenth-century abundance, which "demoralized the average man" (n. 33). Note 12 gives the syndrome its sharpest one-line form: "Not that we are decadent, but that, being predisposed to admit every possibility, we do not exclude that of decadence."

The mass-man, defined at the level of a single thought. Where the body chapters build the mass-man/excellent-man distinction through social description, the endnotes state it as bare psychology: the mass-man "is satisfied with thinking the first thing he finds in his head," while the excellent man "condemns what he finds in his mind without previous effort" (n. 16). Adjacent notes extend this into intellectual ethics — a "barbarian" is simply anyone in discussion "not concerned with adjusting himself to truth" (n. 23), and the poverty of Spanish intellectual culture is "not... greater or less knowledge, but the habitual lack of caution and care to adjust one's self to truth" (n. 24). Note 21 wonders, half in earnest, why no one has written an "Essay on Folly" about the ordinary torture of living among fools — Erasmus, he notes, wrote about something else.

The fragility of a civilization run by specialists. This is the endnotes' clearest extension of the main text's "specialist barbarism," restated as an acute rather than chronic danger. Note 14 quotes the physicist Hermann Weyl: if "ten or twelve specified individuals were to die suddenly," the marvels of contemporary physics "would be lost forever to humanity" — an achievement resting on "many centuries" of preparation, held alive by a handful of living minds. Notes 27 and 28 generalize the diagnosis: a "disproportion... between the complexity of present day problems and the capacity of present-day minds," and the observation that most scientists have "not the slightest suspicion of the very grave and dangerous internal crisis" running through their own field. Note 30 draws the contrast sharply: unlike politics, law, art, morals, and religion, "science alone is not bankrupt" — which is precisely why the average man's neglect of it cannot be excused.

Generations and the mechanics of decline. Several notes sketch a machinery of historical change operating beneath individual will. Horace's Odes (n. 2) supplies the generational-decline motif in epigraph form — each generation "worse" than the last, soon to father "a still more vicious progeny." Note 1 makes the mechanism material: Rome's urban agglomeration coincided with "an absolute decrease of the number of inhabitants in the empire" — growth and depopulation as one process, not two. Note 32 gives the fullest model: "a generation lasts about thirty years," splitting into a propagandizing half and a dominant half, so that an "extremist, and therefore revolutionary" ruling generation is reliably followed by a "restorationist" one — restoration meaning renewal, not "a simple 'return to the old ways.'" Note 40, via Ranke, locates one hinge of large-scale change in military technology and organization: the bourgeois Swiss armies' "new discipline and... rationalization of tactics," not gunpowder alone, defeated the medieval nobility.

The State: dangerous, and often weak precisely when it looks strongest. Note 41 argues that Europe's absolute monarchies coincided with weak States, because the "aristocracies of blood" lacked the technical, organizing, bureaucratic capacity to expand the State at society's expense — and, pointedly, that the absolute State "instinctively respects society much more than our democratic State, which is more intelligent but has less sense of historic responsibility." Septimius Severus's deathbed advice to his sons — "Remain united, pay the soldiers and take no heed of the rest" (n. 42) — is offered without comment as a specimen of naked statecraft. Note 38 gives the counter-ideal: philosophy does not need philosophers as rulers, "as Plato at first wished," but only needs philosophers to remain philosophers rather than becoming, as they have for "nearly a century," politicians, pedagogues, men of letters.

The nation as a wagered project of common life. This cluster elaborates the main text's account of the nation and a united Europe. Note 36 opens the analogy — a nation stands to the "assembly of nations" as a home stands to society. Notes 51–53 argue nationhood is not language or blood: "it is not even true in actual fact that all Spaniards speak Spanish, or all British English, or all Germans High-German," and Roman citizenship, extended to the whole empire, is read as a symptom of imperial decline — granted "precisely when it was losing the character of political status," becoming "mere burden and service to the State." Note 46 finds Catalonia holding two tendencies in productive tension — "the nationalism of Europe and the urbanism of Barcelona" — and note 55 dates the "principle of nationalities" itself to late-eighteenth-century Romanticism, i.e., treats it as a historically recent invention, not a natural category. Note 56 states the thesis as an open wager rather than doctrine: whether England can hold her Empire together "by furnishing [its parts] with an attractive program of existence" is called "a gigantic definitive experiment" whose outcome is still unknown.

An implicit autobiography. Running underneath all of this is a private bibliography: Ortega repeatedly cites his own earlier work — España Invertebrada (nn. 17, 20, 39), El Espectador and "El origen deportivo del Estado" (nn. 9, 19, 44, 45), Meditaciones del Quijote and Las Atlántidas (n. 9), The Dehumanization of Art (n. 6). The endnotes show Revolt less as a single new book than as a synthesis point for two decades of prior essays.

Notable passages

The Weyl note (n. 14) is the endnotes' most legible resonance with World Machines' concern for the fragility of infrastructure that has decayed to depending on a few living carriers: not the slow decay-sequence (Functional → Performative → Allegorical → Ornamental → Invisible → Absent) but its acute limit case — a whole tradition lost not by decay but by the sudden death of the small number of people who still hold it live. The tempo is different (catastrophic vs. gradual) but the underlying fear — capacity for a thing quietly narrowing to zero redundancy — is the same one the decay sequence formalizes.

The England-Empire note (n. 56) reads as a miniature statement of what the modernity-machine's "pull" character — cohesion through a shared attractor rather than force — looks like from inside a single polity: union sustained only for as long as it offers its parts "an attractive program of existence." Ortega frames this as a live experiment with an uncertain outcome, which is the more interesting claim than the general "nation as common life" thesis it illustrates — it treats imperial legitimacy as something that must be continuously re-earned, not once secured.

The generations note (n. 32) is worth flagging as a resonance that only partly fits: its thirty-year alternation of propagandizing and restorationist generations is a periodizing model of historical change, which is the same genus of claim World Machines makes at a completely different scale (three machines on roughly thousand-year Dawn–Day–Dusk cycles, not two generational postures on a thirty-year one) and by a different mechanism (cohort succession and taste, not institutional infrastructure). The two do not map onto each other; they share only the underlying intuition that historical change has a discoverable rhythm rather than being pure contingency.

The mass-man definition (n. 16) and the modernity/"height of time" note (n. 5) are the clearest condensations of concepts this project already treats as load-bearing in the main chapters — included here because the endnotes state them more nakedly, without the surrounding social description, which makes them unusually quotable as definitions in their own right.
