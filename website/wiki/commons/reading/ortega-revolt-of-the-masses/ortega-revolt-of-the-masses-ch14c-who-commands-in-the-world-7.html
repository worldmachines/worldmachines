Clear Heads, Caesar, and the State as an Enterprise in Common

Commons · The Revolt of the Masses · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Ortega defines the rare political "clear head" through Julius Caesar's reading of a collapsing Roman Republic, then argues the State — ancient or national — is not blood, language, or territory but a dynamic project of common life, closing with a polemic against historians who retroject modern nations into remote antiquity.

Section 7 opens with an epistemological portrait of the rare political "clear head" — exemplified by Julius Caesar against the confused "intellectual" Cicero — reads Caesar's career as a diagnosis of a Roman Republic already dead in substance (a corrupt, city-bound electoral system that could no longer represent an empire), and then pivots to Ortega's constructive theory of the State: not blood, language, or "natural frontiers," but a dynamic "project of common life," with the modern national State's capacity for participatory fusion contrasted against the ancient City-State's stark division of governors and governed, before closing on Ortega's polemic against the "philologers" (his name for historians) who retroject modern nations into remote antiquity.

Themes

Clear heads vs. the routine-headed. Political reality, unlike science's abstractions, is concrete and chaotic; genuine ideas are "the ideas of the shipwrecked," arrived at only by first admitting one is lost rather than hiding behind ready-made notions used "as scarecrows to frighten away reality." Ortega counts only Themistocles and Caesar as ancient "clear heads," with Cicero as foil — "a magnificent 'intellectual' head... a man engaged his whole life long in making things confused."

Caesar reads a Republic caught in its own interregnum. Rome's institutions are dead in substance before they are dead in form: "the Republic is nothing more than a word," elections are falsified by hired gangs of veterans and athletes because a voting procedure that requires physical presence in the city cannot represent an empire, and rival generals ("the Mariuses and the Sullas") substitute "empty dictatorships that led to nothing" for real authority. This resonates with the World Machines gramsci-gap — old legitimacy dead, new legitimacy not yet born — though the fit is partial: Ortega locates the cause in "a wretched technical detail," the electoral mechanism, rather than in a civilizational machine-transition, so the interregnum here is narrower and more contingent than the WMP concept was built to describe.

Caesar's unrealized modern State. Caesar's turn West rather than toward Alexander's prestige-laden East, and his ambition for "an immense social body, in which each element is at the same time an active and a passive subject of the State," anticipate the national State against the City-State's center-orders/periphery-obeys model. He is defeated in his own moment by Roman minds Ortega calls too "archaic," too fixed on the City's "tangible materialism," to grasp a polity that lives on its provinces rather than on Rome.

The State as pure dynamism, not a possession. Ortega's constructive claim: the State is neither consanguinity, nor language, nor territory, but "the will to do something in common," always oriented past its present composition toward a terminus ad quem; when the forward impulse stops, the State "breaks up, is dispersed, atomized." This half-rhymes with world-machines's framing of large-scale order as ongoing infrastructural process rather than fixed inheritance — but the rhyme is in the anti-essentialism only, not the mechanism: Ortega's State is explicitly programmatic and teleological ("a plan of action and a program of collaboration"), where WMP's Configurancy insists on non-teleological co-emergence of agents and worlds.

National State vs. City-State, on participation. Modern nations are distinguished from Rome or Athens not by blood or tongue but because "no one has ever been a mere subject of the State, but has always been a participator in it, one with it." Rome, by contrast, never fused Senate and people, let alone conqueror and conquered, and so "could not count on the patriotism of the others" once the Empire was threatened — a direct statement of the nation as a project of common life that this project's own reading of Ortega has flagged as a resonant idea.

The "philologers" accused of retrojection. Ortega's recurring polemic (introduced earlier in the chapter via the Caesar/Alexander comparison, sharpened here) against historians who imagine the modern nation as a permanent, buried fact — Vercingetorix or the Cid pictured as already fighting for a France or Spain that did not yet exist. Nations are not discovered in ethnic depths; they are "hammered out in two thousand years of toil."

Notable passages

The opening "clear heads" passage sets the chapter's epistemic register: political clarity is not intelligence about abstractions but the courage to feel oneself lost in the concrete and vital.

The "ideas of the shipwrecked" passage names the mechanism — to live is to feel oneself lost, and accepting that is already the beginning of firm ground — that Caesar is then shown to exemplify politically.

The electoral-procedure passage is Ortega's clearest causal claim in the unit: Rome's fall is pinned not on moral decline or excessive size in the abstract but on a specific broken mechanism, a voting system that could not scale to an empire.

The passage on Caesar's ambition for a State that "does not live on Rome, but on the periphery" is the unit's turn from diagnosis to vision — Caesar as prophet of a State-form Rome's own conservatives could not conceive.

The "pure dynamism" passage is the unit's theoretical center, stating outright that the State is process and project, never a static possession of blood or soil.

The closing "philologers" passage is Ortega at his most polemical, turning historiographical method itself into a target: to read the nation backward is to misunderstand what a nation is.
