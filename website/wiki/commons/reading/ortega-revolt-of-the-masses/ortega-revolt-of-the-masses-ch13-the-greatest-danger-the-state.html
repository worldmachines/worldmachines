The Greatest Danger, the State

Commons · The Revolt of the Masses · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Ortega names the modern State -- grown from a weak 18th-century apparatus into a self-sustaining bureaucratic-military machine -- as European civilization's gravest danger, because mass-man's craving for effortless intervention lets the State absorb the spontaneous social action that alone sustains a civilization, a mechanism he traces through Rome's decline, Mussolini's Fascism, and a closing contrast between French and English responses to crime.

Ortega argues that the modern State -- built by the bourgeoisie out of the small, weak apparatus it inherited from the nobility and grown in a century and a half into a "formidable machine" -- has become the gravest danger facing European civilization, because mass-man's psychology inclines him to demand State intervention in every difficulty, and that intervention progressively strangles the spontaneous social action that alone sustains a civilization; Ortega traces the mechanism through the decline of Rome, indicts Mussolini's Fascism as its purest contemporary specimen, and closes by contrasting French and English responses to rising crime as two opposite ideas of what the State is for.

Themes

Rebellion redefined, briefly recapped. Ortega opens by restating the book's frame: the mass "has not come into the world" to act of itself but to be "directed, influenced, represented, organized," and rebellion proper means "not accepting one's own destiny" -- illustrated by a wry aside that Lucifer's rebellion would have been no less a rebellion had he refused to be the lowest of the angels instead of striving to be God. When the mass does act alone, Ortega says, "it lynches," and violence becomes the period's "rhetoric" -- a sign, he adds, that its "descent is about to begin" now that it has "reached its full development."

The State's compressed life-cycle. Ortega narrates the State's growth from "quite a small affair" at the end of the 18th century -- built by nobles who had "virtues of the heart" but were "lacking in virtues of the head," unable even to invent gunpowder -- into a "formidable machine which works in marvelous fashion" by his own day. The pivot is 1848: once the middle class took possession of public power after the Revolution and "applied their undeniable qualities to the State," genuine revolutions became impossible in Europe; "the only thing now possible... is their opposite: the coup d'Etat."

Statism is the chapter's real target, and it is defined precisely. The gravest danger is not the State as such but "State intervention; the absorption of all spontaneous social effort by the State." Mass-man wants this absorption: his "great temptation" is the "permanent, sure possibility of obtaining everything -- without effort, struggle, doubt, or risk -- merely by touching a button." Ortega diagnoses the underlying confusion as mass-man's false identification with an anonymous power: "The mass says to itself, 'L'Etat, c'est moi,' which is a complete mistake. The State is the mass only in the sense in which it can be said of two men that they are identical because neither of them is named John."

The machine outlives, then consumes, the body that built it. Once intervention wins, "[s]ociety will have to live for the State, man for the governmental machine," and the State, "after sucking out the very marrow of society, will be left bloodless, a skeleton, dead with that rusty death of machinery." Rome supplies the historical proof: bureaucratization under the Antonines gives way to militarization under the Severi, the army has to be recruited from foreigners, and those foreigners "take possession of the State" while the native population becomes "slaves of people with whom they have nothing in common." Ortega's summary image is exact: "The skeleton eats up the flesh around it. The scaffolding becomes the owner and tenant of the house."

Mussolini's Fascism is named as Statism's contemporary face. The formula "All for the State; nothing outside the State; nothing against the State" is, for Ortega, sufficient by itself "to reveal in Fascism a typical movement of mass-men." He is careful to note the State Mussolini wields was "admirably built up -- not by him, but precisely by the ideas and forces he is combating: by liberal democracy" -- Mussolini's own results, by contrast, are "so minute, so little visible, so lacking in substance."

A closing illustration: two ideas of what the State is for. France, facing a rise in criminality among the new industrial worker around 1800, "hastened to create a large police force." England, facing the same rise a decade later, under a Conservative government, chose not to: "People are content to let disorder alone, considering it the price they pay for liberty." Ortega quotes John William Ward preferring the occasional throat cut on the Ratcliffe Road to "domiciliary visits... spying... and all the machinations of Fouche." The chapter ends on this: "The Englishman demands that the State should have limits set to it."

Notable passages

The opening naming of "the gravest danger" (c1) deliberately echoes the previous chapter's argument about science: just as scientific specialization threatens to strangle the very fertility that produced it, the State's own success -- its "fertility of principles" -- has produced a machine that now threatens the society that built it. This is less a new argument than Ortega re-running his favorite structural move (a good thing's own success reversing on it) on a second target.

The definitional passage (c2) is the chapter's load-bearing center: it is here, not in the chapter title, that Ortega actually specifies what Statism is -- State intervention absorbing spontaneous social effort -- and diagnoses why mass-man welcomes rather than resists it: the "L'Etat, c'est moi" line is his sharpest one-liner in the chapter, a fake identity built only on shared anonymity ("neither of them is named John").

The "machine consumes its host" passage (c3) and the Rome exemplum (c4) work as a pair: the first states the mechanism abstractly, the second cashes it out historically, ending in the same image twice over -- "sucking out the very marrow" mirrored by "the skeleton eats up the flesh around it." This resonates, with real qualification, with capture-resistance and decay-sequence: what Ortega describes is infrastructure (the State-as-machine) that keeps running and even intensifying its demands on a host society well past the point where it still serves that society's vitality -- the shape rhymes with WMP's decay-sequence (form persisting past function), though Ortega's arc ends in violent absorption by outsiders rather than the slower Performative-to-Absent drift WMP tracks. More strikingly, Ortega inverts WMP's usual valence on capture: mass-man does not resist capture by the State, he craves it, because touching a button to get everything "without effort, struggle, doubt, or risk" is exactly what a conatus that has stopped wanting to strive for itself would choose -- a picture of what happens when Conatus's drive to persist and expand is voluntarily traded away rather than suppressed from outside.

The Mussolini passage (c5) is Ortega's most historically dated and concrete indictment -- written in 1930, naming a sitting head of government -- and matters for the book's argument because it makes Statism's abstract danger immediately partisan-neutral: Fascism is condemned not for its ends but for its form, as one more instance of mass-man's psychology rather than a uniquely fascist pathology.

The closing France/England comparison (c6) is the chapter's concrete payoff: two societies facing the identical stimulus (rising industrial-era crime) choose opposite relationships to State power, and Ortega sides unambiguously with the society that accepts more disorder in exchange for a State kept "limits set to it" -- the practical test of everything argued abstractly earlier in the chapter.
