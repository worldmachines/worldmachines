Chapter 6 — The Dissection of the Mass-Man Begins

Commons · The Revolt of the Masses · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
The nineteenth century's liberal-democratic, scientific, and industrial revolution freed the average European from material want and social obstacle so completely that its heirs mistook this engineered civilization for a natural, self-sustaining fact, producing the mass-man's spoilt-child psychology of free-expanding desire and radical ingratitude.

Ortega opens his psychological autopsy of the mass-man by asking not just what he is like but how he was produced, and answers that the nineteenth century engineered an unprecedented emancipation — material, physical, and civil — whose very completeness caused its beneficiaries to experience it as nature rather than as achievement, yielding the entitled, ungrateful "spoilt child" psychology that defines the mass-man.

Themes

Historiography as prophecy. Ortega opens by citing Hegel, Comte, and Nietzsche as thinkers who, a century earlier, read the coming mass ascendancy directly from the "general structure" of their own age — his claim that the historian is "on the reverse side a prophet" and that only structure, never detail, is foreseeable in advance (hence his image of standing just far enough back to lose sight of "Cleopatra's nose"). This is a striking, individual-genius-centered mode of reading emergent historical structure — it credits three named prophetic minds rather than a silent, distributed assembly of causes, which cuts against a history-machine-style account (Lohse's paradigm-assembles-silently-until-it-lurches-into-visibility, explicitly opposed to "great men" historiography); Ortega's move here is the "great men" reading that history-machine defines itself against, which makes the contrast instructive even though it does not fit.

The nineteenth century's unprecedented emancipation. Three principles — liberal democracy, scientific experiment, and industrialism (the latter two, "summed up in one word: technicism") — dissolved the "pressure," "obligation," and "dependence" that had defined common life in every prior century. The average man found "universal material ease," "comfort and public order," equality before the law, and no "estates" or "castes" standing against him: existence as "exempt from restrictions," captured in the chapter's recurring motto, "Wide is Castile." Ortega is careful to note the principles were not invented by the 19th century but merely implanted by it at scale — the glory (and, implicitly, the danger) lies in universal implementation, not discovery.

Perfection mistaken for nature. Ortega's thesis: "the very perfection with which the 19th Century gave an organization to certain orders of existence has caused the masses benefited thereby to consider it, not as an organized, but as a natural system." He drives this home with the motorcar-and-sunrise analogy — people expect next year's cars to be better exactly as they expect the sun to rise, never crediting "the personal efforts of highly-endowed individuals" or the "difficult human virtues" whose failure would collapse "the whole magnificent edifice." This is close kin to the World Machines idea that a completed, dominant order is precisely the one whose constructedness becomes invisible to its inhabitants — modernity-machine's legibility project (Scott: readable, measurable, orderable life; smooth rails, secure tenure, predictable law) is what Ortega is describing from the inside, as psychology rather than institutional history; legibility-as-machine-core is the machinery, this chapter is the subjectivity it manufactures. It only half-maps onto the decay-sequence (Functional → Performative → Allegorical → Ornamental → Invisible → Absent): that sequence tracks infrastructure fading into invisibility as it declines, whereas Ortega describes infrastructure going perceptually invisible at the height of its function — naturalization as a feature of success, not decay, though both name the same blind spot toward the labor that built and maintains an order.

The spoilt-child psychology. Ortega names two "fundamental traits" of the mass-man: "the free expansion of his vital desires" and "his radical ingratitude towards all that has made possible the ease of his existence." Unlike a child who is eventually taught "Here I end and here begins another more powerful than I am" by a harsh, scarce world, the mass-man's world never forces that discipline on him — so he comes to believe, wrongly, that he alone exists and that his position is owed rather than won. This is the psychological core the World Machines context pack flags as central to Ortega's framing (self-satisfied / "spoilt child" psychology), introduced here in its fullest form.

The bakery as symbol. Ortega closes the unit by generalizing a bread riot — a mob that "goes in search of bread" by wrecking "the bakeries" — into an emblem of the mass's posture toward the whole of civilization: demanding its fruits as natural rights while remaining structurally blind to what produces them.

Notable passages

The Hegel/Comte/Nietzsche passage frames the chapter's method: reading the mass-man's rise as something nineteenth-century thinkers had already foreseen in outline. The "Wide is Castile" passage lays out the freedom-from-restriction that nineteenth-century technicism delivered, contrasted with the "burdensome destiny" of every prior common man. The spoilt-child passage supplies the chapter's psychological diagnosis in Ortega's own terms. The motorcar/sunrise passage makes the naturalization thesis concrete and vivid before the chapter states it abstractly in the closing thesis-and-bakery passage, which is the unit's load-bearing claim.
