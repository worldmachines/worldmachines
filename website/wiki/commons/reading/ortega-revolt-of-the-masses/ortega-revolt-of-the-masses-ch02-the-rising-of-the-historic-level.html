The Rising of the Historic Level

Commons · The Revolt of the Masses · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Ortega frames the twentieth-century "empire of the masses" as a genuine historical novelty and argues that the average man now possesses, as unreflective psychology, the appetites, techniques, and self-mastery once reserved to elites, so that history's median level has suddenly risen to a point previously reached only by aristocracies.

Ortega opens by naming the "brutal empire of the masses" as an authentic historical novelty, defends his own "radical and aristocratic" reading of history against the charge of snobbery, then lays out the chapter's operative diagnosis: the average man now enjoys the appetites, techniques, and psychological self-possession once reserved to elites, and refuses to obey the elites he has displaced. He generalizes this into the titular claim that history's "sea-level" has suddenly risen, and closes by reinterpreting Europe's apparent "Americanization" as a leveling of vital tone between the two continents rather than one influencing the other.

Themes

The mass phenomenon as historical novelty, legible only through Rome. Ortega insists the present "brutal empire of the masses" has no precedent within modern history; the nearest analogue is the Roman Empire's decline, where — per Spengler — the same "phenomenon of agglomeration," the "epoch of the colossal," saw a mass absorb and annul its directing elite groups and take their place.

"Aristocratic" as a structural claim about society, not a program. Ortega heads off the obvious misreading: he has never said society ought to be aristocratic, only that it is aristocratic by its very essence — organized around a functioning minority — and ceases to be a society at all once it sheds that core. He distinguishes this "social aristocracy" carefully from mere fashionable "Society" (people who invite one another to eight-hundred-guest dances) and from the State, which he is explicitly not discussing here.

Two-part diagnosis: appropriation plus insubordination. The chapter states its thesis in its sharpest form as a dissection in two headings: masses today (1) exercise functions and enjoy pleasures and techniques — bathrooms, legal rights, laws and society itself — that were until now elite patrimony, and (2) are simultaneously indocile toward the elite groups they absorbed these from, refusing to obey, follow, or respect them, and instead pushing them aside and supplanting them.

Rights of man: from juridical ideal to unreflective appetite. Tracing the eighteenth-century "rights of man" from elite theorem to nineteenth-century aspiration to twentieth-century lived psychology, Ortega argues that an ideal, once achieved as reality, loses its "authorizing magic" and "prestige" and hardens into an unconscious assumption — the mass-man now simply feels himself sovereign rather than aspiring to be so, which he offers as the psychological root of the mass-man's self-satisfaction and refusal of service explored elsewhere in the book.

The titular metaphor: history's sea-level rises. Because the average man now carries "the same vital repertory which before characterized only the superior elite groups," he is history's operative baseline — "sea-level" to history's "geography." Ortega's claim is that this median level has, after long invisible preparation, suddenly leapt to a point previously reached only by aristocracies, "at a bound, in one generation" — his fullest statement in this unit of what elsewhere in the book becomes the "altitude of the times."

Not Americanization, but a leveling of continents. Ortega reinterprets the commonplace that "Europe is becoming Americanized": the resemblance is not influence in either direction but genuine convergence — Europe's internally driven rise in average vitality (two centuries of "progressive education of the multitude" and economic improvement) meeting America's older, "constitutional" high average — so that the uprising of the masses signals rising European vitality rather than the "decadence of Europe" it is usually blamed for.

Notable passages

The Rome comparison (c1) is doing real argumentative work, not ornament: it is Ortega's only accepted precedent for the present crisis, and it supplies the chapter's second key term, the "epoch of the colossal," alongside "the rising of the historic level."

The "Radical because" passage (c2) is the chapter's most important disambiguation. Readers who take "aristocratic interpretation of history" as elitist politics have misread him; the claim is ontological (what a society is), not prescriptive (what it should do), and he separates it pointedly from both drawing-room "Society" and the State.

The two-headed dissection (c3) is the chapter's thesis statement proper — everything before it is throat-clearing and everything after elaborates one or the other heading.

The rights-of-man passage (c4) supplies the mechanism: an ideal that becomes real stops being felt as an ideal and becomes an "unconscious assumption," which is Ortega's account of why the mass-man experiences entitlement rather than gratitude or vigilance.

The sea-level passage (c5) is the chapter's namesake image and its most quotable line — the average man as history's "sea-level," with the level itself now risen "at a bound, in one generation" after invisible subterranean preparation. This mechanism — long silent assembly beneath the surface followed by a sudden lurch into visibility — reads as a strong echo of the World Machines history-machine concept (a paradigm that assembles silently and distributedly until it becomes visible all at once), applied here to a psychological/social shift rather than an institutional one.

The closing "leveling, not Americanization" passage (c6) is Ortega refusing a lazy causal story (America influencing Europe) in favor of a structural one (both converging on a higher average), which matters for the book's argument that the mass-man's rise is a European phenomenon in its own right, not an import.

Ortega's own ambivalence about the transition — elsewhere in this unit he calls the ascending masses "imposing, invincible, and treacherous like all destinies," "a cosmic sign of interrogation... with something in it of the guillotine or the gallows, but also with something that would wish to be a triumphal arch" — has a loose resonance with the World Machines gramsci-gap: an old legitimacy (elite-directed society) visibly dying while what replaces it has not yet shown whether it is "an absolute evil or a possible good." The fit is partial: Gramsci's gap is a crisis of institutional/hegemonic legitimacy, while Ortega's is a crisis of who is psychologically fit to direct a civilization at all — but the shared register of an unresolved, dangerous interregnum is genuine.
