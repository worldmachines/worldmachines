Who Commands in the World: Rule, the Shrunken Nation, and the Invention of the State

Commons · The Revolt of the Masses · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Societies stand or fall on who commands and who obeys, and Europe's true crisis is not exhausted capacity but a nation-state container grown too small for it, a diagnosis Ortega traces back to the Greco-Roman polis, the first state built by an act of political imagination.

Continuing his "Who Commands in the World?" inquiry, Ortega argues that command and obedience are the load-bearing function of any society (Spain his cautionary case of a nation demoralized by a centuries-old "false conscience" about who rules), then reframes the fashionable talk of European "decadence" as not exhausted capacity but capacity trapped inside nation-states too small to hold it, and closes with a history of the Greco-Roman polis to argue that the state, ancient or modern, is never a natural given but always a work of political imagination — one bounded, as Rome's was at Caesar's assassination, by the imaginative limits of the people who must build it.

Themes

Command and obedience as the decisive function of any society. Ortega opens by generalizing: wherever who rules is in doubt, "all the rest will be imperfect and ineffective," down to the individual conscience — a country's whole character can be read off from how cleanly it has settled the question of command. His example is Spain, a nation he says has lived for centuries with a "false conscience" about ruling and obeying, absorbing an original irregularity rather than expelling it, on the model of "one lie makes a hundred."

The "illusion of rule" as Europe's tonic. For Ortega the sense of being in charge of the world's destiny is not vanity but a psychological precondition for civilizational vigor: science, art, and technique all "live on the tonic atmosphere created by the consciousness of authority," and if Europe grows unaccustomed to rule, "a generation and a half" is enough to tip it into "moral inertia, intellectual sterility, and universal barbarism." Real command, he adds, is never mere coercion — it is a shared program of life, an enterprise men can enlist in, not just submit to.

European "decadence" reinterpreted as an outgrown container, not a decline. Ortega's real target is the fashionable diagnosis of European decline. He inverts it: Germans, English, French don't feel less capable than ever — they feel more capable, but "trammeled in the tiny cages" of nation-states too small for what their economies, intellects, and politics can now do. This resonates strongly with the World Machines gramsci-gap: the old container (the small sovereign nation, "her most characteristic invention") has become an obstacle to the very expansion it once enabled, while no successor form yet exists to replace it — "will she be able to shake off these survivals, or will she remain forever their prisoner?"

Parliament's crisis is a crisis of purpose, not of the instrument. Ortega defends Parliament against the "loss of prestige" charge: the institution itself is sound (no 19th-century institution built more efficient states), but Europeans no longer have a "program of a scope adequate" to what the institution could do — a point he generalizes with the motor-car, whose every era's objections are what improve it rather than condemn it.

The state as an act of imagination, not a natural given. Turning to the Greco-Roman polis, Ortega argues the city-state was invented, not inherited: it began as an enclosed, artificial "empty space" — the forum, the agora — carved out of undifferentiated countryside, "the negation of the fields." This founding gesture of walling-off and fixing an interior civic space reads as an origin story for legibility-as-machine-core: the state's first move is to make a bounded space legible and public, distinct from the "vegetative," unbounded life of the open fields. Ortega then generalizes: "the State begins by being absolutely a work of imagination," bounded by the limit nature sets to a people's imaginations — Rome's limit, he notes, was the city itself, and Brutus's assassination of Caesar (the "greatest imagination of antiquity") is offered as the moment that imaginative ceiling closed in. This half-resonates with the World Machines history-machine idea of a paradigm assembling from independent components until it lurches into visibility — Ortega's account shares the sense of a political form built rather than given, and of the "constituted" state as the visible residue of an invisible "constituent" movement — but the fit is partial: Ortega credits a "great company-promoter," an act of deliberate imaginative agency, where the history-machine concept is explicitly framed against "great men" historiography.

Notable passages

The opening definition ("The function of commanding and obeying...") sets the chapter's method: read a nation's inner life off the state of its command-and-obedience settlement — the frame under which Spain, and then Europe, get judged.

The "illusion of rule" passage is Ortega's most exposed statement of stakes: not economic ruin but a slow rot of "Western minds" once the discipline of responsibility that ruling imposes is withdrawn.

The "tiny cages" passage is the chapter's central image and its most Gramsci-gap-legible moment: Europe's pessimism as a "bird of wide-span wings" beating against bars its own past success built.

The Parliament passage isolates Ortega's actual complaint — not institutional defect but missing purpose — and forecloses the "abolish it" reading in favor of "reform it."

The polis passage is Ortega's founding myth for the state as such: an act of enclosure that invents a new, specifically civil kind of space out of the "amorphous limitless" countryside.

The closing passage on imagination states Ortega's thesis in its most compact form — statehood as bounded by a people's imaginative capacity — immediately cashed out in Rome's failure to imagine past the city, dramatized by Caesar's assassination.
