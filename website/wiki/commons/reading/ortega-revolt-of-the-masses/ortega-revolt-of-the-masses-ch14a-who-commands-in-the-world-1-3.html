Who Commands in the World? (§1–§3)

Commons · The Revolt of the Masses · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Ortega redefines historical "rule" as the predominance of public opinion rather than force, then diagnoses early-1930s Europe as a vacuum of that authority in which mass-peoples act out like unruly schoolchildren and Moscow and New York offer only camouflaged, not genuine, successions to European command.

Opening Chapter 14, Ortega argues that every historic period is defined above all by a "displacement of power" — by who currently commands — and builds a theory of "rule" as a spiritual phenomenon resting on public opinion rather than physical force. He then turns that theory on his own moment: since the 16th century, Europe has been the unifying commanding power over a now-single, interconnected humanity, but in the postwar years Europe visibly no longer trusts that it rules, and no successor has appeared. The consequence, he argues, is not liberation but a demoralized, "unemployed" world — smaller nations behaving like children once the schoolmaster leaves the room, and the two apparent candidates for succession, Moscow and New York, revealed as camouflage rather than genuine new commanding civilizations.

Themes

Rule is public opinion, not force. Ortega's central definitional move is to strip "rule" (mandar) of any essential connection to coercion: force is what a ruling power has at its disposal because it already commands, not what produces command. His proof case is Napoleon in Spain — he "had the force, and precisely because he had it" he never ruled there for a single day. True rule is "the tranquil exercise" of authority, not "the gesture of snatching at power"; famously, Talleyrand tells Napoleon "You can do everything with bayonets, Sire, except sit on them." The State, in this account, is nothing but "the state of opinion, a position of equilibrium."

Public opinion as historical gravitation. Ortega elevates this into a general law: "In Newton's physics gravitation is the force which produces movement. And the law of public opinion is the universal law of gravitation in political history." This is offered as a near-mechanical account of how eras cohere — an idealist historiography in which spirit/opinion, not material interest or class, is the causal substrate. When opinion is absent — "as "nature abhors a vacuum"" — the resulting empty space is filled by brute force as a mere substitute, never a true replacement.

Mass-man scaled up to mass-peoples. The book's recurring psychology of the self-satisfied, entitled mass-man reappears here applied to whole nations. With the rumor abroad that Europe has stopped ruling, "every two-bit nation starts skipping, gesticulating, standing on its head," producing what Ortega calls "the vibrionic panorama of "nationalisms."" The image is explicitly a schoolroom one: children who "kicks up its heels, and goes wild" the moment the master leaves, only to discover they have "no task with a meaning" of their own — rebellion against European standards without any capacity to generate replacement standards.

The "unemployed" spirit. Freedom from command is not experienced as liberty but as a void. Losing "commandments, obliging us to live after a certain fashion," leaves existence "unemployed" — which Ortega calls "a worse negation of life than death itself," because "to live means to have something definite to do — a mission to fulfill." This is Ortega's account of why the interwar mood is demoralized rather than exhilarated by Europe's apparent decline: humanity is, in effect, crying out "for someone or something to take command."

Moscow and New York as historical camouflage. Ortega rejects both obvious 1930 candidates to succeed Europe. Both are "two sections of the European order of things, which, by dissociating from the rest, have lost their meaning" — not new civilizations but "child-people[s]" wearing borrowed European commandments: Russia dressed in Marxism ("Russia is Marxist more or less as the Germans of the Holy Roman Empire were Romans"), America dressed in "technicism," itself "another European invention, not an American." Neither, he argues, has yet earned the capacity to command — "Russia will require centuries," and America "has not yet suffered."

Notable passages

The Napoleon/Spain passage and the Talleyrand line do the definitional work the rest of the chapter depends on: rule is authority resting on consent, and force is what obedience makes available, not what compels it. The Newton-gravitation paragraph generalizes this into Ortega's operating law of historiography, and the following paragraph on the vacuum "abhor[red]" by nature states, in miniature, the interregnum he thinks Europe has entered: opinion gone, force filling the gap provisionally. This vacuum diagnosis — an old commanding order visibly failing with no legitimate successor yet able to occupy its place — resonates strongly with gramsci-gap, and the resonance is genuinely double: Ortega (1930) and Gramsci (writing these same years) are describing the identical European interregnum independently, in different vocabularies. Ortega's is idealist and psychological (opinion as a quasi-Newtonian force, command as a "spiritual power"); Gramsci's is materialist and class-based (hegemony, "morbid symptoms"). The shape of the diagnosis — dying legitimacy, no successor yet, disorder in the gap — is shared; the causal machinery is not.

The chapter's organizing question, "who commands in the world," and its claim that a period's "substance or character" is set by a "displacement of power," is Ortega's own route to something like world-machines's question of which infrastructure currently dominates — though the fit is partial and worth qualifying: Ortega's model is a single unified locus of command passing from one civilizational bloc to the next (Europe, possibly a future United Europe), not three machines in permanent coexisting friction. The mass-peoples/schoolchildren passage and the "unemployed" passage carry the chapter's affective core, extending the book's mass-man diagnosis from individuals onto nations and giving a concrete existential account of why a legitimacy vacuum is experienced as demoralization rather than freedom.

The Moscow/New York camouflage passage is the chapter's most pointed application: Ortega's insistence that Marxism-in-Russia and technicism-in-America are borrowed surface forms over a different, immature substance has a loose and partial rhyme with decay-sequence's idea of appearance persisting after a form's original meaning has drained — but the mechanisms differ (Ortega describes a young people borrowing an still-functional foreign form, not an old form hollowing out in place), so the connection is only a formal echo, not the same process.
