Guerrero's Introductory Essays: Biography, Style, and the Roots of Ortega's Pessimism

Commons · The Revolt of the Masses · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Marciano Guerrero's two 2012 prefatory essays sketch Ortega y Gasset's biography, prose style, and translation aims, trace The Revolt of the Masses to three inherited currents (Schopenhauerian pessimism, a Nietzschean cult of the superior individual, and Millian liberalism), and preview the book's mass-man/aristocracy-of-the-mind opposition while reading Ortega's diagnosis of Europe's lost dominance as still relevant to 21st-century coexistence.

This unit collects the 2012 translator-editor Marciano Guerrero's two prefatory essays to his edition of The Revolt of the Masses: "Essay 1 — Introduction," a biographical and stylistic sketch of Ortega y Gasset plus a note defending Guerrero's own translation choices, and "Essay 2 — From Pessimism and Superiority to Peaceful Coexistence," Guerrero's reading of the book's intellectual lineage and continuing relevance. Everything here is Guerrero writing about Ortega, not Ortega writing — with one exception flagged below, where Guerrero quotes Ortega's own words directly inside his essay.

Themes

Biography and career. Guerrero traces Ortega (1883–1955) from his University of Madrid doctorate and 1905 training in Germany (Leipzig, Berlin, Marburg) through his 1910 Madrid professorship, his founding of Revista de Occidente in 1923, and his 1936–1945 exile during the Spanish Civil War, presenting Ortega as Spain's preeminent 20th-century philosopher and listing his major works (Meditations on Quixote, Invertebrate Spain, The Theme of Our Time, The Dehumanization of Art, Kant, The Revolt of the Masses, The Mission of the University).

Stylistic portrait: clarity as method. Guerrero praises Ortega's prose as transparent rather than "mystical and hermetic" (an implicit contrast with Heidegger), built from aphorism and metaphor used to "bring to light difficult ideas" rather than for ornament, and anchored in Ortega's own maxim that "In philosophy, clarity is courtesy."

Translator's apologetic. Guerrero positions his own translation against unnamed prior versions he calls "veritable literal translations" amounting to "rough paraphrasing," staking his authority on fidelity to Ortega's Spanish — a claim the reader should treat as translator's advocacy rather than settled fact.

Three inherited currents. Guerrero names Schopenhauer (a "sense of pessimism for the European race"), Nietzsche (the audience for the "superman" idea), and John Stuart Mill (libertarian individualism) as the three writers who shaped Ortega, and reduces The Revolt of the Masses to their synthesis: pessimism, belief in the superior individual, and liberalism.

Europe's lost dominance. Guerrero frames Ortega's pessimism around a diagnosis of civilizational succession, then quotes Ortega directly: Europe no longer rules and no successor is guaranteed. Guerrero adds his own retrospective correction — Ortega, he argues, failed to foresee that the United States would fill the vacuum — and reads the passage as still live for 21st-century multipolarity and the European Union.

Mass-man vs. aristocracy of the mind. Guerrero previews the book's central opposition: the "mass-man," a homogenizing, "flattening force" that demands equality and displaces the "superior individual," versus an "aristocracy of the mind" that Guerrero is careful to distinguish from inherited, titled aristocracy — Ortega's elitism, he insists, is meritocratic rather than class-based, which is why (per Guerrero) it can coexist with Millian liberalism and inform a vision of peaceful international coexistence.

Notable passages

Guerrero's stylistic credo (c1) insists "there is no doubt" Ortega was "a consummate stylist," landing on the maxim "In philosophy, clarity is courtesy" — a line that doubles as Guerrero's own translation manifesto.

The translator's note (c2) stakes Guerrero's authority against predecessors' "rough paraphrasing," promising English readers that "nothing was lost in the translation" — advocacy, not evidence, but revealing of how this edition wants to be read.

The influence triad (c3) — Schopenhauer, Nietzsche, Mill — is Guerrero's own compressed genealogy, not Ortega's; it supplies the interpretive lens the rest of Guerrero's essay applies to the book.

The Europe passage (c4) is the unit's most directly Ortega-flavored moment: Guerrero introduces it as Ortega's resignation to European "decadence," then quotes Ortega's own opening diagnosis that "the word is beginning to go round that Europe no longer rules in the world," closing on the rhetorical question of civilizational succession — "Who is going to succeed Europe in ruling over the world? ... And if no one, what then is going to happen?" — that structures the whole book. This is a genuine, if partial, resonance with world-machines and gramsci-gap: Ortega is naming the interregnum after one order's legitimacy has failed and before a successor has consolidated, though his frame is a single civilization's world-rule rather than WMP's three-machines-in-friction model, and he is diagnosing a mood, not forecasting via Spine/Gate/Branch.

The mass-man mechanism (c5) — "a flattening force that would homogenize politics" — is Guerrero's summary of Ortega's core complaint. It half-rhymes with WMP's capture-resistance and Conatus frame: the masses' demanded equality, in Guerrero's telling, is a leveling that suppresses the striving of the "superior individual" — though Ortega's target is aesthetic-political elitism, not Spinozist self-persistence, and Guerrero deploys the idea moralistically (a lament for lost distinction) rather than structurally (a account of capture mechanics).

Guerrero's clarification (c6) that Ortega's aristocracy is "of the mind," not inherited title, matters for how later units should read "select minority": it is meritocratic self-selection in Guerrero's account, not hereditary class — a distinction load-bearing for the book's later chapters on the self-satisfied mass-man.
