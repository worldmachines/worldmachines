The deep history of deforestation and the search for Eden

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Grove opens Chapter 1 by declining to hunt for the earliest instance of ‘conservation’ and instead traces deforestation-driven soil erosion, drought, and social collapse from Paleolithic fire-clearing through Mesopotamia, the Levant, Mesoamerica, and classical Greece and Rome, where Theophrastus’s deforestation-rainfall theory and Mediterranean ecological decline fed a literary displacement of Eden onto still-forested distant lands, transmitted through Sanskrit-Arab-Venetian medico-botanical learning into the European botanical garden and the deep motivational ‘search for Eden’ behind coming colonial expansion.

This unit opens Chapter 1 of Green Imperialism with a Humboldt epigraph on the human imagination of lands beyond the horizon, then lays out Grove's methodological premise: rather than hunt for the single earliest instance of ‘conservationist’ thought, he traces how an integrated, relatively ‘objective’ Western discourse about nature and resource use was constructed over millennia. From there the unit surveys deforestation, soil erosion, and social collapse from Paleolithic fire-clearing through Mesopotamian timber empires, the Southern Levant, and Mesoamerica; introduces Theophrastus's classical theory linking deforestation to declining rainfall (the deep ancestor of the book's desiccation theory); and shows how ecological decline in the Mediterranean core displaced the Edenic imagination onto distant, still-forested lands — a template later reattached to colonial tropical islands. It closes by tracing a Sanskrit-Arab-Venetian chain of medico-botanical knowledge transfer that primes the European botanical garden as an institution, and names the ‘search for Eden’ as the deep motivational thread connecting all of this to the coming age of European maritime and colonial expansion.

Themes

Conservation has no clean origin-point; it grows out of ordinary resource management. Grove explicitly refuses to identify “the earliest instances of consciously conservationist interventions,” arguing that so-called conservation practices “cannot ... be distinguished clearly from the complex web of economic, religious and cultural arrangements” societies evolved to safeguard resource access. His chosen method is instead to trace the historical construction of Western discourses and policies about nature — a framing move that sets up the whole book's argument that environmentalism was made, in specific colonial contexts, rather than simply discovered.

Deforestation-driven soil erosion, drought, and social collapse recur across deep history. Paleolithic and Mesolithic fire use cleared forest in Africa and Tasmania tens of thousands of years ago; deforestation and goat-grazing are linked to the collapse of Southern Levantine communities around 6000 B.C.; and recurrent soil-erosion episodes (c. 1500 B.C., A.D. 300, A.D. 1000) tied to maize agriculture are implicated in the rise, fall, and shifting power of Mesoamerican city-states.

Mesopotamian timber economies produced the first known conservation impulses, driven by scarcity and geopolitics rather than ecological insight. The Epic of Gilgamesh casts the god Humbaba as guardian of the cedar forest against the needs of civilization; Mari introduced protective forest measures as Euphrates-catchment timber grew scarce; and Hammurabi's Babylonia responded to the severance of the long-distance hardwood trade with Meluhha (Malabar) with severe forest-conservation measures. These are proto-legible, state-level interventions into a resource base long before anything like the modernity-machine's systematic legibility apparatus — closer to ad hoc crisis response than to comprehensive measurement and control, which resonates with legibility-as-precondition-not-cause in a very early, partial form.

Theophrastus originates the deforestation-rainfall theory that becomes desiccationism. Observing Peloponnesian War-era devastation, Theophrastus developed a theory “which firmly linked deforestation to the decline in rainfall” in Greece and Crete — a genuinely proto-scientific claim about human-caused climate change. Neither Greece nor Rome acted on it (nor on Pliny's and Vitruvius's later warnings about the silted Tiber); the theory only became historically consequential after its Renaissance republication in 1483, more than a millennium later. This is the deep prehistory of the desiccation theory the book will show driving actual colonial state policy on Mauritius and in the Caribbean — an early instance of an ecological model shaping governance only once institutions existed to act on it.

Mediterranean ecological decline displaced the Edenic imagination onto distant, still-forested lands. As Greek and Roman landscapes degraded, literary imagination relocated Eden-like abundance to the Hesperides, and then, after Alexander's expeditions, to India and the Ganges valley — establishing an imaginative template Grove will later show reattached to real tropical islands (St Helena, Mauritius) under European colonization.

A Sanskrit-Arab-Venetian knowledge chain builds the institutional precondition for the European botanical garden. Under Khalif al-Mansur, the Sanskrit Charaka Samhita and Susruta Samhita were translated into Arabic, feeding Indic botanical knowledge into Arab medicine; Avicenna's Kanun (drawing on both Galen and Indic learning) was translated into Latin in Sicily and diffused into Venice; Cairo's gardens and Egyptian tree-planting responded to Mediterranean timber scarcity; and the old Zoroastrian Pairidaeza garden-as-Paradise was gradually transmuted into more “rigorously botanical or experimental concepts.” This is the pre-history of the botanical garden Grove elsewhere treats as an information network of empire (the Cape, Pamplemousses, Calcutta) — already, in embryo, an infrastructure for organizing and moving natural knowledge across cultures, anticipating the informational role of the later colonial modernity-machine.

The ‘search for Eden’ is named as the deep motivational thread. Grove explicitly labels the convergence of these traditions — European, Arabic, and Indian — the “search for Eden,” sharpened by the Renaissance pursuit of “gold and profits, the settlement of distant island colonies, the garnering of botanical knowledge” and the lure of India and China. This sentence is the hinge into the book's actual subject: the island Edens of colonial expansion that the epigraph's Humboldt (alexander-von-humboldt) already gestures toward, imagining another dwelling for mankind beyond the horizon.

Notable passages

Grove's methodological framing (c1) states his choice to trace discourse construction rather than hunt origins, and sets the interpretive terms for the whole book. The Gilgamesh passage (c2) is his earliest textual evidence of a society consciously linking deforestation to drought and encoding forest protection in myth. The Theophrastus passage (c3) is the classical-world ancestor of desiccation theory, explicitly bridged to the Renaissance via its 1483 republication. The ‘search for Eden’ passage (c4) is Grove's own naming of the deep motivational thread the whole book will track through tropical islands. The botanical-garden passage (c5) states the thesis that gardens are Paradise-institutions organizing natural knowledge, with Theophrastus himself as an early practitioner. The Sanskrit-Arabic knowledge-transfer passage (c6) documents the concrete textual route — Charaka and Susruta Samhita into Arabic — by which non-European medico-botanical knowledge entered the tradition that would eventually feed European botanical science.
