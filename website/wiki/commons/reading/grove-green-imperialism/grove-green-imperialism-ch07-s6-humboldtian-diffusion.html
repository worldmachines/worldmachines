The diffusion of Humboldtian environmental ideas, 1800–1837

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Humboldt's universalist theory that deforestation dries springs and disrupts continental climate—inherited from Herder's anti-positivist, Orientalist Naturphilosophie rather than from island-bound British and French empiricism—only reached British colonial administrators in 1837–39, via Boussingault's chemistry paper and the scientific societies and newspapers of Mauritius and Madras, after the East India Company itself had blocked Humboldt's access to India over his anti-colonial politics.

Tracing why the desiccationist critique first sharpened by empirical island observation (Beatson and Burchell on St Helena, the French on Mauritius) took until the 1830s to acquire an authoritative continental and imperial scale, this section follows the intellectual genealogy of Alexander von Humboldt's universalist theory of deforestation-driven climate change—via Georg Forster, Goethe and Johann Gottfried von Herder's anti-positivist Naturphilosophie and Orientalist humanism—from Humboldt's 1819 account of the shrinking Lake Valencia in Venezuela, through the East India Company's political refusal to let Humboldt near India, to its eventual uptake by Joseph Boussingault's 1837 paper and its transmission into British colonial science via the scientific societies of Mauritius and Madras.

Themes

Island empiricism was not yet universal theory. St Helena's officials (Burchell, Beatson) and the French on Mauritius had already connected deforestation to desiccation, but purely from bounded, insular observation; Grove states plainly that desiccationist theories "continued to lack an effectively global dimension and also lacked rigour and scientific principle" until after the 1820s—islands proved the mechanism locally but could not yet certify it as a law of nature applicable to continents.

Humboldt supplied the missing universalism, and he got it from German anti-positivism. Where British and French natural history remained inside the positivist empiricism of david-hume and Lavoisier, Humboldt's teachers—Georg Forster, Goethe, and above all Herder—belonged to a German Naturphilosophie hostile to mechanistic science. Humboldt's 1819 account of Lake Valencia (from the Personal Narrative of his 1799–1804 South American journey) generalised the island model of watershed-and-forest onto a continental "geomorphological model," giving desiccation theory the abstracted, exportable form it had lacked.

Herder's Orientalism and humanitarianism are inseparable from the science. Herder's moral outrage at European plunder and his idealised reading of Hindu cosmology (the Vishnu passage, the "gentlest branch of humanity") fed directly into Humboldt's holism. Grove ties this same mid-eighteenth-century naturalism-as-moral-taste to jean-jacques-rousseau and Goethe, and is explicit that the environmental critique and the anti-colonial, anti-capital humanitarianism shared one cultural root—the same root that had earlier shaped Poivre and Bernardin de Saint-Pierre on Mauritius.

Humboldt's politics blocked his own diffusion. His radicalism and open criticism of Spanish, and by extension European, colonial rule made him "one of the most famous and intellectually feared men in Europe"—and made the East India Company refuse him entry to India outright in 1814 and after. The asymmetry (feared in the metropole, unwelcome in the colony he most wanted to study) is Grove's explanation for why Humboldt's ideas needed a second, less politically toxic carrier.

That carrier was Boussingault, and the vector was colonial scientific infrastructure, not personal reputation. A French agricultural chemist, not Humboldt himself, popularised the desiccation "law" in print (1837); it moved into empire through translation (the Edinburgh New Philosophical Review, 1838), colonial learned societies (the Royal Society of Arts and Sciences of Mauritius, the Madras Society of Literature and Science), and—Grove stresses as a first—the newspaper used deliberately as a "conservationist propaganda tool." This resembles, loosely, the kind of distributed, multi-node information infrastructure the World Machines frame associates with modernity-machine-building; the resemblance is structural rather than exact, since Grove's interest is empire's learned societies and press rather than state cadastral or statistical apparatus.

A genuinely transnational circuit. The idea's path—Germany to France to Venezuela to Britain to Mauritius to Madras, refracted at each stop by local institutions and politics—is the kind of connected-world circulation c-a-bayly and archaic-globalization-bayly describe elsewhere; this is a history of information moving through, and being reshaped by, imperial networks rather than a single-author discovery.

Notable passages

Grove's thesis sentence on desiccation theory's pre-1820s parochialism sets up the unit's organizing problem: why island observation stalled short of a global theory. Humboldt's own Lake Valencia passage—his generalisable claim that felling forests dries springs and turns riverbeds into torrents—is the primary scientific text the entire diffusion story concerns; Grove treats its analytic content as still valid, calling it unsuperseded "by more recent findings." Herder's scathing, unfinished-looking indictment of European wealth and plunder preserves the moral-economic register Grove insists cannot be separated from the science of deforestation. Humboldt's late-life recollection of Forster's writing, Hodges' Ganges paintings, and a "colossal dragon tree" in Berlin's botanical garden names the actual chain of images—not just texts—that seeded his tropical, Edenic scientific imagination, a small case of nature-culture-dialectic-style aesthetic and epistemic entanglement in alexander-von-humboldt's formation, as also told in wulf-invention-of-nature. The East India Company's blunt refusal to let Humboldt travel to India is Grove's clearest evidence that the diffusion of environmental science was gated by colonial politics, not merely by scientific timing. Bouton's Mauritius lectures and their newspaper serialisation mark, in Grove's telling, the first deliberate use of the press as a conservation-advocacy tool—a template later redeployed in India and the Cape.
