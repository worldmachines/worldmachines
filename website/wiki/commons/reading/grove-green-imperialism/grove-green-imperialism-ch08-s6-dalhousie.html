The legacy of Balfour and the Sindhi amirs: Dalhousie and the pre-colonial roots of colonial Indian forestry

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Lord Dalhousie's turn toward centralised state forestry in India, culminating in his 1855 memorandum, was shaped less by Continental forestry science than by Hooker's and Balfour's desiccationist arguments and by the pre-colonial forest-management system of the Sind amirs, which colonial officers such as Napier, Stocks and Gibson adopted as a direct administrative model rather than a mere legitimating precedent.

This unit traces how Lord Dalhousie, arriving in India in 1847 with a utilitarian, state-expanding bent, was converted to centralised forest conservation through a specific chain of people and evidence — J. D. Hooker's shipboard acquaintance and St Helena/Ascension desiccation claims, Edward Balfour's 1849 paper, and Dalhousie's own 1851 observation of the treeless Punjab — and then argues that the actual administrative template for his 1849 Punjab arboriculture project, and much of the subsequent Bombay Presidency system, was not a Continental import but the pre-colonial shikargah forest-reserve system of the Amirs of Sind, absorbed almost intact by Napier, Ellenborough's officers, John Stocks and Alexander Gibson; the unit closes by naming McClelland's and Cleghorn's parallel economic and Madras arguments as the tributaries that fed into the 1855 Dalhousie memorandum, and by crediting six EIC surgeon-naturalists collectively for a submerged, radical critique of colonial environmental and social harm.

Themes

Dalhousie's conversion to state forestry ran through a concrete personal network, not abstract doctrine. Grove is precise about mechanism: Hooker met Dalhousie by chance on the 1847 voyage out and relayed the conviction, formed on his 1842 visit to St Helena and Ascension, that Roberts's and Beatson's reafforestation programmes had measurably increased rainfall — a claim itself enabled by Joseph Banks's meteorological observation programme on St Helena (from 1811, imitating Roxburgh's Madras Presidency weather research). Balfour's 1849 paper, drawing on Boussingault's Ascension findings and the St Helena Almanac, reinforced this before Dalhousie ever set foot in the Punjab. Grove then quotes Dalhousie's own February 1851 minute recording his direct impression of "the almost total absence of forest trees" on the plains, and a second passage where Dalhousie frames afforestation as a multi-generational "heritage for which posterity will be grateful" — a strikingly Foundation-like appeal to a project whose fruits its founders will not live to see.

The unit's central historiographical claim: pre-colonial Sind forestry was a working MODEL for colonial forestry, not merely a legitimation invoked after the fact. Grove states plainly that "the critical influence of pre-colonial forest practices in providing models (rather than just a legitimation) for early management methods in colonial state forestry has not been properly appreciated." The Amirs of Sind ran an enormous shikargah (game-and-forest reserve) system — reafforesting over a million acres between 1783 and 1843 and selling firewood rights for up to 300,000 rupees a year from a single forest. Napier learned of the shikargahs during the 1843 campaign against the Talpur amirs (where they had also served as guerrilla cover) and, on annexing Sind, had Ellenborough appoint Captain Baker with "several scientific officers" to administer the existing canal-and-forest infrastructure directly, rather than import a new one. Until about 1850 this Sind system was, Grove notes, "the only working example of formalised state forest reservation" available to the Company anywhere in India outside Roxburgh's and Wallich's plantations — making it the template, not a footnote, for what followed in the Punjab and Bombay.

Practical strain pushed policy back toward the amirs' stricter, more exclusionary model. Napier and Baker's first move had been to open the Sind forests to free local grazing and foraging — "a complete reversal" of the amirs' exclusionist policy — which won short-term political credit but left a chronically undermanned department overwhelmed by demand. When the Punjab Board of Administration consulted the Sind-trained surgeon John Stocks in 1852, his report recommended reverting to the amirs' methods of enclosure by "stakes or walls," exclusion of "goats and... any living being," and a resident "Government Keeper." Grove draws out the counterintuitive implication: the pre-colonial regime "was run a great deal more tightly and unsympathetically to small farmers... than was the case after colonial annexation" — inverting the usual assumption that indigenous rule was laxer than colonial rule on customary access. Stocks's report, adopted by Dalhousie and the Punjab authorities, became a basis for aggressively enlarging state forest area even at the expense of agricultural revenue.

Even Dietrich Brandis, the figurehead of "German-style" scientific Indian forestry, credited the amirs' system as foundational. Grove treats Brandis's own acknowledgment — "we owe the maintenance of forests in Sindh and of the rukhs in the Punjab entirely to the actions taken by the former rulers," with early British rule often "not... favourable to the preservation of forests" by comparison — as "a more authoritative testament" to indigenous priority than any outside argument could supply, since it comes from inside the colonial forestry establishment itself. Grove uses the same point to puncture the standard assumption that colonial Indian forestry slavishly copied Continental (German/French) systems: before 1865, Bombay Conservancy officers were "employed and named according to indigenous usage rather than according to imported terms."

Economic reasoning converged with climatic theory to produce the 1855 Dalhousie memorandum and the first forest departments. Surgeon John McClelland's Burma survey supplied an early "sustainable yield" argument, recasting a forest as "a growing capital" that "degenerate[s]... to that of a sinking fund" if young trees are felled before maturity — an economic case for conserving standing forest over planting new. Combined with Hugh Cleghorn's Madras/BAAS work, this fed directly into the 1855 Dalhousie memorandum, the basis for India's post-1860 centralised forest policy, and the near-simultaneous founding of the Madras (1855) and Mysore (1856) forest departments under Cleghorn.

Grove closes by recovering a "precocious" state-environmentalist story eclipsed by subaltern historiography, and naming its collective authors. He names six EIC surgeon-naturalists — Roxburgh, Gibson, Balfour, Cleghorn, Stocks and McClelland — as the joint agents of this policy turn, its intellectual DNA a blend of "physiocratic, Priestleyite, Scottish Hippocratic and Humboldtian thinking." Grove is careful to add that their scientific critique carried a submerged radical charge: real anxiety about environmental health doubled as a vehicle for anxieties about colonialism's and industrialism's social and moral costs, sometimes cast in millennial terms as famine and deforestation being consequences of a "social transgression" against the Indian peasantry — a reading strongest in Balfour, via his debt to Bernardin de Saint-Pierre's physiocratic reformism and his family's Scottish radical connections. But Grove is equally clear this radicalism only gained purchase because it was "fronted by climatic arguments threatening famine, economic failure and societal breakdown" — the moral critique rode in on the coattails of a technical, alarmist science.

World Machines resonances. The conversion of the amirs' informal, orally-administered shikargah system into a titled colonial "forest department" with a Government Keeper, boundary stakes and a district establishment is a clean instance of Legibility — rendering a customary resource-and-revenue arrangement into a countable, enforceable administrative object — though the twist here, which Grove insists on, is that the template for legibility was inherited from a pre-colonial state rather than invented by the colonial one; the modernity-machine's legibility apparatus is shown absorbing an existing indigenous apparatus rather than building from nothing. Separately, Grove's own closing argument — that a centralised, visible policy doctrine (the 1855 memorandum) crystallised out of the independent, distributed careers of six surgeon-naturalists rather than from any single architect — rhymes with the history-machine idea of a paradigm assembling silently from independent components before it "lurches" into visibility, though Grove's agents are a small named network rather than a fully anonymous distributed process. The unit's explicit invocation of "Humboldtian thinking" among the surgeons' influences is a direct, unforced link to alexander-von-humboldt, and its repeated appeal to "Scottish Hippocratic" medicine and a "Scottish radical tradition" (Balfour) connects genuinely to scottish-enlightenment as the intellectual seedbed of this cohort of EIC medical naturalists.

Notable passages

The Hooker/Balfour network passage (c1) shows the concrete chain of persuasion — Hooker's St Helena and Ascension observations, enabled by Banks's meteorological programme, reinforced by Balfour's 1849 literature survey — that primed Dalhousie for desiccationist argument before he ever saw the Punjab himself.

Dalhousie's Punjab minute (c2) is his own first-hand observation of "the almost total absence of forest trees," the empirical trigger, in his words, for the "manifest and... increasing evil" that state arboriculture was meant to remedy.

Dalhousie's "heritage for posterity" passage (c3) is the clearest statement of the utilitarian-paternalist ethic behind the project — planting for a future its planners will not live to harvest.

The historiography-and-Napier passage (c4) carries Grove's central corrective claim (models, not just legitimation) directly into the primary evidence of Napier's 1843 discovery of the shikargahs' revenue value and his push for a dedicated forest establishment.

Stocks's 1852 report (c5) is the primary-source proof that colonial policy consciously reverted to the amirs' stricter enclosure methods, the evidentiary basis for Grove's claim that pre-colonial rule was tighter on small farmers than colonial rule initially was.

Brandis's testament (c6) closes the argument with an admission from inside the colonial forestry establishment itself, crediting the Sind amirs and Punjab rukh-holders with the forests' survival and conceding early British neglect by comparison.
