Crusoe and Rousseau: The Island as Utopian Refuge and Botanical Microcosm

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Grove traces how Robinson Crusoe's Calvinist solitary 'natural man' shaped Rousseau's literary cult of the island as Utopian refuge and botanical microcosm, a genealogy Grove himself connects to Pierre Poivre's real conservation politics on Mauritius.

Grove traces a literary-psychological genealogy running from Defoe's Robinson Crusoe through Rousseau's fiction, pedagogy, and late autobiographical writing (La Nouvelle Héloïse, Émile, the Confessions, the Reveries), arguing that Rousseau's obsessive attachment to Crusoe's island — read as a Calvinist 'natural man' cut loose from a corrupt, urban, money-bound society — supplied the imaginative template that later, in the hands of Poivre, Commerson and Bernardin de Saint-Pierre, would be enacted for real on Mauritius: the island reimagined not just as a refuge for the self but as a garden-microcosm in which nature could be observed, classified and protected outside the 'denatured' reach of civilization.

Themes

Calvinist isolation underwrites both Crusoe's and Rousseau's cult of the island. Grove reads Crusoe's solitude, via Ian Watt and Max Weber, as an expression of the 'historically unprecedented isolation' produced by Calvinist religious individualism, and extends the same diagnosis to Rousseau: a 'citizen of Geneva' bred in a city that saw itself as 'a New Jerusalem, a New Zion set amidst a corrupt Europe,' predisposed by that inheritance to identify with Crusoe's earthly Utopia and to keep experimenting, across his career, with 'natural and Utopian structures.'

Detachment and journey make the island a vantage point on society, replicated in miniature inside the island itself. Grove isolates two structural elements of the Crusoe story — the detachment of the outside observer (continuous with Defoe's travel writing) and the journey away from a corrupt city toward nature — and notes that Defoe reproduces the town/country split even within the island, giving Crusoe a 'town house' on the shore and a 'country bower' inland, 'symbolising an innate alienation of the urban, capitalistic, money-bound condition... from the country.'

Rousseau's fiction moves the Utopian topos from the ideal city to the tropical island. In La Nouvelle Héloïse, Saint-Preux travels from Geneva to a garden Utopia at Etange, then to the mountains and valleys of the Valais, and finally — because neither suffices — to the Pacific islands of Tinian and Juan Fernandez drawn from Anson's voyages (and, via Juan Fernandez, from Selkirk). Grove traces this to a real tension in Rousseau between Plato's urban Republic and More's rural Utopia, and argues it was specifically the 'wildness' of Anson's islands, which Rousseau wanted to reintroduce into 'the enclosed garden context of Etange,' that fed forward into 'the project of Commerson and his colleagues' — the literary Utopia becoming the template for a real colonial-botanical one. Resonates with jean-jacques-rousseau and, in its city-vs-island literary lineage, with thomas-more and more-utopia.

The island fuses with the botanical garden as a feminised, pre-lapsarian object of classification. Rousseau equates the island with 'the female part and... the return to woman' (a theme Grove says Commerson would later 'act out in reality'), and treats both island and garden as sites where nature can be known 'in their natural state, as they are before they have been cultivated and denatured... by the hand of man.' His own two months botanizing on the Ile de Saint-Pierre are quoted directly: 'taking the entire island as my botanical garden,' he ran through woods and meadows to examine plants in situ — a private, pre-institutional version of the colonial botanical-garden networks the book elsewhere treats as information infrastructure. Grove also notes Rousseau's remark that new discoveries from voyages like Bougainville's forced naturalists to 'attach themselves... seriously to method' to escape the classificatory 'labyrinth' — a small, personal ordering-of-nature that only faintly rhymes with Legibility, since here the impulse is aesthetic and self-directed rather than a state project. The town/country, cultivated/'denatured' opposition running through the whole unit resonates with nature-culture-dialectic; the garden-as-site-of-moral-knowledge resonates more loosely with the-english-garden-as-epistemic-object (Grove's garden is Swiss and French, not English, but plays the same epistemic role); and Crusoe-as-'natural man' rhymes with the themes of noble-savages-and-english-gardeners, though Crusoe is a shipwrecked European rather than an idealised indigene.

Rousseau's own island-erosion parable anticipates the book's central argument, and Grove explicitly bridges it to Poivre. In the Reveries, Rousseau describes the smaller of the two islands in the lake of Bienne as 'desert and fallow,' being destroyed by the 'transportations of earth... carried out incessantly in order to repair the damage which the storms do to the greater island,' concluding 'it is thus that the substance of the weak is always employed to the profit of the powerful.' Grove reads this as a literary anticipation of ecological damage as political metaphor, and draws the connecting line himself: it is 'precisely the phenomenon which had concerned Poivre,' except Poivre translated the same intuition into an explicit 'moral economy of the state,' an option Grove says 'was not available to Rousseau in view of his prior criticism of the state.' This is the unit's hinge sentence — literary and psychological material becomes explicitly instrumental to Grove's book-length argument about where real conservation politics came from.

Notable passages

The unit's spine mirrors its argument: Crusoe's Calvinist isolation (c1) licenses Rousseau's turn from an ideal city to an ideal island (c2, c6), which is then materialized as a garden-microcosm for classifying 'undenatured' nature (c3), whose fragility Rousseau himself renders as an erosion parable (c4) that Grove explicitly, and consequentially, attaches to Poivre's real environmental-administrative argument (c5) — the pivot from literary Romanticism to the colonial conservation politics that are this book's actual subject.
