Managing ‘Het Paradijs’: Dutch Attitudes to the Cape Environment, 1600–1770

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
The Dutch East India Company's Cape settlement produced some of the earliest colonial forest legislation and, through Andries Cleyer's and Maarten Huydecoper's botanical-garden network and Hendrik van Reede's 1685 reforms, an institutional and scientific model whose influence on later French and British colonial policy outlasted its own conservation regime, which broke down once the 1712 discovery of new forests ended the Cape's frontier scarcity.

Grove traces the Dutch East India Company's environmental management of the Cape of Good Hope from the earliest provisioning-base decisions of the 1630s–1650s through Hendrik van Reede's landmark 1685 reforms to the eventual breakdown of Cape forest conservation once frontier expansion opened new timber reserves after 1712, arguing that the Cape, alongside the sister VOC colonies of Ceylon and Java and the botanical-garden network radiating from it, is an early and largely overlooked site of colonial environmental legislation and scientific institution-building whose botanical-garden model went on to shape later French and British colonial conservation practice.

Themes

Timber scarcity, not aesthetic sentiment, drove the Cape's earliest conservation law. From the first years of settlement, reports from Jordain (1608), Downton (1610) and van Riebeeck's own journal describe wood as scarce near the fort; within five months of the Free Burghers' arrival van Riebeeck issued the July 1657 placaat and, more decisively, the October 1658 placaat forbidding felling in the forests outright and licensing only two ‘free woodcutters’ through whom everyone — VOC officials and Free Burghers alike — had to buy timber, with yellow-wood singled out for special protection as ‘the best wood for making planks, being scarcer than any other kind of wood in the Cape forests’. Grove frames this as among the earliest sustained colonial forest legislation of its kind.

Legibility, enclosure and racial boundary-making were the same act. The wild-almond and thorn hedges planted from 1659–60 to enclose Free Burgher smallholdings did triple duty: they conserved indigenous timber by substituting a living boundary for cut wood, they formalized the settlement's land grid, and they built ‘a highly symbolic hedge between the white settlement and the Hottentots’ — turning the colony into what Grove calls ‘an administrative island with a permanent timber shortage’. This is a clean instance of environmental control and social control executed by a single physical infrastructure.

Placaats accumulated without preventing deforestation. Despite a continuous run of proclamations restricting felling, licensing woodcutters, and imposing harsh penalties for damaging the Company Garden (two years in chains, twelve months' hard labour), ‘the demand for timber was still so great that van Riebeeck and his successors could not prevent the slopes of Table Mountain and the surrounding area from being denuded of timber’ — forcing the colony into timber imports (ebony from Mauritius, teak from Batavia and Malabar) within its first two decades. The regulatory apparatus was real; its failure to prevent the outcome it targeted shows the apparatus alone was not sufficient.

The Cape garden became an imperial medical and scientific node well before its French or British counterparts. After 1669, Andries Cleyer's push for medicinal-plant research and Maarten Huydecoper's informal, technically unauthorized network linking the Cape, Ceylon, Bengal, Coromandel and Mauritius turned the garden from a provisioning stop into an institution: the 1676 appointment of the medical botanist Jan Hendriksz de Beck ‘institutionalised the role of medical expertise and the place of natural history in the colony’, a step Grove dates as sixty years ahead of the equivalent French development on Mauritius and over a century ahead of the British in India. Isaac Lamotius's 1677 drawing of deforested Mauritius — ‘probably the earliest pictorial evidence we have of tropical deforestation in the Indian Ocean region’ — fed back into Cape awareness through this same network.

Van Reede transplanted a Malabar-forged sensibility onto the Cape, and it stuck. Returning to the Cape in 1685 after building the Hortus malabaricus, van Reede toured the colony's woods as a critic: he condemned wasteful felling of young trees as a policy that had ‘provided only for themselves and not at all for the future’, ordered systematic replanting of cleared land and the sparing of seed-tree blocks during felling, and reacted with real irritation to discovering that the plantation optimistically named ‘Het Paradijs’ had reverted to indigenous woodland — objecting less to the reversion itself than to VOC woodmen's habit of calling indigenous trees by European names, a practice that ‘conflicted with every principle that van Reede had learnt in his days in Malabar’. His prior immersion in Malayali and Ezhava plant knowledge is presented as the direct source of his insistence on reading the Cape's vegetation on its own terms rather than assimilating it to European taxonomy.

Conservation was a function of enclosure, and it collapsed once the frontier opened. Van der Stel was dismissed in 1696 for perceived neglect of arboriculture — an irony Grove underlines, since the dismissal came from ‘a colonial state structure now fully convinced of the value of state forest protection’ that owed that conviction to van der Stel's and van Reede's own work. The settlement's ‘psychologically insular status’, and the conservation regime it had sustained, ended once the 1712 survey of the Riviersonderend forests reported timber sufficient for ‘a hundred years' worth of consumption’: outmigration, not conservation, became the logical response to scarcity, and soil exhaustion and erosion complaints followed within a decade.

The Cape's institutional legacy outran its conservation record. Grove closes by contrasting the VOC's forest-management systems in the Cape, Ceylon (adapting Kandyan ‘chena’ village-headman practices) and Java (formal teak reserves from 1722, sustaining VOC shipbuilding empire-wide) — self-contained systems that did not directly transmit to other colonial powers — with the Cape botanical garden model, whose influence on later French and British colonial environmental policy he judges ‘out of all proportion to its small area’, comparable to St Helena's.

Notable passages

The 1657–58 placaats (c1) are the unit's clearest documentary anchor for ‘earliest colonial forest law’ claims. The hedge/boundary-making passage (c2) is the strongest single image of legibility and racial control fused in landscape form — pega-pega and almond hedges doing the work of both a forestry regulation and a colour line, resonant with Legibility and legibility-as-machine-core's account of the Modernity Machine rendering land, people and resources simultaneously readable and controllable; the resonance is with the mechanism, not with any claim Grove himself makes about a "modernity machine." The ‘in spite of all the laws’ passage (c3) is worth reading against legibility-as-precondition-not-cause: the Cape had placaats, licensing and penal enforcement — a fairly complete legibility stack for a small colony — and it still could not prevent denudation, the same descriptive-versus-causal gap that synthesis note draws out of much later Appleby and Braudel material, appearing here a century or more earlier in a colonial forest rather than a metropolitan economy. The Huydecoper/de Beck passage (c4) documents the Cape garden's precocious institutionalization as an information network spanning several VOC factories, which half-rhymes with modernity-machine's account of infrastructure built to scale control — though Grove's interest is conservationist and medical science, not fiscal extraction, so the analogy is about the networked, legibility-producing form rather than its purpose. The Het Paradijs passage (c5) is the unit's title-bearing moment: an ironic gap between a plantation's Edenic name and its reverted, indigenous reality, and the occasion for van Reede's Malabar-trained insistence that indigenous specimens be known and named as themselves. The closing passage (c6) states Grove's larger claim about the Cape garden's outsized downstream influence, framing why this unit matters to the book's argument beyond its own chronology.
