Soame Jenyns and the Desiccationist Politics of Forest Reservation in the Ceded Islands

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Soame Jenyns, a Cambridgeshire landowner and Lords Commissioner for Trade steeped in Newtonian plant science, the Great Chain of Being, and his family's fen-drainage and garden-improvement practices, channelled desiccation theory into the physiocratic survey-and-forest-reservation policy imposed on Tobago, St Vincent and the other Ceded Islands after 1763, a legible land-use regime that protected timber and rainfall while erasing the indigenous Caribs from both map and land.

Grove traces how desiccation theory — the Newtonian-physiological claim that felling trees dries out a climate and shrinks its rainfall — travelled from Stephen Hales and Duhamel du Monceau into British colonial statute through Soame Jenyns, a Cambridgeshire landowner, natural theologian and member of the Lords Commissioners for Trade, who used his standing in the Society of Arts to press the Commissioners toward a survey-and-forest-reserve land settlement for Tobago, St Vincent and the other islands ceded to Britain by the Peace of Paris in 1763. The resulting regime, modelled less on any Edenic sentiment than on the English estate-improvement and fen-drainage practices of Jenyns’s own family, protected timber and rainfall while leaving no place on the map, or the land, for the islands’ indigenous Caribs.

Themes

Jenyns as the conduit from Newtonian plant science to colonial statute. Stephen Hales — ‘probably the most distinguished Newtonian scientist in early-eighteenth-century Britain’ and a founder member of the Society of Arts — supplied the plant-physiological basis for desiccation theory but died in 1761 before its ‘practical implications’ reached policy. Grove identifies Jenyns, Hales’s friend and a fellow Commissioner for Trade, as the man who picked the theory up — via Duhamel du Monceau’s arboricultural writings, themselves a product of close French–British scientific exchange — and lobbied to ‘institutionalise’ it in the Ceded Islands settlement.

An estate-improver’s biography made empire legible as landscape. Jenyns’s father, Sir Roger Jenyns, had made the family fortune draining the fens alongside Cornelius Vermuyden, and Jenyns himself planted long avenues of limes at Bottisham Hall in imitation of his neighbour Lord Hardwicke’s grounds at Wimpole Hall. Grove reads this directly into the colonial project: for a man ‘schooled in ideas about the Great Chain of Being,’ improving and controlling the Ceded Islands was simply the fen-drainage-and-garden logic of the home estate scaled up to an empire — a resonance with WMP’s the-english-garden-as-epistemic-object, though here the garden-as-mastery template serves a slave-plantation economy rather than a purely aesthetic or philosophical project.

Barbados furnished the empirical warrant. Already ‘almost totally deforested’ by 1665, its soil eroding and its sugar boilers reduced to importing wood from England, Barbados stood in stark contrast to the untouched forests of Tobago — forests that had supplied Barbadian timber-cutters for a century and whose 1683 description by John Poyntz gave Defoe raw material for Robinson Crusoe. Grove uses the contrast to argue that Barbados’s visible ecological collapse, independent of any imported climatic theory, gave the desiccationist argument urgency and plausibility with the Commissioners.

Physiocratic control, not Utopian Edenism. Grove is explicit that the Ceded Islands commissioners, unlike Pierre Poivre on Mauritius, pursued no slave-emancipation agenda alongside their forest conservation — theirs was a ‘remarkably cautious,’ survey-driven physiocratic land policy for a slave-dependent plantation economy, not a Romantic recovery of paradise. Yet the institutional form still replicated Mauritius’s: the botanical garden founded at Kingstown, St Vincent, in 1765 was so closely linked in practice to Pamplemousses that Grove treats the two as ‘elements of a single institutional construct or network’ — evidence for the colonial-garden-as-information-infrastructure reading of the modernity-machine.

Legibility as dispossession. The survey that divided the Ceded Islands into plantation, town and forest-reserve lots made no provision for Carib land; on Tobago the Caribs were ‘left out of the map-making process altogether,’ and Grove treats that cartographic omission itself as historically decisive — within twenty years the Tobago Caribs ‘had disappeared as a separate population.’ This is a clean instance of WMP’s Legibility and legibility-as-machine-core: the same survey instrument that let the colonial state protect rainfall and timber also erased the population the map did not need to see, though Grove’s legibility here is climatic-territorial control rather than Scott’s fiscal-administrative case.

The noble savage dies where the Mascarenes had no one to kill it. Because Tobago, St Vincent and Dominica — unlike the uninhabited Mascarene Islands — held real Carib populations, some intermarried with escaped slaves, the tropical-Eden and noble-savage discourse curdled within thirty years into open war, with the ‘black Caribs’ becoming, in Grove’s words, ‘one of the most effective groups of organised military (and non-military) resisters ever encountered during British colonial rule.’ The resonance with noble-savages-and-english-gardeners is direct, though Grove’s interest is in a discourse’s violent collapse under colonial land pressure rather than in the philosophical genealogy of the trope itself.

A durable institution despite settler resistance. The forest reserves proclaimed in 1764 survived a Tobago Assembly law of 1776 that tried to curtail the Crown’s power to alienate land for reserve, repeated French occupations, and settler pressure to convert reserved land to sugar during a buoyant market — Grove notes the boundaries were ‘little altered during the next two centuries,’ underscoring how firmly the desiccationist land-use apparatus had taken root.

Notable passages

Jenyns’s role as the man who moved desiccation theory from dead science (Hales) into live statute anchors the unit’s opening argument (c1); the Hales–Duhamel du Monceau–Jenyns chain is the unit’s clearest statement of intellectual transmission.

The Barbados/Tobago contrast (c2) does double duty: it is Grove’s ecological evidence for urgency and, via Poyntz and Defoe, his literary-historical link between an untouched tropical forest and the European Eden/desert-island imagination that recurs across the book.

The Great Chain of Being passage (c3) is the psychological hinge of the unit — where Grove states outright that Jenyns’s colonial land policy was continuous with his own domestic estate-improvement practice.

The map-making passage (c4) is Grove’s most explicit statement, in this unit, of cartography as an instrument of exclusion: what a survey omits is as consequential as what it records.

The ‘noble savage died a violent metaphorical death’ passage (c5) is Grove’s summary judgment on how the Eden discourse could not survive contact with an actually-inhabited island, distinguishing the Ceded Islands sharply from Mauritius.

The St Vincent–Pamplemousses passage (c6) documents the transoceanic botanical-garden network the World Machines context flags as an information infrastructure, here operating even where, unlike Mauritius, the surrounding land was violently contested.
