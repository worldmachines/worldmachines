Early colonial expansion and the growing significance of island environments in the European mind, 1400-1660

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
European maritime expansion after 1400 fused classical ideas of a designed earth, Hippocratic environmental influence, and man as geographical agent into a new colonial-periphery awareness of deforestation's ecological costs, running from medieval German and Venetian forest law through Atlantic-island water crises to Columbus's claim that clearing forests reduces rainfall, and closing on the tropical island's emergence as a bounded, knowable microcosm.

This unit opens Grove's argument by tracing how the great wave of European maritime travel and settlement after 1400 produced a genuinely new way of perceiving the relationship between humans and nature, one built from classical intellectual inheritances but transformed by the specific shock of colonial encounter. It moves from a conceptual framework (three inherited ideas about man and nature) through concrete premodern European precedents of forest law and conservation failure (Germany, Venice) to the first Atlantic-island colonies (Canaries, Madeira, Azores), where deforestation-driven water crises and the rise of ship-borne surgeon-naturalists set the stage for Columbus's pioneering, explicit claim that clearing forests reduces rainfall. The unit closes by naming the tropical island itself as a new kind of object in the European mind: a bounded, mentally graspable microcosm that could be treated as knowable in a way continents could not.

Themes

A new dualism between metropole and periphery. Grove argues that the sheer distance of long maritime voyages and early colonial settlement split European environmental perception in two: attitudes at the temperate metropole and attitudes among those settled at the periphery diverged, and "the circumstances of colonial expansion increasingly exerted the more potent influence on European perceptions of the human relationship with the environment" than domestic metropolitan change did. This periphery-first thesis is the spine of the whole book.

Classical inheritances repurposed by empire. Grove organizes the unit around three ideas he says have "predominated in the West since classical times": the designed earth (mythology/theology), environmental influence on human character (Hippocratic "airs, waters and places"), and man as geographical agent (the everyday arts of cultivation, carpentry, timber-cutting). His key move is dating when the third idea matured: not in antiquity, but only once "professional natural historians and scientists began to appreciate the full impact of western economic penetration on colonial and largely tropical environments." This resonates, loosely, with nature-culture-dialectic — Grove is charting a shift in where agency over nature is located, though his frame is intellectual history of a specific colonial encounter rather than a general dialectic.

Premodern forest law as a rehearsal for colonial conservation. Long before any colonial forestry department, German territorial rulers were already legislating against deforestation: Henry VII's restatement of the 1289 Nuremberg forest ordinance, and orders under Henry VII and Albert I in the fourteenth century compelling "formerly forested lands" back into forest. Grove is explicit that "French and British colonial governments were able to draw upon the skills and practices that had developed as a part of this early transition in Germany" — legal and administrative technique migrating from medieval European forest management into colonial statecraft centuries later.

Venice as a failed prototype of state conservation. The unit's richest case study is Venice: deforestation of the mainland hinterland silting the lagoon, the Council of Ten ordering replanting along streams, and a 1470–92 policy to conserve oak for the Arsenale's shipbuilding. Grove is blunt that this state effort "failed almost entirely," contributing to Venice's commercial and military eclipse by the Dutch and British, and that "local peasant opposition to state conservation, and even active sabotage and incendiarism, also contributed to the decline of the city state." The episode is a striking instance of a state building the instruments of environmental legibility — ordinances, inspection, a dedicated conservation policy — without that legibility translating into control; it half-resonates with legibility-as-precondition-not-cause, though Grove's interest is economic and ecological decline rather than governmentality as such. The Mediterranean timber-and-trade dynamics here also sit close to fernand-braudel's longue-durée history of the same region, though Grove's lens is narrowly ecological where Braudel's is total history.

Slow, institutionally starved diffusion of environmental knowledge. Giuseppe Paulini's 1606 proto-geomorphological study of Venetian deforestation was ignored even by Venetian officials, and Grove states that this experience "did not diffuse into the limited pool of British environmental knowledge until as late as 1851, when Venetian forest-conservancy regulations were used as a precedent by Indian colonial conservation propagandists" — a two-and-a-half-century lag Grove attributes to weak institutional links between scientists before the eighteenth century. This is a quiet but important scaffolding claim for the book's larger arc toward the India-focused conservation lobby (Wallich, Gibson, Cleghorn) named in later chapters.

The Atlantic islands as the first colonial ecological laboratory. At the Canaries, Madeira, and Azores, rapid deforestation dried up perennial streams, forcing settlers toward Moorish-derived irrigation channels (levadas); the sugar industry (from Pedro de Vera's 1484 mill) accelerated forest clearance, and even explicit 1600 Tenerife forest-protection regulations failed to stop it. Alongside this, the tropical disease burden of long voyages raised the status of ship's surgeons into informed naturalist-observers (Dr Chanca on Columbus's second voyage) — an early, informal precursor to the East India Company surgeon-naturalists the source material names as central to the book's later India chapters.

Columbus and the origin of desiccation theory. Grove credits Columbus, via his son Ferdinand, with knowing "from experience" that clearing the forests of the Canaries, Madeira, and the Azores had reduced rain and mist, and with believing Caribbean forests themselves produced the region's afternoon rains — "the first documented post-classical instance in a colonial setting of a conscious connection being made between deforestation and a change in rainfall." This is the seed of the desiccation theory (Hales, Poivre, Commerson) that the book's later chapters develop into full climatic environmentalism and state forest policy.

The island as bounded, knowable microcosm. The unit ends by naming the perceptual precondition for everything that follows: an island was "knowable in terms of the ease with which the island could be mentally circumscribed," offering "fewer risks to colonists than did the continent of America, Africa or India," and so became an object of economic, literary, and mythical interest. This bounded-and-graspable quality resonates with Legibility and legibility-as-machine-core — though here it is a perceptual, pre-administrative legibility (an island is easy to hold in the mind) rather than the cadastral/statistical legibility those pages describe; it is the imaginative condition that later colonial administrative legibility (surveys, forest departments) would build on. The unit's whole arc — medieval German and Venetian forest law slowly assembling into instruments later inherited by colonial states — also sits at the edge of modernity-machine's Dawn-phase construction (1200–1600), though Grove tells this as environmental and intellectual history, not as machine theory.

Notable passages

  • The opening thesis (c1) names the two-part perceptual shift — growing awareness of human capacity to alter environments, and new symbolic weight attached to nature via the botanical garden and the island — that structures the whole book.
  • The account of the "third idea," man as geographical agent (c2), pins the moment Grove says it matured: only once natural historians confronted the ecological impact of "western economic penetration on colonial and largely tropical environments."
  • The Venice passage (c3) is Grove's paradigm case of an early state conservation effort — the Council of Ten's replanting order and 1470–92 oak policy — undone by economic pressure and "peasant opposition to state conservation, and even active sabotage and incendiarism."
  • The diffusion passage (c4) shows how slowly this Venetian precedent traveled: not until 1851, when it resurfaced as a citation for Indian colonial conservation propagandists.
  • The Columbus passage (c5) is the book's founding instance of desiccation theory: a colonial actor explicitly linking forest clearance to rainfall decline.
  • The closing passage (c6) names the tropical island's essential appeal to colonizers: a world small and bounded enough to be "mentally circumscribed," setting up the island-Eden material developed in later chapters.
