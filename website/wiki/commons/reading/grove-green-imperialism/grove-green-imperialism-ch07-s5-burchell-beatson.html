The environmentalism of William Burchell and Alexander Beatson

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
William Burchell's Romantic-naturalist diary of endemism, extinction anxiety, and deforestation on St Helena (1805-10), and Governor Alexander Beatson's subsequent Humboldt-influenced desiccationist tree-planting and rainfall-recording programme (1808-13), together turned the island into an evidentiary base that shaped Roxburgh, Wallich, Darwin, and Hooker.

William John Burchell, schoolteacher and 'Acting Botanist' on St Helena from 1805 to 1810, kept a diary that documents a fully developed consciousness of the island's floral endemism, an acute awareness that its rarest plants stood close to extinction, and a practical anxiety about deforestation's effects on flooding and soil erosion — framed throughout in the borrowed Romantic idiom of the sublime. Alexander Beatson, governor from 1808 to 1813, took the opposite temperament — utilitarian, authoritarian, and directly influenced by Humboldt and the physiocrats — into a concrete desiccationist policy: prize-incentivised tree planting, systematic rainfall recording, and irrigation works modelled on southern Indian tanks. Grove traces how the two men, who detested each other, together made St Helena's compact, already-degraded landscape into a portable dataset that fed forward through Roxburgh's 1816 flora, Webster's 1828 Narrative, Darwin's 1836 visit and the Origin of Species, and Joseph Hooker's 1839 and 1843 visits — eventually reaching India via Hooker's 1847 arrival with Lord Dalhousie.

Themes

  • Two personae of colonial environmental consciousness. Burchell is the romantic, aesthetically absorbed naturalist-diarist; Beatson the assertive, technocratic 'improver' with 'no immediate consequences' for Burchell's own on-island management but a direct, documented policy programme for Beatson's. Grove uses the pairing — 'diametrically opposed' outlooks that 'quarrelled violently' — to show two complementary tributaries feeding a single stream of environmentalist thought.
  • Romantic sublime as scientific instrument, not decoration. Burchell's language of the sublime, borrowed from Rousseau, Wordsworth, Coleridge and Southey, is not incidental to his natural history but generative of it — his 'consciously aesthetic approach to landscape' produces the sustained attention that yields 'a quite unprecedented awareness of endemism.' Grove goes further, following Bernard Smith, to argue the debt ran the other way too: Coleridge's own weather-and-sea imagery derived from his schoolboy tutelage under the Resolution's meteorologist William Wales, so literary Romanticism and colonial voyage science were mutually constitutive, not sequential.
  • Anthropomorphic empathy for the vegetable kingdom. Burchell explicitly parallels the 'degrading treatment' of enslaved people with the destruction of 'helpless vegetation,' mourning individual 'ancient gum trees' as irreplaceable losses felt 'with the feeling of a fellow creature.' Grove reads this as part of a wider structure of feeling in which new geological and evolutionary uncertainties about deep time and mortality were displaced onto anxiety for endangered species.
  • Desiccation theory operationalised into administration. Beatson's conviction, formed through reading Humboldt, 'that deforestation had caused rainfall changes' was not left as theory: he built a prize scheme for tree planting (adapted from the Society of Arts, 1756), instituted regular rainfall measurement (encouraged by Joseph Banks and modelled on Roxburgh's Madras precedent), and imported southern Indian irrigation-tank methods — an unusually direct chain from climatic hypothesis to colonial state technique.
  • St Helena as an evidentiary machine that outlived its moment. The island's bounded, already-ruined ecology becomes reusable data: Beatson's 1816 Tracts, Roxburgh's 1816 annotated flora list, and W. H. B. Webster's 1828 Narrative are each carried forward — literally, in Darwin's case, who 'carried the Tracts around the world with him on the Beagle' — into the construction of evolutionary and climatic theory, and later into Hooker's 1843 Admiralty report urging tree conservation on Ascension.
  • The limits of the moment. Grove is careful to note that neither Burchell's romantic naturalism nor Beatson's desiccationism moved the East India Company to comparable action in India — Wallich's own 1823 anxieties about timber depredation 'failed to call in aid any really credible scientific critique.' The St Helena programme succeeded locally under a receptive governor and Company but did not yet generalise; it would take Humboldt's wider reception to shift India.

Notable passages

The opening frame (c1) establishes Burchell's diary itself as the dated, documentable origin of an environmental consciousness — 'a fully developed consciousness of the degree of endemism,' extinction anxiety, and 'a practical anxiety about the effects of deforestation on flooding and soil erosion' — before Grove even turns to argument. c2 gives Burchell's own diary voice describing St Helena's soldiers and inhabitants who, 'barbarian-like,' cut down trees 'with a wanton waste,' the primary evidence underlying Grove's claims. c3 is Grove's own analytical passage connecting the era's new geological and evolutionary uncertainty about deep time to a 'corresponding instability to notions of man's place in the universe,' which he reads as displaced into Burchell's fear that 'the beauty of the endemic redwoods of the islands might fade unseen' — the clearest statement of Grove's thesis about Romanticism and extinction anxiety.

c4 shows desiccation theory converted into policy: Beatson's Humboldt-derived conviction about deforestation and rainfall becomes a prize scheme, a rainfall-recording regime, and irrigation works, closing with Beatson's own remark that 'philosophers of all ages' have hoped 'to discover by repeated observations some rules concerning variations of seasons and changes of weather.' c5 traces the downstream institutional effect: Hooker's 1843 report to the Admiralty crediting Beatson's plantations with 'doubling the fall of rain in St Helena within fifteen years,' the hinge between St Helena's local experiment and the wider Company/Admiralty conservation lobby that would reach India through Hooker and Dalhousie. c6 closes the unit's arc with Darwin's own reflection on St Helena as a 'little world, within itself,' paired with his Malthusian question about what would 'become of the little state of St Helena' once the freed population began to grow — evidence that the island's bounded, legible ecology shaped not only conservation policy but the conceptual vocabulary of natural selection itself.

Grove's chain here resonates with legibility-as-machine-core and Legibility: Beatson's rainfall ledgers and planting-prize incentives are a small, literal apparatus for rendering an island's climate and vegetation calculable and governable, well before any theory of the modernity-machine existed to name it — though Grove's frame is causal-historical (desiccation panic drove intervention), not Legibility's more general claim about state-building through measurement. The Humboldt filiation running through Beatson's rainfall theorising into Darwin's island biogeography lines up with alexander-von-humboldt and with wulf-invention-of-nature's account of a holistic, aesthetically charged natural science, though Wulf centres Humboldt's own travels while this unit traces his second-hand influence through a colonial administrator who never met him. Burchell's explicit borrowing of Wordsworth, Coleridge, Southey, and Rousseau's imagery of mountains and glens also touches jean-jacques-rousseau, and his anthropomorphic collapsing of tree-suffering into slave-suffering is a small, vivid instance of nature-culture-dialectic.
