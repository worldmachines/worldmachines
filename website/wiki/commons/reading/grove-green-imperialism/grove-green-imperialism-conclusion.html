Conclusion: The colonial state and the origins of western environmentalism

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Grove closes the book by arguing that modern environmentalism arose from the vulnerable colonial periphery — above all on islands like St Helena and Mauritius, and later in India — because an insecure colonial state proved far more persuadable to scientific risk-narratives (desiccation theory, species extinction, famine) than the metropole ever was, and that this environmentalist critique was consistently bound up with a parallel critique of slavery, famine policy, and colonial treatment of indigenous peoples.

Grove's closing chapter restates the book's thesis at its most general: modern environmentalism was not born in the European metropole but on the colonial periphery, above all on small tropical islands and then in India, because it was there that ecological degradation became visible, measurable and politically threatening fast enough to force an unusually insecure colonial state into conservationist action — action driven less by philosophy than by scientific elites who turned ecological risk into political leverage, and whose environmentalism was persistently entangled with critiques of slavery, famine policy and the treatment of colonized and indigenous peoples.

Themes

Periphery before metropole. Grove inverts the standard genealogy of environmentalism: it was the islands, not "better-known western environmentalists writing outside the colonial context (G. P. Marsh is a noteworthy case in point)," that produced real conservation policy, and "the significance of initiatives taken by local actors on the basis of local and indigenous knowledge cannot be overestimated." Metropolitan environmentalist writing is treated as marginal to actual state practice.

Islands as legible, bounded test cases. Small islands made ecological cause and effect observable within a human lifetime, which Grove treats as the real precondition for intervention: rates of ecological change mattered politically only once they could be "noticed, quantified and then worried about," and only "the 'scientist' could effectively do the latter." This is close to a claim about legibility as a precondition for state action rather than its cause — resonant with legibility-as-precondition-not-cause, though Grove's own vocabulary is about risk and evidence rather than legibility as such.

Institutions of colonial science. The colonial botanical garden (the Cape, Mauritius, St Vincent) is presented as the load-bearing institution: it "formed the basis for a new kind of learning, information collecting and networking," and more generally botanical gardens functioned "as symbolic texts, centres of calculation and repositories of information and expertise." This maps cleanly onto Legibility and legibility-as-machine-core — an information infrastructure that renders tropical nature calculable — and fits the brief's framing of the garden network as infrastructure of the modernity-machine; Grove's twist is that this same infrastructure could be turned against the extractive project it served, arming critics as much as administrators.

Desiccation theory as the scientific engine. The revival of the theory that deforestation reduces rainfall, "not least in the minds of Theophrastus and Christopher Columbus," is Grove's third pillar of ancien-régime conservationism. Its eighteenth-century credibility came from the Royal Society/Académie des Sciences connection and "the plant-physiological researches of Stephen Hales and the English Newtonians" — a genuine if narrow point of contact with isaac-newton's natural philosophy as applied science of plants and atmosphere. Later, Priestley and (explicitly named) alexander-von-humboldt gave desiccation theory the "authoritative application of global climatic and atmospheric theories" that let it travel to a subcontinental scale in India. Humboldt's role here is a single sentence in Grove, and any resonance with wulf-invention-of-nature's holistic, single-genius Humboldt is only partial: Wulf tells a biographical arc, while Grove folds Humboldt into an institutional and comparative account of scientific lobbying.

The colonial state as unusually persuadable. Grove calls it "a paradoxical kind of development" that environmentalist policy emerged earlier at the periphery than the centre, and that the colonial state "was so readily influenced by independent groups of scientists" whose motives were not the state's own — indeed "they were sometimes actively anti-colonial." The mechanism was risk, not conviction: scientists made the state's own survival, in the form of "death, disease, famine and economic ruin," credible enough to act on, particularly after shocks like the 1791–2 El Niño drought.

Risk, the unfamiliar, and the scientist as diviner. Invoking Mary Douglas and Shakespeare's Tempest, Grove treats the colonial scientist's authority as arising from the structural need to interpret risk in an unfamiliar environment and among an unfamiliar population at once — Prospero's own passage "from magician to natural scientist" standing in for the colonizer's need to convert unknown ecology and unknown people into governable knowledge.

Environmentalism twinned with social reform. Poivre, Bernardin de Saint-Pierre, Alexander Anderson, Colonel Kyd and Edward Balfour are catalogued as a consistent type: critics of deforestation and soil erosion who were "also" critics of slavery, Carib treatment, or famine-inducing revenue policy. Grove calls this a "duality" that recurs later at the metropole in figures like Octavia Hill, and treats the Scottish and Central European origins of many EIC scientists — socially peripheral to the imperial establishment — as reinforcing it.

Utopian islands and the literary imagination. From Godwin's Man in the Moone and Defoe's Robinson Crusoe to "the French cult of the Utopian South Sea island" fed by jean-jacques-rousseau, Commerson, Bougainville and Bernardin de Saint-Pierre, Grove treats literature and science as mutually shaping: expansion "opened up a vast new mental domain" continuous with the older Utopian lineage of Dante, Columbus, thomas-more (whose genre is named directly, cf. more-utopia), Shakespeare and Marvell.

From island extinction to Darwin. Grove draws a line from insular extinction-watchers (Burchell, Corneille, Anderson, Hooker, Strickland) to Darwin's 1859 theory of natural selection, treating the biological anxiety of island isolation as continuous with the existential anxiety of colonial ecological crisis.

Globalizing the critique. International institutions (the Royal Society/Académie des Sciences link, the British Association for the Advancement of Science in 1851, the Royal Geographical Society in 1865 and 1866) and shock events turned scattered island observations into "a sense of a global environmental crisis" that "no single colonial state could safely decide to ignore." This resonates, loosely, with Bayly's account of an increasingly networked, information-stitched eighteenth-/nineteenth-century world (c-a-bayly, archaic-globalization-bayly) — though Grove's convergence is narrower and more scientific-institutional than Bayly's broader social and economic connectivity, and the two should not be collapsed into the same claim.

Notable passages

The chapter's final paragraph is its thesis in miniature: colonial environmental policy arose from "highly structured tensions between colonial periphery and metropolitan centre," and modern environmentalism is "now clear" to be a direct response to colonial ecological destruction rather than a metropolitan invention (c1). The botanical-garden passage (c2) is the clearest statement of the institutional, information-network argument that carries the Legibility resonance. The desiccation-theory passage (c3) names the Royal Society/Newtonian lineage that gives climatic environmentalism its scientific credibility. The "paradoxical kind of development" passage (c4) is Grove's sharpest statement of the colonial state's susceptibility to scientist-lobbyists and of their sometimes anti-colonial motives. The Tempest passage (c5) supplies Grove's own figure — risk, the unfamiliar, the scientist as diviner — for why colonial science acquired this leverage. The St Helena/Godwin/Defoe/Rousseau passage (c6) anchors the Utopian-island literary lineage that runs underneath the whole book's argument about islands as Eden.
