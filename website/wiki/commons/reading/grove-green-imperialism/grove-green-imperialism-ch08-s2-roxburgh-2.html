Wallich, Sleeman and the Making of an Indian Forest Crisis, 1817–1840

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Nathaniel Wallich institutionalises Roxburgh's plantation work into Bengal's 1823 Plantation Committee and a ‘social forestry’ argument that fails to move government, while W. H. Sleeman's tree-planting and a decade of mounting deforestation reports from Bombay, Madras and the semi-independent Malabar country force the presidencies, by 1840, from timber-shortage economics toward a Humboldtian desiccationist science championed by a new generation of East India Company surgeon-naturalists, even as London's Court of Directors remains committed to protecting private property rights.

Nathaniel Wallich, superintendent of the Calcutta Botanic Garden from 1817, turns Roxburgh's plantation experiments into a formal 1823 Plantation Committee and an argument for indigenous-precedent ‘social forestry’ addressed to Bengal's bamboo and sissoo shortages, but his 1825–27 recommendation of state intervention to save the submontane forests of Oudh is ignored, and a declining East India Company faith in the economic value of botany leaves him, for years, “a conservationist voice crying in the wilderness.” In parallel, W. H. Sleeman's roadside tree-planting and his documentation of Company-caused deforestation in the Ganga–Jumna Doab, together with a decade of escalating reports from Bombay, Madras, and the still-indigenous-controlled Malabar country, push both presidencies toward a new institutional response: by 1840 assistant surgeon Alexander Gibson is formally appointed to investigate Western India's forests, heading a new generation of Company surgeon-naturalists who, drawing on Humboldt, reframe deforestation as a climatic and civilisational crisis rather than a timber-supply problem — a reframing London's Court of Directors has not yet caught up with.

Themes

  • Institutionalising plantation science. Wallich converts Roxburgh's ad hoc plantations into a standing 1823 Plantation Committee chaired by the governor-general, triggered partly by an 1819 Marine Board correspondence on the difficulty of shipping coir and timber from the west coast — the first sign that the colonial state, not individual botanists, would own forest policy.

  • ‘Social forestry’ on a weak evidentiary footing. Wallich frames the bamboo and sissoo shortage as a ‘formidable evil’ threatening ‘the lower orders of the natives of India’ and invokes the precedent of Indian rulers (‘the judicious Rajahs of Rampore and Rohilcund’) who protected forests for local needs — an appeal to indigenous conservation practice against the colonial state's own recent forest clearance. Grove notes, however, that Wallich stuck to timber-shortage arguments alone, lacking ‘any credible scientific basis or statistical data’ to challenge policy, which weakened his hand.

  • The Oudh recommendations and their failure. Wallich's 1825–27 surveys of Oudh, the Irrawaddy and the Salween turn what government intended as a routine audit into open advocacy for ‘extensive state intervention’ in forests ‘in every way deserving of being preserved for the exclusive use of government.’ The recommendation goes nowhere: ‘the time was not yet ripe.’

  • The Company's retreat from economic botany. After Hastings's departure the EIC loses confidence that scientific botany pays for itself; an 1830 Retrenchment Committee recommends cutting the Botanic Garden's funding, and it is the Hookers' scientific lobbying in Britain — not any economic argument — that keeps the Indian gardens alive through the early 1830s.

  • Wallich takes the case to Westminster. Informed by a Mauritius sojourn where he admired French ‘intensive land-use methods,’ Wallich presses an emergent ‘Indian forest crisis’ on House of Commons committees in 1831–32, warning that bamboo — ‘nothing less than the seacoal of England’ — faces exhaustion. The testimony is heard and ignored.

  • Sleeman's different route into conservation. Unlike the medical-service naturalists, Sleeman is drawn to tree-planting through ‘Oriental’ literary and religious sources — Shaikh Sadi's poetry, the Hindu sacredness of kalpa briksha — and through his unusual access, as a colonial official, to the North Indian countryside. His documentation of the Ganga–Jumna Doab is the unit's sharpest institutional indictment: he traces an initial Mughal-era deforestation but finds ‘the greatest damage’ done by Company revenue policy itself, which in practice prohibited villagers from maintaining mango groves. Grove treats this as prophetic of the soil erosion, salinisation and rural immiseration that followed, and as the seed of Dalhousie's later, more comprehensive forest policy.

  • Western India's institutional gridlock. Thomas Munro's dismantling of the early forest conservancies, and the colonial preference for private property rights over common rights, accelerates deforestation through the 1820s–30s in areas under direct Company control — while indigenously governed Travancore retains real conservancy. The Nilumbur rajah's 1830 warning to Bombay is endorsed by the Indian Navy Board but stalls for years in the ‘institutional sand’ of the Madras Revenue Board, which remains committed to protecting timber merchants' and landowners' property rights. A cascade of further reports (Clementson on Malabar, Blane on Canara, G. A. Smith on Rajahmundry, Harris for the Bombay Navy Board) repeats the same pattern through 1838–39: technical evidence of crisis accumulates while the Revenue Board stonewalls and the Navy Board, Military Board and Bombay government contest jurisdiction.

  • Famine as catalyst. The famines of 1837–9 turn technical forest disputes into a broader crisis of governing doctrine. J. E. Thomas argues in the Madras Journal of Literature and Science that ‘the followers of adam-smith’ misapplied his theories to Indian conditions of land tenure and climate, worsening scarcity at Nagpur into famine; Howitt's anti-colonial tract revives Raynal's account of the 1770 Bengal famine to indict laissez-faire administration as a cause of mass death. Forest, revenue and famine critiques converge on the same target.

  • The Humboldtian turn. By 1840 the appointment of Alexander Gibson (following the earlier informal precedent of Buchanan-Hamilton, Wallich and Helfer in Bengal) formalises a new role for Company surgeons as state forest experts. A denser scientific infrastructure — provincial societies and journals, and alexander-von-humboldt's network of Kew-trained surgeon-correspondents consolidated by William and then J. D. Hooker — diffuses a ‘Humboldtian analysis’ (via Priestley and Boussingault too) that redefines deforestation as a driver of climatic, not merely economic, catastrophe. Gibson, Hugh Cleghorn and Edward Balfour become its chief propagandists.

  • A widening presidency–metropole gap. By the early 1840s the Bombay and Madras governments are questioning the ‘sanctity’ of private property rights over forests, while the Court of Directors' February 1840 review still worries chiefly about avoiding ‘any infringement of the rights of or any inconvenience to . . . private persons’ and doubts a resurvey is even needed. The scientific and administrative apparatus for recognising the crisis had been substantially built by 1840; what it lacked, at the imperial centre, was the political will to act on what it showed — a gap this unit's own evidence, read against legibility-as-precondition-not-cause, illustrates directly: reports, surveys and surgeon-expertise accumulate as Legibility infrastructure for a decade before the Court of Directors' laissez-faire commitments even register the crisis they describe.

Notable passages

The opening passage on Wallich's Plantation Committee (c1) shows the colonial state formalising botanical-garden expertise into a standing administrative body, with a strategic (shipbuilding-timber) trigger and a paternalist ‘social forestry’ justification layered on top — Wallich's own framing, in Grove's narration.

The Oudh passage (c2) is Grove's clearest statement of Wallich's political failure: an audit exercise turned into advocacy for direct state control of Himalayan forests, defeated not by counter-argument but by institutional unreadiness — ‘the time was not yet ripe.’

The Commons-testimony passage (c3) catches Wallich translating Mauritius's French-style land management into a metropolitan political register, down to the vivid comparison of Indian bamboo to English ‘seacoal’; it is one of the few moments the Indian forest question reaches Westminster before 1840, and it still goes nowhere.

The Sleeman/Doab passage (c4) is the unit's most direct causal claim: not disorder or Mughal-era clearance but three and a half decades of Company revenue policy destroying the ordinary village mango grove, with consequences Grove calls, following Sleeman himself, ‘irreparable.’

The Nilumbur/institutional-sand passage (c5) is the clearest single image of the gridlock theme — sound advice from an indigenous ruler, endorsed by one arm of the colonial state, absorbed without trace into another. It resonates, with real qualification, with Legibility: the state can gather and transmit the relevant facts (rajah, Navy Board, revenue board correspondence) without that legibility translating into any capacity, or will, to act.

The closing passage (c6) states the unit's pivot to Humboldtian desiccationism directly, and sits beside the Court of Directors' 1840 property-rights caution to make the same point at the level of the whole apparatus: an increasingly sophisticated, scientifically grounded picture of ecological crisis had accumulated in the presidencies well before the metropolitan centre was prepared to act on it, a case that fits legibility-as-precondition-not-cause — though Grove's interest is in the sequence of institutional decision-making, not in legibility as a general theoretical category.
