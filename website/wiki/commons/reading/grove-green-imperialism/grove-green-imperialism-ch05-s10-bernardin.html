Bernardin de Saint-Pierre and the Philosophy of Island Nature

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Bernardin de Saint-Pierre turned Poivre's physiocratic conservation practice on Mauritius into a holistic, quasi-religious philosophy of ecological interdependence across four texts, denouncing colonial deforestation and extinction while giving the island a lasting Romantic and Orientalist literary form, even as the colonial state itself kept enforcing forest conservation after its founding figures had gone.

After Poivre's physiocratic conservation programme lost political backing in France after 1771, Bernardin de Saint-Pierre — engineer, Mauritius resident, and intimate of Rousseau — converted the practical ecological alarm of Poivre and Commerson into an explicitly philosophical and literary environmentalism, worked out across four texts (A Voyage to the Isle of France, the Studies of Nature, the Harmonies of Nature, and the novel Paul et Virginie) that reached, and outlasted, the narrower audience of state policy.

Themes

  • From colonial policy to philosophy of nature. Grove presents Saint-Pierre as the "philosophical offspring" of Poivre's conservation programme: his two nature-books are among "the earliest fully developed, fully argued and fully evidenced critiques of the European impact on tropical nature," and their insights on extinction and human impact (drawn partly from Commerson) later fed into Lamarck's and Cuvier's very different theorising — a direct line from colonial island observation to metropolitan natural science.

  • The island as motto and hydrological microcosm. Saint-Pierre declared the island his explicit "motto" and illustrated the Studies of Nature with a frontispiece of Samos showing a self-contained hydrological cycle — sun-driven evaporation, cloud, mountain precipitation, and river return — which he called the "harmonies" of nature. This is paired with a niche-interdependence vignette (squirrels in firs, beavers at birches) that Grove reads as anticipating modern ecological niche theory, with ecological harmony treated as direct evidence of divine order — an Enlightenment fusion of natural theology and proto-ecology.

  • A more radical critique than the physiocrats'. Where Poivre indicted mismanagement, Saint-Pierre indicts improvement itself: touring newly cleared land in 1770 he saw "monstrous trunks of trees over-turned by the axe and sometimes by gunpowder," and blamed agriculture, commerce, and capital together — "Pride and Avarice" — for reducing land to a state of "villainage," echoing Poivre's own 1767 language of land in "servitude." Empirical, unlettered ('savage') observation is elevated above the corrupted "repositories of Arts and Sciences."

  • Extinction as proof of universal ecological alliance. Citing a Canadian governor's report that cod vanished from a coast the year after its forests burned, Saint-Pierre generalises to the principle that "everything in Nature is in strict alliance" — destruction in one part of nature propagates consequences elsewhere. This interdependence argument, Grove notes, is the same logic Saint-Pierre uses to link deforestation, climate, and species loss.

  • Orientalism and the retreat from the West. Despairing of a ‘denatured’ West, Saint-Pierre looked to "the East" for restitution, an Orientalism Grove connects — speculatively — forward to Alexander von Humboldt's unity-of-nature philosophy (itself indebted to Schelling, Herder, Goethe, and William von Humboldt's Oriental interests) and sideways to his own Indian-set novel La chaumière indienne, with its early vegetarianism.

  • Fictionalising the island: Paul et Virginie. The novel fixed the island motif in Romantic culture, reconciling Crusoe-style isolation with an idyllic Edenic society and, per Grove, restoring a fictional "gender balance" that echoes the real unmasking of Jeanne Baret on the Bougainville voyage — connecting the environmentalist and literary strands of Saint-Pierre's project to the broader post-1768 remaking of desert-island narrative.

  • The colonial state outlives its philosophers. Saint-Pierre left Mauritius in 1770, Poivre resigned in 1772, Commerson died in 1773 — yet forest conservation as state practice continued: the 1769 Forest Ordinance, an 1777 Inspecteur des Forêts (Lartigue) policing abuses and enforcing tree-planting, and an 1781 Ministère de Marine memoir urging the governor to conserve woods for shipbuilding while also protecting them "to maintain a fresh atmosphere and to prevent the drying up of rivers and streams." Desiccation anxiety had become institutionally self-sustaining, independent of the individuals who first articulated it.

Notable passages

The opening passage frames the whole unit: physiocratic conservation's political defeat after 1771 did not end its influence but drove it into Saint-Pierre's "holistic and environmentalist theory of nature," expressed across the four named texts (the OCR here reads "not feast" for "not least," preserved as scanned).

The Samos frontispiece passage is Saint-Pierre's own account of why he chose that image: a hydrological cycle of vapour, wind, and mountain condensation standing for the "elementary harmonies peculiar to islands." This resonates, with real distance, with alexander-von-humboldt's later holistic physical geography and the tradition traced in wulf-invention-of-nature — Saint-Pierre's is theological where Humboldt's became a secular science of connection, but Grove draws the genealogical line himself.

The squirrels-and-beavers passage is Saint-Pierre's clearest statement of niche interdependence, concluding that "ecological interdependence could be directly equated with a quality of the divine" — a formulation that folds proto-ecology into natural theology; it also sits close to nature-culture-dialectic as a period statement of nature's internal order set against human "culture."

The rising-colony / villainage passage is Grove's clearest evidence that Saint-Pierre's critique went further than physiocratic reformism, condemning agriculture, commerce, and "Pride and Avarice" as a unit rather than proposing better management.

The cod-extinction passage is the unit's tightest ecological-causal claim: forest fire to coastal cooling to loss of a fish stock, concluding that "everything in Nature is in strict alliance" — an early articulation of ecosystem-level feedback used here to generalise the Mauritius extinctions into a universal law.

The 1781 Ministère de Marine passage shows the desiccationist argument fully absorbed into ordinary colonial administration — naval timber supply and climate/river protection cited in the same breath, evidence for the unit's argument (per Grove's broader thesis) that the colonial state proved more durably interventionist than any one reformer, a state capacity for environmental legibility that half-resonates with legibility-as-machine-core, though the goal here is climatic stability and timber supply rather than legibility for its own sake.

Saint-Pierre's closeness to jean-jacques-rousseau is named explicitly by Grove as shaping his intensified attachment to the island and to the 'robinsonnade' form, and Paul et Virginie's negotiation between isolated European man and "noble savage" society touches, loosely, on the themes gathered in noble-savages-and-english-gardeners — again a literary and Romantic resonance rather than an English-garden one, which Grove does not draw himself.
