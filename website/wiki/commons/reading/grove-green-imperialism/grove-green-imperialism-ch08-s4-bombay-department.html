The Bombay Forest Department and the Company's Conversion to Desiccation Theory

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
The 1847 founding of the Bombay Forest Department under Alexander Gibson marks the East India Company's institutional acceptance of Humboldtian desiccation theory as a global threat to the 'welfare of mankind', triggering an empire-wide information-gathering despatch that Madras, prodded by Gibson's and Blane's lobbying, answered with newspaper advertisements and official circulars, all against a backdrop of Irish and Indian famine and 1848 European political unrest.

Grove argues that the 1847 establishment of a permanent conservancy in the Bombay Presidency, with Alexander Gibson as its first conservator, was a genuine turning point in British colonial environmental policy — not because it addressed a timber shortage (the earlier 1805–23 conservancy had already done that) but because the East India Company's Court of Directors, in response to Gibson's lobbying, formally accepted a Humboldtian, desiccationist account of deforestation as a threat to climate, agriculture, and imperial stability across the whole of India and beyond. The chapter traces how this acceptance produced a concrete bureaucratic instrument — Despatch No. 21 of July 1847 — and how that despatch's demand for empire-wide evidence was answered with striking speed by the Madras government, itself the target of sustained lobbying by Gibson and his Canara ally Blane.

Themes

From a local timber problem to a global environmental risk. Grove is emphatic that the 1847 conservancy differs in kind from its 1805–23 predecessor: the earlier body was justified by fear of running out of wood, while Gibson's conservancy rested on "acceptance by the East India Company of a set of much wider and longer-term environmental considerations and risks," balancing short-term land revenue against long-term "sustainability in patterns of resource use" and social stability. Grove is careful to hedge that this was not the state adopting "a new ideology in any wholesale fashion" — the shift was pragmatic and political rather than doctrinal, a distinction he returns to when weighing how much genuine scientific conviction, as opposed to administrative risk-aversion, drove the Directors.

The Company as a reluctant but thorough convert to Humboldtian science. Despatch No. 21 explicitly frames the deforestation–climate link as having "a strong practical bearing on the welfare of mankind" and instructs subordinate governments to gather "extensive and accurate information." Grove stresses the irony that this came from "a traditionally conservative body" that had previously been hostile to Humboldt personally, yet now accepted the reputation of his South American research "unquestioningly." He reads the despatch as evidence that the Company had internalised a specifically global mode of environmental reasoning — comparing Penang, the Americas, St Helena, Mauritius, and India as instances of one phenomenon — which he calls "a direct inheritance from Humboldt."

Famine and revolution supplied the political context for scientific credibility. Grove links the Directors' sudden anxiety to the coincidence of the Irish famine, the 1837–9 famines in southern India, and the Chartist and early-syndicalist unrest that made 1847–8 feel dangerous across Europe and its colonies. The scientific argument about rainfall and agricultural failure gained force, in his account, because it resonated with an already-frightened metropolitan political class, not on the strength of evidence alone.

Despatches, circulars, and newspaper advertisements as instruments of colonial legibility. Once the Court of Directors demanded evidence, the government of India — "usually tardy" — moved immediately, and the Madras government answered by soliciting information through newspaper advertisements and circularising officials via the Revenue Board and military authorities. Grove notes this method of empire-wide solicited testimony was not new, having precedent in Patrick Russell's 1790s zoological surveys, situating the forest-climate inquiry within a longer administrative habit of manufacturing knowledge about colonial territory through standardised, replicable circulars. This resonates with Legibility and legibility-as-machine-core as machine-core operations of the modernity-machine: a state making a previously invisible ecological process (forest cover, rainfall) into a governable, reportable fact through circular letters and press notices — though Grove's interest is the specific institutional and scientific contingency of the moment, not a general theory of state capacity.

Personal networks, not top-down doctrine, moved policy. The despatch's mention of Azimghur traces to Butter's report on Oudh, itself shaped by Wallich; C. S. Logan's 1846 lecture on Malay deforestation reinforced the same conclusions independently; and Gibson's direct correspondence with Blane explains why Madras responded so readily. Grove's picture of policy formation here is a networked, semi-accidental convergence of individual scientific correspondents rather than a coherent research programme — a texture that complicates any reading of this as a fully rationalised bureaucratic "machine."

Notable passages

The opening paragraph states Grove's central claim directly: the conservancy mattered because it rested on "wider and longer-term environmental considerations," not a simple timber-depletion panic, while carefully noting the Bombay government's motive was an economic-political balance rather than full ideological conversion.

Sir George Arthur's 1848 statement — quoted verbatim — gives the administrator's own voice: securing "general resources of the state" and a "permanent supply of timber" must outweigh "an excessive profit at the outset," the clearest primary articulation of the new long-termism.

The extract from Despatch No. 21 itself is the Company's own instruction to gather evidence because the deforestation–climate question has "a strong practical bearing on the welfare of mankind" — Grove treats this vocabulary, coming from a conservative institutional body, as remarkable in itself.

Grove's own analytical summary — that the Directors now saw Indian deforestation as "part of a global phenomenon" inherited from Humboldt, so that "Deforestation could no longer be treated as a purely local problem amenable to local solutions" — is his thesis sentence for the unit and arguably for the desiccation-theory chapters as a whole.

The passage on 1847–8 political turbulence (Chartism, the Irish famine, memories of the 1837–9 Indian famines) supplies Grove's materialist counterweight to a purely intellectual-history account of why the desiccation argument suddenly carried such institutional weight.

Finally, the description of the Madras government's response — newspaper advertisements and circulars through the Revenue Board and military authorities — is the concrete bureaucratic residue of the whole episode: the moment abstract climatic anxiety became an administrative information-gathering exercise, with an explicit precedent in Patrick Russell's earlier surveys.
