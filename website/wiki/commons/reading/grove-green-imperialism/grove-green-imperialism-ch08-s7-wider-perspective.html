Setting the commencement of environmental protection in India in a wider perspective

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Grove situates India's 1847–65 turn to state forest conservation against Britain's slower, aesthetically- and ethically-motivated environmentalism, traces how colonial extinction anxiety and desiccation theory globalized through Darwin, the 1865–6 Royal Geographical Society debates and an 1858 proto-greenhouse paper, argues the colonial doctrine predated and shaped rather than derived from G. P. Marsh's Man and Nature, and closes with the Indian Forest Act of 1865 and an 1862 state letter that made desiccationism official Crown policy.

This closing section of the chapter steps back from the Bombay and Madras forest-conservation narrative to ask why comparable state action was so much slower and weaker in Britain itself, then traces how the colonial extinction-and-desiccation complex fed into Darwin, globalized through mid-1860s scientific debate, and was consolidated into Crown policy by 1865 — explicitly displacing G. P. Marsh from the originating role usually assigned to him.

Themes

Britain's environmental sensibility was real but differently shaped, and politically inert. By the 1860s metropolitan Britain had rural-landscape aesthetics (Clare, Southey, Gilpin, Raymond Williams's "green language", Peel's taste for "wild" landscape pictures) and an effective single-issue success in animal protection — but the latter succeeded because it was fused with Quaker-led anti-slavery and urban-reform campaigning, not because of a perceived ecological threat. Because Europe's own biota were not visibly collapsing the way India's were, landscape and extinction worries stayed the concern of "a largely ineffective minority"; Wordsworth's failed campaign against railway building in the Lake District stands as Grove's emblem of this powerlessness before the mid-1850s.

Colonial botanists reached extinction-consciousness decades before Europe, and fed it to Darwin. Saint-Pierre and Commerson on Mauritius, Alexander Anderson on St Vincent, Burchell on St Helena, and Kyd and Roxburgh in Bengal had all grasped the extinction phenomenon by around 1810 — a generation ahead of Charles Waterton's idiosyncratic private nature reserve in 1830s Britain. Grove credits this specifically colonial, island-honed awareness (above all from St Helena) with shaping Darwin's ideas about extinction and endemism during the Beagle voyage, and traces a parallel line through Ernst Dieffenbach (New Zealand, Chatham Islands, Mauritius) and Hugh Edwin Strickland, whose dodo paleontology led him to propose making the whole of New Zealand a nature reserve.

Extinction fear was a real but usually hidden motive; desiccation fear was the lever that actually moved governments. Grove is careful to separate the arguments conservation propagandists privately held from the ones that won policy. Species-loss anxiety "rarely made overt appearances" and almost never drove government decisions — the 1846 protection of Knysna Forest elephants in the Cape is treated as a rare, probably coincidental, exception. Likewise the anti-capital, anti-"development" hostility of surgeons like Hugh Cleghorn and Robert Wight (blaming European planters and railway building for Nilgiri deforestation and, later, for the famines of the 1870s) was rarely used directly in lobbying. What worked, repeatedly, was the threat of artificially induced drought and famine, reinforced after the 1860s by fears of urban firewood and railway-sleeper timber shortages.

Drought, not steady argument, is what actually triggered state intervention. Grove notes the pattern bluntly: Indian droughts of 1835–9, the early 1860s, and 1877–8 were each followed by new or renewed state forest-protection programmes, with the side effect of expanding state control over land and timber at the expense of customary rights — a control the state also wanted for reasons of taxation and pacifying "rebellious minority tribal groups." The same mechanism recurs in South Africa: John Croumbie Brown secured Cape conservation legislation only after the 1861–3 drought devastated settler agriculture.

By the mid-1860s the desiccation debate had become a genuinely international scientific argument. The 1865 Royal Geographical Society paper by James Fox Wilson on the "Progressing Desiccation" of the Orange River basin drew direct rebuttal from David Livingstone (who called rainfall decline a purely geophysical process) and competing explanations from Francis Galton (cheap European axes enabling deforestation) and Colonel George Balfour, who marshalled explicit evidence of colonial-state tree-protection laws in Trinidad, India, and Mauritius as both diagnosis and cure. J. Spotswood Wilson's 1858 BAAS paper on the "General and Gradual Desiccation of the Earth and Atmosphere" pushed the same discourse toward an early, non-canonical anticipation of greenhouse thinking — attributing climatic decline to shifting atmospheric oxygen/carbonic-acid ratios and forecasting, starkly, the eventual unsuitability of Earth for human life.

Grove explicitly decenters Marsh from the origin story of conservation. Man and Nature (1864) is usually credited as the fountainhead of North American conservation; Grove argues it instead reinforced an already fully formed Indian and Cape desiccationist consensus that predated it, built independently by Butter, Gibson, Balfour and Cleghorn from the late 1840s. Indian conservators such as N. A. Dalzell drew their inspiration from St Helena, Mauritius, and eighteenth-century French practice, rarely citing Marsh at all — and when the US Congress explored forest conservation in the 1870s, its adviser F. B. Hough held up Indian (alongside German and French) methods as the model to imitate. This is a historiographical correction as much as an environmental-history claim: priority runs periphery-to-center, not center-to-periphery.

The chapter closes by naming the institutional convergence that produced the 1865 Indian Forest Act: EIC Medical Service lobbying on climatic grounds, Joseph Hooker's advocacy, McClelland's economic case persuading Dalhousie, and Napier and Stocks repurposing the pre-colonial Talpur amirs' Sind shikargah system as a template for state forestry. An 1862 letter from the Secretary of State for India to the governor-general — quoted at length — explicitly endorses the doctrine that "individuals cannot be relied upon for due care in the management of forests" and that private capital must yield to public interest, which Grove reads as the moment the argument moved definitively "from its earlier location at the colonial periphery" to "centre stage in the imperial apparatus."

For World Machines, the desiccation doctrine's career here is a clean case of scientific claims converting into a colonial state's expanded capacity for Legibility and control — forest reservation justified by climatic catastrophe simultaneously served (and was known by contemporaries to serve) fiscal and political control over forest-dependent populations, resonant with legibility-as-machine-core though Grove frames it as an intentional double motive rather than a structural machine logic. The Marsh-decentering argument — a doctrine assembled from distributed colonial-medical and scientific actors (Butter, Gibson, Balfour, Cleghorn, Hooker, McClelland, Napier, Stocks) rather than a single credited originator — rhymes with the history-machine idea of a paradigm that assembles silently before lurching into visibility, against "great man" historiography; the resonance is partial since Grove's interest is priority and diffusion between empires, not Lohse's distributed-components model as such. The internationalizing of the debate across India, the Cape, the Caribbean, and the Royal Geographical Society by the 1860s also echoes Bayly's account of a converging, interconnected nineteenth-century world (c-a-bayly), though Grove's lens stays scientific-institutional rather than Bayly's full political-economic synthesis.

Notable passages

The opening comparison sets Britain's aestheticized, politically ineffective landscape concern against India's earlier, state-engaged one, anchored in the claim that European biota were "simply not perceived as being threatened." A second passage traces the direct line from colonial island-and-India botanists' extinction awareness to Darwin's Beagle-voyage thinking on St Helena. A third curates Colonel Balfour's Royal Geographical Society testimony citing Trinidad's anti-felling law, pre-colonial Indian well-and-tope planting, and Mauritius's tree-protection legislation as evidence for the desiccation thesis and its state remedy. A fourth preserves J. Spotswood Wilson's 1858 paper and its stark closing prophecy of humanity's eventual climatic extinction — the proto-greenhouse moment. A fifth carries Grove's direct argument that Marsh's Man and Nature reinforced rather than founded the colonial desiccationist consensus, which "long predated" American conservation. The sixth pairs Grove's framing of the 1862 secretary-of-state letter as the "appropriate summary" of the conservation lobby's success with the letter's own words rejecting reliance on private capital, and Grove's closing sentence on climatic environmentalism's move from periphery to imperial centre stage.
