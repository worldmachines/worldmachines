The Dutch template diffuses: French and British botanical networks in the eighteenth century

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
The Dutch Leiden-Cape-Malabar-Batavia botanical network, built on the Dutch–Ezhava alliance at Cochin, became the model that French and British colonial powers imitated in founding their own botanical gardens, naturalist postings, and global plant-knowledge networks over the following century, with the Cape's 1654 Company Garden and Dutch sensibilities toward indigenous knowledge producing the first developed European awareness of colonial ecological degradation.

Grove traces how the Dutch achievement at Cochin and Leiden — an alliance with Ezhava botanical knowledge, organised through a network running Leiden–the Cape–Malabar–Batavia — became both the intellectual base and the institutional template that French botany under Buffon and British botany under Banks copied in the eighteenth century. The chapter's argument is diffusionist: a single colonial-scientific method (in-situ naturalists systematically harvesting indigenous knowledge, organised through a garden network) radiates outward from the Dutch centre of calculation, and the resulting global inventory of plant and animal distribution becomes the epistemic precondition for the first colonial recognition of ecological damage — which appears at the Cape before it appears anywhere in the English Caribbean or St Helena.

Themes

The Leiden model as a portable institutional package. Grove shows the Leiden/Amsterdam garden complex was not just a source of specimens but a reproducible institutional form: Paris, Oxford, and Edinburgh's gardens and medical schools were "all founded on the Leiden model," and Chelsea Physic Garden was strengthened through direct Leiden contacts. He attributes Leiden's centrality partly to the University's unusual religious tolerance, which drew students of every confession ("Jewish, Roman Catholic, Anabaptist, Anglican, Calvinist, Lutheran, Quaker etc.") to study medicine there — a striking claim that a tolerant, low-war Dutch polity, not a nationally bounded scientific culture, produced Europe's most exportable botanical infrastructure.

Indigenous knowledge survives appropriation, but is redirected toward commerce. The van Reede method — employing professional naturalists in situ and instructing them to gather "as much indigenous information as possible" — was adopted by French and English as well as Dutch companies. Grove is careful to register the ambivalence here: Ezhava-level indigenous knowledge continued to flow into colonial floras and materia medica, but "frequently turned to more mercantile uses" — extraction and appropriation coexist with genuine epistemic debt.

The garden as an instrument, and a symbol, of environmental control. The 1654 Cape Town 'Company Garden' is presented as the first real colonial botanical garden, and Grove reads its collections as "an accurate analogue of the current state of botanical knowledge and endeavour, one that was symbolic of the degree of economic or colonised control which was being acquired over the global environment." He links this directly to a prior Dutch culture of engineered environmental control at home, and to a conceptual parallel between cataloguing the botanical world and cataloguing the human body (VOC surgeons held responsibility for both). This is one of the unit's clearest statements that comprehensive botanical inventory is a technology of control, not merely of curiosity.

State sponsorship turns natural knowledge into political leverage. The French crown's backing of the Jardin du Roi in the 1630s mattered, Grove argues, because it gave individual scientists a route "through the connection with government, to exercise their knowledge and interpretations of the dynamics of the natural world in political terms" — a mechanism (state-sponsored science converting into policy authority) that recurs later in the book with the East India Company surgeons. England's Royal Society, by contrast, issued its own traveller's instructions (1666, 1704) partly to compensate for weaker direct state interest.

Islands, and the Cape, as the first laboratories of visible ecological damage. Grove closes the unit by arguing that the global inventory of species was a precondition for noticing anthropogenic environmental change at all — rarity and value could only be assessed against global knowledge. He locates "the first well-developed awareness of ecological constraints" at the Dutch Cape colony, crediting both its botanical garden (with "paradisal antecedents") and a Dutch willingness to value indigenous/local medico-botanical knowledge. He pointedly contrasts this with the English on St Helena, who lacked "such reinforcement," setting up the comparison the book pursues with English colonial ecological blindness elsewhere.

Notable passages

The opening paragraph frames the whole unit's diffusion thesis and the ambivalent fate of indigenous knowledge; the Cape Town garden paragraph is the unit's most direct statement equating botanical inventory with colonial environmental control; the Jardin du Roi paragraph names the science-state feedback loop; the closing paragraph makes the causal claim that global botanical knowledge enabled recognition of ecological damage, with the Cape ahead of St Helena.

The Cape Town garden passage in particular resonates with Legibility and legibility-as-machine-core: Grove's own language — a garden as "an accurate analogue" of knowledge that is "symbolic of the degree of... control... being acquired over the global environment" — is close kin to the legibility thesis (comprehensive, standardised inventory as the precondition of state control), though Grove's frame is environmental-historical rather than a general theory of state power. The Leiden-to-Paris-to-Cape-to-Calcutta garden network also reads as an early information infrastructure of what World Machines calls the modernity-machine — a distributed apparatus for rendering the natural world legible and administrable well before industrial modernity. The paragraph on van Reede's method and the Ezhava debt gestures at nature-culture-dialectic, since indigenous epistemology is simultaneously respected as a knowledge source and subordinated to European commercial classification; Grove keeps this a live tension rather than resolving it.
