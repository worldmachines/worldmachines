Naval Timber, the Malabar Forests, and the Collapse of the First Company Conservancy, 1796–1823

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
War with France and the closure of American timber supply after 1776 redirected East India Company anxiety about naval and commercial timber onto the Malabar Coast, where political consolidation after Tipu Sultan's defeat let the Company survey, monopolise and staff a forest conservancy (1796–1805) that private mercantile lobbying and Sir Thomas Munro's free-market opposition then dismantled in 1823, clearing the way for unchecked deforestation.

This unit follows East India Company anxiety over naval and commercial timber supply as it shifts from Bengal to the Malabar Coast: the loss of American timber sources after 1776 and the long naval war with France raised the value of Indian teak just as the defeat of Tipu Sultan removed the political fragmentation that had previously limited access to west-coast forests, prompting a decade of Company survey, monopolisation and regulation (Maconochie's syndicate, the 1805 Forest Committee, the proclamation vesting royalty rights in the Company, and India's first Conservator of Forests) — only for that conservancy to be abolished in 1823 under private lobbying and Sir Thomas Munro's free-market opposition, opening the way to accelerated deforestation and a subsequent shift of environmental concern back to Bengal and William Roxburgh.

Themes

Political consolidation converts an inaccessible resource into an exploitable, then governable, one. Grove ties the acceleration of Malabar deforestation directly to a change in political structure rather than only to demand: previously, ‘political division and traditional controls had had the effect of cushioning Indian forests from the increasing pressures imposed by external markets and small private enterprises,’ but once India became, after Tipu Sultan's defeat, ‘a single political and colonial entity,’ it ‘became much more vulnerable to both externally and internally generated economic and ecological pressures.’ Parsi shipbuilding and banking enterprises (the Jamsedjee–Jardine Matheson collaboration, the Pestonji Brothers on the Godavery) are named as the internal commercial force exploiting the new access.

The Company manufactures legal visibility in order to seize a customary resource. The 1805 Forest Committee's proprietary-rights inquiry, the same year's proclamation vesting ‘in the East India Company itself all royalty rights once claimed by indigenous governments,’ and the appointment of Captain Watson as the first formalised ‘Conservator of Forests in India’ together constitute an administrative apparatus built specifically to render a previously plural, largely unpapered forest tenure into a single, state-administered category. Francis Wrede's memoir supplies the legal logic explicitly: royal title is presumed to pass with sovereignty, so ‘the onus probandi that any part of it is private property lies with the claimants,’ a burden of documentary proof indigenous customary use had never had reason to produce and, Grove notes, drily, one ‘none of them has been able to make out ... in a satisfactory manner nor probably ever will.’ This is a clean case of legibility-as-dispossession — resonant with Legibility and legibility-as-machine-core — though Grove's own frame here is legal-administrative history rather than a general theory of state sight.

Legibility built is not legibility kept. Despite the survey, the proclamation, and a conservator invested with ‘wide-ranging powers,’ the apparatus lasted under two decades: ‘after 1815 the conservationist position became more vulnerable, and the pioneering timber conservancies came to an end in 1823,’ abolished ‘largely as a result of the private views of Sir Thomas Munro and public pressure.’ The state had made the forests visible and, on paper, entirely its own property; what collapsed was not the administrative machinery but the political will to use it against an organised mercantile lobby. This fits legibility-as-precondition-not-cause closely: rendering a resource legible to the state is what makes later intervention possible, not what guarantees it will happen.

Free-market rhetoric against a depletable commons. Munro's 1823 case against regulation — that Malabar's forest-owners are ‘too proud traders not to cultivate teak,’ needing only ‘a free market’ and ‘good prices’ rather than restriction — is textbook laissez-faire confidence that supply will track demand, loosely in the spirit of adam-smith-style market self-correction, though Grove is explicit that Munro misapplies it: the argument assumed ‘that the forests were illimitable and that the dictates of capital and the market would encourage economy,’ a ‘romanticist view’ Grove treats as false on both counts when applied to an exhaustible natural stock rather than manufactures.

A Romantic administrator dismantles the regime he might have defended. Grove flags an explicit irony: the same Munro who ends the conservancy had, years earlier, described a near-Wordsworthian communion with the Mysore landscape in letters to his sister — and it is precisely that landscape's forests his 1823 decision, taken partly from an idealised, ‘highly inaccurate’ view of indigenous property rights, throws open to unregulated felling over 'the next two decades.'

Company surgeons and continental expertise as the survey network. As elsewhere in the book, the labour of forest survey and administration runs through Company medical officers (surgeon William Maconochie, surgeon Thomas Palmer as conservator 1810–13) and an imported continental specialist (Francis Wrede, ‘a gentleman from Germany long resident in Malabar’) — the same professional class the book elsewhere credits with diagnosing ecological crisis and lobbying for conservation (Wallich, Gibson, Cleghorn). The unit closes by pivoting the story back to Bengal and William Roxburgh's climate- and famine-driven interest in tree-planting, the thread the following unit is set to pick up.

Notable passages

The opening reasoning about political consolidation (c1) states Grove's causal claim plainly: unified colonial sovereignty, not merely demand, is what exposed the forests to exploitation. Wrede's own words on the depth of Company involvement (c2) show a company official narrating the transition from mere commercial interest to formal administrative crisis in his own voice. His legal reasoning about proprietary right and the onus probandi (c3) is the unit's sharpest primary-source evidence for how sovereignty claims were converted into forest monopoly. Munro's 1823 free-market argument (c4) is the counter-voice that ends the first conservancy, and is explicitly a target of Grove's own critique rather than an endorsed position. Munro's letter to his sister (c5) is included for the irony Grove draws out between the administrator's private aesthetic relationship to the Mysore landscape and his public dismantling of the regime that might have protected it — a passage that is a historical actor's voice, not Grove's argument, but one Grove frames and uses deliberately.
