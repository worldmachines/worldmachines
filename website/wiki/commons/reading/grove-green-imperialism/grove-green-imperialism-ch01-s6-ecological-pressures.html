New Ecological Pressures at Centre and Periphery: Setting the Stage for Early Conservationism

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Seventeenth-century naval and colonial timber shortages drove starkly divergent English (laissez-faire, colonial displacement, blocked by common rights) and French (Colbert's centralized 1669 Forest Ordinance) responses to deforestation, while small tropical islands and colonial peripheries such as Barbados suffered ecological collapse far faster and more visibly than the slow-building pressure at the European metropolitan core, establishing the comparative centre-periphery framework for the book's account of early colonial conservationism.

Grove opens his comparative argument by tracing how the seventeenth-century surge in joint-stock overseas trade and naval competition put new pressure on European timber supplies, producing sharply different English and French institutional responses, and then shows that this pressure was dwarfed by the far faster, more visible ecological collapse that colonization produced on small tropical islands and peripheral zones such as Barbados — a centre/periphery asymmetry he adapts from, and partly corrects, Wallerstein and Braudel's world-system model.

Themes

England's common-rights deadlock versus France's centralized ordinance. Naval rearmament against Holland and Cromwell's wars created urgent demand for shipbuilding timber in both countries, but England's Commonwealth and Restoration governments, wary of alienating the politically potent constituency of common-rights holders, let repeated conservation proposals (the 1674 Wood and Timber Preservation Bill, John Evelyn's Sylva) die in "the minefield of counter-claims between private property and common rights," while Colbert's 1669 Forest Ordinance — the product of eight years of survey work by twenty-one commissioners — imposed uniform state regulation over private forest exploitation "to have re-established order and discipline" for the sake of posterity. This is a clean instance of a state extending administrative Legibility over a natural resource where the rival polity could not: Colbert's ordinance is exactly the kind of centralizing, survey-based, rule-making apparatus that legibility-as-machine-core names, whereas England's outcome shows how strong local/customary claims can stall a legibility project even when the state's own navy is agitating for it.

Colonial displacement as England's escape valve. Rather than confront common rights at home, England exported its timber problem to the American colonies after 1690, a strategy that "provide[d] administrative and operational precedents for later colonial excursions into state land control" in North America and then Western India. Evelyn's Sylva, though precociously alert to a possible link between deforestation and climate, concluded that clearing Ireland and America had been beneficial, and imagined New England as a "Holy Land" whose "mentally unbounded and apparently inexhaustible" bounty could substitute for husbanding English woods — the Arcadian myth of the colony as limitless resource frontier licensing metropolitan carelessness. Evelyn draws on francis-bacon and Palissy for this scientific framing, but the ideological work the passage does is closer to the boundless-Eden trope Grove tracks throughout the book than to disinterested forest science.

The Dutch exception. Dutch colonial environmental policy was shaped less by timber anxiety than by an ongoing transition in medicine and natural history, expressed through the early colonial botanical garden and a serious engagement with indigenous botanical knowledge at Leiden, Amsterdam and the Cape — a different institutional route from the French and British one, and, Grove signals, a more consequential one for the later chapters on tropical botany.

The core centre-periphery thesis. Grove states his organizing claim directly: pressure on the European metropolitan environment (population growth, arable expansion, urban and naval timber demand) built up gradually between 1500 and 1800, but "ecological pressures at the colonial periphery were felt far more rapidly and catastrophically." This asymmetry of tempo, not merely of degree, is what makes the periphery — and especially small islands — diagnostically useful: effects that take centuries to become visible in Europe compress into decades or years on a bounded colonial site.

Revising Wallerstein: multiple centres, not one. Grove uses Wallerstein's world-system model to explain metropolitan pressure on peripheral environments but immediately qualifies it as Eurocentric and insufficient: India, Japan, China and Formosa show "relatively autonomous economic and ecological transformations" — population growth, proto-industrialisation, deforestation, and state responses — developing independently of Europe. The Tokugawa shogunate's forest and soil conservation programme, compared explicitly to Venice's 1470s conservationism, is offered as evidence that sophisticated state ecological control was not a European monopoly, and Grove links it to Japan's own insular geography: "the significance of the island situation in stimulating conservationist thinking was a common factor in most early conservation thinking." This multi-centred revision of world-system diffusionism rhymes with c-a-bayly's case against single-origin accounts of early modern globalisation, and specifically with the multi-centred picture in archaic-globalization-bayly — though Grove's interest is narrowly ecological/administrative where Bayly's is broader connective and cultural history, so the resonance is partial. For World Machines, the island material is the clearest statement in this unit of islands as bounded test beds where a system's feedback becomes legible fast, prior to any explicit theory of why.

Barbados as the paradigm of catastrophic acceleration. Small islands were preferred for plantations (easier defence, more wind, fewer slave-insurrection risks, high coastline-to-land ratio), which concentrated the sugar boom's ecological cost. Barbados went from first English colonization (1627) to near-total deforestation (1665) within a single generation; governors' reports documented soil losing two-thirds of its yield, landslides on cleared hillsides, and a scramble to import wood from New England and Tobago once local supply vanished. Legal countermeasures — an 1656 Act against illegal felling, parish ordinances rationing glebe-land wood — were "simply superseded by uncontrolled felling" once Portuguese-Jewish refugee expertise from Brazil and local capital drove the sugar revolution of the late 1640s. Later naturalists (Sloane 1707, Lyell in the 1820s, Moffat in the 1840s) kept citing Barbados as the exemplary warning of deforestation-driven decline, and Montserrat's 1702 ghaut-forest ordinance — protecting streamside vegetation because clearing it dried up the watercourses — is flagged as an early, still-marginal instance of the conservation logic the book will trace to full maturity later.

Cultural and medical drivers, and an emergent desiccation theory. Clearing was not only economic: English settlers carried an ideology that felled, tilled land was more beautiful and "improved," directly contradicting the island-as-Eden trope even as it operated alongside it; miasma theory held that forests exuded disease-causing vapours, so colonial governors (Antigua, 1677) ordered clearing explicitly "for more health for the English." Against this, Oviedo's (via Columbus) observation that clearing Hispaniola's forests reduced rainfall, and Evelyn's own ambivalent note that Barbados "every year it becomes more torrid," are the unit's earliest sightings of what will become full desiccation theory — evidence-based climatic anxiety competing with, and eventually displacing, the improvement and miasma ideologies.

St Helena as the coming contrast case. Grove closes by flagging St Helena (annexed from the Dutch in 1659) and the Cape as sites where deforestation triggered a comparatively "structured and interventionist" official response, unlike the Caribbean's laissez-faire drift — explained by the islands' small size and their strategic importance to East India Company shipping rather than by economic value of the land itself. The chapter's closing move ties this back to the book's Utopian/Edenic literary register (Francis Godwin's The Man in the Moone, set on St Helena) and forward to the tropical-botany and indigenous-knowledge material that follows.

Notable passages

The Braudel epigraph-quote (c1) supplies the deep-time frame Grove will spend the chapter complicating: a "biological Ancien Régime" shattered by human intrusion, which Grove immediately qualifies as starting earlier and more unevenly than Braudel's own dating implies. The Colbert ordinance passage (c2) is Grove's own framing prose plus the ordinance's own appeal to posterity — the clearest textual evidence for the France/England legibility contrast. The Evelyn passage (c3) captures the Arcadian-inexhaustibility logic that licensed English complacency about metropolitan deforestation. The short centre-periphery thesis statement (c4) is the unit's structural keystone — everything else in the unit either supports or complicates this one claim. The Japan/Tokugawa passage (c5) is curated because it is Grove's strongest evidence against a Eurocentric reading of early conservationism and his most explicit statement of the island-consciousness argument. The Barbados soil passage (c6), drawn from a governor's own report, is the primary empirical anchor for the chapter's claim that peripheral ecological damage was rapid, catastrophic, and officially documented in real time.
