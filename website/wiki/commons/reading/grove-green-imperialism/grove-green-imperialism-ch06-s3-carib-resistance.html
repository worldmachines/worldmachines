Resisting survey and fighting from the forest: the black Caribs of St Vincent

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Between 1764 and 1770 the black Caribs of St Vincent used legal counter-argument and armed forest guerrilla resistance to stall British cadastral survey and land allocation, forcing the colonial commissioners to concede communal woodland rights where Tobago's Caribs, unable to resist, simply lost their land and disappeared.

Grove contrasts the smooth, uncontested cadastral survey of Tobago under the 1764 Grenada Governorate proclamation with its near-total failure on St Vincent, where the black Carib population fought a five-year legal and military campaign against James Simpson's survey team and the settlement commissioners, permanently deforming the colonial land-allocation scheme and forcing an official retreat from private appropriation of the island's forested common lands.

Themes

Legibility resisted, not just delayed. The 1764 proclamation's survey-and-allotment machinery worked on Tobago because the indigenous population there was small and could not resist; on St Vincent the much larger, better-organized black Caribs "contrived to enforce their own alterations to the settlement and survey scheme," so that colonial land allocation had to be renegotiated on the ground rather than simply imposed from above. Grove is explicit that "indigenous meanings in the environment survived and even changed the colonial set of meanings, at least for some years."

Two rival theories of land right. Chief settlement commissioner William Young justified expropriation through a settled-agriculture theory of property: the Caribs had no "obligation to cultivate the earth" and thus "no right to appropriate more than they could cultivate," their forest holdings being merely land they "claim in common" and "make no use" of. Grove reads this as an important early instance of the ideological genealogy that would later underwrite the general colonial critique of shifting cultivation and pastoral nomadism — cultivation, not occupancy, as the test of legitimate sovereignty.

Carib legal sophistication, not naive resistance. Against Young's condescension, the black Caribs argued their claim in the coloniser's own legal idiom, invoking the "Base de Visle," a boundary line drawn in 1700 through the mediated intervention of the governor of Martinique in a dispute between black and red Caribs. Young dismissed this as belonging to "the short memories of a savage people"; Grove's framing prose corrects him directly — "The social memory of this legitimation was, of course, long rather than 'short', as Young mistakenly thought."

The forest as a weapon. From December 1768 the Caribs moved from legal stalling to direct action: burning surveyor Levi Porter's house and equipment, destroying newly built military barracks, forcing a British infantry company to retreat rather than risk engagement in the trees, and hunting down and burning cartographer John Byres's half-finished survey maps. The settlement commissioners themselves admitted the tactical logic: dense forest cover made the Caribs' settlements a place where "any access to them impracticable."

A colonial power divided against itself. The Commissioners for Trade in London wanted forest reserved and the Caribs removed to Bequia; the island's own settlers, fearing Martinican backing for the Caribs and worried more about their own safety than about ideological consistency, wrote to Lord Hillsborough arguing for protection over extermination and — pulling directly against the reserve policy — that "all efforts should be made to clear the forest." Racial ambiguity compounded the split: black Caribs' partial African ancestry denied them the more indulgent "noble savage" treatment sometimes extended elsewhere to indigenous peoples, even as their guerrilla discipline won grudging colonial respect.

A real if temporary indigenous victory. By December 1770 the commissioners' own report to Whitehall conceded there was no way to "prove any natural right or title" that could override Carib communal claims to woodland, explicitly naming this "a common right of nature"; all colonist purchases of Carib land were formally set aside. Grove is careful to mark this as provisional — the 1764 proclamation became, in his words, "a dead letter, at least until the Carib rebellion of 1791" — foreshadowing the harsher, forced 1790s removal to Bequia that this same land dispute had first floated as an option.

Notable passages

Young's rationale for expropriation turns on a cultivation test for sovereignty: Carib land was merely "occupied mostly in common," with cultivation confined to "small disturbed spots of provision ground... worked entirely by women," so the Caribs supposedly had no legitimate title to the woodlands they claimed. Grove's counter-narrative of Carib legal literacy follows immediately: their invocation of the Martinique-mediated "Base de Visle" line shows a durable, institutionally maintained claim, not the fleeting memory Young imputed to them. The Porter–Byres episode dramatizes how thoroughly the Caribs disrupted the survey apparatus itself — burning maps, not just resisting settlement — while the settlers' 1770 letter to Hillsborough shows the fracture inside colonial ranks between a reserve-minded metropolitan policy and a deforestation-minded, self-protective settler population. The commissioners' own December 1770 statement, admitting a "common right of nature" in the woods, is Grove's clinching evidence that the survey-and-allotment project was not just delayed but substantively defeated by Carib resistance, at least for a generation.

The episode is a striking case of a Legibility-imposing project — cadastral survey, private allotment, forest reservation as Crown land — failing to convert into actual control once it met organized, well-informed resistance; the outcome resonates with legibility-as-precondition-not-cause, since the survey (mapping, allotment plans) was necessary colonial infrastructure but plainly insufficient by itself to produce compliance without the coercive power to enforce it, which the Caribs' forest guerrilla tactics denied the settlement commissioners for years. Grove's own frame is historiographical rather than machine-theoretic, and the resonance should be read loosely: St Vincent's forest, here, is a site of contested legibility rather than of desiccation theory or scientific conservation, the more typical modernity-machine-adjacent material elsewhere in this book.
