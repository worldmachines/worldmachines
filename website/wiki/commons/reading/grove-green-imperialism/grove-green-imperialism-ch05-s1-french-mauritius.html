The economic basis of French Mauritius: from spice ambitions to sugar deforestation and early regulation (1722–1735)

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
French Mauritius's economy pivoted from failed spice-transplantation schemes to sugar-cane and timber exploitation between 1722 and 1735, and the resulting deforestation provoked the island's first ad hoc land, hunting and forest regulations decades before Pierre Poivre's climatic conservation programme.

Opening Chapter 5 with Pierre Poivre's climatic conservation programme of 1767–72, Grove then backs up to lay the economic and institutional groundwork: French Mauritius was colonised as a strategic and commercial move against the Dutch spice monopoly, not for settlement's own sake; when the spice-transplantation schemes failed, the colony turned to sugar cane and timber, and the resulting deforestation provoked a sequence of ad hoc land, game and tax regulations from the 1720s onward — regulations driven by a dawning sense of the island's finite resources rather than by the developed climatic theory Poivre would later marshal.

Themes

Poivre's environmentalism had heterodox, non-European roots, not just a Newtonian pedigree. Grove opens by crediting Poivre with successfully "marshalling" the climatic anti-deforestation arguments that Hales's Newtonian physics and Duhamel du Monceau's physiocratic circle had already made persuasive in French government circles — but he is emphatic that this borrowed apparatus was not the source of Poivre's own convictions. Poivre's actual views on deforestation, climate and man–nature relations, Grove argues, were "strongly affected by an acquaintance with the Indian, Zoroastrian and Chinese systems of knowledge" gathered during his career as a travelling naturalist — a heterodox heritage the chapter promises to trace, though this unit does not develop it further.

Mauritius was colonised for spice-trade rivalry with the Dutch, not for settlement. The island's two harbours and its position athwart French routes to India made it a strategic entrepôt against British and Dutch interests. Grove traces the Compagnie des Indes' repeated, largely abortive attempts from 1719 to smuggle clove and nutmeg plants out of the Dutch spice monopoly (fear of discovery at Pondicherry, La Rivière's aborted 1726 mission), and notes that the Directors' resulting 1723 blueprint for a spice-gathering expedition to the Moluccas was the same document that later "provide[d] Poivre with the blueprint he needed to plan his own series of spice expeditions in 1747–70" — tying company strategy directly to Poivre's later career.

Mauritius carried outsized cultural prestige in France. Renamed the ‘Isle de France’, the island attracted more socially eminent figures than Britain's or Holland's island colonies and became, Grove says, "at least as significant" to French Romantic literature as the later South Sea discoveries — Bernardin de Saint-Pierre's epigraph is itself evidence. This cultural centrality, Grove argues, shaped how the colony's environment was "perceived, evaluated and then managed."

Failure of the spice schemes redirected the economy to sugar cane and timber, accelerating deforestation. Sugar cane — already devastating West Indian forests — became the fallback crop, and naval/construction timber demand (intensified by the 1740s wars) drove rapid clearance on top of it.

Regulation preceded climatic theory by decades, driven by a perception of finite insular resources. The 1723 Concessions Regulations imposed a fifty-pace forest ‘cordon sanitaire’ around the island's circumference (echoing France's own 1669 Forest Ordinance), and the 1726 Game Law nearly banned all hunting and ordered most of the island's dogs killed. Grove reads the episode as diagnostic: "the perceptually strictly finite nature of the island’s resources, whether of land or game, helped to give rise to a mentality encouraging further regulation" — any "lingering ideas of limitless bounty" were gone almost from the start of colonisation. This is an early instance of what World Machines calls Legibility: converting a bounded, opaque natural endowment into countable, licensable, enforceable units (concessions, quotas, cordons) so the state can act on it — though Grove's own frame here is ecological anxiety and company self-interest, not an epistemic legibility project as such.

Ecological fragility was registered empirically before it was theorised. The Council of Government's 1730 report on soil quality — quoted at length — describes land that looks fertile under forest cover only because of a thin layer of "rotting of leaves and trees," and turns "reddish and fiery" (laterised) once cleared. Grove contrasts this sober, empirical account with the "ecstatic and unreal" earlier depictions of St Helena, marking Mauritius's colonial administrators as unusually clear-eyed, early.

State intervention could produce perverse ecological effects, and the Mauritius state was more nearly unconstrained than St Helena's. The 1730 rule forfeiting any concession not cleared within three years was meant to keep uncleared land under company control, but "actually had the effect of stimulating deforestation, although this does not appear to have been the official intention." Grove pointedly contrasts this with St Helena, where the East India Company faced planter agitation for common land; the Compagnie des Indes on Mauritius had no such check. This rhymes with the modernity-machine's picture of a colonial state as a comparatively unencumbered laboratory of control — a resonance Grove would likely resist as anachronistic, since his own argument is about administrative expedience and ecological ignorance rather than a deliberate ordering project.

Labourdonnais's governorship (from 1735) intensified extraction and produced the colony's first visible subsistence and extinction crises. Durable Mauritian timber was exported for shipbuilding using slave and ox labour; food shortages hit in 1735 and 1741, driven partly by rapid growth of the slave population; and Rodriguez's giant tortoises, imported as an emergency meat supply, were "hunted to extinction by the 1740s." Grove frames Labourdonnais's "early acquaintance with dearth" as the spur toward "improvement" — an anticipation, thirty years early, of the conservationist turn Poivre would formalise.

Notable passages

The chapter-opening framing (c1) stakes Grove's revisionist claim before any Mauritius detail is given: Poivre's conservation programme was not simply applied Newtonian science but had roots in non-European epistemologies Poivre absorbed as a travelling naturalist — a claim this unit sets up but does not yet substantiate.

The strategic-rationale passage (c2) establishes that Mauritius's value was geopolitical and commercial (harbours, a forward base against Britain and the Dutch) well before it was environmental, which matters for reading everything that follows as economically overdetermined.

The 1726 Game Law passage (c3) is the clearest statement in the unit of colonial regulation arising from a felt sense of insular finitude, prior to any climatic theory — the island itself, small and legible, forces the recognition that "limitless bounty" is already over.

The Council of Government's 1730 soil report (c4) is a primary-source voice Grove quotes to demonstrate that colonial administrators, not only later scientists, could observe ecological fragility (laterisation) directly and skeptically.

The 1730 land-forfeiture regulation (c5) shows Grove's characteristic move of tracing a policy's unintended ecological consequences, and using a cross-colony comparison (Mauritius vs St Helena) to isolate what made the Compagnie des Indes' authority distinctively unchecked.

The Labourdonnais/Rodriguez tortoise passage (c6) closes the unit on the sharpest image of loss — an entire population of tortoises "hunted to extinction" as a stopgap food source — tightly binding subsistence crisis, extraction and extinction just as the stage is set for Poivre's arrival three decades later.
