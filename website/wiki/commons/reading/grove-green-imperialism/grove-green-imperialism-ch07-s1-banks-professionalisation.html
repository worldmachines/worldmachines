Chapter 7 opening: Banks, the Cook voyages and the professionalisation of natural history

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Grove opens Chapter 7 by arguing that state environmentalism globalised after 1789 not through a single idea but through the professionalisation of colonial natural history, tracing a lineage from the Mauritian physiocrats through Cook's circumnavigations, Joseph Banks and Johann Reinhold Forster, to Alexander von Humboldt.

Grove opens his chapter on the globalisation of environmentalism (1768-1838) by arguing that after the confined, fast-acting island crises of Mauritius and the Caribbean, the same environmentalist critique had to be carried onto continental scales — India, Southern Africa — by a different mechanism: the professionalisation of the colonial naturalist. He traces this through the great circumnavigatory expeditions, above all Cook's, which shaped Johann Reinhold Forster and Sir Joseph Banks and, through them, set the terms for Alexander von Humboldt's later environmental world view.

Themes

Institutional diffusion took a half-century longer on continental scales than on islands. Grove's throughline (established in earlier chapters) is that severely bounded island environments forced the colonial state to act quickly and visibly on scientists' warnings. On continental landmasses like India and Southern Africa, no such forcing pressure existed between 1770 and 1840 despite comparably sharp ecological transition, so "the strength of any environmentalist message had to rely, instead, on the power of ideas and the institutional influence of scientists and their state patrons" rather than on immediate crisis. This is Grove's own analytic claim, not a quoted source, and it reframes the island case studies of prior chapters as a kind of accelerated proof-of-concept whose lessons had to be exported by other means once the terrain scaled up.

Banks inherited and adapted the physiocratic model of scientific patronage. Where the Mauritian physiocrats had state backing for a coherent policy programme, Banks — lacking comparable state machinery in Britain — used his own "enormous private means" to manufacture an equivalent, first succeeding at the Cape and then under the East India Company. Grove stresses that Banks's motives were more tangled than the physiocrats': genuinely academic goals wrapped in a commercial justification he was personally comfortable with, unlike the more Rousseauian French naturalists. This is Grove's causal argument for why British colonial science professionalised through a wealthy individual entrepreneur rather than a ministry.

The professional network Banks built was deliberately international, ranking training over nationality. Grove singles out as "the most outstanding characteristic" of Banks's network that it depended on Scottish and Central European (especially German) expertise rather than English naturalists — a choice with real consequences for how Indian and colonial ecological change would later be conceptualised, since it opened the field to Continental scientific traditions (and, elsewhere in the book, to Scottish-trained East India Company surgeon-naturalists) rather than a narrowly English one.

Linnaean classification is presented as a totalising, map-like ordering of nature, in direct lineage from the Renaissance Paradise garden. Grove quotes Linnaeus comparing his taxonomic "genera" to a map or chart, and Grenovius telling Linnaeus his "charts" should hang on every wall, before arguing that the whole project of a global classificatory system — shared by Linnaeus and, in Grove's account, endorsed by Rousseau — was an extension of the Renaissance botanical garden's ambition to reassemble all the world's flora in one place as a re-creation of Paradise. Naturalists in colonial employ are cast as the direct heirs of these Paradise botanists, and Grove folds in his earlier Rousseau material (the island in Lake Bienne as "a great botanic garden") to tie the classificatory and Edenic-island themes together.

The Cook voyages made natural history autonomous from mercantile interest and normalised social critique of colonialism. Grove argues the voyages, financed partly by Banks's private fortune, separated the naturalist's role from direct commercial sponsorship and created room for a "detached kind of social empiricism" — participants, including Cook himself, began doubting the effects Europeans were having on the peoples and environments they encountered. Cook's 1768 instructions (explicitly scientific, echoing Royal Society instructions of 1666 and 1704) and Samuel Johnson's public framing of the expedition's disinterested, non-mercantile, non-military ideal of curiosity are Grove's evidence for how contemporaries understood this new, ostensibly autonomous scientific persona.

Notable passages

The opening paragraph states Grove's organising claim for the whole chapter: institutional diffusion of environmentalism after the island phase "achieved this institutional diffusion through the wide development of professional science in the colonial context," with "no simple way to tell the story of the globalisation of an intellectual concept." The passage on the "half-century delay" makes explicit why continental colonies lagged islands institutionally — the colonial state there was never forced to confront confined-environment pressures the way island administrations were, so scientists and their state patrons had to carry the argument on persuasion alone, a resonance with Legibility and legibility-as-machine-core: islands functioned as bounded, fast-feedback test cases for a mode of state environmental control that then had to be argued for, rather than forced, at continental scale — though Grove's frame is ecological-institutional history, not machine theory. The Forster/Banks/Humboldt genealogy paragraph is the chapter's spine, naming alexander-von-humboldt directly as the figure whose "intellectual weight... then determined much of the character and pattern of development of state environmental discourse and policy in British India, Southern Africa and elsewhere." The Linnaeus map/chart passage is the sharpest point of resonance with modernity-machine's legibility core: an explicit contemporary comparison of taxonomic classification to cartography, framed by Grove as continuous with jean-jacques-rousseau's and Linnaeus's shared ambition for a total, wall-hangable ordering of all the world's organisms — nature rendered legible one genus at a time, descended from the Renaissance garden's Paradise-project. Finally, the Samuel Johnson quotation on the Endeavour's naturalists being "not intent like merchants only on the arts of commerce... but... vacant to the very objects of curiosity" is a period voice (not Grove's own) articulating the ideology of disinterested science that Grove treats as historically consequential rather than simply true.
