Forster and St Helena: From an Island Comparison to a Colonial Science Network

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Johann Reinhold Forster's 1775 comparison of St Helena's moist, vegetated highlands with barren, denuded Ascension Island produces an inductive vegetation-moisture feedback model that, carried forward through personal scientific networks to Humboldt and the Cape Colony naturalists and through Forster's own advocacy to Joseph Banks and the East India Company, seeds both desiccationist theory and the institution of professional colonial natural history in British India.

Grove traces how Johann Reinhold Forster, naturalist on Cook's Resolution voyage, moved in May 1775 from empirical observation of erosion and moisture on St Helena to a proto-desiccationist causal model at Ascension Island, and how that reasoning — filtered through Pierre Poivre's writings and reinforced by evidence of human destructiveness toward fragile island fauna — was transmitted forward through personal scientific networks to Alexander von Humboldt and Cape Colony naturalists, while Forster's own advocacy for professional, state-sponsored natural history helped install a permanent naturalist apparatus in British India via Joseph Banks and the East India Company.

Themes

Island comparison as the empirical engine of desiccation theory. Forster's St Helena journal entries (18–19 May 1775) note erosion gullies cut by rain and a hill-and-cloud microclimate sustaining shaded rivulets, but he cannot yet explain the island's agricultural underdevelopment despite that moisture. The puzzle resolves only when he reaches desiccated, barren Ascension Island days later: the contrast between the two islands — one vegetated and moist, one bare and dry — lets him infer, reasoning from Poivre, that vegetation cover itself retains and even attracts atmospheric moisture, builds soil, and perpetuates springs. Grove is explicit that Forster never extends this to the reverse inference (that removing vegetation would desiccate a place), but "all the elements for further reasoning on this basis were present" — the logical machinery of desiccationism is assembled before anyone states it as a general law.

Human destructive capacity as a parallel awareness. Alongside the vegetation-moisture reasoning, Forster's Ascension entries record wanton slaughter of turtles by a Bermuda sloop's crew, killing far more than they could use purely "for the wanton gratification of having their eggs." Grove reads this as part of the same "gradual transition" in Forster's thinking toward a "more critical awareness" of human impact on tropical environments — the moral register (cruelty, waste) and the ecological register (vegetation and climate) develop together rather than separately, and both generalize almost immediately: Forster runs the same restorative logic at New Caledonia and, retrospectively, imagines replanting a "poor, parched-up" Easter Island with yams, coconut and breadfruit to "cause more moisture by their shade."

Transmission as personal network, not just print. Grove is careful to trace how Forster's ideas travelled: not only through his published Observations and Poivre's Travels of a Philosopher, but through direct personal and institutional lineages — Forster's return to Halle's botanical garden in 1780, his collaboration with Kurt Sprengel, and Sprengel's subsequent mentorship of both Humboldt and (via a letter to Hooker) Ludwig Pappe in the Cape Colony. Forster's son Georg's personal contact with Humboldt is a second channel. The desiccationist thread that later drives the East India Company surgeons after 1837 and Pappe and John Croumbie Brown at the Cape after 1835 is, in Grove's account, genealogical as much as textual.

Forster's advocacy converts observation into institution. Beyond the science itself, Forster explicitly argued in Observations that India's flora were known only from "inaccurate, unfruitful and unscientific" old herbals and urged that "a new, accurate, and modern observer" be stationed there — a case made in both scientific and "ostensibly patriotic" terms. Banks, despite personal jealousy of Forster (who had displaced him as the Resolution's naturalist), acted on this logic: he recommended the East India Company prefer naturalists "trained in the universities of Northern Europe," which led directly to John Gerhard Koenig's 1778 appointment as EIC naturalist at Madras, and later to Nathaniel Wallich's appointment following the same German-trained model (though Koenig's immediate successor, Patrick Russell, was English, and it was Russell who pushed for a naturalist network reporting back to Kew).

A wider botanical-institutional web. The unit closes by widening the frame: Banks's 1771 meeting with Pierre Sonnerat (Poivre's nephew) at Cape Town connected him to Commerson's collecting and Poivre's Mauritius spice-tree transfers, feeding forward into Banks's own later plant-transfer schemes (the Bligh breadfruit voyage). The 1785 correspondence establishing Banks's standing with the Court of Directors coincides with the founding of the Asiatic Society of Bengal, which Grove frames — echoing Commerson's own observation about Mauritius — as solving a structural problem of empire: individual colonial officials' short tenures lose accumulated knowledge, while a standing scientific society gives it institutional continuity.

Notable passages

Forster's own field notes carry the ecological argument. His St Helena entry registers the hill-cloud-rivulet relationship first-hand, and his Ascension reflection extends it into an explicit (if untested) causal chain: furze, once established, would let "grass and other plants... grow between them, form a coat and of course gradually form a soil of Mould," which would in turn "attract the moisture of the air, and keep it within the soil" so that "springs in the soil... would soon increase their water and perpetuate their supplies." This is Forster's own inductive model of a positive vegetation-moisture-soil feedback loop, built from comparing two islands rather than derived from theory — resonant with Legibility's interest in how bounded, small-scale sites let a system's internal feedback become visible fast, though Forster is reasoning as a field naturalist, not building anything like an administrative apparatus.

The turtle-slaughter quotation, reported from "a New York vessel['s]" master, is Grove's evidence that Forster's ecological attentiveness had a moral counterpart: cruelty and waste toward island fauna register alongside soil and moisture as symptoms of the same fragile, easily-damaged island environment.

Grove's own framing paragraph on Forster's "long-term influence" is the hinge of the unit: it names Humboldt and Boussingault as inheritors of Forster's desiccationism, and the East India Company surgeons after 1837 and Pappe/Brown at the Cape after 1835 as its downstream policy-relevant users — a genealogy that overlaps with alexander-von-humboldt and the intellectual-formation story told in wulf-invention-of-nature, though Grove's interest is narrower and more archival (naming the specific personal links — Sprengel, Georg Forster — that carried the idea) rather than Humboldt's own synthesis.

Finally, Forster's Observations passage arguing for a professional naturalist in India — lamenting that the subcontinent "wants the labour of a new, accurate, and modern observer" and appealing to "patriotic" pride in an empire "so extensive" — is the clearest statement in the unit of science as an instrument for rendering a colonial territory knowable and administrable, a move that resonates with Legibility and, in its institutional afterlife (Koenig, Russell, Wallich, the Asiatic Society of Bengal, Banks's Kew-centered correspondence network), with the wider administrative-scientific web Grove's book associates with the modernity-machine's expanding colonial state — though Grove tells this as a story of scientific patronage and personal networks, not of machine theory.
