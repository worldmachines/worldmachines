Defoe, Rousseau and Commerson: The Island Motif and the Utopian Antecedents of Romantic Environmentalism

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Grove derives Poivre's Mauritius conservation programme not only from physiocracy but from a French Romantic cult of the tropical island as Utopian antecedent, tracing the desert-island genre from Thomas More, Godwin, Leguat and Defoe's Robinson Crusoe through Rousseau's philosophy to Philibert Commerson's 1769 popularisation and Bernardin de Saint-Pierre's fiction, and arguing that this Romantic environmentalism — distinct from but supportive of Poivre's rationalism — made the safeguarding of 'natural law' inseparable from the projection of an ideal society.

Having characterised Pierre Poivre's Mauritius conservation programme as an outcome of physiocracy filtered through Dutch, Indian and Chinese land-use influences, Grove turns to the less tangible but equally formative influence of a French Romantic cult of the tropical island — genealogically rooted in Utopian and ‘robinsonnade’ literature from Thomas More through Defoe to Rousseau — that shaped Philibert Commerson and Bernardin de Saint-Pierre's more frankly Romantic environmentalism on Mauritius and, in turn, buttressed Poivre's own policies.

Themes

Environmentalism, Grove argues, was born from a marriage of physiocracy and the French Utopian obsession with islands. He states the claim baldly: the mid-eighteenth-century French fascination with the tropical island as “the speculative and Utopian location” for discovering idyllic societies or constructing new ones was, together with physiocracy, an essential part of the Romantic reformism behind the French Revolution, and “a fully fledged” environmentalism (rather than “a rather utilitarian shadow of it”) could not have emerged without it. Commerson and Saint-Pierre's Romantic naturalism was “substantially different in character” from Poivre's more rationalised physiocracy, yet it “did serve to buttress his policies” — two distinct intellectual currents converging on the same island.

A two-century genealogy of the desert-island Utopia builds the ground Commerson and Saint-Pierre stand on. Grove traces the ‘robinsonnade’ tradition from thomas-more, Shakespeare and Marvell — More's own more-utopia being the template Grove has in mind for an island commonwealth built around dissent and social experiment — through Bishop Francis Godwin's The Man in the Moone (1638) — which set its castaways on St Helena and combined social dissent with an explicit awareness of resource limits — to Henry Neville's Isle of Pines (1668) and Denis Vairasse d'Alais's History of the Severambians, both of which used the island to experiment with gender and social hierarchy, and to François Leguat's Huguenot castaway ‘diary’, the first to explicitly decry extinction and landscape damage. The Alexander Selkirk/Juan Fernandez episode (via Richard Steele's 1713 account, praising the man who “confines his wants to natural necessities”) feeds directly into Defoe. This lineage is a small-scale instance of how a paradigm assembles from scattered, independent components before crystallising into doctrine: no single author invents the ‘island as bounded ecological and social laboratory’ idea; it accretes across a century of popular fiction before Rousseau and Commerson make it explicit.

Defoe is the hinge figure, and Grove reads him as an environmental writer as much as a novelist. Robinson Crusoe's physical setting was “closely modelled” on John Poyntz's 1683 real-estate promotional tract on Tobago, and Defoe's fascination extended beyond Crusoe's slow mastery of “natural processes” to the catastrophic: his pamphlet on the 1703 ‘Great Hurricane’ and, especially, ‘The Destruction of the Isle of St Vincent’ (written alongside Crusoe), which frames an indigenous society's extinction — “blown up out of the very sea with a dreadful force” — as a demonstration of human vulnerability to “the immediate hand of Nature, directed by Providence.” Grove draws a direct line from Crusoe's individual survival to this collective extinction narrative, and notes it was precisely this “retreat to the elemental” that drew Rousseau to Crusoe.

jean-jacques-rousseau is the absent but decisive influence, and Commerson is his practical translator. Grove pushes back against LaFreniere's characterisation of Rousseau as merely a ‘pre-environmentalist’, arguing Rousseau was more directly tied to an actual, practically-oriented conservation programme than previously recognised. Commerson did the work of translation: Bougainville's circumnavigation carried him “from Utopia to Eden” — from Otaheite to Mauritius — and his February 1769 letter to the Mercure converted Rousseau's “vague and book-learned perorations” on tropical islands into elite French fashion. This is a genuinely Rousseauian rhetorical move: the island stands in for Rousseau's state of nature, a ‘pure’ environment “untouched by the imprint of European hands,” resonating with the noble-savage rhetoric noble-savages-and-english-gardeners traces from the same Rousseau through Schiller and Goethe — though Kempf's Kulturkritik plays out in the domesticated English garden, where Grove's is projected onto a real, colonised tropical island whose ‘untouched’ status is itself a colonial fiction.

For Commerson and Saint-Pierre, safeguarding nature and imagining an ideal society become the same task. “For an island to be a Utopia,” Grove writes, “the ‘laws of nature’ had to be obeyed and human intervention and artifice minimised”; environmentalism (“the safeguarding and nurturing of the natural order”) thus became “a vital condition for sustaining a physical and social Utopia.” Grove reads this as a hidden agenda in Poivre too, whose climate preservation is dressed as obedience to ‘Providence’, but says Commerson and Saint-Pierre were “far more frank”: for them extinction of ‘natural’ peoples or organisms alike was simply an evil to be combated. The irony — that a supposedly untouched island is being remade into a European philosophical stage by the very colonial apparatus (Bougainville's voyage, Poivre's administration) that also endangers it — is the same paradox nature-culture-dialectic locates in Kulturkritik's noble savage: an ideal of unmediated nature that is itself a cultural, and here colonial, artefact.

Notable passages

The book's thesis statement (c1) puts the marriage of physiocracy and island-Utopianism in Grove's own voice, including his claim that without the Romantic cult of islands, environmentalism would have stayed “a rather utilitarian shadow.” The Godwin/St Helena passage (c2) anchors the genealogy at its earliest ecologically-explicit point, where survival on a real island already required rationing shared resources. The Destruction of St Vincent extract (c3) is Grove's clearest evidence that Defoe fused the Crusoe survival narrative with a documented, named colonial catastrophe and its indigenous victims. The Poyntz/Tobago passage (c4) grounds Crusoe's fiction in the ‘commoditisation’ of real colonial real estate, a theme Grove treats as continuous with, not opposed to, the novel's environmental attentiveness. The Rousseau/Commerson/LaFreniere passage (c5) is Grove's explicit historiographical intervention, crediting Commerson with turning Rousseau's tropical abstractions into a practical elite cult via the 1769 Mercure letter. The closing passage on natural law and Utopia (c6) states most concisely how, for Commerson and Saint-Pierre, ecological and social projects became a single undertaking.
