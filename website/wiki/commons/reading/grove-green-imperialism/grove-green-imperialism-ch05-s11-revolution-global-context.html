Wartime conservation law on Mauritius and the global afterlife of Poivre's environmentalism

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mauritius's colonial state kept legislating against ecological and industrial risk straight through the Revolution and the Napoleonic wars — bird and fish protection, indigo-effluent control, and Governor Decaen's 1804 river-bank and mountain-slope laws — while Poivre's desiccationist conservation model spread outward through Flinders, Fabre, Humboldt, Jefferson, and eventually the East India Company's post-1840 Indian forest schemes, prompting Grove to argue that early environmentalism itself emerged from contact between Western science, tropical ecological crisis, and non-European learning.

The unit covers two consecutive sections of Chapter 5. The first traces how Mauritius's colonial government, left largely to itself by revolutionary and Napoleonic-era Paris, kept issuing environmental legislation despite the disruption — bird-culling and fish-stock laws, an early industrial-pollution measure against indigo effluent, and Governor-General Decaen's sweeping 1804 rules protecting river-banks and mountain forests. The second follows how Pierre Poivre's conservation rationale, once formulated on Mauritius, diffused globally — to French forestry science, to Thomas Jefferson, and eventually to the East India Company's forest-conservation campaigns in India — and closes with Grove's own theoretical claim about where modern environmentalism actually came from.

Themes

Legislating risk through wartime disruption. Even as the Revolution's onset and a British blockade encouraged coffee, tea and forest clearance to continue, the increasingly autonomous Colonial Assembly kept legislating against ecological risk: an 1803 arrêté capping concessions at two-thirds of a mountain slope, an 1792 bird-control law sparing named endemic species ('martins, messanges, merles, tourterelles') while bounty-hunting grain-eating birds, and an 1798 fish-stock measure. Grove reads the 1792 bird law as a precocious, if imperfectly evidenced, exercise in selective biological control tied to an emerging awareness of endemism — legislative reflexes inherited from Poivre's precedent and redeployed reflexively whenever a new risk appeared.

Industrial pollution as a new kind of risk. The indigo industry's poisonous effluent, draining into rivers and irrigation canals, produced 'an early form of industrial pollution' that the Moka municipal council pushed the Colonial Assembly to regulate in 1791 — decades before comparable pollution went unrestricted in Europe. Grove attributes this responsiveness to the island's inherited legislative culture and its acute, visible dependence on a limited water supply.

Decaen's 1804 laws as institutional culmination. Governor-General Decaen's arrêté (14 Vendémiaire An XIII) reinforced Poivre's 1769 Réglement économique with new enforcement machinery: a 'conservateur des eaux et forêts', sacrosanct 120-foot river-bank strips, formal reservation of the upper two-thirds of all mountain slopes (a step even Poivre had hesitated to take), and a corps of white 'gardes' and free black 'chasseurs' des eaux et forêts. Grove is careful to separate this domestic, desiccation-driven conservation agenda — aimed at soil, crops and water — from Paris's parallel but distinct military 'Réserves de la République', coastal strips reserved for troop movement; after the republican representatives were expelled (largely over slavery), colonial domestic priorities won out over metropolitan naval-security ones.

Knowledge outlives the disruption that produced it. Matthew Flinders's captivity diary, Alexander Beatson's 1794 East India Company intelligence report, and Fabre's 1797 treatise on torrents each carried Poivrean desiccation ideas outward even as revolutionary France let its own metropolitan forests be stripped and lost administrative memory of the very conservation practice its colony had pioneered — an irony Grove notes explicitly.

Global diffusion of the Poivre model. Thomas Jefferson, through his friendship with Poivre's son-in-law Dupont de Nemours, absorbed Poivre's physiocratic conservationism into his own agricultural experiments (contour ploughing, plant introduction). Anglo-French naval rivalry — sharpened by the loss of American timber supplies after 1776 — redirected British attention to Cape and Indian teak, seeding later concern over Indian deforestation. When Britain took Mauritius in 1810 its colonists initially misread and dismantled the forest reserves, then (per William Chambers's account) watched streams shrink and soil fail before reforesting — a sequence Grove presents as a direct model, via Bernardin de Saint-Pierre's writings and the Mauritius precedent, for Edward Green Balfour's post-1840 East India Company forest-conservation schemes.

Grove's own synthesis: where environmentalism came from. The unit closes with Grove's thesis in his own analytical voice: early environmentalism was not Newtonian reductionism suppressing a Rousseauist harmony with nature, but their fusion — Stephen Hales's Newtonian science let risk be reinterpreted in measurable, state-usable terms, while Rousseauist and physiocratic language supplied the reformist framing. Two linked anxieties drove it: about the moral state of Western (especially French) society, and about humanity's capacity to destroy nature and climate. Poivre himself, Grove argues, embodied the convergence — versed in Chinese, Indian and Dutch environmental management, physiocratic conviction, and the island as Utopian social metaphor.

This is a strong instance of a colonial state building legislative and enforcement apparatus — bird bounties, river-bank easements, a forest-guard corps — to make ecological risk visible and governable; it resonates with Legibility and legibility-as-machine-core, though Grove's interest is the desiccation science that justified the apparatus, not legibility as such. The transmission chain (Flinders's diary, Beatson's intelligence report, Fabre's treatise, Humboldt and Boussingault, Jefferson's correspondence, Balfour's Indian schemes) reads as an early information network diffusing a governance model across empires well before 1815, which rhymes loosely with archaic-globalization-bayly and with the colonial-state-as-information-infrastructure motif associated with the modernity-machine — though Grove frames it as an intellectual and administrative diffusion, not an integrated machine. alexander-von-humboldt appears only in passing here, as a conduit for Fabre's forestry theories into India, a much thinner engagement than in wulf-invention-of-nature. Grove's explicit invocation of 'Rousseauist language of nature' and 'Newtonian insights' also connects directly to jean-jacques-rousseau and isaac-newton, though Grove's point is that these frames turned out to be allies rather than opposites in shaping colonial conservation.

Notable passages

The 1792 bird-protection arrêté (naming the birds to be spared, 'martins, messanges, merles, tourterelles') shows the legislative apparatus at its most granular — a state trying to regulate ecological relationships species by species.

Decaen's 1804 rules are curated for their institutional ambition: a named conservation office, a fixed 120-foot river-bank easement, and mountain-slope reservation going further than Poivre himself dared, all of which 'encountered immediate opposition.'

Flinders's diary entry, made while climbing the hills above Port Louis in August 1805, is curated as first-hand evidence that the desiccation rationale behind the Decaen laws — 'the rains having been found to decrease in late years, owing as it is thought to the hills being stripped of their covering' — was legible to an outside observer, not just internal colonial doctrine.

William Chambers's account of British Mauritius's early clear-cutting and subsequent crisis ('it was noticed that the streams were shrinking... the earth, in short, ceased to be productive') is curated as the pivot passage linking Mauritius's experience directly to Grove's claim about Indian forest policy after the 1830s.

The Jefferson passage is curated for its striking, almost verbatim transmission of Poivre's own conservationist language ('the greatest service which can be rendered any country is to add a useful plant to its culture') into early American agricultural science.

The closing theoretical passage is curated because it is Grove's own argument, not a quoted source: his claim that Newtonian science and Rousseauist nature-language were not opposed but interdependent elements of a single new environmental discourse, driven by anxiety about both society and ecological survival.
