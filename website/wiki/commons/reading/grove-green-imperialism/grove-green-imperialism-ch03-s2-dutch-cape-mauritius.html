Dutch conservationism at the Cape and the plunder of Mauritius's ebony, 1599-1655

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Dutch colonial administrators built an unusually interventionist, garden- and forest-conscious construction of nature at the Cape—rooted in Hippocratic medicine and the Malabar forest experience of Hendrik van Reede—while the same Dutch mercantile system's earlier exploitation of Mauritius (1599-1655) stripped the island's ebony forests purely for the European luxury market, provoking alarm even from the Cape itself.

Grove opens with Sir Joseph Banks's 1771 verdict that the Dutch Cape, though naturally the poorer site, was managed far better than the English-run St Helena, and uses that outsider's comparison to introduce the Dutch state's unusually interventionist, symbolically freighted construction of colonial nature—one traceable through institutional history (Huydecoper, van der Stel) and above all through Hendrik van Reede's biography, whose formative encounter with the Malabar forests fed directly into Cape forest policy. The unit then pivots to the very different early history of Mauritius (1599-1655), where the same Dutch mercantile system ran a purely extractive ebony frontier, one that stripped the island's forests for the European luxury trade well before any Dutch conservation measures reached it, and that drew alarm even from the Cape administration to which Mauritius was formally a dependency.

Themes

  • An outsider's comparison frames Dutch exceptionalism. Banks's 1771 account, quoted at length, blames St Helena's decline on English colonial laziness and contrasts it with the Cape, which he judges would become 'a Paradise' under Dutch management even though the Cape's natural environment—an island of favourable rainfall surrounded by aridity—was in fact more climatically hazardous than St Helena's. Grove uses this rare outside testimony to establish that contemporaries themselves recognized a national difference in colonial land-management style, not merely a natural-endowment difference.

  • The colonial state as measure of its own prestige. Where the English East India Company showed no concern for ecological decline on St Helena, the government of the United Provinces took "a highly interventionist role," to the point that tree planting, forest protection and botanical-garden maintenance became, in the eyes of the metropolitan authorities, a direct index of the colonial state's effectiveness and prestige. This resonates with Legibility and legibility-as-machine-core—environmental order as a visible proxy for governmental competence—though Grove's frame is administrative prestige and mercantile risk management rather than Scott-style measurement-for-control, and the same state proves highly selective about where it exercises that legibility (see below).

  • The garden as ideological sign. Grove argues that Dutch environmental practice, at home and in the colonies, was organized around the garden as symbol: the new heraldic crest of Holland placed a lion above a walled garden, expressing a secularised paradisal notion of managed, safeguarded land that was then projected onto colonial landscapes. The botanical garden—Leiden, the 1682 Amsterdam Hortus Botanicus, the Cape garden, and eventually Pamplemousses—forms a linked institutional and informational network across the Dutch empire, which resonates with modernity-machine as an early information infrastructure, though here it is explicitly paradisal and prestige-driven rather than purely bureaucratic.

  • Indigenous Malabar knowledge as the intellectual foundation. Grove states plainly that the incorporation of indigenous constructions of nature 'underlay almost the whole structure' of the Dutch response to the tropical environment, mediated by a Hippocratic medical rationale for valuing forest cover and by the intellectual lineage running from Garcia da Orta and Clusius to Hendrik Adriaan van Reede—the network that also produced the Hortus Malabaricus.

  • Van Reede's biography as the conservation policy's hinge. The Cape's environmental turn is traced to a person, not just a policy: van Reede's own confession that the 'primaeval' forests of Malabar overwhelmed him emotionally is offered as the direct source of the forest policy he later imposed on the Cape, with 1682 (Amsterdam's Hortus Botanicus) and 1685 (van Reede's return to Malabar) marking the transition's decisive dates.

  • Mauritius as the mercantile exception. The chapter's second half shows the same Dutch system running the opposite policy. From first settlement in 1598, Mauritius was valued chiefly as a source of ebony and heavy timber; van der Stel alone shipped over a thousand logs in 1640, and the VOC deliberately raced to cut all exploitable ebony before French and English rivals could, producing observer alarm by 1644-45 (an observer's report and Frans van Beverwyk's report to the Lords XVII) and a further report on deforestation in 1654, with no effective response until a market glut and abandonment (1657-64) slowed the cutting. Grove closes the unit by noting that a prediction of the island's total ebony depletion within twenty years was voiced not on Mauritius but from the Cape, where the 'obvious profligacy' of a commerce serving the urban Dutch market was already causing disquiet in a timber-hungry sister colony—underscoring that Dutch interventionism at the Cape and Dutch extraction on Mauritius were two faces of one mercantile system, not a uniform conservation ethic.

Notable passages

Banks's block quotation (c1) is the unit's outside benchmark, contrasting English neglect with Dutch management and judging that the roles of Cape and island would reverse if the colonising nations swapped places. The passage on the Dutch heraldic crest (c2) is Grove's own claim that a secularised, garden-centred idea of paradise organized Dutch colonial environmental symbolism. The paragraph on the Malabar environment's significance (c3) is Grove's most direct statement that indigenous knowledge structured Dutch tropical environmentalism. The van Reede passage (c4) supplies the biographical mechanism connecting Malabar experience to Cape policy. The VOC ebony-cutting decision and its 1644 eyewitness quotation (c5) are the primary evidence that Mauritius's early management was, in Grove's words, 'the very opposite of conservationist.' Finally, the governor-general's prediction and the Cape's resentment (c6) show contemporaries already diagnosing unsustainable extraction as a foreseeable crisis, decades before any conservation measure reached the island.
