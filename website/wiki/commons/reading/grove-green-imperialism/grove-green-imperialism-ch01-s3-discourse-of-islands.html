New Worlds, Nature and the Discourse of Islands

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Europe projected Eden, Arcadia, and Golden Age fantasies onto newly discovered islands, a discourse whose most self-aware articulation is Shakespeare's The Tempest, and which travel chapbooks and poets then popularized into a stock vocabulary of perpetual spring and Promised Land that would later harden into scientific and colonial-administrative attention to tropical islands.

This unit traces the literary-cultural prehistory of the ‘island as Eden’ trope in Renaissance and early colonial Europe. It opens with Columbus’s Eden-driven voyage and the classical and Italian Renaissance roots of the island-as-paradise convention (Dante’s Purgatorio, the Canaries and Madeira as the ‘Fortunate Islands’), moves into an extended reading of Shakespeare’s The Tempest as the era’s most sophisticated dramatization of the contradiction between the island as Utopian Eden and the island as a site of real hardship, disease, and colonial dispossession, and closes by tracing the trope’s popularization through travel chapbooks (Brooksby’s account of Drake’s circumnavigation) and poets (Waller, Marvell, Cowley, Evelyn, Donne) into a stock vocabulary of perpetual spring, exotic fruit, and Promised Land that would persist into the eighteenth century.

Themes

The island as a screen for European fantasy. From the earliest decades after 1492, islands (real, imagined, or conflated with a barely-known American mainland) gave Renaissance Europe a bounded, geographically locatable stage on which to project Gardens of Eden, Arcadias, and Golden Ages. Grove treats Columbus’s own ‘obstinate conviction’ that an earthly paradise lay beyond the Atlantic as itself a motivating psychological force behind the voyages, not mere post-hoc rhetoric — while noting, and resisting, an alternative thesis that reads the islands as an eroticized, exploitable ‘Indies’ licensing violence.

Shakespeare's The Tempest as the discourse's most self-aware text. Grove reads the play — via the Gonzalo/Sebastian/Antonio/Adrian exchanges and Caliban's confrontation with Prospero — as dramatizing, rather than simply repeating, the tension between Edenic/Utopian projection and the empirical hardship (drought, disease, an unfamiliar climate) of actual island settlement. Caliban, a ‘noble savage’ figure drawing on Montaigne, embodies a holistic claim on the island's real, heterogeneous character (‘barren place, and fertile’) against the colonists' need to resolve it into either paradise or wasteland.

Knowledge as the instrument of dispossession. Prospero's arc from magician to empirical ‘natural scientist’, and Grove's observation that written knowledge would directly assist the colonist in gaining actual control of the land, makes control-through-knowing explicit — Caliban's wish to burn Prospero's books is read as the logical response to this power struggle. This is an early, literary anticipation of the classificatory, information-gathering apparatus that Grove's later chapters (botanical gardens, colonial surveys) show becoming institutionalized; here it is still magic-adjacent and dramatic rather than bureaucratic. legibility-as-machine-core names the general pattern of rendering a place knowable in order to make it governable, though Grove's Prospero is centuries and genres away from any actual colonial state apparatus — the resonance is with the logic, not the institution.

Rarity as a proto-scientific category. Gonzalo's unfinished remark on the island's ‘rarity’ is glossed by Grove as an early glimmer of the eighteenth-century empirical framework of classification against which rarity could be measured, one that would go on to drive botanical exploration — an explicit throughline from literary fascination with the singular and exotic to the taxonomic science Grove's book is ultimately about.

From high literature to popular chapbook and verse. The Eden-island trope did not stay confined to More and Shakespeare's exceptional, precocious treatments. Grove traces its diffusion through descriptive, often derivative travel writing into Restoration-era street literature (Brooksby's chapbook life of Drake, describing Fogo and its neighbour in the Cape Verdes) and into poetry (Waller, Marvell, Cowley, Evelyn), which fixed a stock vocabulary of perpetual spring, exotic fruit, and Promised Land onto real tropical islands — converging with Puritan and Dissenter pilgrimage imagery and, Grove notes, with the Indic elements of the iter ad Paradisum tradition (the Gangetic pilgrimage, Svarga-Loka) present in Donne and Milton.

Utopia as backdrop, not source. Grove is careful to place The Tempest a century after Thomas More's more-utopia and to show the play moving away from More's stereotyped ‘redemption of European political economy’ toward a more empirical register — the island genre inherits Utopian form while beginning to outgrow it. thomas-more's template of the island-as-political-thought-experiment is present here as inheritance, already being revised.

Notable passages

  • Columbus's Eden-conviction as a motive force behind the Atlantic crossing, set against the countervailing reading of the voyage as driven by an eroticized, exploitable ‘Indies’.
  • The Gonzalo–Sebastian–Antonio–Adrian exchange, in which the same island is by turns ‘everything advantageous to life’ and lacking ‘means to live’, ‘lush and lusty’ and ‘tawny’ — Grove's clearest textual evidence for the era's inability to hold the island's heterogeneous reality without resolving it into stereotype.
  • Prospero's books and the passage from magic to natural-historical control over Caliban and the land.
  • Gonzalo's ‘rarity’ aside as the seed of an eighteenth-century classificatory science.
  • Brooksby's 1683 chapbook description of Fogo and its neighbouring island, ‘always clad with green’, which Grove reads as the popularization of the Eden-garden image onto an actual, nameable tropical geography.
