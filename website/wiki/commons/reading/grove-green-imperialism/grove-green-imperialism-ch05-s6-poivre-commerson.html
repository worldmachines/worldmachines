Poivre and Commerson: State Science and the 1769 Mauritius Conservation Ordinance

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Pierre Poivre detaches the naturalist Philibert Commerson from the Bougainville expedition under a medicinal-survey pretext and uses Commerson's state-sponsored natural history survey of Mauritius, together with physiocratic theory, to win passage of the pioneering 1769 Réglement économique establishing mountain reserves, mandatory replanting, and river reserves.

When Philibert Commerson leaves the Bougainville expedition at Isle de France (Mauritius) in November 1768, Intendant Pierre Poivre absorbs him into the colonial administration under the diplomatically safe cover of a medicinal-plant survey, then deploys him as the scientific authority behind conservation policies Poivre had already conceived; the resulting Commerson–Cossigny survey of the island's woodlands supplies the documented evidence that lets Poivre push the pioneering 1769 Réglement économique — mountain reserves, compulsory replanting, river reserves — through opposition in Port Louis and Paris, grounding the legislation in both a Baconian ambition for total natural-history inventory and physiocratic economic theory.

Themes

A pretext that outgrows itself. Poivre and Bougainville justify Commerson's transfer to Isle de France as a survey of medicinal plants for hospital supply, a “cachet of authority and respectability” that outside observers would find credible — while Poivre's actual intentions extend far beyond medicine into a full natural-history census of the Mascarene islands, on a scale he implicitly likens to Joseph Banks's later work on the Endeavour voyage.

Science as legitimating apparatus, not cause. Poivre's ideas linking deforestation, desiccation, and soil erosion were “well developed long before the arrival of Commerson,” and he had “hesitated over such draconian attempts at land-use control” out of fear of opposition, despite ministerial backing from Praslin. Only once the Commerson–Cossigny survey delivers a “scientifically sanctioned and properly documented assessment” does he act — and Grove is explicit that the resulting controls were “guided by quite other motivations” than the stated fear of a timber shortage. The survey functions as evidence legitimating a policy Poivre already wanted rather than generating it — a colonial-administrative case where documentation is legibility-as-precondition-not-cause: the state's capacity to make the forest legible (surveyed, annotated, reported) is necessary machinery for the law to pass, but the will to regulate long precedes it.

Baconian inventory as instrument of colonial control. Commerson's brief — a “grand natural history or natural resource survey of the whole Mascarene island area” — turns the island itself into, in Grove's words, “one taxonomic laboratory.” Grove traces this explicitly to an “essentially Baconian tradition” running through Commerson's Montpellier/English-Huguenot scientific lineage and Colbert's 1669 Forest Ordinance. This is francis-bacon's programme of exhaustive natural inventory redeployed as Legibility over colonial land: the forest rendered visible, catalogued, and countable so that the-legible-commonwealth — Poivre's colonial state — can regulate it. Grove frames this as scientific patronage and personal moral vision, not machine theory, but the pattern (inventory precedes and enables control) is exact.

Physiocratic theory supplies the economic logic. The ordinance's core articles rest not only on empirical observation of tropical deforestation but on physiocratic doctrine — Cantillon's image of the state as “a tree with its roots in the land,” and a circular flow of wealth binding land, crops, and society, applied to an island where human ecological impact was unusually visible and fast-moving.

Conservation as moral economy, not just technocracy. Behind the legislation lies Poivre's vision of Mauritius as a new Garden of Eden and Jardin du Roi with a “climatically genuine ‘perpetual spring,’” an antidote to the “corrupt life of France” and its monopolists — Arcadian rhetoric married to the “severely practical” mechanisms of land regulation.

Notable passages

The Praslin correspondence shows Poissonier and Bougainville constructing the medicinal cover story after the fact, legitimating what was, as Poivre's own actions imply, a much broader and more personal scientific project. The account of Commerson's four years under Poivre's roof (via Francois Beau) captures “state patronage of natural science ... on the basis of the value of the study itself” rather than any immediate utility — yet within roughly a year that patronage produces binding law. The Réglement économique's Article 4 (mountain reserves), Article 5 (mandatory replanting), and Article 6 (river reserves) together form regulation Grove calls “quite distinctly different” from anything in the European tradition, addressing soil erosion “little known in Europe but highly characteristic of denuded tropical lands” — and Article 4's mountain reserves are explicitly the template later “formalised by Governor Decaen in 1804 and much later emulated by the British in India,” making this unit a hinge between the Mauritius conservation experiment and the Indian forestry state Grove traces in later chapters.
