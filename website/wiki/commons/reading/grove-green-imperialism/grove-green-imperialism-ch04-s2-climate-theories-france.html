The rise of French climate theory and Pierre Poivre's 1763 turn to conservation

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Enlightenment climatic-social theory in France—Montesquieu, Hume and Buffon's controversial degeneration thesis—fused during the 1750s with Newtonian plant-atmosphere science transmitted via Stephen Hales, and with earthquake-driven doubts about human mastery of nature, to produce Pierre Poivre's 1763 Lyons speech linking deforestation to rainfall decline, the direct trigger for French and British colonial forest-conservation policy.

Grove traces how a mechanistic, Newtonian science of vegetation-atmosphere relations (1696-1740) combined, in 1740s-60s France, with a boom in climatic social theory—Montesquieu, Hume, Buffon's controversial theory of racial and species 'degeneration'—and with an intense Anglo-French scientific exchange centred on Stephen Hales, to produce by the early 1760s a genuinely activist idea that humans could control climate by controlling forests. The chapter's climax is Pierre Poivre's 1763 Lyons speech, which Grove identifies as the precise hinge where this metropolitan theory met tropical colonial experience and became state forest-conservation policy, first on Mauritius and then, independently, in the British Caribbean.

Themes

  • Newtonian plant science preceded, but did not by itself cause, environmental critique. Between 1696 and 1740 English and French naturalists built a systematic, mechanistic account of vegetation-atmosphere relationships. Grove is explicit that this theoretical achievement, even carried by a figure as influential as Stephen Hales, "did not lead by itself to an environmental critique of human disruptions of the landscape"—something further was required before science became an environmental attitude. This resonates with isaac-newton and with the brief's desiccation-theory theme as an early instance of natural science awaiting a policy trigger.

  • Climatic social theory flourished in France partly as displaced political critique. Grove argues the "political atmosphere of mid-century France was far more conducive to the popularity of climatic theory than that in Britain," because "discussions about relations between climate and society became a surrogate for more direct criticisms of a corrupt and oppressive polity." The travel-account genre supplied raw comparative material, and the same climatic determinism appeared just as early among Scottish thinkers—Grove names david-hume's 1741 essay on climate, society and population as parallel to Montesquieu, tying the vogue to the scottish-enlightenment as much as to France.

  • Buffon turned climatic determinism into a theory of human and organic 'degeneration'. His 1749 Histoire naturelle argued a single human species had diverged under climate, diet and habit, and extended this logic to a broader theory of climate-driven bodily and moral degeneration in plants and animals—"elevating the study of climate and its causes to new heights," in Grove's words, even as the French continued to depend on English Newtonian science for its empirical grounding.

  • Hales's 1753 Académie appointment fused the English and French scientific establishments into one network. Grove characterises the resulting traffic between the Académie des Sciences and the London Society of Arts as, in effect, "a single intellectual community or 'centre of calculation'" for the climate debate, with named cross-memberships (Turbilly, the Comte d'Abeille). This resonates with modernity-machine as an early transnational information infrastructure, though Grove's frame is scientific-institutional exchange rather than bureaucratic measurement.

  • Climatic theory shifted from passive fatalism to a doctrine of human control via forests. Until the 1750s, with the exception of John Woodward, theorists saw man as a passive victim of climate; Grove shows how the Newtonian linkage of forests to atmospheric moisture let theorists propose that "man might contro! the constitution of the atmosphere through his control of forest cover"—a claim Duhamel du Monceau's 1760 arboricultural writings approached but did not generalise beyond the temperate zone. This is close to the brief's "desiccationism as an early earth-system model" and anticipates state environmental intervention, though it remains a scientific claim rather than yet a policy—the fragile edge of legibility-as-machine-core where a natural process becomes something a state might act on.

  • Natural catastrophe ironically opened the door to climatic activism. The London earthquakes of 1750 and the Lisbon earthquake of 1755—which provoked voltaire's "anguished commentaries on the cosmic lessons" of the disaster—undercut Buffon's confident picture of human mastery over nature by exposing Europeans themselves to the same "earthly hazards" long associated with the colonies. Grove frames this, paradoxically, as clearing the ground for an active climatic conservationism rather than reinforcing fatalism.

  • Pierre Poivre supplied the missing element: a tropical administrator fluent in the new theory. Grove argues that only "a learned encounter between the new social and 'Newtonian' climatic theories and the realities of a rapidly changing and closely observed tropical forest environment" could turn theory into policy, and that Poivre—naturalist, traveller, and expert on Mauritius—was that person. His early-1763 Lyons speech explicitly warned of deforestation-driven rainfall change in tropical colonies, praised Dutch reafforestation at the Cape, and called for French forest protection and planting in the Indies. Grove traces its direct consequences: Poivre's own 1769 Reglement Economique on Mauritius, and the British incorporation of forest rain reserves into plans for the newly Ceded Islands (Tobago, Grenada, St Vincent, St Lucia, Dominica) in late 1763—"at least partly derivative of Poivre's environmentalism."

Notable passages

The d'Alembert gloss on Montesquieu (c1) shows climatic-social theory at its most legally consequential: climate as something law must either follow or resist. Buffon's own block-quoted claim of a single, climate-differentiated human species (c2) is the theoretical root of the 'degeneration' idea that runs through the whole period. The passage on the Hales-driven Anglo-French 'centre of calculation' (c3) is Grove's own analytic claim about the scientific network's unity. The Duhamel du Monceau passage (c4) captures, in Grove's words, the precise moment theorists proposed that man could control atmospheric moisture through forest management—including the OCR-preserved glitch "contro!" in the source text. The earthquakes-and-Voltaire passage (c5) is the ironic hinge Grove uses to explain why an activist climatic conservationism could emerge from events that exposed human vulnerability rather than mastery. Finally, the Poivre passage (c6) is the chapter's culmination: Grove's narrative of the 1763 Lyons speech and its immediate French and British colonial policy consequences, the direct bridge into the book's chapters on Mauritius and the Caribbean.
