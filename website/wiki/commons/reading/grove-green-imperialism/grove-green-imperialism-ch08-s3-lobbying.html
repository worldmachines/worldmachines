Gibson's campaign for a Bombay forest conservancy, 1840-1847

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Alexander Gibson combines Scottish-medical desiccation science, botanical-network correspondence, and Konkani and Parsi testimony on drying streams to win East India Company sanction for a funded Bombay Forest Conservancy between 1840 and 1847.

Between 1840 and 1847, the surgeon-botanist Alexander Gibson built the evidentiary and institutional case that turned a Bombay government inspection appointment into a funded, staffed Forest Conservancy. Grove traces how Gibson drew on a transnational scientific literature (Humboldt and Boussingault, read through Edinburgh's Scottish-medical readership), moved cautiously from an economically framed lobbying strategy to an explicitly climatic one, and finally broke years of inter-departmental stalemate — Bombay government, Military Board, government of India, Court of Directors — by appealing directly to London and to his professional peers in print.

Themes

Desiccation science reached India through a Scottish medical pipeline, not directly from Humboldt. Boussingault's 1838 Edinburgh New Philosophical Journal paper on forest clearance and catchment run-off (translated from a Paris lecture) landed among Scottish-trained Company surgeons already primed by decades of Scottish medical teaching to read disease environmentally; Newbold, Balfour and Butter picked up the connection between deforestation, sand dunes, flooding and drought years before Gibson used it in lobbying.

Gibson withheld the climatic argument for eight years out of scientific caution. Grove is explicit that Gibson knew Boussingault's reasoning from the start but judged the catchment-run-off claim scientifically verifiable and the forest-cover-to-regional-climate claim much less so; he only deployed desiccation as a "psychological weapon" in 1846, after firewood-shortage arguments alone had failed to move the government of India.

Three motives for conservation coexisted and shifted in weight. Gibson's own early letters to Hooker at Kew show a naturalist's and economist's regret at deforestation; by the mid-1840s a third motive — social stability, public health, and famine prevention — became the dominant register because it matched the government's own anxieties and the surgeons' Hippocratic training.

The colonial bureaucracy had no single body actually empowered over forests. Responsibility was split across the Military Department, the Political Department and the Board of Revenue, none of which exerted any real executive function; Jervis's and Boyce's 1841-3 reports and the abortive 1842 proposal to make Boyce a conservator show the state groping toward, but not yet building, an administrative organ for forest control.

Indigenous testimony supplied the evidence Gibson's own science could not verify. A February 1846 letter from a Mahabaleshwar correspondent, reporting Konkani belief that stream flow follows deforestation and Parsi merchant observation of declining Nilgiri rainfall, gave Gibson the confirmation he needed to extend Boussingault's local run-off argument into a claim about regional climate change — evidence Grove notes explicitly as originating with indigenous rather than European authorities.

A calculated act of insubordination broke the stalemate. Rather than wait on the government of India's latest procrastination, Gibson sent his March 1846 report on Konkan deforestation directly to the Court of Directors in London and published a companion paper in the widely read Transactions of the Bombay Medical and Physical Society — a gamble that paid off within nine months in a funded forest-management establishment and, by March 1847, his own appointment as Conservator of Forests alongside his existing post as superintendent of the Dapoori Botanic Garden.

For World Machines: the eventual "establishment for the management of the forests" is a small, dated case of the colonial state manufacturing new administrative legibility over a resource it had not previously tracked or controlled (Legibility, legibility-as-machine-core) — though Grove's own frame is institutional and scientific history, not machine theory, and the legibility arrives only after years of departmental stalemate, not by fiat. Gibson's correspondence with Hooker at Kew, and his own post running the Dapoori garden, also show the botanical-garden network functioning as an information conduit for the wider colonial-science infrastructure through which alexander-von-humboldt and Boussingault's ideas travelled on their way to India.

Notable passages

  • Boussingault's paper crossing from Paris to Edinburgh to Scottish-trained surgeons in India: the Scottish-French academic tie was "built on the tradition of close contact between the two communities established as early as the mid eighteenth century," and the Edinburgh New Philosophical Journal itself was "widely read among surgeons trained at the Scottish universities, as the majority of surgeons working in India were by the late 1830s."
  • Donald Butter's report on Oudh gives the unit's fullest desiccationist set piece, warning that deforested land is "no longer capable of entangling and detaining water in the meshes of a herbaceous covering" so that "its rainfall and the volume of its rivers diminished."
  • Gibson's early letter to Hooker frames deforestation as naturalist's loss before it becomes a lobbying argument: the Ghat forests are disappearing under demand "for spade husbandry," which he calls a matter "for regret ... for the naturalist, perhaps also for the economist."
  • The Mahabaleshwar letter of February 1846 supplies Konkani and Parsi testimony that stream drying and rainfall decline followed local deforestation — evidence Grove flags as indigenous rather than European in origin.
  • Gibson's decision to send his report straight to the Court of Directors, bypassing both the Bombay government and the government of India, is described as a gamble that "quickly paid off by breaking at a stroke the administrative logjam," producing the funded conservancy establishment within the year.
