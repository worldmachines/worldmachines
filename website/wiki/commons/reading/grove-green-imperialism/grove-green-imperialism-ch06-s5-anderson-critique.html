A Crisis of Conscience and Chronology: Alexander Anderson's Environmental Critique, 1785-1811

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Alexander Anderson's environmentalism on St Vincent grew out of Poivre-like plant-transfer botany and an inherited theology of atmospheric purification (Hales and Priestley, mediated by Edward Long) into a forest-protection creed entangled with guilt over the violent expulsion of the Caribs and with a private crisis over geological time that Soufrière's volcanism forced on his religious beliefs.

Drawing mainly on Anderson's unpublished manuscripts — 'The Geography and History of St Vincent' (c. 1800) and the unpublished 'Delugia' — and on his correspondence with William Forsyth at the Chelsea Physic Garden, Grove reconstructs how the curator of the St Vincent Botanic Garden moved from a Poivre-like interest in plant transfer to a full desiccationist, forest-protection philosophy, and argues that this environmentalism was inseparable both from Anderson's guilt over the British expulsion of the Caribs and from a private crisis over geological time that his own island's volcano, Soufrière, forced on his inherited religious chronology.

Themes

  • A shared origin with Poivre in plant transfer. Anderson traced his own interest in soil conditions, like Poivre's, to the Dutch botanist Rumphius's Herbarium Amboinense, but Grove stresses that no exact date can be put on his 'espousal of environmental fears and climatic anxieties': his reading of Edward Long's History of Jamaica (which digests Priestley and Hales) is only firmly attested after 1800, though he may have absorbed desiccationist ideas earlier, perhaps via Dr George Young's correspondence with Poivre's own Pamplemousses garden on Mauritius.

  • The volcano as climatic instrument. An 1784 letter to Forsyth, written after Anderson climbed Soufrière, records his observation that the wooded mountain was nightly 'covered with thick clouds, from which it frequently rains', and his regret at having no instruments to measure the air; Grove sets this beside Benjamin Franklin's near-contemporary theory that the severe winter of 1783-4 was caused by volcanic dust from Iceland's Hekla, both men reading climate through volcanic and atmospheric physics.

  • The island as an accelerated laboratory of deforestation. Decades as garden curator let Anderson watch St Vincent's 'thickest and most impenetrable forests' disappear within his own tenure; Grove treats this direct, dated, small-scale witnessing — rather than inherited theory alone — as decisive for Anderson's 'growing sensibility'.

  • Utopian aesthetics entangled with colonial guilt. Anderson describes St Vincent through a borrowed Tahitian idiom ('Otaheite') and a 'nouvelle Cythère' Utopian register that Grove has already traced through Commerson and Poivre; the same manuscript registers his 'very disturbed and ambivalent response' to the suppression and forced removal of the Caribs, whom Anderson's own reasoning links directly to the forests being cleared for cane — an admiration for indigenous ecological equilibrium that echoes, without quite matching, the noble-savage discourse Grove traces elsewhere (noble-savages-and-english-gardeners).

  • A practical-economic credo against monoculture. The long-quoted 'Turn all into cane' passage argues, from timber supply, cattle shelter, protection from sea spray, landscape aesthetics, human health and rainfall attraction, against clearing every acre for sugar. Grove reads it as Anderson's fullest statement of 'colonial environmental notions ... by 1800' and is careful not to reduce its idiom to a borrowing from Rousseau and the French Romantics, insisting instead on 'a distinctively empirical and botanical consciousness' — a resonance with jean-jacques-rousseau that Grove himself raises only to qualify.

  • An inherited theology of atmospheric purification. Via Long, Anderson absorbed Hales's and Priestley's atmospheric chemistry and Sir John Pringle's providential claim that 'no vegetable grows in vain', purifying air even in 'the most remote and unpeopled regions' for the benefit of all. Grove links this 'aerial system' to the rise of medical police and cameralism, in which physicians used meteorology to license the policing of health and, in the tropics, of landscape itself — a loose resonance with the World Machines idea of state legibility exercised through scientific claims-making, though here the instrument is providential medical theology rather than administrative apparatus (legibility-as-machine-core).

  • A Scottish inheritance, only opened here. Grove calls Anderson 'the first in a long line of Scottish colonial experts who linked together their biological, religious and social insights' — a lineage this unit only begins, not the later Indian medical-service conservationists it anticipates; the connection is to a colonial medical-botanical training network rather than the Edinburgh philosophical circle proper, so any link to scottish-enlightenment should be read loosely, as adjacent institutional culture rather than shared doctrine.

  • The private crisis of chronology. Anderson's own geological observations of Soufrière's 'great convulsions of nature' conflicted with his religious beliefs about creation, worked through at length in the unpublished 'Delugia' manuscript. Grove's closing claim is that Anderson's climatic environmentalism functioned psychologically to 'restore the apparent power of the colonist to act' amid this threatened chronology — entangled with, not separate from, his unresolved guilt over the Caribs' removal.

Notable passages

The Forsyth letter (c1) is the earliest hard evidence for Anderson's environmental thinking: a first-hand, dated observation connecting woodland, cloud cover and rain, paired with a scientist's frustration at lacking instruments — the empirical seed of everything that follows.

The 'Turn all into cane' excerpt (c2) is the passage Grove quotes 'at length' as Anderson's own conservationist credo, moving in one breath from timber economics to the claim that 'trees have a very considerable effect in attracting rain, upon the certainty of which no crop depends so much as that of sugar cane' — desiccation theory as a planter's practical argument, not an abstraction.

The Otaheite comparison (c3) shows Anderson mapping St Vincent onto the Tahitian/Utopian island imaginary at the very moment two Tahitian visitors mistake it for home — the passage Grove uses to explain why Anderson's 'personal cosmology' made him so sensitive to the destruction of the forests the Caribs inhabited.

Anderson's own words on the Carib expulsion (c4) — asking who could avoid 'melancholy sensations' at a whole people 'transported forever from their native land' — is the clearest evidence that his environmental and political consciences were, for Grove, the same crisis viewed from two sides.

The Pringle-via-Long passage (c5) carries the providential argument that even unpeopled forests purify air for humanity at large, the intellectual inheritance (through Priestley and Hales) that let Anderson generalise his island observations into a universal claim about vegetation, climate and health.

The closing passage on the volcano and the 'Delugia' manuscript (c6) is the unit's thesis in miniature: geological evidence of 'catastrophic change' threatening Anderson's religious chronology, with forest protection read by Grove as the strategy by which Anderson 'restored the apparent power of the colonist to act amid a chaos' of chronology, origins and belief.
