Mauritius's Botanical Gardens and the First Anxieties over Deforestation, c.1735–1764

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mauritius's early botanical gardens — Labourdonnais's Mon Plaisir, Pierre David's La Reduit, and Aublet's research garden linking the island to Joseph Banks and Parisian taxonomy — grew up alongside a colonial administration that surveyed, confiscated concessions, and by 1761–62 began restricting forest clearance for reasons of defence and revenue rather than ecological theory, years before Pierre Poivre's official conservation programme.

Labourdonnais's crop-trial garden at Mon Plaisir (Pamplemousses) begat a lineage of Mauritius botanical and acclimatisation gardens — Pierre David's La Reduit, then Jean-Baptiste Fusée Aublet's dedicated medical-botanical garden from 1753 — that combined the Cape's acclimatisation tradition with the Paris Jardin du Roi's academic one and an older wish to recreate an earthly paradise; at the same time, and partly through the very land-clearing and timber use that colonial settlement required, Mauritius's forests came under increasing pressure, and from the 1750s onward a succession of governors, memorialists and, in 1761, the English East India Company itself began registering alarm — for reasons of empire defence, naval timber supply and company revenue as much as ecological principle — years before Pierre Poivre's official conservation programme (1767–72) or any developed desiccation theory.

Themes

The botanical garden was never a single-purpose institution. Grove traces Mauritius's gardens to at least three braided motives: the commercial/subsistence motive (Labourdonnais's manioc, introduced from Brazil, as slave food), the Edenic/paradisal motive shared with the Jardin du Roi, Chelsea Physic Garden and the Cape VOC garden (recreating “a Garden of Eden, or an earthly paradise,” reinforced by “the Dantean conceptualisation of the tropical island as an earthly paradise”), and the medical/academic motive that brought the Paris-trained apothecary-botanist Aublet to the island in 1753. The Edenic motive is a rougher cousin of nature-culture-dialectic's garden-as-staged-nature: where that page's English garden performs “nature did it all,” Grove's tropical garden performs the opposite claim — an artificial, comprehensively stocked collection standing in for a lost, literal Paradise — but both use horticultural artifice to make a claim about nature's meaning. Only by holding all three motives together does the garden's later role as an information network — Grove's own phrase — make sense.

The garden network turned an island crisis into a metropolitan one. Aublet's decade of meticulous cataloguing at Le Reduit, his correspondence with Joseph Banks (who ultimately bought his herbarium), and his influence on Banks's idea of stationing resident ‘colonial botanists’ across the tropics tied Mauritius directly into the Parisian revolution in taxonomy (Buffon on species mutability). Grove's own argument is explicit: this link “gave a new quality and urgency to subsequent anxiety about deforestation” on Mauritius that the “ad hoc response” on St Helena — facing the same causes — never received. This resonates with modernity-machine's picture of scientific-administrative networks as information infrastructure, and with the-english-garden-as-epistemic-object's framing of the garden as a device for producing knowledge, though Grove's gardens here are tropical acclimatisation and medical stations rather than the English landscape garden that page treats.

Deforestation was a by-product of the same colonial machinery that built the gardens. Labourdonnais, the garden's founder, also used ships “constructed of —Mauritius timber” to capture Madras in 1746, which Grove flags as itself accelerating clearance; the wider concession system (the 1734 ‘petite’/‘grande habitation’ regulation, with its cultivation deadlines) drove enough clearing that the Compagnie des Indes confiscated 204 concessions in 1752 for non-compliance. Grove's later governors — Magon worrying about grass fires in 1756, Saint-Muan accusing Magon of fatalism in 1757 — describe a landscape already visibly stripped near Port Louis and along the leeward coast, even while La Caille's 1753 survey still found the interior “almost entirely covered with woods,” dense enough to shelter Maroon (escaped-slave) communities.

State legibility preceded, but did not by itself cause, conservation. La Caille's 1753 survey — the island's first accurate map — let the colonial administration take full stock of forest extent for the first time, a textbook act of Legibility — but the interventions that followed were driven by defence and revenue, not ecological theory: the 1761 English East India Company intelligence report on Mauritius's “military capabilities” records the Company's earliest interest in forest matters purely because shoreline woods screened the coast from naval descents, and the French Company's own 1761 felling ban around Grand Port and Port Louis served the identical defensive logic. This is a clean instance of legibility-as-precondition-not-cause: mapping made the forest visible and countable, but what moved the state to act on that visibility was military and fiscal anxiety, arriving well before any climatic or desiccationist argument.

The pieces of desiccation theory are just starting to appear, unevenly and administratively rather than scientifically. Governor Desforges-Boucher's 1762 regulation explicitly yokes “the control of waters and forests” together, and Grove notes it is “hard to say” whether this coupling originated in Paris or on Mauritius — but it anticipates, without yet articulating, the rainfall-and-forest-cover logic Poivre would formalise from 1767. Desforges-Boucher's account of vigorous oak and chestnut plantations at La Reduit and Mon Plaisir (probably inspired by Poivre's earlier observations at the Cape) shows exotic afforestation already being tried as a practical remedy, alongside proposals — echoed again in 1764 by de Landevisian and Charpentier de Cossigny — to reserve woods near the ports and to compel settlers to plant trees around their own holdings.

Notable passages

Labourdonnais's founding of the Mon Plaisir crop-trial garden and his use of Mauritius timber to build the fleet that took Madras (c1) sets up the book's central irony: the same governor advances both the institution that will become the island's scientific garden and the deforestation that institution will later be marshalled to diagnose. The passage on the Garden-of-Eden motive behind the Jardin du Roi, Chelsea and the Cape gardens (c2) states in Grove's own voice the island/garden-as-Eden thesis that runs through the whole book. The Aublet–Banks passage (c3) is where Grove makes his comparative argument explicit, contrasting Mauritius's early metropolitan visibility with St Helena's neglect. La Caille's 1753 description of the forest (c4) is the primary-source anchor for how much woodland still stood, and for its double character as ecological resource and refuge for Maroon communities — a reminder that “forest” here is also contested political geography, not only timber stock. The 1762 Desforges-Boucher letter (c5) is the clearest sign, this early, of water and forest management being administratively linked. The 1761 East India Company report (c6) shows the English Company's interest in Mauritius's forests emerging from military intelligence rather than science — a precursor, via a wholly different logic, to its later, scientifically argued forest conservation in India.
