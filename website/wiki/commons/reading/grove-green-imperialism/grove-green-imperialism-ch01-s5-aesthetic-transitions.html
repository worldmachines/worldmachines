Seventeenth-century aesthetic transitions and the European roots of environmental sensibility

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Grove locates the decisive transition in European attitudes to nature not in the eighteenth-century Enlightenment or Romanticism but in a seventeenth-century convergence of religious secularisation, mechanistic science, joint-stock capital, and Dutch landscape and land-control culture that produced the literate, empirically minded society which would go on to build colonial institutions like the Cape Colony's botanical garden.

Before returning to the islands themselves, Grove pauses to supply the European domestic background to the colonial discourse of nature he has been tracing: he argues that the decisive transition in European attitudes toward the natural world happened not in the eighteenth-century Enlightenment or Romanticism, which he thinks have absorbed too much credit, but in a longer, largely secular process running from the late sixteenth century — driven by religious reform, mechanistic natural philosophy, the accumulation of mobile capital, and Dutch landscape culture — that produced a literate, empirically minded society ready to build institutions like the Cape Colony's botanical garden as a "planned Eden."

Themes

Pre-Enlightenment origins. Grove's central claim is periodizing: "Enlightenment" and "Romanticism" are, in his view, minority intellectual labels that have distracted historians from a "far more wide-ranging" transition already underway two centuries earlier. He credits Charlton with identifying a seventeenth-century "movement" more consequential than either, and insists the philosophical concepts underlying later environmental anxieties were already fully developed by the mid-eighteenth century — so it is the seventeenth century's colonial environmental policy, not only the eighteenth's, that needs explaining.

Secularisation displaces meaning onto nature. The mechanism Grove proposes is a shift of "social and symbolic meanings" out of church contexts into secular ones, aided by the printing press and by radical dissenting sects (Quakers, Huguenots) who treated a knowledge of physical creation as a path to God. He roots this in older precedents (Saint Francis, Bonaventure, Aquinas) but sees the link between religious or political dissent and changed environmental attitudes sharpen after the late seventeenth century, alongside a broader decline of belief in comets, omens and magic, which he reads through Shakespeare's Tempest.

Mechanistic science cuts both ways. Grove explicitly resists the simple story that Galileo, Descartes, Hobbes and Newton's reductionism was straightforwardly destructive of a "natural" and implicitly feminine holism. He calls that reading "superficially attractive" but flawed, because the same mechanistic, measurement-based habit of mind that objectified nature also enabled the rational observation and organised conservationist response that a later Newtonian, Stephen Hales, would turn into a critique of deforestation — a dialectic central to his larger argument that scientific reductionism is a precondition of, and not just an obstacle to, environmentalism.

Capital and the new scale of damage. Growing floating capital in France, Britain and the Low Countries financed joint-stock companies and land-manipulation schemes (the East Anglian fens' "Adventurers," the Arno and Po drainage projects) that exceeded the scale of any individual estate — and by the mid-seventeenth century that same capital, channelled through Dutch, English and French East India Company plantation investment, was driving soil erosion and deforestation on Caribbean and Atlantic islands at rates "unprecedented in Europe."

The Dutch precedent: control, painting, and the planned Eden. Amsterdam's accumulated capital and the Netherlands' land reclamation gave the Dutch, per Te Brake, "a highly developed form of environmental control," and a landscape-painting tradition (feeding into Claude Lorraine's pastorals) that made an engineered, secular countryside aesthetically visible before the rest of Europe caught up. Maps of England sold "printed on cheap handkerchiefs," growing literacy, and a travel-literature boom gave readers a more detached, objective view of their own society and of the tropics — a sensibility the Dutch then projected onto the Cape Colony, where, Grove writes, "a planned Eden could be constructed far more comprehensively than in Europe" in the form of a colonial botanical garden fed by company ships "from the four quarters of the globe."

Notable passages

The unit's argumentative spine (c1) is Grove's periodizing move against Enlightenment- and Romanticism-centred historiography of environmental attitudes. c2 carries his most theoretically loaded claim: that mechanistic science is not simply the antagonist of ecological sensibility but, via figures like Hales, its enabling instrument. c3 threads the book's Edenic-island theme back into European letters, reading Shakespeare's Tempest as anticipating a world in which paradise becomes "a realisable geographical reality" rather than a lost condition. c4 is the hinge to the book's Caribbean and Atlantic material: floating capital, not ideas alone, scaled up deforestation. c5 closes the unit by tying Dutch land-control and print literacy directly to the founding logic of the colonial botanical garden, the institution the rest of the book treats as the information infrastructure of colonial environmentalism.

WM resonance: the whole unit reads as an account of the Dawn-phase construction of the modernity-machine — secularisation, reductionist science, joint-stock capital, and cartographic and print literacy assembling into the era's characteristic drive to render land, nation and colony knowable and controllable, well before that machine's eighteenth-century dominance. The Dutch material — land reclamation as "a highly developed form of environmental control," maps of England sold as a commodity, "a better-defined and more systematic image of geographical space" — resonates with Legibility and legibility-as-machine-core, though Grove frames it as aesthetic and religious history rather than state theory; the drive he describes is more diffuse and cultural than administrative legibility proper, closer to a precondition than to the machine's core mechanism, which is why legibility-as-precondition-not-cause fits at least as well. isaac-newton appears by name as part of a reductionist current Grove refuses to treat as simply anti-ecological — a genuine complication of any easy nature-culture-dialectic reading that pits mechanistic, "masculine" science against a "natural, sustainable" feminine holism; Grove's point is that the same Newtonian instruments that objectified nature also measured its degradation.
