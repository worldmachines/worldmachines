St Helena, 1700-1715: Arrack, Drought, and Governor Roberts's Forest Law

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Governor John Roberts's 1708-1710 campaign to halt St Helena's drought and soil erosion with a compulsory one-acre-in-ten enclosure and tree-planting law, negotiated with islanders and undone by goats, dry winds, arrack distilling, and an inattentive East India Company, is vindicated too late when the uninformed Governor Boucher presides over the catastrophic drought of 1712-14.

Continuing the St Helena case, Grove traces how early-eighteenth-century observers on the island explicitly linked deforestation to declining stream flow and drought risk, how arrack distilling accelerated that deforestation, and how Governor John Roberts (1708-1710) turned this diagnosis into the island's first systematic, negotiated forest-conservation law — a law whose partial success and ultimate defeat by goats, weather, and institutional forgetting is then borne out by the severe 1712-14 drought under his uninformed successor, Governor Boucher.

Themes

Deforestation is diagnosed as the cause of drought before 1700, via a concrete subsistence stake. Grove opens by noting that fears of drought on St Helena were "directly due to observations that clearly demonstrated the effects of deforestation on the regularity of stream flow," and ties this immediately to the yam, the slaves' staple crop, which "required large amounts of irrigation water for successful cultivation." This is desiccation theory in embryo: not yet a general climatic doctrine, but a locally observed causal chain (tree loss to stream failure to crop failure) with an identifiable, at-risk population. The island's small, bounded scale is what makes the causal link visible within a single generation rather than across centuries.

Arrack distilling, not just fuelwood and shipbuilding demand, is identified as a primary driver of forest loss, and provokes one of the book's earliest instances of a colonial administration curbing an economic activity for conservation reasons: the 1701 duty on arrack sales was imposed explicitly to "prevent ye distilling trade which we understand is grown very ripe in the island to the general destruction of the wood." Grove frames this as "a radical measure" for its time — a state fiscal instrument deployed against a licensed, revenue-generating trade for the sake of forest survival, prefiguring the later, larger-scale collisions between conservation and capital that recur throughout the book.

An anonymous pre-1708 reafforestation plan proposes a rotational planting quota decades before Roberts acts on it, calling for the Company to plant "10 or 12 acres of ground yearly for about fifty years to come" parcelled around the island, paired with settler usufruct rights over enclosed Company commons — a scheme designed, in its own words, to "supply to Futurity." Grove's point in juxtaposing this ignored proposal with the crisis that follows is that the diagnosis long preceded any institutional will to act on it; expert advice sat unused until soil erosion made inaction politically untenable.

Governor Roberts personalizes conservation policy, and Grove is explicit that this personal enthusiasm — not durable institutional capacity — was the actual engine of reform. Roberts arrives in 1708 relatively complacent ("about wood we do apprehend that there can be no want this fifty year") but is transformed within a year by touring the depleted Great Wood and tallying fifteen years of undocumented decay. His subsequent programme — his own tree-planting, his detective work on tanners over-barking redwood and ebony, his tracking of dead ebony stocks near Sandy Bay — reads less as bureaucratic administration than as one man's investigation. Grove underscores this with the Council's later, "confused and defeatist" letter minimizing the goat threat, offered as "an indication of how much conservationist efforts depended on the convictions of an enthusiastic individual."

The 1709 enclosure law is negotiated with the island's landholders, not simply imposed from above. Roberts rejects the Company's blanket order to enclose the Great Wood outright and instead invites proposals from islanders, who submit a formal address on 14 June 1709 accepting a compulsory quota — "that every planter possessed of twenty acres of land shall be obliged to enclose one acre and plant it with wood" — while resisting long-term fencing obligations. Roberts concedes most of their terms but insists fencing must eventually happen, since the Company would otherwise seize unfenced land. The result, "the inhabitants shall enclose and plant one acre in ten within two years," is a quantified, uniform, land-proportional rule — a small, local instance of administrative Legibility: a numeric quota applied evenly across landholdings, enforceable by threat of confiscation, replacing an unworkable order to enclose Company land wholesale. It resonates only partially with the full legibility-as-machine-core picture, though: this is a single governor's ad hoc bargain with a few dozen landholders, not a standing bureaucratic apparatus, and its numeric neatness did not translate into durable capacity — which is exactly what the unit goes on to show.

Quantified legibility is not sufficient cause for conservation to succeed — biophysical reality and institutional memory both defeat it. Most young trees died to "the dry wind blast" and "the constant depredations of goats," and even successful plantations could not outpace ongoing soil loss ("the ground wears away strangely"). Roberts himself predicts catastrophe with striking precision — "the island in 20 years time will be utterly ruined for want of wood... it will die with age" — yet his own restoration programme collapses for want of continuity: on his 1710 departure, "no attempt seems to have been made by the company to inform Roberts's successor, Governor Boucher, of the situation he might have to confront." A legible rule (one acre in ten, measurable and dated) existed on paper, but the administrative memory needed to sustain it did not survive a change of governor — a clean case for legibility-as-precondition-not-cause: having a countable, enforceable rule was necessary but not remotely sufficient once the individual who cared about it left.

Roberts blames his predecessor's negligence and the Company's institutional passivity, not lack of information, for the crisis. His December 1710 letter is scathing about Governor "Poiryer," who had pursued fashionable botany and viticulture on the island but "remained quite oblivious to the erosive consequences of the plantation projects which he had supported," and who left the Great Wood unfenced despite ample warning. Grove uses this to show that the Company possessed the facts throughout — repeated despatches, minutes, and petitions — and still could not translate them into continuous policy, an early instance of the gap between diagnosis and institutional capacity that recurs across the book's colonial-state case studies.

Company land grants for private profit directly threatened the Council's conservation programme, exposing a conflict between commercial policy and ecological policy inside the same institution. The Council's objection to enlarging George Hoskins's estate — "we must bid farewell to most part of the island and expect no wood from thence... it will render your island useless after all your great charge" — paired with an implicit threat of settler emigration, shows local administrators using ecological collapse and economic self-interest as leverage against Directors more concerned with individual land titles than aggregate forest cover.

The 1712-14 drought vindicates Roberts's warnings after the fact, and closes the unit on the same social pathology (arrack) that opened it. Under the "severe maladministration" of Governor Boucher, goat control lapsed entirely, plantations were "merely burnt or scorcht up," and more than two thousand cattle died. Crucially, hardship revived exactly the vice the 1701 duty had targeted: Directors complained in 1713 that "excessive drinking of arrack has grown up upon all the people strangely," and by 1715 that "the people are grown sottish." The Council's terse verdict — the island was "in a very bad and deplorable condition" — closes a fifteen-year loop from the unit's opening diagnosis, with the causal chain (deforestation to drought to crop and cattle failure to social distress to arrack) now empirically completed rather than merely feared.

Notable passages

The observer's testimony on arrack-driven deforestation and the 1701 duty (c1) is the unit's primary evidence that colonial administrators recognized and acted against a specific economic driver of forest loss well before any general conservation programme existed. The anonymous reafforestation proposal (c2) shows expert diagnosis arriving years ahead of institutional will. Roberts's 1709 enclosure order (c3) and the islanders' own petition (c4) together show conservation policy being negotiated rather than dictated — a rare case in the book of colonial subjects actively co-authoring the rule that will bind them. Roberts's 1710 prediction of a twenty-year die-off (c5) is his starkest and most precise warning, curated because it is later shown to be essentially correct. The closing passage on the 1712-14 drought (c6) is curated because it functions as the unit's empirical payoff: everything Roberts predicted comes to pass under a successor who was never told the diagnosis existed.
