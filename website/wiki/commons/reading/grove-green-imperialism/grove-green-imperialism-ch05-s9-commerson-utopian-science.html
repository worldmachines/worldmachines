Commerson's Tahitian Utopia, Species Variation, and the Limits of Poivre's Mauritius Programme, 1768–73

Commons · Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Philibert Commerson's Tahiti-derived vision of an unspoiled, Rousseauian Utopia, filtered through his self-styled 'missionary naturalist' persona and an early migration-based theory of racial variation, fed directly into Poivre's Mauritius conservation programme even as the island's 18,000-strong slave population exposed the gap between Utopian discourse and colonial reality, and institutional fragility left the whole scientific-conservationist project collapsing by the early 1770s.

This unit traces how Philibert Commerson's 1768 encounter with Tahiti, filtered through his February 1769 letter to the Paris Mercure, gave Pierre Poivre's Mauritius conservation project its Utopian idiom and part of its horticultural policy, while also seeding Commerson's own proto-evolutionary speculation about the origin of human 'races'; Grove then sets this idealism against the brute fact of Mauritius as a slave colony, follows Bernardin de Saint-Pierre's parallel turn from social to natural 'harmony', and closes on the institutional fragility — Commerson's unrealised 1770 Académie proposal, his 1773 death, and the political collapse of Poivre's position — that left the 'pioneering conservation ideology' only briefly triumphant.

Themes

Commerson renames Cythera as Utopia. Bougainville had christened Tahiti 'La Nouvelle Cythére' in deliberate homage to classical and Watteauesque visions of Aphrodite's idyllic island; Commerson, dissatisfied with this backward-looking mythological register, substituted the more 'modern' term 'Utopia', explicitly crediting Thomas More's ideal republic (thomas-more, more-utopia). The substitution matters: it reframes Tahiti from a timeless erotic myth into a proposed, arguable model of a better polity, which is exactly the register Commerson and Poivre needed for a live conservation and land-use argument.

The Mercure letter as ethnographic Utopia. Commerson's own description — a people without 'vices, prejudices, wants or dissensions', ruled by a paterfamilias rather than a monarch, worshipping only Love, undressed and unashamed — draws heavily on Rousseau's state-of-nature vocabulary (jean-jacques-rousseau), including an explicit aside crediting Rousseau's doubts about whether a 'sailor's soul' even exists, and an anticipation in Rousseau's own La Nouvelle Héloïse. The register half-echoes a noble-savage discourse (noble-savages-and-english-gardeners), though Grove's interest is Commerson's attempt to use Tahiti to indict French colonial corruption specifically, not (as in the English-garden material) an aesthetic domestication of wildness. The episode of Jeanne Baret — Commerson's cross-dressed companion, unmasked as a woman by the Tahitians themselves and only then 'restored to her sex' — gives the Utopian encounter a gendered edge that Commerson does not fully theorise.

The 'missionary naturalist': botany as redemptive science. Commerson's self-description, 'I am a missionary naturalist, I ought to speak accordingly', fuses religiosity with empirical natural history. Grove reads this as the hinge by which Commerson extends the older idea of a botanical Eden compressed into a single garden (hortus) outward to embrace the whole tropical world as a redemptive refuge from French social corruption — a project measured in raw numbers (Commerson claims 25,000 species personally collected, a fraction, he insists, of the true total) and in the island-as-microcosm conceit that made Mauritius knowable as 'a whole small world' in a way the entire planet's flora could never be.

From admired Tahitian horticulture to Mauritius forest law. Grove draws a direct line from Commerson's praise of Tahitian fruit trees — planted at 'judicious intervals', without 'the tiresome monotony of orchards' — to the tree-planting provisions of Mauritius's first Conservation Ordinance, promulgated barely a year later. It is a striking, almost literal case of Utopian literary description becoming colonial-state regulation: an island's imagined natural order translated into another island's legislated one, a small but concrete instance of the kind of environmental legibility the colonial state increasingly claimed for itself (Legibility), even though Grove himself frames it as intellectual influence and policy borrowing rather than any general theory of state control.

A proto-migrationist theory of racial difference. Pressed by his own comparative and taxonomic expertise to explain why Tahitians differed so markedly from Europeans, Commerson worked through migration, extinction and 'Protoplast peoples' as alternatives to spontaneous generation of species — a line of reasoning Grove says anticipates, by roughly thirty years, the single-stock migration concept usually credited to Cuvier, possibly transmitted through Commerson's friendship with Bernardin de Saint-Pierre (himself close to Cuvier, Lamarck and Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire). Grove is careful to flag the limit of Commerson's radicalism here: ideological attachment to an uncorrupted, non-European Tahiti kept him from drawing an explicit evolutionary kinship between Tahitians and Europeans, even as his empirical instincts pushed toward exactly that conclusion.

Utopia meets slavery. Mauritius, Grove insists, was 'far from being a Utopia': a slave state of roughly 18,000 Black slaves and 2,000 Europeans. Bernardin de Saint-Pierre's anecdote of Aoatorau — the Tahitian exhibited in Paris — recoiling at the sight of men of his own colour in chains during a stop at Mauritius makes the contradiction explicit, and Grove links Poivre's failure to meaningfully improve slaves' conditions to a personal disillusionment that, paradoxically, hardened rather than dampened his conservation zeal. Bernardin de Saint-Pierre's own response was to relocate Utopian hope from society to nature itself — documenting vanishing flamingoes and turtles, dried-up rivers, and treating individual trees and species with an anthropomorphic, quasi-religious solicitude ('I had much rather look upon a tree as a republic') that this unit frames as a deification of nature standing in for the sociopolitical ideal Mauritius could not deliver; the same sensibility later carried him to the directorship of the Jardin des Plantes and a professorship of ethics in revolutionary Paris.

Institutional fragility. The chapter closes on the precariousness of the whole enterprise: Commerson's 1770 proposal for an Isle de France 'Académie' (which would anticipate the Batavia Society of Arts and Sciences, the Asiatic Society of Bengal, and the Linnaean Society of London by years or decades) never materialised in his lifetime; his death in 1773 removed Poivre's most important ally just as the political fall of Choiseul and Praslin ended ministerial physiocratic sympathy and left Poivre's forest-conservation battles against the new governor Desroches increasingly exposed, so that, in Grove's summary, 'the brief period during which a pioneering conservation ideology had reigned had, at least temporarily, come to an end.'

Notable passages

  • Bougainville's Cythera versus Commerson's Utopia (c1) marks the pivot from a classical, mythological register for Tahiti to an explicitly Utopian, More-derived one that could carry an argument about policy, not just poetry.
  • Commerson's own Mercure description of Tahiti (c2) is the primary-source Utopia itself: paterfamilias rule, no property or prejudice, a cult of Love — the raw material Poivre's Mauritius project drew on.
  • The Tahitian-tree-planting-to-Conservation-Ordinance passage (c3) is Grove's most concrete causal claim in the unit, tying literary Utopianism directly to colonial forest law.
  • The 'missionary naturalist' passage (c4) gives Commerson's own justification for treating botany as a redemptive, quasi-religious vocation, extending the Eden-in-a-garden idea to the whole tropical world.
  • Commerson's migration/origins block quotation (c5) is his own naturalist reasoning about 'Protoplast peoples' and racial variation, the passage Grove reads as anticipating Cuvier.
  • The slavery-and-disillusionment passage (c6) supplies the unit's sharpest complication: Mauritius's slave economy stood in flat contradiction to the Tahitian Utopia, and that contradiction, filtered through Poivre's and Bernardin de Saint-Pierre's differing responses, is where this unit's argument about the fragility and displacement of colonial Utopian ideals lands.
