Converging Revolutions: The Global Military-Fiscal Crisis of 1720–1820

Commons · The Birth of the Modern World · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Bayly opens Chapter 3 by treating the world crisis of 1720-1820 as a single interlocking system in which the collapse of the Safavid and Mughal empires, the Seven Years War, and the American and French Revolutions fed on and into each other through a shared 'military-fiscal' trap of rising war costs and revenues that could not be raised without provoking revolt.

Opening Chapter 3, Bayly rejects a Europe-centered "age of revolutions" story in favor of an explicitly global and reciprocal one: fighting between Europeans and indigenous peoples in the Americas and Asia during the Seven Years War (1756-63) helped crack the finances and legitimacy of Europe's own old regimes, whose subsequent revolutions then deepened crises already underway in the Americas, Asia, and North Africa, which in turn fed back into the European convulsions that ran through to the Terror of 1793. Before tracing the ideological shockwaves of 1776 and 1789, the chapter lays out a "summary anatomy" of this longer crisis running back to 1720: the end of a century-old Afro-Eurasian peace, the collapse of the Safavid and Mughal empires, and a shared "military-fiscal" trap that caught agrarian Asian empires and European monarchies alike as war costs rose faster than taxable revenue.

Themes

A single global system with feedback loops running in both directions, not parallel local stories. Bayly's opening move is architectural: 1756-63 warfare "hastened the crisis of the old regimes in Europe," the aftershocks of the European revolutions then "deepened the crisis of the old order" in the Americas, Asia, and North Africa, and those extra-European convulsions "fed back into the European convulsions" in turn — a loop he closes with Napoleon's own remark that Waterloo was lost in India.

Rights language as a self-evident, freestanding claim that became instantly portable. The "No taxation without representation" and "rights of man" formulas were received as needing no external warrant — "no king, no divine authority, no imperial interest, no superiority of race or creed could nullify them" — which let Caribbean slaves, Bengali readers of Paine, and Latin American revolutionaries around simon-bolivar each seize the same doctrine for locally specific emancipations that Bayly treats as instances of one idea rather than scattered imitation of Europe.

Non-European crises of legitimacy predate and run parallel to the Atlantic revolutions, rather than merely absorbing their shockwaves. Sikh dharma-politics against Mughal power, Wahhabi purism against the Ottoman and Cairene establishments, and Chinese sectarians denying the Qing emperor the Mandate of Heaven all surface from the 1710s-1760s, before or alongside 1776 and 1789. Bayly's point is that "provincials, cherishing old, godly beliefs and resisting intrusive empires" were already producing their own "new order of the ages" independently of Europe.

The military-fiscal trap catches agrarian empires and European monarchies from opposite directions. Rising costs of war — new firearms, engineered fortification, European- and Ottoman-style armies — collided with revenue that could not be raised without provoking revolt: raise land taxes and peasants desert or rebel, expand the frontier and buy a costly new war, farm out revenue to strongmen or foreign mercenaries and lose control of it. Aurangzeb's Deccan wars are Bayly's paradigm case for the Mughal state described in mughal-administration, and "much the same problem faced all the other Afro-Asian regimes." European governments hit the identical bind from the other side — unable to tax landlords without political risk or the urban poor without riot — which is what drove "enlightened despot" reform experiments even as naval costs, Frederick the Great's firepower discipline, and eventually Napoleon's mass levée tightened the vice further with each round.

The Safavid and Mughal collapse ends a century of Afro-Eurasian peace and hands Europeans their opening. The "long peace" of 1660-1720 unravels first in the Middle East and South Asia, not Europe: the Safavid regime falls apart after 1722 and the Mughal center fragments, sacked by Persian and Afghan armies in 1739 and 1759. The resulting turbulence stretching "from Mesopotamia in the west to Thailand in the east" is what lets the English East India Company seize Bengal (1757-65) and "build an army in India," tilting the world balance of power well before industrialization. Ottoman decline is more gradual — Egypt effectively independent through the century — but converges on the same fiscal-military vice.

The Seven Years War is the hinge where regional turbulences fuse into one crisis. Bayly dates the moment discrete "pockets of turbulence in Eurasia and the Americas flowed together" to the 1760s: the war pushes Spain into risky colonial reform, sharpens Anglo-French rivalry into a fight for Bengal and North America, and leaves Britain's post-1763 taxation of the American colonies as the proximate trigger of the American Revolution — whose debt-financed French intervention then becomes, "ironically," the fiscal crisis that undoes the French monarchy itself, feeding into the Estates General, the Girondin-Jacobin split, and the Terror of 1793. The free-trade philosopher nicolas-de-condorcet is cited as an example of how directly the American example was read onto French soil.

A resonance worth flagging, with a hedge. Several old-regime legitimacies collapsing at overlapping moments — Safavid, Mughal, Ottoman, Qing, French Bourbon — while no successor order is yet consolidated anywhere rhymes with the WMP gramsci-gap: an interregnum where the old dies before the new can be born. The fit is partial, though: Bayly's is a decades-long, acutely contingent fiscal-military mechanism operating inside a single generation, not the centuries-long Dawn/Day/Dusk cycle the concept was built to describe, and he offers a specific causal chain (war costs outrunning revenue) rather than a structural law of machine succession.

Notable passages

The opening feedback-loop passage (c1) states the chapter's central architectural claim — that European and extra-European crises drove each other in both directions — compressed into Bayly's own closing image of Waterloo being lost in India. The rights-of-man passage (c2) isolates the specific ideological mechanism, self-evidence and freestandingness, that let the doctrine travel without local warrant. The Sikh/Wahhabi/Qing passage (c3) is Bayly's clearest statement that non-European legitimacy crises were not simply belated reactions to Atlantic events. The Aurangzeb passage (c4) is the paradigm case for the "military-fiscal" conundrum Bayly argues trapped every major Asian and Ottoman regime. The Seven Years War passage (c5) marks the moment Bayly identifies as when separate regional crises "flowed together" into one global convulsion. The Bengal passage (c6) names the concrete event — the East India Company's seizure of Bengal — that Bayly credits with tilting the world balance of power before industrialization had properly begun.
