Representation, Positivism, and Socialism: Universal Doctrines, Local Refractions

Commons · The Birth of the Modern World · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Popular sovereignty, secular positivism, and socialism each spread across the nineteenth-century world as ostensibly universal doctrines, but were everywhere narrowed, contested, or reshaped by existing hierarchies, religious traditions, and local political idioms.

Bayly traces three nineteenth-century intellectual exports — the theory of popular sovereignty and representative government, secular/positivist philosophy, and socialism — and shows that everywhere they landed, from Britain and France to India, Japan, China, the Ottoman lands, and the Americas, they were narrowed, reinterpreted, or fused with pre-existing hierarchies and religious idioms rather than adopted wholesale.

Themes

Popular sovereignty rarely meant popular government. From Locke onward, theorists who demolished divine-right monarchy nonetheless defended narrow, property-weighted franchises; Britain's Parliament kept a "tiny electorate" until 1867, the Lords kept a veto into the twentieth century, and even the most liberal continental states built in checks that preserved elite and bureaucratic control over a nominally sovereign people. The gap between the principle (proclaimed from around 1700) and its institutional realization (adult male suffrage barely achieved anywhere before 1914, women's suffrage later still) stretches across two centuries — a slow-motion version of an old legitimacy dying while the new one struggles to be born, though Bayly frames it as calculated elite caution rather than a gramsci-gap-style crisis.

Race, empire, and gender supplied the era's working exclusions. The United States enfranchised white men broadly while blocking black suffrage into the twentieth century; British and French colonial administrations created token native councils and electorates — "sops" to nationalist opinion, "adjuncts" to taxation, instruments of "divide and rule" — while liberal imperialists including John Stuart Mill denied colonized peoples any capacity for self-rule, a bind Indian reformers could only escape by the fiction of being honorary "Aryans." Women were excluded almost everywhere on grounds argued as political principle (social dependence, incapacity for independent judgment), not merely prejudice; the rare frontier suffrage grants (Wyoming, Utah, New Zealand, South Australia) were framed as putting women in charge of civilizing men, not as recognition of individual rights.

Non-Western societies received popular sovereignty through their own consultative traditions, not onto a blank slate: Islamic shura, Confucian gentry councils, and Brahmin advisory bodies all supplied a prior grammar that recast what an "elected assembly" could mean — as in Nakae Chomin's fusion of Rousseau with Confucian veneration of the Meiji emperor, or Kang Youwei's parallel synthesis in China.

Positivism replaced theology with a scientific philosophy of history, but travelled unpredictably. Auguste Comte's stage theory (theological, then metaphysical, then positive) fed directly into Durkheim's functionalist sociology and, Bayly argues, into Marx's own claim to scientific status. Yet as positivism spread it mutated: French devotees turned it into a "religion of humanity" with its own liturgy, British adherents made it resemble an austere Protestant sect, and in India it became a resource for Hindus to reject priestly authority while preserving caste — the near-opposite of its anticlerical purpose in Europe. In Russia, Japan, and Latin America it was redeployed as an anti-autocratic or anti-feudal weapon, sometimes in service of the modernizing military strongmen it should logically have unsettled.

Socialism's claim to be "scientific," like positivism's, concealed a deeply millenarian and locally particular substance. Much early socialism was Christian and apocalyptic before it was Marxist, and each national "subcenter" grew from a distinct prior tradition: French insurrectionism (Blanqui, henri-de-saint-simon) rooted in Jacobin memory; British "feudal socialism" rooted in Nonconformist religiosity and nostalgia for guild labor, which the Labour Party inherited more than it inherited Marx; German Marxism itself emerging from Pietist revivalist hopes for a "reform of consciousness"; Russian socialism resonating with peasant communalism and the Old Believer sect; and Chinese radicals reaching for Confucian moral-economy language (the "well-field" system) and memories of the Taiping and Boxer risings rather than European class theory.

The chapter's closing claim ties all three doctrines to one intellectual rupture. Liberalism, secularism, and socialism all broke, after roughly 1700, from the older idiom of a corrupted republic or a badly-advised king toward the claim that the people alone, in the present, constitute legitimacy. But in popular translation this new claim did not stay secular or novel — it fused with millenarian Christian, Buddhist, and Hindu expectations of a coming better world, which is precisely why the doctrines took such different shapes as they moved. The pattern rhymes, with real hedges, with the modernity-machine's export of legibility-as-machine-core: a universal calculating category (the electorate, Comte's stages, Marx's "iron laws") travels outward from Europe, but is never received as pure uniformity — each locality re-encodes it against its own prior order. Bayly's own name for this is "contested uniformity," not world-machines theory, but the rhyme between legible export and local re-embedding is close.

Notable passages

The opening paragraph states the chapter's organizing paradox directly: theorists who won the argument for popular sovereignty in principle spent the rest of the century engineering ways to keep government out of the people's actual hands, ending "not in their degree of fear of uncontrolled democracy, but simply in the degree to which they were prepared to allow popular representation at all."

The passage on Nakae Chomin and Kang Youwei shows non-Western liberals reaching for indigenous consultative idioms rather than importing representative theory wholesale, closing on Kang Youwei's own 1898 "hundred days" reform effort.

The paragraph on women's exclusion insists the bar was theorized, not just assumed, and that even the frontier exceptions served patriarchal logics — reformers "were mainly concerned with the rights of subject populations and of nations, not those of individuals."

The Comte passage lays out positivism's three-stage philosophy of history and draws the direct line from Comte through Durkheim to Marx's own "parallel evolution."

The passage on socialism's "vague and heterogeneous" character argues that its scientific self-image obscured an apocalyptic, often literally Christian, substrate — a claim Bayly uses to explain why socialism rooted so unevenly from one society to the next.

The closing paragraph is the unit's thesis statement in miniature: liberalism, secularism, and socialism shared one rupture with the past, yet each was re-conjoined with older millenarian idioms, "Buddhist, and even Hindu ideas, which predicted the coming of a better life," the moment it reached ordinary political discourse.
