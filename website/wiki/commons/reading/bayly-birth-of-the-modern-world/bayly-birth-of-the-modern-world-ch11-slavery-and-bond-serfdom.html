The Reinforcement of Bondage: Slavery's Persistence and the Peasant as Bond Serf

Commons · The Birth of the Modern World · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Slavery and coerced peasant labor did not fade before nineteenth-century modernity but were reinforced, relegitimated, and in places reinvented—through global legal and religious sanction, continuing market demand, indentured-labor substitution, and the deepening reach of indigenous and colonial states—across the Atlantic world, the Islamic world, and Asia.

This unit argues that unfree and semi-free labor did not recede before nineteenth-century modernity but was actively reinforced, relegitimated, and in places reinvented by it. The first half tracks chattel slavery's 'Indian summer': its continuing legal and religious legitimation worldwide, its Atlantic peak in the 1780s, the limited and even counterproductive effect of British abolition, the cliometric debate over slavery's compatibility with capitalism in the U.S. South, and its mutation into indentured 'coolie' labor and hardening racial ideology after formal emancipation. The second half pivots—via a comparison of a freed Jamaican slave and a rent-crushed Indian peasant—to argue that nominally free rural populations across the world (Latin American debt peons, Chinese, Egyptian and Javanese peasants, Tokugawa/Meiji Japan's outcaste kawata, Indian 'untouchables') were bound into deepening dependency by population pressure, adverse terms of trade, and above all by the strengthening reach of the modern fiscal-administrative state, whether indigenous or colonial.

Themes

Slavery's nineteenth-century survival rested on near-universal legal and religious legitimation, not merely Atlantic economic inertia. Aristotelian and Roman natural-slavery doctrine, a Church that had nodded and winked at bondage, Islamic captivity law, Buddhist appeals to karmic 'cosmic retribution,' a revived Hindu-Buddhist legal code in the Burmese kingdom of Ava, and a reinvigorated Catholic hierarchy in Brazil and Cuba all supplied cover—so slavery's persistence past 1780 cannot be read simply as a lag behind universalizing Enlightenment norms.

The slave trade's numerical peak, and the limits of abolition, both sit inside the supposed era of Enlightenment breakthrough. Bayly stresses that the 1780s were the all-time high point of the Atlantic slave trade, and that Britain's 1807 abolition—evaded by Spanish and Portuguese traders and unenforceable against African polities such as Asante and Dahomey—plausibly increased internal African slaveholding, especially of women, rather than ending the trade.

Plantation slavery in the U.S. South was defended both as efficient capitalism and as anti-capitalist patriarchy. The Fogel/Engerman cliometric thesis (slavery as materially 'diverse,' productive, even relatively humane) and Eugene Genovese's counter-emphasis on slaveholders' self-image as biblical patriarchs resisting manufactures and 'mechanical or manufacturing classes' are presented as complementary rather than opposed explanations for its survival.

Formal abolition produced functional substitutes for slavery rather than freedom. Post-emancipation 'apprenticeship,' and above all the global indentured-labor system that Hugh Tinker (quoting Lord John Russell) called 'a new system of slavery,' moved millions of Indians, Chinese, and others across empires—into the Caribbean, Natal and Cape Colony (where indentured Indians found, Bayly notes, their champion in the young M. K. Gandhi mahatma-gandhi), Burma, and the Americas—under debt-bound conditions Bayly likens directly to bondage. Racial ideology shifted in step, from a biblical curse to a 'scientific' hierarchy of races that justified the same labor extraction in new terms.

The unit's second half argues that free peasants could be as bound as slaves, for reasons that rival explanatory frameworks contest. Boeke's 'dual economy' (a backward rural sector resisting dynamic capitalism) and the dependency/world-systems theory of André Gunder Frank and Immanuel Wallerstein (rural 'underdevelopment' as a product, not an absence, of capitalist expansion) are set against each other, alongside population pressure and adverse terms of trade, as competing or complementary causes of deepening peasant subordination.

State strengthening—legible, taxing, categorizing government—deepened hierarchy as much as markets did. Egypt's corvée labor on the Suez Canal, Meiji Japan's aggressive tax extraction, Tokugawa and Meiji regulation of the outcaste kawata, and British codification of Indian caste custom all show administratively modernizing states hardening status distinctions rather than dissolving them. Bayly makes the parallel explicit, comparing Herman Ooms's account of Japan's kawata to Eugene Genovese's account of the American South, and David Washbrook's argument that colonial rule 'traditionalized'—rather than merely preserved—Indian caste hierarchy.

This last theme resonates, with real limits, with the World Machines idea that legibility-building state infrastructure (legibility-as-machine-core) is not intrinsically emancipatory: Bayly's cases show the modernizing, more legible state hardening rather than dissolving inherited status categories. The fit is partial—Bayly's own frame is a historian's 'reconstitution of hierarchies,' not a machine-theoretic claim about a world machine's construction phase, and he treats each case (Egypt, Japan, India) as institutionally specific rather than as evidence of one infrastructural logic.

Notable passages

The unit opens by insisting slavery's endurance had global sanction: Islamic and Buddhist norms, Bayly writes, 'bore family resemblances to the European slavery of the classical world,' alongside Aristotelian, Roman, and Church legitimation—a claim that forecloses reading nineteenth-century slavery as mere holdover.

Bayly's demographic evidence undercuts any tidy Enlightenment-vs-slavery story: citing P. E. Lovejoy's transport figures, he notes the 1780s 'appears to have been the all-time peak of slave trading,' the same decade normally cast as modernity's dawn.

On the U.S. South, Genovese's reading of planter ideology is given real weight: the plantation is described as 'an idyllic world of rural community,' with a slaveholder quoted resisting 'the vicious class conflict and anomie of North American and European capitalism'—bondage defended as a bulwark against modernity even while feeding capitalist cotton markets.

The section break pivots on Sir Charles Metcalfe's jarring comparison: a freed Jamaican slave population that seemed to him better fed than the Indian peasant 'staggering under the weight of rent and land revenue payments'—the image that licenses treating peasant dependency as a form of bond-serfdom. (Bayly's later discussion of demographic pressure explicitly invokes the Annales-school paradigm associated with fernand-braudel.)

The theoretical stakes of the peasant section are set by crediting dependency theory to André Gunder Frank and 'developed as a historical model by Immanuel Wallerstein,' against Boeke's rival 'dual economy' diagnosis.

The unit's clearest statement that state power, not only markets, manufactures hierarchy comes via Herman Ooms on Tokugawa/Meiji Japan: officials 'puts more stress on the role of the state in extending subordination' of the outcaste kawata, a dynamic Bayly explicitly likens to 'the racism of Europe or the Americas' and to Genovese's account of the American South—tying the unit's two halves into a single argument about the reconstitution of hierarchy.
