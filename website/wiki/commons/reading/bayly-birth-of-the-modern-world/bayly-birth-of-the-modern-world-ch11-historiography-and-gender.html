The Reconstitution of Hierarchies: Historiography of Continuity and Gender in the Liberal Age

Commons · The Birth of the Modern World · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Opening Bayly's chapter on the survival of old-regime hierarchies, the unit stages a historiographical debate over continuity versus change in the long nineteenth century — from whiggish narratives of inevitable progress to Arno Mayer's ‘persistence of the old regime’ thesis and its revisionist afterlife in Qing and Ottoman history — then argues through the case of gender relations that liberal-age economic, legal, and medical change more often entrenched than dissolved patriarchal subordination worldwide.

This unit opens Bayly's Chapter 11, "The Reconstitution of Social Hierarchies," by defining hierarchy as domination and subordination legitimated by honor, embodied worth, or divine dispensation, and by arguing that old-regime forms of hierarchy — monarchic and oligarchic-republican alike — survived and were often deliberately embellished across the nineteenth century rather than dissolved by liberalism, science, and industrial modernity. It then works through two of the chapter's building blocks in turn: a historiographical survey ("Change and the Historians") of the scholarly debate over how much actually changed, and a first case study ("Gender and Subordination in the ‘Liberal Age’") arguing that relations between men and women barely loosened, and in important respects tightened, across the century.

Themes

Historiography is itself a moving target, not a neutral backdrop. Bayly traces a shift from "whiggish" national narratives (explaining 1917, Nazism, or Labour's rise as endpoints toward which social process was already tending) to Arno Mayer's 1981 The Persistence of the Old Regime, which argued 1914 Europe resembled 1789 more than 1945 and that war, not the nineteenth century's "wordy social movements," made the modern world. Bayly then notes a further turn after 1989, when the Soviet collapse discredited revolution-centered narratives generally, and historians of the Qing and Ottoman empires began reappraising "hollow crown" and "Sick Man of Europe" readings as retrospective illusions produced by hindsight from the eventual collapse.

Hierarchies survived because they were useful or valued, not merely inherited. The chapter's organizing thesis, stated directly, is that people did not simply fail to dismantle old hierarchies out of inertia; elites selected them for survival because they held together relationships growing "more uniform yet also more complex," and sometimes hierarchies persisted for no functional reason at all, "simply because people valued them." This is Bayly's own version of the book's recurring paradox of "contested uniformity": modernizing, standardizing pressures do not simply erase older forms of domination, they can give elites fresh reasons to keep them.

Colonial rule preserved archaic power rather than modernizing it. Across Europe, the extra-European empires, and nominally independent states (Qing China, the Ottoman Empire, Meiji Japan), the unit surveys evidence that urbanization stalled, living standards may have fallen, and colonial administrations (Lugard's and Lyautey's "indirect rule") propped up sultans, rajas, and marabouts rather than replacing them, while slavery gave way not to freedom but to indenture, tribal labor reserves, and domestic servitude.

Gender is the deepest test case of reconstituted, not dissolved, hierarchy. Against the mid-twentieth-century assumption that economic change would break down extended-family patriarchy and equalize the sexes, Bayly finds formal family structures largely unchanged, and argues that where change did occur — male labor migration into mines and factories, the medicalization of women's bodies via "hysteria" and gynecology, colonial law codifying male family headship — it tended to deepen women's dependence rather than loosen it. Elite Western women's gains (suffrage, university degrees, property rights) were real but narrow and slow; non-Western women's status, Bayly suggests, may well have declined.

Reform was frequently a discursive weapon, not a remedy. Missionary and colonial campaigns against infanticide, child marriage, footbinding, and genital mutilation are shown to have functioned largely as arguments for the "civilizing" legitimacy of imperialism rather than as sustained efforts on women's behalf — in Bayly's own words, "discursive offensives by Europeans against ‘depraved natives’" — in contrast with more targeted indigenous reform efforts such as Mohamed Abduh's in Egypt and the Meiji school system.

Notable passages

The Mayer paragraph is the historiographical hinge of the whole unit: it names the book (The Persistence of the Old Regime) and the claim (1914 looked more like 1789 than 1945) that reorients the chapter away from a story of liberal triumph and toward one of survival and reconstitution, while also tracing Mayer's own intellectual target back to de Tocqueville's reading of the French Revolution's consequences.

The definitional passage on "hierarchies" matters because the whole chapter, and much of the rest of Part IV, depends on this specific sense of the word — domination legitimated by honor, worth, or divine sanction — rather than a loose sense of "social stratification"; it also does the work of including oligarchic republics (Venice, Hungary, the Netherlands) and non-European chiefly councils inside the same analytic category as monarchies.

The "part of the answer" passage states the chapter's causal thesis in its own terms: hierarchies were selected for survival because they held together a world becoming simultaneously more uniform and more complex, with a residual admission that some survived for no functional reason, simply because they were valued. This is the clearest single statement of the mechanism the rest of the chapter will apply to labor, aristocracy, monarchy, and, first, gender.

The closing paradox of the gender section — "vaunted modernity may in some respects have re-empowered hierarchy" — generalizes the chapter's argument from gender outward, proposing that women's subordination was not incidental to the survival of "other helot classes, especially slaves and peasant bondsmen" but causally linked to it: keeping female labor and reproduction subordinated was itself part of the infrastructure that kept other hierarchies standing.

The Age of Consent Bill passage is the unit's sharpest evidence that "women's issues" reform could be a discursive instrument of empire rather than a genuine remedy: the 1882 Indian bill against child marriage is quoted defending itself in the language of "raising the physical, moral and mental status of the native" and stopping "evils which sap national vigour and morality" — imperial self-justification dressed as protection.

Two resonances with the World Machines frame are worth naming, both partial. Colonial regimes fixing "male family heads" into written law is a small-scale instance of the drive toward legibility-as-machine-core — rendering a population's kinship legible and administrable — but where legibility-cuts-both-ways documents legibility artifacts escaping the control of the regime that commissioned them, here the codified categories stayed gripped by the hand that wrote them, hardening rather than loosening domination; the direction of the "cuts both ways" claim runs the other way in this case. Separately, the historiographical arc from confident "hollow crown" declinism to a later reappraisal of Qing and Ottoman resilience only visible after (or just before) their actual collapse faintly echoes the history-machine idea of retroactive legibility — though Bayly's subject here is historians revising their own paradigms, not the object-level assembly of a historical era, so the fit is loose.
