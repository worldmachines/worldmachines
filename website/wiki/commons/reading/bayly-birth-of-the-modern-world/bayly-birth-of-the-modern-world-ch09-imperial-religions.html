Formalizing Authority and Expanding "Imperial Religions"

Commons · The Birth of the Modern World · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Facing ancien-régime fragmentation and partial expulsion from state power, nineteenth-century world religions centralized authority, fixed doctrine into scripture-like canons, and expanded aggressively through missions and print, converging organizationally with the modern nation-state as “imperial religions.”

Bayly argues that the nineteenth century's major religions responded to two shocks — the "ancien régime" fragmentation of religious jurisdiction and their partial expulsion from state power — by centralizing authority, fixing doctrine into scripture-like canons, and expanding aggressively through missions, print, and reform associations, so that Hinduism, Confucianism, Islam, and the Christian churches converged, functionally, on the same organizational form as the modern bureaucratic nation-state.

Themes

Ancien-régime jurisdiction gives way under the global crisis of 1780–1820. Bayly opens by insisting that pre-modern religious authority — loose, overlapping, riven by rival claims to succession and by contradictory local jurists' rulings — was as disordered as ancien-régime politics, and that this disorder was "further confounded" by the era's political convulsions, which forced religious leaderships to seek "clearer lines of authority" before doctrine or dealings with the state and secularism could be organized at all.

Disestablishment is a paradoxical tonic, not simply a defeat. Across the United States, Britain, and Catholic Europe, religion's loss of formal political power — the First Amendment, the repeal of the Test Acts, the loss of Church lands and legal privileges in France and Italy — freed clergy to become "guardians of the poor" and custodians of purely spiritual authority, unencumbered by association with aristocratic privilege or taxation. Colonial rule produced the same effect more drastically: Islamic and Buddhist legal officers were absorbed into secular colonial law codes, and the Meiji state suppressed Buddhism in favor of a manufactured State Shinto.

Religions formalize doctrine on the model of scriptural religions. Papal Infallibility (1870), the Vatican's centralized priest-training, the Anglican Communion's Lambeth conferences, and Sunni Islam's Dars-i-Nizamiya curriculum and al-Azhar network all represent a convergent move: loosely bounded traditions acquire fixed canons, credentialed hierarchies, and periodic congresses that manufacture "a surprising degree of common purpose" where none had been institutionally guaranteed before.

"Confucianism" and "Hinduism" are substantially nineteenth-century constructions. Missionaries (James Legge) and reformers (Kang Youwei, Swami Dayananda) each treated an inherited, diffuse body of texts and practices as if it were a bounded "religion" comparable to Christianity — Confucius recast as an "uncrowned king," the Vedas as scripture for a manufactured Hindu church — precisely because a defensible national or civilizational religion was needed to compete with, and rebuff, an aggressive missionary Christianity.

Missionary expansion is uneven and politically contingent. Christianity's spectacular nineteenth-century growth in Africa, the Pacific, and among American slaves and Native peoples ran through Sufi and Christian networks alike, was frequently captured by local chiefs for their own legitimacy, and only later became self-sustaining as mission education produced literate, socially mobile converts. It made almost no headway in India, China, or Japan, where powerful literate religious hierarchies simply "write back" using the missionaries' own print methods — producing purifying Hindu shuddhi sabhas and YMCA-modeled Buddhist associations rather than conversions.

Empires of religion mirror nation-states. Bayly's closing move makes the chapter's stakes explicit: reformed world religions "spawned larger bureaucracies," policed syncretism the way states policed dual loyalty, and rode the same infrastructure of print, transport, and communications that built the modern state. This resonates strongly with World Machines' account of legibility-as-machine-core — the making of populations, doctrines, and jurisdictions countable, bounded, and administrable — extended here from the state into the religious sphere during the modernity-machine's consolidating "Day," though Bayly's own frame is institutional and comparative history, not machine theory. The fusing of "Confucianism," "Hinduism," and Orthodox Slavophilism into markers of national or racial essence also rhymes with benedict-anderson's account of print-mediated imagined communities, except that here the imagined community is confessional as much as national — Bayly's chapter is in effect the religious half of Anderson's story. The Ottoman state's push to install "men loyal to the state" at the head of the religious hierarchy is a case of a state instrumentalizing the ulama rather than the reverse.

Notable passages

The chapter opens by describing an "ancien régime in religion" — jurisdictions as tangled as pre-revolutionary politics — undone by "the global crisis of 1780—1820 and the new doctrines it unleashed" (c1), which frames formalization as a reaction to crisis rather than a natural unfolding. The paradox that disestablishment strengthened rather than weakened clerical authority is captured directly: the churches, cut loose from state privilege, became better able to serve "people whom the state could hardly reach" (c2). Papal Infallibility (1870) is Bayly's most concrete case of doctrinal centralization matching state-style bureaucratization (c3). The invention of "Confucianism" as a bounded religion — with Confucius as Kang Youwei's "uncrowned king" — shows reformers reverse-engineering a national religion to compete with Christian missionary claims (c4). The passage on pre-1780 "Hinduism" is the chapter's sharpest constructivist claim, insisting that what educated Hindus and colonial law alike later treated as one religion had been, at best, "a huge extended family" of loosely related beliefs and practices (c5). Finally, the chapter's closing thesis draws the whole argument together: reformed "empires of religion" resembled nation-states in their pursuit of unified authority, larger bureaucracies, and suppressed syncretism, riding the same technological and communications infrastructure — Bayly's plainest statement that "the processes of uniformity were working at world level" (c6).
