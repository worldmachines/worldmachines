The Riddle of the Modern: Uniformity, the Body, and Growing Complexity

Commons · The Birth of the Modern World · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Bayly answers the historiographical "riddle of the modern" by treating modernity partly as a self-conscious aspiration people between 1780 and 1914 shared, then traces its clearest evidence in a contested global convergence of bodily practice — dress, timekeeping, naming, language, food, and sport — running alongside a parallel deepening of internal social specialization.

The unit opens with Bayly's third methodological "problem": what does it mean to call 1780–1914 the age of "the modern"? He surveys the historiography — Eisenstadt's and Weber-descended modernization theory, its 1980s collapse under demographic, economic, and religious counter-evidence, the turn to "multiple modernities," Latour's "we were never modern," and hold-outs (Gellner, Macfarlane, Landes) who still insist on a real "riddle of the modern" — before proposing his own resolution: modernity is in part an aspiration, a belief that one is "up with the times," which does not by itself prove a real structural shift but which he then argues did correspond to real, rapid, interacting change. The chapter then descends from this abstract debate to concrete, embodied evidence: "Conforming to Standards: Bodily Practice" tracks the global convergence of dress, and "Building Outward from the Body: Communications and Complexity" extends the argument to language, naming, food, sport, print, and telegraphy, closing with Bayly's second organizing claim — that outward uniformity between societies grew in tandem with inward complexity and specialization within them.

Themes

Modernity as aspiration, not only measurable trend. Bayly's pivot move is to accept "that an essential part of being modern is thinking you are modern" (c2) — a claim he uses to defuse the Eisenstadt-vs-Latour standoff (c1) without abandoning the idea that something real changed. People from Bengal to Fiji adopted "badges of this mythical modernity, whether these were fob watches, umbrellas, or new religious texts" because they believed doing so improved their status, not only because objective conditions forced it. This lets Bayly hold two claims at once: modernity is partly performative belief, and it is also borne out in recoverable political, social, and economic trends.

Contested uniformity in dress. Between 1780 and 1914 elite male dress converges dramatically on a Western template — Chinese mandarin robes and Pacific "ritualized undress" give way to the top hat and black morning coat worn by Chinese nationalists and Meiji Japan's leaders alike (c3). But Bayly insists "uniformity is not the same as homogeneity": Maori chiefs pair a black coat and bow tie with their tattoos, and Geronimo poses in a suit as readily as with a rifle (c4). Convergence is uneven by class (poor and peasant dress lagged), by region (colonial administrators sometimes enforced "indigenous dress" to mark racial inferiority), and by gender — women's clothing remained "ornamental and impractical" for longer, and even a garment read today as traditionalist, the burkah, is presented as itself a modern innovation that let women enter public and commercial life. The process was also actively contested: a Muslim Ottoman conservative denounced a hybrid-uniformed army as "a grotesque parody of Europe."

The body as a site of state and religious legibility. Beyond dress, Bayly follows uniformity into timekeeping — pocket watches, plantation bells keeping slaves "to the time of the master's watch," telegraph-synchronized regional time, and municipal clock towers built where temples or mosques once anchored civic life (c5) — and into naming and language: Hindi/Urdu sentence structure converging on English models, Creole and Swahili acquiring formal grammars, and personal names standardizing around Vishnu's avatars, the Prophet and Ayesha, or slavery-imposed Christian names, driven equally by state bureaucracies ("tag and docket people for taxation and military service") and by ordinary people's need to use state forms for relief, education, or emigration. This resonates strongly with legibility-as-machine-core — the body, its clothing, its name, and its clock become surfaces the state (and rival religious/commercial institutions) render legible and interchangeable — though Bayly's causal story is broader than pure state coercion, crediting fashion, advertising, missionary work, and self-fashioned aspiration as much as bureaucratic demand.

Uniformity and internal complexity grow together. Bayly's "second major theme" is that as societies converged outwardly, they differentiated inwardly (c6): specialist legal, medical, financial, and administrative professions split off from kinship and from the older military/religious poles of authority, even in societies (Ottoman Middle East, Japan, Chinese treaty ports) with no prior tradition of such separation. Global communication accelerated the outward half of this — newspaper titles worldwide grew from roughly 3,168 in 1828 to 31,026 by 1900, and the telegraph let Irish, Indian, Egyptian, and Chinese nationalists correspond and meet, "globalizing" nationalism itself (a thread worth tracking under nationalism) — while cautioning that "formal similarity and mutual translatability still often masked significant difference in intrinsic style," as when the 1893 World Parliament of Religions in Chicago let traditions be formally ranked as "religions" without erasing what a common religious rite meant differently across Islam, Hinduism, and Christianity.

Notable passages

  • c1 — the riddle-of-the-modern debate. Bruno Latour's "We were never modern" stands against Gellner, Macfarlane, and david-s-landes, who "resolutely insisted on the reality of...the once-and-for-all step forward of mankind"; Bayly stages this as the live disagreement his own account has to answer.
  • c2 — modernity as aspiration. The book's thesis sentence: being modern is partly a matter of "thinking you are modern," an aspiration and "a process of emulation and borrowing" rather than a fact settled only by structural measurement.
  • c3 — dress convergence, 1780→1914. The clearest single before/after image in the unit: mandarin robes and frock coats give way to a worldwide elite uniform of top hat and morning coat.
  • c4 — uniformity vs. homogeneity. The conceptual safety valve that keeps the whole argument from collapsing into a simple Westernization story — convergent form can carry, or be paired with, preserved distinctiveness.
  • c5 — timekeeping as bodily discipline. From fob watch to plantation bell to telegraph-synchronized clock tower, this passage is the unit's most legible (in the Scott sense) infrastructural through-line, tracing time-discipline being layered directly onto the body.
  • c6 — the second theme stated directly. Names, in Bayly's own words, the pairing this whole unit exists to establish: outward uniformity and inward complexity are not opposed but co-produced.

The unit's resonance with modernity-machine is real but partial: Bayly's "birth of the modern world" names a step-change beginning around 1780, close to what WMP would place inside the Day of an already-built (1200–1600) world-machines-style Modernity Machine rather than its Dawn — a periodization mismatch worth flagging rather than smoothing over. More productive is the fit with legibility-and-divergence: Bayly's "contested uniformity" — convergence that simultaneously "heightened difference and antagonism" — is close kin to WMP's legibility-vs-divergence tension, though Bayly keeps the two forces braided within the same bodies and institutions rather than treating them as opposed machines. mahatma-gandhi appears briefly and concretely: he "briefly considered the idea of a meat diet to build up Indians whom he thought had been made 'effeminate' by imperialism," an aspiration-to-modern-strength he and his generation later rejected.
