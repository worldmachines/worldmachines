Ideological Origins of the Modern State, Nationalism, and the Third Revolution, 1780–1830

Commons · The Birth of the Modern World · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Revolutionary and Napoleonic-era ideologies of universal reason, racial hierarchy, and “reason of state” rearmed governments even as they hardened rival nationalities and launched a worldwide “third revolution” of commercial middle classes, leaving the post-1815 order an unresolved equipoise rather than a restoration.

This unit covers three interlocking developments in the wake of the American, French, and Haitian revolutions and the Napoleonic wars: the ideological “rearmament” of the state, which learns to wield popular sovereignty, racial hierarchy, and “reason of state” for its own ends; the crystallization of fluid patriotisms into modern nationalities across Europe, Asia, and Africa; and a “third, quieter revolution” — the worldwide rise of a polite, commercial middle class that precedes rather than follows industrialization. A closing “Prospect” section frames 1815–1830 as an unresolved equipoise rather than a genuine restoration.

Themes

The revolutionary age armed the state more than it armed the people. Bayly's central irony: popular sovereignty, antislavery agitation, and demands for the franchise spread as a portable “cultural template” of “the people”, yet within a generation it was the state, not the crowd, that absorbed the most durable ideological gains. “Universal rights of man” slid into “universal standards of enlightenment and benevolence” that any “invigorated state” — Napoleonic France, Muhammad Ali's Egypt, post-Bourbon Latin America — could invoke as “reason of state”. The American Revolution, exceptionally, stayed “a revolution against the state” because the new republic faced no prolonged war for survival after 1783.

Radicalism provoked its own corporatist and reactionary countercurrents. Edmund Burke (edmund-burke) supplies Bayly's example of the conservative reading of 1789 as mob tyranny and Terror; more interesting to Bayly is how artisans and guild members, their status undermined by revolutionary economic change, generated protectionist and corporatist ideologies rather than simply liberal ones — communal, sometimes anti-Semitic reaction in Europe; Indian swadeshi demands to protect “our own valued products”; Islamic purist and anti-Western artisan movements in Egypt and the Ottoman lands. These are framed as constructive claims to “honor, civility, and modernity” in their own right, not merely defensive reflexes.

Enlightenment universalism was reworked into a ranked civilizational ladder. Drawing on adam-smith and William Robertson, administrators recast “the whole map of mankind” as a single scale of commercial and political development, with tribal peoples and Africans at the bottom, Islamic, Hindu, and Chinese societies arrested at a Greco-Roman stage, and “superior races” alone at the summit. This was administrative ideology rather than yet biological race-science, and it licensed the “improving” interventions — Cornwallis's Permanent Settlement in Bengal, French rule in Italy and Algeria — that were as much land-grab and taxation regime as tutelage. The parallel state projects of surveying, mapping, and encyclopaedic classification (the Survey of India, Napoleon's savants in Egypt, Joseph Banks's networks) read as a clear instance of legibility-as-machine-core — rendering populations and territory countable and comparable in order to extend control — though Bayly frames much of it as “declaratory” prestige-knowledge for the conquering power as much as a direct instrument of rule.

Nationalities crystallized as an unplanned byproduct of conquest, occupation, and circulating news. Napoleonic occupation turned fluid eighteenth-century patriotisms into hard-edged nationalism: Mazzini's Italy, Goethe's German Volk, a Russian peasantry “discovered” on the 1812 battlefield, Polish identity forged under partition, a specifically Catholic Irish identity displacing Protestant patriotism after the Union. Bayly insists this was global, not only European — Moroccan and Algerian jihad against a Napoleonic “Crusade,” Muhammad Ali's cultivated Egyptian identity, and, most strikingly, Bengali intellectuals around Ram Mohun Roy who read newspaper accounts of Irish and Genoese struggles and reasoned that if those peoples had rights, “surely there must be rights for ‘Indians’ as well”. That chain — news of one people's claim licensing another's self-conception — rhymes with benedict-anderson's account of print-mediated imagined community, though Bayly's mechanism here is transnational news circulation among literate elites rather than a vernacular mass-reading public bound by simultaneity.

A worldwide “third revolution” of commercial middling classes preceded industrial revolution proper. Modern industry was still confined to a corner of England; the real driver was reorganized consumption and associational life plus small-scale artisanal intensification (an accelerated “industrious revolution”, after Jan de Vries) — the explosion of American business corporations and inland settlement, Hamburg's post-1783 commercial revival, an emergent “international civil society” of literate clubs and libraries in Calcutta, Bombay, Penang, and Batavia, and Christian missions doubling as presses and schools. Wedgwood's mass-produced classical china is Bayly's own emblem of this middlebrow consumption boom in Britain (josiah-wedgwood). This globally distributed, trade-and-consumption-led middle-class formation resonates with the kind of dispersed, multi-centered zone-building fernand-braudel and braudel-world-economy describe for commercial civilization, though Bayly's evidentiary base — post-1780 print, missions, voluntary societies — and his post-revolutionary chronology are distinctively his own. Participation was sharply unequal throughout: women gained narrow openings via religion and charity even as formal politics excluded them, and the new “sober” middle-class societies were often more racially exclusionary than the aristocratic “mestizo societies” they displaced.

The post-1815 order was an equipoise, not a restoration. The closing “Prospect” insists no state form — “whether democratic or neo-absolutist” — had decisively won by 1830; control and revolt, prosperity and starvation, liberalism and repression remained “everywhere finely balanced”. This reads as a Bayly-native version of the gramsci-gap interregnum — an old legitimacy destabilized and a new one not yet consolidated — though Bayly's timescale (a few post-Napoleonic decades within the modernity-machine's much longer Dawn) is far shorter than the multi-century machine transitions the World Machines frame describes, and his “morbid symptoms” are racial exclusion and social inequality rather than a specifically hegemonic crisis.

Notable passages

Napoleon's own words — on winning the Vendée as a Catholic, Egypt as a Muslim, Italy as an Ultramontane, and hypothetically a Jewish people by rebuilding Solomon's Temple — crystallize the chapter's claim that the new imperial state's power lay in performing whatever legitimating creed a population required, discarding dynastic or confessional consistency for pure “reason of state”.

The paragraph on the globalized “symbol of ‘the people’” traces how a single “cultural template” — that the people have rights and can act as a revolutionary force — let Caribbean slave rebels, Indian low-caste activists, and Genoese artisans invoke the rights of “the people” and be feared far beyond their localities, regardless of whether the actual leaders were “embittered but relatively privileged” men.

The racial-ladder paragraph (Adam Smith, William Robertson, the “map of mankind”) is the clearest statement of how Enlightenment universalism became an administrative hierarchy licensing intervention against peoples judged “stuck” at an earlier stage of development.

The Calcutta paragraph shows Ram Mohun Roy's circle inferring a right to Indian nationhood by direct analogy from newspaper reports of Irish and Genoese revolt — concrete evidence for Bayly's claim that expanding communications, not only conquest, forged new identities.

The “third revolution” opening paragraph states the chapter's most original claim in its own terms: a global, pre-industrial commercial middle-class revolution that “more closely represented” an accelerated industrious revolution than a factory-driven one.

The closing “Prospect” paragraph delivers Bayly's judgment that 1815–1830 was equipoise, not resolution — essential context for the book's next chapter.
