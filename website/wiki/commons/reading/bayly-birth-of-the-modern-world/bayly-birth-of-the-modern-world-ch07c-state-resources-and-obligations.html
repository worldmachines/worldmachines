The State's Resources and Its Obligations to Society

Commons · The Birth of the Modern World · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Between 1780 and 1914 the state — first in Europe, then unevenly across Eurasia, East Asia, and the colonial world — accumulated unprecedented military, fiscal, and administrative capacity through war, plunder, and industrial revenue, and this new capacity increasingly obliged it, after roughly 1850, to reciprocate with famine relief, public health, education, and welfare, though this obligation reached settler colonies and European metropoles far sooner and more fully than it reached colonized Asia and Africa.

This unit tracks two linked developments across the long nineteenth century: first, how the state — weak and often humiliated within its own borders as late as 1780 — accumulated a historically unprecedented capacity to tax, conscript, police, and monitor its population, built out of war, plunder, and industrial revenue rather than any single design; and second, how this new capacity, because it increasingly rested on the cooperation of taxed and conscripted subjects, pushed states after roughly 1850 to reciprocate with famine relief, public health, education, and eventually social insurance — an obligation that reached settler colonies and European metropoles far sooner and more completely than it ever reached colonized Asia and Africa.

Themes

The eighteenth-century state was far weaker than its international standing suggested. Bayly opens by contrasting the grandeur of Eurasian states around 1780 with their near-daily inability to govern their own territory: Louis XVI could not control Paris, the Gordon Riots left London ablaze, Catherine the Great's modernized Russia was paralyzed for years by peasant and cossack revolt, and "Insurgent Jat peasant leaders regularly looted the heart of the Mughal Empire within 20 miles of the imperial throne" even under Aurangzeb — a directly relevant case for mughal-administration. By 1900 this had reversed: most Western and colonial states could tax and police "most of the time," and even 1917 is reread not as the culmination of endemic Russian weakness but as "a sudden rupture in a pattern of gradually increasing and effective governance."

Plunder, war, and monopolized military technology drove the buildup. Bayly is blunt that "state building across the world remained, as it always had been, a massive act of plunder," with entrepreneurs and commission agents following invading armies to help root new regimes while collaborators worked to obscure the fact. What changed after 1800 was scale: matchlocks and Vauban-style fortresses had once let local magnates and rebels keep pace with the state, but ironclads, heavy artillery, and chemical shells required capital and organization only big states could marshal. Steam power let a handful of gunboats humiliate the Qing during the Opium Wars and force open Tokugawa Japan's ports; telegraphs and railways let Britain suppress the 1857 mutiny and defeat the Sudanese Khalifa, and let Prussia outmaneuver Austria and France. The same military machinery turned inward for "internal pacification" — white terror in 1798 Ireland, the crushing of Polish autonomy in 1831, the massacre of the Paris Commune in 1871.

Military crisis forced fiscal and social re-engineering, most dramatically outside Europe. Meiji Japan is the unit's clearest case: humiliation by Western gunboats forced the new regime to revoke the samurai's fiscal privileges, pension the old warrior class off on fixed stipends, and register land under a national tax system, which "ended the decentralized patrimonial style of state structure" and turned peasant, samurai, merchant, and daimyo alike into a single administrative category, "Japanese subjects" — a small, concrete instance of the flattening of particular statuses into one legible category that modernity-machine and legibility-as-machine-core name as a core operation of state-building. Qing China's parallel self-strengthening movement (new arsenals under Li Hongzhang, a revamped Maritime Customs Service Bayly calls "the first completely modern bureaucratic institution in China") shows the same logic working more haltingly, without the fiscal base to match.

Industrial and demographic growth made populations easier to tax than to till. Income, license, and trade taxes on urban, transparent economic activity were "easier to tax than fractious landholders and peasantry" who could resist or flee; population growth (following plague's retreat and better nutrition) supplied both the armies and the labor a fiscal-military state needed. Free trade, meanwhile, was less a principled retreat than "a tactic": Britain and its rivals dismantled tariff regimes at home while gunboats forced the same regime on China and the Ottoman Empire, and protection returned once it suited the powers after 1890.

A reciprocal welfare bargain emerged after 1850, though it was not entirely new. Bayly credits older regimes — Chinese, Islamic, Indo-Islamic, and early modern European poor-law states — with pre-existing, if circumscribed, obligations to their subjects: wells, famine relief, flood and fire control, charity. What changed was the systematic linking of extraction to provision: taxed citizens "demanded something back," and reformers (Shaftesbury against child labor, abolitionists against slavery, campaigns against sati, footbinding, and Pacific "ritual cannibalism") channeled moral energy into strengthening state authority over rival institutions like "the mosque, the church, or the local magnate." Bayly's own aside earlier in the unit — that the state's claim to embody the nation was "a circular one, for states had as often created nations as vice versa" — resonates, at real distance, with imagined-communities's account of the nation as much a product of state action as its cause, though Bayly's interest here is fiscal-military capacity rather than print culture.

The welfare state arrived unevenly, and colonial subjects were largely excluded from the "obligations" side of the bargain. Bismarck's 1880s pensions and health insurance were explicitly a weapon against socialist and Catholic rivals for working-class loyalty; Britain's welfare state trailed until Labour pressure and Lloyd George's Liberals forced the issue; settler colonies (New Zealand, Australia) actually led the world with "cradle to grave" provision. In Asian and African colonies, by contrast, life expectancy "scarcely crept above 35 years of age" and laissez-faire neglect was reinforced by the racist assumption that natives "naturally died like flies" — the same states extracting resources from colonial subjects felt no matching obligation toward them, until rising nationalism made minimal education and famine codes a matter of colonial legitimacy. This is Bayly's "contested uniformity" at its starkest: the apparatus of the modern state spreads everywhere, but the reciprocal contract it enables in the metropole is withheld at the periphery.

Notable passages

The opening contrast (c1) between 1780 and 1914 sets the unit's thesis in one paragraph: greater manpower, authority, resources, and destructive capacity, bureaucratized and dressed in the circular idea that nation produces state as much as state produces nation. The "massive act of plunder" passage (c2) is Bayly's demystifying move: state formation is not typically a legitimate design but violence retrofitted with justification, its origins obscured by the collaborators who profited from it. The Meiji Japan passage (c3) shows legibility as lived transformation — ranked estates collapsed into a single administrative category even as older social statuses lingered informally in daily life. The obligations thesis passage (c4) establishes continuity as well as change: pre-modern states already owed subjects flood control, famine relief, and charity; what is new after 1850 is the scale and systematicity of the exchange. The Bismarck passage (c5) ties welfare provision directly to regime survival and class conflict rather than to pure humanitarianism. The colonial exclusion passage (c6) is the unit's sharpest statement of asymmetry: the same resource-extraction logic applied to colonized populations without the reciprocal obligations logic, sustained by an explicit racial double standard.
