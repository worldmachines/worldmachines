The Last Great Domestication and the World's Industrious Revolutions

Commons · The Birth of the Modern World · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Before the Industrial Revolution had proceeded far, the decisive global economic shifts of the long eighteenth century were a final worldwide 'great domestication' of nomadic and forested land and a set of household-level 'industrious revolutions' in consumption and labor found across Europe, China, Japan, India, and the Ottoman lands, with Europe's eventual dominance owing to contingent advantages in slavery and colonial extraction and to social and state context rather than to any inherent economic exceptionalism.

Opening Chapter 2, Bayly sets aside the conventional starting point of global economic history — Britain's Industrial Revolution, which he and other historians now doubt had progressed far by 1800 — and argues that two other processes mattered more for most of the world before about 1830: a final, rapid phase of agrarian 'great domestication' on the world's remaining nomadic and forest frontiers, and parallel 'industrious revolutions' in household consumption and labor found in Europe, China, Japan, India, and the Ottoman world alike. The chapter then turns to the debate over why these Afro-Asian dynamisms did not translate into sustained development, rejecting both a simple internalist 'decline of the rest' and an account that credits Western capitalism alone, and closing on the more modest claim that northwestern Europe's social and state context gave its industrious (and later industrial) revolutions unusual staying power.

Themes

  • The last 'great domestication' displaces the Industrial Revolution as the era's central economic fact. From roughly 1650, and accelerating after 1770, pioneer peasants, herders-turned-settlers, and expanding states pushed cultivation into forests, steppe, and pampas from Siberia to Buenos Aires to New Zealand — driven by population recovery after the retreat of plague and epidemic disease, new American food crops, and states' fiscal appetite for taxable, settled subjects. This is Bayly's alternative prime mover: not factories, but land brought under the plough, whose 'sedenterized' herdsmen and forest-dwellers became the coolies and indentured labor of the nineteenth-century commercial economy.
  • 'Industrious revolutions' (borrowing Jan de Vries) reorganize household labor and demand rather than the means of production. Families bought in goods and services — the 'invention of breakfast' (coffee, tea, sugar, disposable crockery) is Bayly's paradigm case — generating new time discipline and sociability. Crucially, industrious revolution is a demand-side phenomenon distinct from, and not a reliable predictor of, industrialization: it appears without early factory production across the Low Countries, Germany, coastal North America, Mexico, and coastal Brazil.
  • Industrious revolutions were not a European monopoly. China (the spread of mandarin luxury taste and 'objectified' consumption down the social scale, women drawn into artisanal work), Japan (samurai-mandated dress and swordsmanship boosting metallurgy, a 'domesticated' tea ceremony), and parts of India, Iran, and the Ottoman Empire (revisionist evidence against the old 'tribute economy' model, entrepreneurial rural producers) show the same reorganization of household consumption and labor, on Bayly's account, well before serious Western economic impact.
  • Europe's advantage was extractive and contingent, not intrinsic. Tropical consumables (tobacco, coffee, sugar, tea) were the linchpin goods of the Atlantic industrious revolution, but Bayly insists this was 'far from' a tame, domestic Delft-vase story: Caribbean brutality and the slave system generated the 'value added' that Chinese, Arab, and African merchants, despite prospering, could not match, while also building the armed shipping that later projected European power globally.
  • The 'decline of the rest' argument must be rebalanced in both directions. Bayly credits real internal strains — Qing 'imperial overstretch' and frontier wars, South Asia's zero-sum regional competition for people and capital, Ottoman tension between center and notables/tribes — as genuine brakes on Afro-Asian industrious revolutions by the 1770s. But he rejects both older nationalist narratives that blame only internal tributary extraction and rebellion, and André Gunder Frank's inversion that Europe's 'rise' was merely the fortuitous byproduct of non-European crises. Neither reading matches the huge wealth gap that had opened by 1900, nor the fact that Ottoman Egypt and much of South Asia stayed relatively prosperous into the 1820s–30s.
  • The chapter's payoff is multi-centred, not mono-causal. Northwestern Europe's and North America's dynamism between 1750 and 1850 is explained by Bayly as neither pure economic exceptionalism nor simply the mirror image of Asian/African failure, but by social and state context — foreshadowing the book's later turn to war-finance and the 'activist, patriotic state' — that gave European industrious/industrial revolutions more cumulative 'staying power' than their counterparts elsewhere. This resonates with world-machines's own insistence on multi-centred, interacting origins for the Modernity Machine rather than a single prime mover — though Bayly's register stays comparative-economic rather than infrastructural/legibility-based, and his 'great domestication' and 'industrious revolution' are pre-machine, household- and land-scale processes rather than the state-legibility apparatus that WMP treats as the Modernity Machine's core.
  • The chapter's China puzzle — buoyant eighteenth-century trade and consumption alongside a 'gathering social crisis' by the White Lotus Rebellion (1796–1804) — rhymes with the braudel-china-precocity-puzzle pattern of technical/commercial precocity without cumulative breakthrough, though Bayly's frame here is demand-side industrious revolution and imperial overstretch rather than Braudel's supervising-state-plus-cheap-labor diagnosis; the two are compatible but not identical explanations of the same nonevent.

Notable passages

The great-domestication synthesis (c1) states the chapter's alternative to industrialization as prime mover in the most compressed form: a violent, global-scale conversion of forest and steppe populations into the labor force of nineteenth-century commerce. The de Vries passage (c2) is the chapter's working definition of 'industrious revolution,' anchored in the vivid 'invention of breakfast' case that Bayly returns to repeatedly. The China passage (c3) extends the concept to a non-European instance in detail — household labor reorganization, the downward diffusion of luxury consumption, and the 'objectification' of luxury as a goal distinct from older forms of status built on control over people (compare braudel-world-economy and fernand-braudel on material-civilization consumption goods as markers of distinction). The India/Ottoman passage (c4), leaning on Prasannan Parthasarathi's comparative wage-standard-of-living finding for South Asian weavers, is Bayly's clearest single piece of evidence against 'tribute economy' and static-caste readings of the region — set against the backdrop of Mughal political fragmentation touched on via mughal-administration. The rebalancing passage (c5) is the chapter's methodological pivot, refusing both the internalist-decline and Gunder-Frank-style inversion accounts. The closing synthesis (c6) delivers the chapter's actual thesis: Europe's edge was social and institutional 'staying power,' not an essential economic superiority — the seed of the book's later argument for war-finance and the activist state as differentiators.
