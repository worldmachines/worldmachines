Old Supremacies Reconstituted: Landed Elites and the Continuity Argument

Commons · The Birth of the Modern World · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Europe's landed aristocracies and the world's leading monarchies converted rather than lost their power across the long nineteenth century, and the chapter closes by arguing that industrial, imperial and ideological change actually empowered these continuities rather than erasing them.

In the closing pages of this chapter, Bayly surveys how Europe's landed gentries and aristocracies, and then the world's leading monarchies, weathered — and were reshaped by — a century of revolution, industrialization, and democratization, before drawing the chapter to a close with an explicit historiographical reconciliation: that industrial and imperial change was real and epochal, yet the old forms of power did not simply die but bent, converted, and were "empowered by change itself."

Themes

Landed elites survived by converting, not resisting. From Italian nobility fusing with the bourgeoisie into an "amalgame" that outlasted the Napoleonic shock and 1848, to Monaco's princes trading feudal tax rights for a casino economy, to Prussian junkers moving into "agro-business" and officering the new imperial army, Bayly's examples share a pattern: gentries kept power by reinvesting inherited status — marriage, patronage, chivalric prestige — into the new economy and the new nation-state's institutions: the army, the professions, colonial administration. Middling capitalists (German naval captains sporting junker-style dueling scars) emulated the old elite's manners upward, showing status flowing both ways across the class line.

Monarchy persisted because it did political work, not despite doing none. Bayly explicitly rejects the older historiographical convention that treated nineteenth-century monarchies (Ottoman, Qing, Austro-Hungarian, German) as absurd ancien-régime holdovers "bound to fail." Louis Napoleon, Franz Joseph, and Kaiser Wilhelm II each held together plural, potentially antagonistic constituencies (army, church, industry, region) precisely because monarchy's "ill-assorted mishmash of charismatic authority and populism, of ritual and base privilege" let one figure occupy several incompatible legitimating roles at once — republican hero and anointed sovereign, industrial moderniser and church protector.

Only military defeat, not internal illegitimacy, brought most of these dynasties down. Bayly repeats this point across cases — Napoleon III, Franz Joseph, Wilhelm II, the Ottomans — insisting these monarchies show more resilience than terminal decline, and that it is defeat in war, not democratization or nationalism alone, that ends them. Russia and China are the acknowledged exceptions, carrying "crippling problems of legitimacy" that predate 1914, though Bayly still stresses these regimes retained real support and that "their complete disappearance from the scene could not really have been predicted."

Symbolic authority intensified precisely as constitutional power receded. Queen Victoria is the pivot case: her real political power narrowed constitutionally over the century even as royal ritual — coronations, jubilees, state openings — grew more elaborate, and she came to personify both "a discreet modernity" (industry, telegraphy) and a pre-modern lattice of "assorted bailiwicks, corporate bodies, and arcane aristocracies." This is Bayly's own paradox in miniature: the more legible and uniform the surrounding nation-state and empire became, the more useful — not less — an ornately non-uniform, charismatic monarchy proved as social glue. It resonates with the decay-sequence's Functional-to-Performative stage, where an institution that loses the operational job it was built for is elaborated and re-staged rather than abandoned; Bayly's monarchies, though, are mostly cut off by war before completing any slide toward the Ornamental or Absent end of that sequence.

The chapter's explicit thesis stakes out a position between transformation and persistence. Bayly stages, then synthesizes, two historiographical camps — mid-twentieth-century historians who stressed industry, empire, science, and labor as transformative, and post-1970 historians who stressed the persistence of the old order — concluding both are right: epochal change happened, and it is precisely what "reconstituted" and "empowered" the old supremacies rather than eliminating them. This rhymes with the World Machines picture of multiple world-machines coexisting in friction rather than cleanly superseding one another: an emerging, more uniform and legible modernity-machine (bureaucratic armies, industrial cities, national churches) does not erase the older dynastic-aristocratic order but repurposes it — special "jurisdictions, corporations, and privileges" persist as functional, semi-legible carve-outs that serve the new legibility-as-machine-core project rather than simply resisting it. Bayly is a historian of connections and comparisons, not a machine theorist, and frames this as functional sociology rather than infrastructure layering, but the resonance is structural rather than merely verbal. The Russian and Chinese cases — monarchies retaining real support amid terminal legitimacy problems whose outcome "could not really have been predicted" — gesture at something like a gramsci-gap, an old order that can no longer rule outright but has not yet been replaced; here, though, the gap is closed by external war rather than by any new legitimating order being born.

Notable passages

The unit's clearest statement of elite adaptability comes early: Prussian, Italian, and Russian gentries alike had their "embodied schemes of gentlemanliness... transmuted into the professions of the nation-state," down to junkers moving into industrial agriculture while the rising bourgeoisie borrowed aristocratic style (dueling scars). Britain supplies the starkest numbers: in 1900 "less than 1 percent of the population owned 80 percent of the land," and the House of Lords held "the emerging British democracy in a tight headlock" until 1911 — evidence that formal constitutional change and material elite dominance moved on different clocks. On monarchy, Bayly's thesis sentence is blunt: these systems "were valuable to nineteenth-century polities in many ways," useful to "political forces trying to mediate an increasingly complex society" — not survivals despite modern conditions but instruments suited to them. The Victoria passage develops this most fully, holding together royal ritual's growing elaborateness with the queen's role as a modernizing, industry-promoting, telegraph-using monarch. The Ottoman material extends the same revisionist move outside Europe: some historians now read the late empire as a "long peace" in which "different religious confessions, tribal coalitions, and language groups jostled together... but did not fight," with only "Western attack, culminating in the First World War," ending it — a claim Bayly flags as likely underplaying indigenous nationalism even as he endorses its core insight. The chapter closes by naming its own argument directly: change and continuity are not opposed findings to adjudicate between but a single process, since "the old supremacies bent in the face of these changes and reconstituted themselves in relation to it," so that, "paradoxically... these continuities were empowered by change itself."
