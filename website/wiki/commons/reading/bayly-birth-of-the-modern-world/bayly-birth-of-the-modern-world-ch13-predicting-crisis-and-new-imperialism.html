The Great Acceleration, 1890–1914: Predicting Crisis, New Imperialism, and New Nationalism

Commons · The Birth of the Modern World · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Opening Bayly's concluding chapter, the two decades before 1914 brought a historiographically contested but, Bayly argues, genuine acceleration of change: an agricultural depression that toppled old landed elites, a financially entangled new imperialism, a telegraph-and-press revolution in mass publics, and a worldwide surge of new nationalisms crowned by Japan's defeat of Russia and the near-simultaneous Ottoman and Chinese revolutions.

Opening Bayly's concluding chapter, the unit restates the book's core themes — multi-centric change, contested uniformity, functional complexity, and the rise of and challenges to Western dominance — before asking a historiographical question: were the two decades before 1914 really a crisis-driven acceleration, as contemporaries from Lenin to apocalyptic novelists believed, or, as later revisionists argued, an illusion of continuity? Bayly comes down on the side of real, decisive change, tracing it through the social fallout of the agricultural depression, a new financially entangled imperialism, a telegraph-and-press-driven public sphere, and a worldwide wave of "new nationalism" running from Ireland and the Transvaal to Bengal, Istanbul, and Beijing, with Japan's 1905 defeat of Russia as its psychological turning point.

Themes

Millennial panic and the historiographical pendulum. Contemporaries across the political spectrum read the years after 1890 as a terminal crisis: Lenin's Imperialism recast capitalism as entering "a new and final phase of monopoly and imperialism"; liberal Hobson feared a state-capital alliance would corrupt the constitution; and European fiction (H. G. Wells, Arthur Conan Doyle) and Tagore's apocalyptic verse absorbed the same dread of "things to come." Historians later swung between two poles: mid-century labor and diplomatic historians followed contemporaries in charting an "inexorable growth of political and social conflict," arms races, and alliance-building; then, from the 1970s, Arno Mayer, Robinson and Gallagher, Cain and Hopkins, Anil Seal, Elie Kedourie, and Richard Price argued for underlying continuity, recasting "new imperialism" and colonial nationalism (Indian nationalism as a "microscopic minority," Arab nationalism as a wartime invention) as elite artifacts rather than mass rupture. Bayly positions his own chapter as reversing this second swing back toward genuine, decisive change after 1890.

The agricultural depression as social solvent. Fifteen years of falling grain and commodity prices (1870s–80s) hollowed out landed elites worldwide and forced their fusion with new industrial and financial wealth: British aristocratic families intermarrying with the daughters of bankers and mill-owners (80 of Britain's 100 wealthiest families by 1910 owed their fortunes to manufacturing, not land), German Junkers leaning on industrial shares and political office, Russian gentry decline, an Italian agrarian crisis that reshaped the elite, and a severe 1893–7 American downturn that fed directly into the Progressive Era.

A new, financially entangled imperialism. Bayly's answer to the "was it real?" debate: Trans-Siberian and Berlin–Baghdad railway finance, Western bankers converting Ottoman and Egyptian sovereign debt directly into territorial administration to secure interest payments, Rhodes's schemes in southern Africa, and intensified inter-state commercial rivalry in Persia, China, and the Ottoman Empire show political and economic conflict deepening together, even without a single "master plan" (his explicit rebuttal to a crude Leninist reading). Britain's relative decline — embarrassed in the Boer War, overtaken in iron and steel production — coincided with the rise of Germany, the United States, and Japan.

The communications revolution and mass publics. Telegraph traffic tripled between 1900 and 1909; press barons (Hearst, The Times) mobilized public opinion behind imperial ventures (Hearst's telegram, "You furnish the pictures and I will furnish the war," is Bayly's emblem of this); and the same infrastructure let colonized and marginal peoples — Chinese, Indian, Egyptian, Indochinese, Irish — recognize a shared plight and organize transnationally, meeting as socialists, pan-Africanists, students, and journalists in London, Paris, and Chicago.

The new nationalism, East and West. Minority-language nationalism matured in Russia and Austria-Hungary; Sinn Féin fused rural grievance with Catholic self-confidence in Ireland; Boer settler nationalism under Paul Kruger fused language and religion against Britain; and in Egypt, India (Curzon's 1905 partition of Bengal), and French Indochina, colonial repression pushed young intellectuals toward terrorist violence. Most dramatically, the Young Turk revolution (1908) and the Chinese revolution (1911) toppled two ancient monarchies — the Ottomans, ruling since 1453, and the Qing, since the 1640s — within a few years of each other, a demonstration Bayly treats as epochal: that even "stubbornly conservative" empires could remake themselves as modern states, giving fresh energy to pan-Islamic solidarity worldwide. Japan's earlier victories over China (1894–5) and Russia (1905) did comparable psychological work everywhere from Indian villages (newborns named after Japanese admirals) to Phan Boi Chau's "look to the east" movement in French Indochina and Ethiopia's first social scientist urging emulation of Japan.

World Machines resonance. The chapter's own framing — contested uniformity, multi-centric causation, and connection intensifying alongside antagonism — reads as an on-the-ground description of world-machines friction rather than a single triumphant modernity, though Bayly is a historian of connections, not a machine theorist, and never generalizes to a single infrastructural logic. The more uniform, bureaucratized state forms Bayly notes spreading in this period, and constitutional government replacing "despotic" sultans, resonate with modernity-machine and legibility-as-machine-core. The near-simultaneous collapse of Qing and Ottoman legitimacy — ancient monarchical authority dying while republican or constitutional forms had not yet stabilized — is a vivid instance of a gramsci-gap, even though Bayly never uses that vocabulary; his account leaves the outcome genuinely open rather than assuming smooth succession. The chapter's own retelling of how historians' consensus on 1890–1914 swung from "hectic change" to "immobility" and now back again is itself a small case study in how a history-machine assembles and reassembles around a period in the disciplinary literature before lurching into a new orthodoxy. The transnational networks of Chinese, Indian, Egyptian, Indochinese, and Irish activists meeting in London, Paris, and Chicago, made possible by telegraph-and-press infrastructure, echo benedict-anderson's account of print-mediated national imagining, extended here into an internationalist, anti-colonial register that Anderson's original argument addresses only obliquely. And Bayly's language of a "core" of the world economy being newly intruded upon by peripheral change rhymes with fernand-braudel's and braudel-world-economy's core–periphery model, though Bayly's core is as much political and cultural as economic.

Notable passages

  • The chapter's opening restatement of the book's "big themes" — multi-centric change, contested uniformity, functional complexity, rising Western dominance and its challengers — functions as Bayly's own table of contents for the conclusion.
  • The historiographical about-face from labor and diplomatic historians' inexorable-conflict thesis to Mayer's and Robinson and Gallagher's revisionism, after which late-nineteenth-century imperialism "began to seem like a trick of the light," sets up Bayly's own return to a genuine-acceleration reading.
  • The agricultural depression's fusion of old and new wealth, exemplified by Britain's aristocracy marrying into banking and manufacturing fortunes, is Bayly's preferred material explanation for the dissolution of the old order.
  • Western bankers converting Ottoman and Egyptian debt into territorial control, alongside Rhodes's African schemes, illustrates the "new imperialism" as inseparably financial and political.
  • Japanese victories over China and Russia produced a worldwide psychological rupture — down to Indian villagers naming children after Japanese admirals — that Bayly treats as proof colonized peoples now saw Western dominance as "neither inevitable nor everlasting."
  • The near-simultaneous fall of the Qing (ruling since the 1640s) and the Ottomans (since 1453), replaced respectively by a republic and a constitutional monarchy, crowns Bayly's account of the "new nationalism" as a genuine world-historical rupture.
