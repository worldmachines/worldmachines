Old Regimes, Peasant Society, and the Politics of Difference

Commons · The Birth of the Modern World · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Eighteenth-century agrarian empires, home to roughly 70-80 percent of humanity, ran on an internally varied but not rigid peasant social order, and their rulers treated ideological and political pluralism as a governing resource to be cultivated rather than a weakness to be cured.

This unit covers the opening two sections of Bayly's Chapter 1 -- "Peasants and Lords" and "The Politics of Difference" -- which sketch the eighteenth-century "old regime" baseline against which the book's story of nineteenth-century uniformity and contested modernity will be measured. Bayly first establishes that agrarian empires (Qing China, Mughal India, Tokugawa Japan, Safavid Iran, Java, the Ottoman Empire, Russia, the Habsburg lands) held roughly 70 percent of the world's population around 1750, and that peasants made up perhaps 80 percent of people within them; he then complicates the flat category "peasant" by ecology (irrigated rice zones versus dry-grain/pastoral zones), by internal stratification (elites, peasant-proper, landless laborers), and by debunking three stock caricatures of peasants. The second section, "The Politics of Difference," argues against the "oriental despotism" / uniform-weak-state caricature of pre-modern Eurasian empires: old-regime rulers intervened forcefully in narrow domains (irrigation, core provinces, taxation near the capital) while leaving most of society to local elites, and -- crucially for Bayly's argument -- often cultivated religious and ethnic pluralism deliberately, as a technique of rule, rather than merely tolerating it out of weakness. Note: despite the chapter's title, this particular span does not yet use the phrase "archaic globalization" or develop that concept directly -- it lays the old-regime groundwork the later "archaic globalization" material (elsewhere in the chapter) will build on.

Themes

Agrarian empires were demographically dominant and economically simple. Bayly opens with scale: eight named agrarian empires "must have accounted for at least 70 percent of the world's population," and peasants "80 percent of that gross population," with urbanization above 20 percent only in pockets (northwestern Europe, maritime/riverine China, coastal Japan). Political and religious authority in these polities was "highly variegated and intertwined," but the economies underneath were "relatively simply, dominated by agriculture and still dependent on the seasons" -- the harvest, not the market or the factory, set the rhythm of most human lives, and subsistence crises (Komlos on central Europe, France's crises de subsistence) were routine.

Peasant society had a family resemblance worldwide but was not one thing. Ecology drove real variation: intensive rice zones (southern China, Southeast Asia, Indian river valleys) needed large tied or dependent-peasant labor forces to maintain irrigation, while dry-grain and pastoral zones (north China, northern India, the Middle East, western Europe) supported sparser, more independent but often poorer and indebted farmers. Local religious institutions and elite structures further shaped land tenure. Peasants were also frequently part-time artisans, carriers, and soldiers rather than fixed to the land -- Bayly is explicit that "there were intricate differences in the ways in which peasants and lords lived their lives and related to each other."

Bayly rejects three stock images of the peasant. Not "boors," not innocents of an "unspoiled arcadia," and not perpetual rebels: peasant risings, though real and frequent in the later eighteenth century, reflected "near-despair at the accumulation of abuses" rather than any essential propensity to violence. Instead peasants were "quite entrepreneurial," seeking "more land, more money, and more honor" -- a "huge fund of canny talent" that, once political and economic conditions allowed (especially in southern/eastern Europe, Japan, and the settler "new worlds"), became a major engine of nineteenth-century economic dynamism. Social hierarchy, too, was "bound by status" but not rigid -- new men and even poor peasants could rise within a generation or two, in China, India, Japan, and the Middle East as much as in Europe.

Old-regime state power was real but narrowly targeted, not uniformly weak or strong. Against the "oriental despotism" stereotype applied to Qing China and Mughal India, and against an equally naive image of a centralized ancien-regime France, Bayly shows old states intervening effectively in specific domains -- French tax collection near Paris, Ottoman control within a thousand miles of Istanbul, imperial management of canals and the Grand Canal/Yellow River system, the Asante state's bureaucracy and legal code -- while leaving most infrastructure and everyday governance to local communities and magnates. Rulers routinely "farmed out" rights and privileges to raise revenue, which itself became a driver of rural revolt (French tax-farming, Spanish New World "tyrannical abuse"). By 1800 the decay of China's granaries, Grand Canal, and dike systems had begun to compromise the Qing regime's legitimacy -- an early thread of the "world crisis" this book traces.

States and empires cultivated -- rather than merely tolerated -- ideological and ethnic difference. This is the section's sharpest and most WMP-relevant claim. Bayly revisits Marshall Hodgson's "gunpowder empires" thesis (parallels among Ottomans, Safavids, Mughals, and even Qing China, Russia, and the Habsburgs, and courtly-ritual echoes among Louis XIV, the Qian Long emperor, and Peter the Great) only to stress that recent scholarship emphasizes difference over family resemblance. Max Weber's ideal types of military aristocracy versus bureaucracy blur in practice everywhere -- France's noblesse de robe, Qing scholar-gentry turned landowners, Vietnamese mandarins intermarrying with the Tay -- so that "the distinct ideal types of bureaucrat, warrior-landholder, and man of religion merged into each other in complex patterns." Religiously, Qing emperors ran a "cosmic spirit empire" patronizing Tibetan Lamas and Mongol shamans alongside Confucian rule (citing Pamela Crossley on rulers fostering separate ethnicities under cult leaders); Ottoman sultans were khan, Caesar, and Khalifa at once, patronizing Jewish, Druze, and Christian institutions; the Mughal emperor blessed Hindu ascetics from the Red Fort despite Islam's doctrinal objection to "polytheism"; Russian tsars were simultaneously Orthodox sacred kings and khans to Muslim and Mongol subjects; and even Britain governed Anglicans, Presbyterians, Catholics, Hindus, Muslims, Orthodox Christians, and Buddhists under one crown by 1815. Bayly's conclusion is explicit: old regimes "helped to create, even gloried in, complexity and difference" -- this was strategy, not mere incapacity.

Old regimes moved in developmental cycles, and several were already overstretched by 1780. Centralization, decentralization, and attempted recentralization recurred as a pattern; by the eighteenth century Ottoman authority had ceded much of Egypt, Syria, Lebanon, and North Africa to regional ayan, the Mughal emperor's revenue and authority were shrinking against "over-mighty" regional subjects by 1720, and the Habsburg lands remained "a conglomerate of separate territorial units" with entrenched local identities. This sets up the book's broader argument (elsewhere in the chapter and after) that the coming push toward uniformity would not simply modernize these polities but would actively suppress and contest the difference they had built into their own foundations.

Notable passages

The demographic-scale passage (c1) grounds the whole chapter's claim that "the largest part of humanity" lived under agrarian-empire conditions in 1750 -- the baseline the rest of the book measures transformation against. The peasant-stereotypes passage (c2) is Bayly doing historiographical housekeeping in miniature, clearing away three competing myths before crediting peasants with real entrepreneurial agency. The "trick of the light" passage (c3) is the section's central metaphor for pre-modern statecraft: power was neither absent nor total but performed selectively, and rulers deliberately avoided "making windows on men's souls," settling for "a broad recognition of the supremacy of the emperor's cult, not uniformity of belief." The bureaucrat/warrior/priest passage (c4) shows Weber's clean ideal types dissolving in every actual case Bayly names. The developmental-cycles passage (c5) supplies the chronology of overstretch (Ottoman, Mughal, Habsburg) that primes the reader for the "world crisis" chapters to come. The closing pluralism passage (c6) states the section's thesis outright: difference was manufactured and gloried in, not merely inherited.

World Machines resonances

This section is a portrait of political order organized almost oppositely to what legibility-as-machine-core describes as the modernity-machine's core drive: these old-regime rulers "were only just beginning to find out who and how many people lived within their diverse territories" and had no interest in closing that gap -- co-opting local elites and leaving "the political forms and religious beliefs of the localities" alone was the strategy, not a failure to modernize. The claim that dynasties "often promoted these very differences" and "helped to create, even gloried in, complexity and difference" resonates strongly with illegibility-as-elite-defense's finding that apparent incoherence or unreadability by a ruling stratum can be a deliberate resource rather than weakness -- though the register is different (premodern multi-confessional statecraft, not modern literary gatekeeping) and the goal here is co-optive rule over heterogeneous subjects rather than exclusion of a rising literate public. The whole picture also anchors legibility-vs-complexity-the-master-tension at its pre-modern end: this is what irreducible political and religious complexity looked like just before the "next four chapters'" story of imperfect, contested uniformization -- what world-machines would frame as the ground the coming legibility project had to work against. The book's own Mughal material (revenue and allegiance draining from Delhi to "over-mighty" regional subjects by 1720) sits in productive tension with the wiki's mughal-administration entry, which foregrounds the empire's centralized matrix-reporting design -- Bayly's 1720 snapshot is that same design already fraying at the edges. Carry the hedge the brief supplies: Bayly is a historian of connections and comparisons, not a machine theorist, and none of this section names globalization, legibility, or a machine directly.
