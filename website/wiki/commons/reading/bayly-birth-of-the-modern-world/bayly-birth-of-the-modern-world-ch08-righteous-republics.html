Righteous Republics: Civic Virtue as a Global Political Language Before Liberalism

Commons · The Birth of the Modern World · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Bayly opens Chapter 8 by arguing that liberalism, socialism, and science were dramatically recast rather than simply diffused as they crossed continents, and recovers a much older, near-universal ‘righteous republic’ language of anti-corruption civic virtue — running through Atlantic civic republicanism, Creole patriotism, English Chartism, Yoruba kingship, Tokugawa/Meiji Japan, Indian anti-colonial rhetoric, and Islamic and Chinese engagements with Aristotle — that predates and complicates any simple story of Western liberal diffusion.

This unit opens Chapter 8 of Bayly's book: it states the chapter's thesis that liberalism, socialism, and science were “dramatically recast” rather than simply exported as they moved across the nineteenth-century world; it criticizes mainstream intellectual history for treating non-European reception of Western ideas as passive diffusion and proposes the history of science's more hybrid model instead; and it uses the post-1960s historiographical revival of “civic republicanism” to argue that a shared, much older political language of anti-corruption civic virtue — the “righteous republic” — already existed at a near-global level, independently of and prior to Western liberal individualism, surfacing in strikingly similar forms from the Atlantic revolutions to Yoruba Africa, Tokugawa and Meiji Japan, colonial India, and the Islamic and Chinese worlds.

Themes

Ideas travel and get recast, not simply transplanted. The chapter's opening claim is that liberalism, socialism, and science, though “potentially revolutionary,” were “dramatically recast as they passed from continent to continent, often losing their revolutionary character” — with the implication, developed in chapter 9, that competing global religious systems did more to shape everyday mental life than these secular doctrines did. This is a modest, hedged echo of the modernity-machine's ideational exports failing to transplant cleanly onto other substrates, though Bayly's unit is a piece of comparative intellectual history rather than an account of legibility or institutional infrastructure — the “machine” at work here is closer to ethical vocabulary and myths of legitimacy than to census, cadastre, or bureaucracy.

Against diffusionism; toward a hybrid history of ideas. Bayly faults intellectual historians for assuming Western doctrines simply diffused outward to Westernized non-European elites, and holds up the history of science — where scholarship now credits Asians, Africans, and other non-Europeans with creating “hybrid bodies of learning” — as the model to generalize. Even where Western ideas rode colonial power and schooling, “intellectual domination was not a simple process”: indigenous writers “subtly altered, adjusted, and set to new purposes” the imported languages of debate.

Civic republicanism revisited: virtue against corruption, not individualism against the state. Bayly draws on the historiographical shift away from C. B. Macpherson's “possessive individualism” (rooted in adam-smith, Locke, and Hume) toward Pocock's and Bailyn's “civic republicanism” to recover an Atlantic tradition, running from classical and Renaissance sources through the English “Commonwealth” divines to the American and French revolutions, that prized the “patrimonial household” and civic virtue over the market and treated commerce, usury, and court corruption as enemies of the republic. The same frame, Bayly notes, explains the durability of Latin American Creole patriotism (Our Lady of Guadalupe, Aztec descent) and English Chartist communitarianism alongside — not simply displaced by — liberal individualism.

A near-universal pre-industrial political language. The unit's comparative payoff is that strikingly similar “righteous republic” ideals — good kingship overthrown for corruption, purity set against pollution, the honorable householder defended — recur independently across Yoruba West Africa, Tokugawa and Meiji Japan (Ogyu Sorai's attack on samurai corruption; the 1868 restoration's debt to the “ethnic myth of divine monarchy and good rulership”), Hindu and Muslim India (colonial rule read through the idiom of purity and pollution even as Indian National Congress orators built modern economic arguments alongside it), the Islamic world (the akhlaq tradition reconciling Aristotelian civic ethics with prophetic norms, carried by an Ottoman-era translation of Aristotle and invoked with the “lesser jihad” used only sparingly), and China (Aristotle arriving via Jesuit translation in the seventeenth century, taken up in earnest only by nineteenth-century reformers).

The secular/religious divide between Europe and the rest is overdrawn. The unit closes by rejecting the view that Europeans thought progressively and secularly while Asians and Africans “fell back” on religion, calling that distinction “definitely too stark”: European liberals such as Mill worked within a “Christian humanist tradition” and Marxists inherited the structure of “millenarian thought,” while non-Western “religious” political idiom was itself a live language for governing self, household, and polity, not a static mentality.

Notable passages

The chapter's thesis statement (c1) frames everything that follows: ideas do not travel intact, and religion (taken up in chapter 9) outcompeted the secular ideologies as a shaper of mentality. The “righteous republic” passage (c2) names the unit's organizing concept and immediately globalizes it — a “widespread theme throughout Eurasia, Africa, and the Americas” predating the age of revolution. The Pocock/Bailyn passage (c3) spells out what civic republicanism actually opposed: not the state but the market, luxury, and the “court party,” with the tradesman and usurer as figures of detestation rather than harbingers of a free market — a useful corrective to any assumption that pre-liberal republicanism was proto-capitalist. The Japan passage (c4) shows the theme operating as living legitimacy discourse into the Meiji restoration itself, not merely as an antiquarian survival. The Islamic-world passage (c5) makes the connection to Euro-American civic republicanism “explicit” rather than merely structural, via the shared inheritance of Aristotle. The closing passage (c6) delivers Bayly's rebuttal of the secular-Europe/religious-rest dichotomy, the methodological payoff of the whole unit.

A resonance worth flagging with a hedge: the unit's insistence that a “righteous republic” political grammar assembled independently and near-simultaneously across unconnected civilizations, rather than radiating outward from one European “prime mover,” rhymes loosely with WMP's suspicion of great-man, single-origin accounts of large-scale institutional change — though Bayly's claim here is about parallel ethical vocabularies of virtue and corruption, not about a machine's infrastructure assembling itself, so the fit is only partial.
