Empires of Religion: The Myth of Decline and the Rise of New-Style Religion

Commons · The Birth of the Modern World · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Bayly opens "Empires of Religion" by rejecting the shared assumption of Enlightenment philosophes, liberals, and Marxist and postmodernist historians alike that the nineteenth century was an age of religious decline, arguing instead that the world religions underwent a global, mutually reinforcing revival after 1815 that imposed new doctrinal uniformity through tactical retreats, internal discipline, and geographic expansion, while depending throughout on middling converts, women, and local intermediaries who reshaped the faiths they received.

This unit opens Bayly's Chapter 9, "Empires of Religion," by dismantling the assumption — shared by Enlightenment philosophes, American and French revolutionaries, mid-century liberals, and later Marxist and postmodernist historians alike — that science, liberalism, and modernization steadily eroded religious belief. Bayly instead argues that the nineteenth century saw a "triumphal reemergence and expansion" of the great world religions after 1815: they transformed doctrine, organization, and authority on a global, mutually entangled scale, deploying the same technologies of print, travel, and bureaucracy as the modern state and capitalism, and depending throughout on the agency of middling converts, women, and local intermediaries who reshaped the faiths they received even as they propagated them.

Themes

The secularization thesis was itself an ideological artifact, not an empirical finding. From Voltaire's fantasy of Chinese "philosopher-kings" and Gibbon's preference for Islam's supposed freedom from "superstition," through Mill's wish to privatize belief, to Marxist "false consciousness" and the "opiate of the people," progressives across two centuries assumed religion was declining or should decline. Twentieth-century historiography reproduced the same assumption under the authority of Max Weber's "demystified" world and Owen Chadwick's "secularisation of the European mind" — a consensus so pervasive that even anti-colonial Marxist historians (Bipan Chandra, Irfan Habib) and Chinese and Japanese Marxists largely ignored religion, leaving them unprepared for its late-twentieth-century resurgence.

What actually happened was reinvention through tactical retreat, not retreat itself. Religious authorities conceded ground where indefensible — disestablishment in Brazil (1889) and France, the end of religious tests at Oxford and Cambridge — precisely "in order to conquer new areas of cultural and social life." Secularization, in Bayly's own formulation, "was the fly in the wheel, not the wheel itself": a visible concession that masked a deeper, successful program of doctrinal consolidation and expansion into previously unclaimed social and family life.

Reform traveled in all directions, not just outward from a Christian/Western center. Hindu Brahmo Samaj rationalists, the Ceylonese Buddhist revivalist Anagarika Dharmapala, and Muslim modernizers Sayyid Ahmad Khan and Muhammad Abduh all recast their traditions by recovering indigenous rationalist strands, frequently in direct competitive response to Christian missionary pressure — and the pressure ran both ways: missionary techniques honed in Asia, Africa, and the Pacific were "rerouted back" into domestic evangelism in Europe and America. Within Islam it was often struggles at the periphery (Wahhabi Arabia, Nigeria, Sudan, the Dutch East Indies) that "fed back into and revivified" theology and organization at the center — a pattern Bayly reads as visible only at the global scale, invisible to regional or single-tradition specialists.

A taxonomy of spiritual forms, and pressure on all of them. Bayly distinguishes true "world religions" (scriptural, preaching-based, congregational — Christianity, Islam, and to a lesser degree Hinduism and Confucianism) from non-proselytizing community faiths (Sikhism, Judaism, Orthodox/Syriac Christianity), from localized "cults within religions" (Daoism, Shinto, Shakti, ancestor worship), and from non-textual "mystery" religions of Melanesia, Polynesia, Africa, and native America. Even the latter categories felt the "organizing and categorizing power of the world religions": Syriac Christians began printing their own scripture under missionary pressure, and by the 1870s Sikhs were insisting "We are not Hindus" against Hindu revivalist claims on them.

Uniformity was imposed both "down" into societies and outward across geography. Religious authorities disciplined heterodox internal currents — Sufi brotherhoods and religious orders, Hindu fakhirs, Buddhist monkhoods newly regulated in Thailand, Burma, and Tokugawa Japan, Confucian ritual reasserted in Vietnam and China — while missionary and pilgrimage networks pushed geographically into "decentralized spirituality" in Africa, the Pacific, and Asia's inner frontiers. Bayly frames this explicitly as running in parallel with, and using the same infrastructure as, state-building: he contrasts Benedict Anderson's print-mediated "imagined communities" of the nation (benedict-anderson) with the far larger readerships mobilized by Bibles, Qurans, and Buddhist jatakas. In the Ottoman world this "modes of dominion" project meant secular rulers asserting control over waqf endowments against the clerical establishment (ulama) that had administered them.

Agency ran from below and from the periphery, not from missionary or colonial centers. The "middling sort" — schoolmasters, clerks, small tradesmen, prosperous farmers — along with women as nuns, schoolmistresses, and household reformers, did much of the actual work of propagating standardized observance. Conversion succeeded only where it adapted to, rather than replaced, existing belief and social need: Krio pastors, not British ones, evangelized West Africa; Maori chiefs sought to "capture" a missionary's charisma and convert "on their own terms"; Christianity moved easily where societies already had a "high god," and Islam filled the space of Buddhist dharma. Bayly's closing move, following Susan Bayly, inverts the standard question: not only why peripheral peoples converted to Christianity, but how Christianity itself "was converted" by them — and by 1914 the process of standardization was still far from complete, with spirit cults, witchcraft, and millenarian movements persisting or expanding alongside it.

Notable passages

The unit's opening thesis statement — the nineteenth century as an age of religious "reemergence" rather than decline — is the counter-claim the rest of the chapter exists to substantiate, so it is curated whole as the frame for everything that follows. The historiographical paragraph on Weber and Chadwick is curated because it shows exactly how deep and unexamined the secularization consensus ran among professional historians, which is what makes Bayly's reversal consequential rather than merely contrarian. The "fly in the wheel, not the wheel itself" passage is Bayly's sharpest single formulation of the tactical-retreat argument and is worth preserving verbatim for that phrase alone. The "Religions expanded 'down' ... by imposing uniformity" passage states directly, in miniature, the dual internal/external mechanism that gives the chapter's next section its title — this resonates with the World Machines idea of legibility-as-machine-core as a project shared across state, capital, and church (hedge: Bayly's religious authorities are pursuing doctrinal purity and salvation, not administrative control as such, even where the technologies and the uniformizing effect — Bayly's own "contested uniformity" — closely rhyme with legibility's push toward standardization within the modernity-machine frame). The passage on Maori and African converts "capturing" missionary charisma "on their own terms" is curated because it is the clearest statement that world-religion expansion depended on local reinterpretation rather than passive reception. The closing Susan Bayly passage is curated as the unit's thesis in reverse — a reminder that the uniformizing project this chapter otherwise describes as a rising "empire" was, in Bayly's own account, incomplete, contested, and shaped by the peoples it claimed to convert, a qualification that keeps this chapter's resonance with world-machines-style top-down legibility building honest rather than overstated.
