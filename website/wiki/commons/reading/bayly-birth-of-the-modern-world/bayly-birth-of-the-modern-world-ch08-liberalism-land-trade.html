Liberalism, Land, and the Politics of Free Trade in the Long Nineteenth Century

Commons · The Birth of the Modern World · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Eighteenth-century European liberalism developed a distinctively rupture-oriented intellectual style that traveled worldwide to be creatively reworked by non-Western thinkers, while its practical application to land tenure stalled into pacts between bureaucrats and landowners and its property theory doubled as a license to dispossess nomadic peoples, and its free-trade doctrine, enforced imperially, provoked a global backlash of protectionist economic nationalism.

The unit opens by asking how distinctive eighteenth-century European liberal thought (Descartes, Locke, Hume, Kant, Smith, Condorcet, Vico) really was against comparable rational and empirical traditions elsewhere, then follows liberal doctrine as it travels worldwide and is reworked by figures such as Raja Ram Mohun Roy, before turning to two arenas where liberal theory collided with practice: the near-universal failure of radical land reform, and a global free-trade doctrine that provoked its own protectionist, nationalist counter-doctrine.

Themes

A qualified Western exceptionalism. Bayly credits Islamic, Chinese and Buddhist traditions with genuine rational and empirical sophistication, but argues eighteenth-century European thought is distinctive in its willingness to treat the past as fully redundant rather than reconciled with: david-hume's skepticism eroded the authority of tradition, priesthood and monarchy without an anti-religious program, and even Buddhist anti-theism or radical Sufism's inversion of the shahada never produced the same abstract, secular concept of "society" that giambattista-vico's work inspired in Europe and that Bengali radicals later imported into the word samaj.

Creative, non-Western re-authorship of liberalism. Liberal ideas were not simply diffused but reinvented: Raja Ram Mohun Roy fused late-Mughal statecraft, a reconstructed Hindu monotheism, and adam-smith-style free-trade economics into what Bayly calls the first Indian liberal politics, and parallel improvisations recur worldwide — Russian Decembrist nobles invoking natural rights against the tsar, Mexican radical Ignacio Altamirano, an Ottoman ambassador reading "liberty" through classical Islamic justice, a Brazilian abolitionist calling himself an English liberal in the Brazilian parliament, Meiji reformers studying George Washington, Munshi Abdullah in the Malay world.

Liberalism's hierarchy paradox. The liberal assault on the "embodied hierarchy" of priests, nobles and kings, and on feudal land monopoly, did not abolish hierarchy but re-founded it as a hierarchy of races and cultures ranked by enlightenment, commerce and market freedom — a move that resonates with Bayly's larger thesis (elsewhere named "contested uniformity") that a globally uniform doctrine produces new, unequal differentiation even where this unit does not use that phrase directly.

Land reform stalls into a landlord-bureaucrat pact. Despite liberal hostility to latifundia (Irish and Latin American estates, Prussian holdings, south Indian "fortholding" warrior-kings), radical land redistribution rarely happened: fiscally weak nineteenth-century states needed landowners on the spot to tax, raise troops, and police unrest, so reform settled into a bargain in which landlords reinvented themselves as agro-businessmen (Prussian junkers, Mexican hacendados, Javanese regenten) in exchange for state-built roads, railways and canals — a pact that resonates with legibility-as-machine-core: the state's demand for taxable, provable, "useful" property outran its capacity to enforce that legibility without local intermediaries.

Property doctrine as a dispossession technology. The same liberal theory that grounded rights in property became, when applied to peoples judged not to hold property in a legible, "useful" form — nomads, herders, hunter-gatherers, shifting cultivators — a license for expropriation: terra nullius migrated from Protestant-Catholic polemics over papal land grants into a "stages of history" doctrine used against Aboriginal Australians, Amerindians and Siberian peoples, merging with older "chosen people" theology in Boer, American-settler and, by century's end, Zionist land claims.

Free trade as imperial enforcement, then national counter-doctrine. Free-trade doctrine (Smith, Condorcet, Quesnay) displaced chartered monopoly companies and was imposed by force on China, the Ottomans and Latin America after 1830, but Friedrich List's 1830s critique — that a global free-trade regime favors whoever industrialized first — fed a genuinely worldwide economic nationalism that, via institutions such as China's Cohong and mughal-administration's ideal of territorial self-sufficiency, in several cases predated organized political nationalism against colonial rule, a sequencing worth setting beside benedict-anderson's account of nationalism's own conditions of emergence, though Bayly's claim here is about economic priority rather than print-cultural priority.

Notable passages

The Hume/Kant passage (c1) shows the mechanism of intellectual rupture Bayly wants to isolate: not a program of atheism, but the incremental removal of divine warrant from politics. The Ram Mohun Roy passage (c2) is the unit's central case of creative colonial reception, a genuinely hybrid politics rather than imitation. The hierarchy-paradox passage (c3) states plainly that liberal anti-feudalism produced a new race-and-culture ranking. The landlord-bureaucrat pact passage (c4) explains why land reform failed in practice from Prussia to Java to Mexico. The terra nullius passage (c5) is the sharpest statement of property theory's exclusionary underside, tying it to the era's dispossession of native peoples across three continents. The economic-nationalism passage (c6) makes the unit's least obvious claim: that non-Western protectionism against free trade often preceded, and fed, organized nationalist politics.
