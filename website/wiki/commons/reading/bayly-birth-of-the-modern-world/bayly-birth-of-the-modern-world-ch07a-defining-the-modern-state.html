Defining the Modern State: Myth, Technology, and a Century of Historiography

Commons · The Birth of the Modern World · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Opening Chapter 7 (“Myths and Technologies of the Modern State”), Bayly defines the nineteenth-century “modern state” as a uniformizing, territorially bounded administrative technology that also functions as a self-aggrandizing idea, then traces a century of historiographical argument about the state before insisting that outside Europe and Japan “statishness” remained diffuse, contested, and often more myth than institution.

Opening Chapter 7 (“Myths and Technologies of the Modern State”), Bayly defines what he means by the nineteenth-century “modern state” — a regime claiming uniform administrative, legal, and educational control over a closely bounded territory and a monopolistic claim on its subjects’ loyalty — and then surveys, tradition by tradition, how historians and social theorists came to treat “the state” as an object of study at all, before arguing that outside the strong-state cores of Europe and Japan, “statishness” was diffuse, contested, and frequently more myth than machine.

Themes

The modern state is defined by uniformity and monopoly, not mere intrusiveness. Earlier polities had been intrusive in specific places and times; the nineteenth-century state instead sought coherent, cartographic, taxable knowledge of its territory, and jealously guarded against trans-territorial loyalties (religious, ethnic, dynastic) that had characterized the old order. Bayly locates the real “turning point” not in the Napoleonic state (“almost a freak development,” soon deflated after 1815) but in 1850–70, when industrialization, armaments, fear of revolution, and nationalism combined to harden Bismarck’s Prussia, the Second French Empire, and British India into recognizably modern states.

The chapter reads almost as a case study in what World Machines calls a world machine’s Dawn, though Bayly refuses a single prime mover. The nineteenth-century state’s drive toward maps, censuses, uniform law, and territorial monopoly is exactly the kind of deep, slowly-assembling infrastructure modernity-machine names — but Bayly's state-building is explicitly multi-causal (military, fiscal, ideological, and demand-driven all at once) and uneven rather than a clean phased construction, so the resonance is partial: infrastructure-building without WMP's tidier lifecycle.

The state is simultaneously a technology and an idea. Alongside its administrative and military machinery, Bayly insists the state “was also an idea” — an aspiration to complete sovereignty invoked in the name of “the people” or “the nation,” with a life of its own irreducible to class interest or military necessity. This double nature is the chapter's organizing thesis and structures everything that follows: top-down state-building by threatened elites (Japan, China, Ethiopia, the European empires) sits alongside bottom-up demand for state services (US farmers wanting cheap rail access; continental businessmen wanting tariffs).

A century of historiography had to converge before “the state” became a category at all. Bayly traces a genuinely comparative intellectual history: the Anglo-American liberal tradition (Macaulay, Trevelyan) was constitutionally suspicious of the state and wrote around it, treating it as a slightly sinister Continental invention; Continental Europeans (Tocqueville, Hegel, Marx, Weber) could not avoid theorizing it directly. Only after 1960 — via Brewer's Sinews of Power, E. P. Thompson's class-domination reading, MacDonagh's and Harris's work on British administration, and then, after Vietnam, an explosion of “colonial state” scholarship (Cohn, Scott, michel-foucault) and Skowronek's account of American state-building — did English-language historiography catch up to the Continental one. This is close to a textbook case of a paradigm assembling itself silently across nationally-siloed traditions until it becomes visible as a shared object — resonant with history-machine's account of intellectual paradigms accreting from distributed, independent components, though here it is a discipline's attention shifting rather than a civilizational infrastructure emerging.

Bayly pushes back against the “homogeneous, all-seeing” state of Scott and the new imperial history. He credits James Scott's Seeing Like a State and Foucault's governmentality with correctly naming the state's ordering ambition, but warns that historians' reliance on state documents makes the argument circular (“It is very easy to assume that states are therefore responsible for any and all social change”), and that state power visibly rose and fell — in Britain, Germany, and the US alike — rather than growing monotonically. The state's “urge to order and control” that Scott describes is precisely the legibility-as-machine-core impulse WMP tracks in the Modernity Machine — maps, surveys, censuses, uniform administration — but Bayly's insistence on peaks, troughs, and outright fiction (colonial administrators in parts of Africa “could be easily accommodated in a single small hut”) is a sharp corrective against reading legibility projects as automatically effective: the aspiration to legibility and its actual reach are different things.

Outside the European/Japanese core, “statishness” was diffuse, appropriable, and sometimes mythical rather than institutional. Bayly's most striking move is to treat the state as something that “can be magicked into existence” by anyone with the right performance of authority: Cecil Rhodes's chartered companies, missionaries in southern Africa, and millenarian movements (the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom, Mormon Utah) all did state-like work — surveying, recording, organizing labor, claiming sovereignty — without being states. In Iran, clerical religious charisma actually displaced the weaker, merely secular Qajar state. He closes with a five-part typology of “statishness” that never centralized: power diffused among local elites (Britain, the US); a competing “counter-state” institution (Islamic law, the Buddhist monkhood); chartered corporate bodies (the Hudson's Bay Company); lineage- and age-set-based authority (much of Africa, Asia, the Pacific); and mobile peoples for whom state power was “little more than a dream of once-and-future kings.”

Notable passages

The chapter's operating definition — legible, uniform, territorially bounded, jealous of rival loyalties — is set out in the “Dimensions of the Modern State” passage (c1), which reads almost as a checklist of the Modernity Machine's legibility program. Bayly immediately complicates this technological picture by insisting the state “was also an idea” with its own ideological force (c2) — the “myths and technologies” pairing that titles the whole chapter. The historiographical survey turns explicitly to Scott and Foucault (c3), naming the two frameworks — ordering/improving projects, and diffuse “governmentality” — that most directly anticipate a WMP-style reading of the state as infrastructure rather than actor. Bayly's account of the state as something “magicked into existence” by companies, missions, and millenarian kingdoms (c4) is the chapter's most vivid demonstration that “state” names a repertoire of performances as much as an institution. He closes the argument (c5) with a five-fold typology of non-centralized “statishness” that refuses any single global convergence — a caution against treating even the Modernity Machine's legibility drive as a completed, uniform project rather than a contested and geographically uneven one.
