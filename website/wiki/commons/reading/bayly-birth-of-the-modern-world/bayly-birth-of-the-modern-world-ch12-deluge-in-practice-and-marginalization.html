The Deluge in Practice: Dispossession, Missions, and the Uneven Rescue of Native Peoples

Commons · The Birth of the Modern World · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Comparative case studies of Maori, southern African, and North American dispossession trace a common sequence of demographic collapse and land loss whose tempo depended on how centralized the native polity was, followed by missionary activity and administrative "trusteeship" that alternately eroded and belatedly protected native peoples, while the parallel destruction of game, forests, and fisheries went largely unchecked.

Working through paired case studies — New Zealand, southern Africa, and the United States and Canada — this unit shows how the abstract "deluge" of settler expansion actually played out on the ground between roughly 1780 and 1914: early ambiguous coexistence between indigenous polities and traders or settlers curdled, once numbers, firearms, and steam and rail communications tipped the balance, into demographic catastrophe, land seizure, and war. It then turns from conquest to its aftermath — the shifting role of Christian missions, the rise and collapse of assimilation policy into racialized segregation, and a late-nineteenth-century partial reversal in which colonial states and the new science of anthropology began, unevenly and self-interestedly, to protect the native peoples they had spent a century dispossessing. A final section extends the same logic of unchecked exploitation to nature itself: furs, whales, fish, and forests, destroyed by the same technologies that destroyed native polities, with no working conservation regime yet in place by 1914.

Themes

A common sequence of dispossession. Across New Zealand, southern Africa, and North America, contact begins in relative parity — Bay of Islands whaling stations trading with enriched Maori chieftains, a mixed Cape Colony of Dutch settlers, slaves, and Khoikhoi, US treaties that "tempted" Sioux and Cheyenne leaders with guarantees over the interior — before settler numbers, missionary and state backing, and superior technology ("the steamship, telegraph, and leaps forward in medicine and techniques of war") tip the balance decisively. Bayly's New Zealand census figures (whites rising from about 2,000 in 1842 to 700,000 in 1896 while Maori fall from roughly 80,000 to 40,000) stand for the pattern repeated with the Xhosa, Zulu, and Ndebele, and with the Sioux, Cheyenne, and Great Plains peoples generally.

Centralization shaped the tempo, not the fact, of dispossession. The unit's clearest comparative claim is that centralized polities with a resource surplus — Maori, Zulu, Ndebele, the buffalo-rich peoples of the American plains — could mount patriotic, pitched resistance and win real if temporary concessions, while more decentralized, dispersed peoples (Canadian prairie nations weakened by the over-hunting of buffalo, the San) succumbed more gradually to "piecemeal settlement, disease, alcoholism, and slow cession of their lands." Bayly is explicit that the mechanisms of erosion were not always violent: "dependency eroded their tribal autonomy as readily as violence," as with the medical supplies and food used to secure Canadian treaty cessions in the 1870s.

Missions cut both ways. Roman Catholic missionary effort, strong from Latin America to the Philippines and Indonesia, slackened in the later eighteenth century as regimes closed their doors and Jesuits were expelled from Spanish and French territory; a newly energized Protestant missionary public then expanded rapidly after 1800. At first, missions could act as "a counterweight to the growing rush of white settlement," and produced genuinely hybrid communities — chiefs grafting Old Testament genealogies onto their own lineages, congregations built around a borrowed "chosen people" identity. Later, in the age of conquest, conversion became "the only resort" for demoralized survivors, and missionary time discipline and cash-crop training helped finish what war had begun: "neither assimilation nor reservation, but widespread demoralization, was the longer-term result."

Assimilation curdled into segregation. US policy briefly held, via figures like the anthropologist Lewis Henry Morgan, that "total cultural assimilation was not only possible, but desirable," encouraging individual land allotment out of tribal reservations. That project was abandoned in the early twentieth century, replaced by a harder biological racism that filed Native Americans alongside Black Americans and Chinese immigrants under a "newly 'scientific' version of racist segregation" — one instance, in Bayly's telling, of a broader global reclassification of peoples by race and civilizational rank.

Late-century administrators reversed course, imperfectly. By the 1880s–1900s, colonial and national states increasingly saw the settler frontier itself, not the native population, as the disorderly and costly element, and moved toward an ambivalent trusteeship: "they sought to count, classify, and section off native populations which they had been taught were their special responsibility." This is a legibility project in the fullest sense — census, boundary, jurisdiction — and it could immiserate as readily as protect, "cutting them off from opportunities in the new economy." The new discipline of anthropology performed a parallel double duty, classifying native peoples as "living fossils" worthy of the same conservation as museum specimens — objectifying and protecting in the same gesture. This resonates with legibility-as-machine-core and modernity-machine: the state's counting-and-classifying apparatus turned, here, on populations it was simultaneously destroying and safeguarding — though Bayly's actors are colonial trustees and field anthropologists, not a machine-scale abstraction, and the "protection" on offer was often as bleak as the dispossession it replaced.

Global homogenization beyond the settler frontier. Bayly widens the frame past white settlement to Taiwan, Hokkaido, and the South Chinese mountains (absorbed by expanding Chinese and Japanese populations), to Vietnamese settlement of the Moi highlands, and to Indian farmers pushing into the Dang forests and Bengal's Santal country — all instances of the same "inroads into the variety of human cultures and life-styles." The destroyers here are not only European; uniformity-producing expansion is carried as readily by Chinese, Vietnamese, and Indian agrarian settlers. This loss of variety under advancing uniformity rhymes with divergence-machine's framing of variety as exactly what legibility and standardization consume, though Bayly registers it as a historical and demographic tragedy rather than a machine-theoretic dynamic.

Adaptation survived where sovereignty did not. The unit closes its dispossession narrative on a qualified, unsentimental note: some native peoples found paying niches inside the system that had broken them — as guides and trackers for European hunting parties, as producers of "curios" and (in New Zealand) a trade in shrunken heads for the European market, and as recruits into colonial armies (Fijians, Naga, Kachin, Karen and Chin levies, Pathans, North African Tuareg, the Russian "Savage Division"). Bayly explicitly denies these peoples were the world's "first conservationists" — their historically light ecological footprint reflected simple tools and small numbers, not restraint as principle.

Ecological depredation ran in parallel, unchecked. The same technologies that dispossessed native peoples — firearms, steamships, railways, industrial tools — drove fur-bearing and meat-bearing animals of the Russian steppes and American prairies to near-extermination between 1780 and 1914, collapsed whale and fish stocks in the southern oceans, and stripped teak and lesser timber for shipping, railway sleepers, and pit props. Naturalists and administrators were already alarming over "desiccation" — soil drying and erosion — by the 1830s–40s, especially in dependent empires, "but the lessons of conservation... were not yet learned even 100 years after the end of the period."

Notable passages

The New Zealand census figures (c1) are the unit's single clearest quantification of the "deluge," making an abstract demographic reversal checkable in raw numbers. The comparison of centralized and decentralized polities (c2) supplies the unit's organizing analytical claim about why some peoples fought and lost dramatically while others eroded more quietly. The passage on state "trusteeship" (c3) is the sharpest statement of the paradox running through the whole unit: counting, classifying, and sectioning off a population is simultaneously an act of care and of control. The ecological-depredation passage (c4) generalizes the deluge from human to natural populations, and the "living fossils" passage (c5) shows the same ambivalence at work inside the new science of anthropology. Finally, the passage on hybrid mission communities (c6) captures the two-way, syncretic character of early contact before later chapters in the same unit show it hardening into an instrument of assimilation.
