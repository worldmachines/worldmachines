The Social Darwinist Alibi: Nietzsche, Sumner, and the American Contest over Aggression, Wealth, and Law

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay traces the international vogue for Social Darwinism as a bourgeois alibi for aggression, from German popularizers and Nietzsche's will to power through the American clash among moralizing skeptics, propagandizing novelists, philanthropic robber barons, and a legal system that entrenched laissez-faire in the Fourteenth Amendment.

This unit surveys the international debate, from roughly the 1870s to 1905, over reading the "struggle for existence" into human affairs. It moves from German cultural popularizers (Haeckel, von Hellwald, Lippert, the two-voiced Otto Ammon) through Nietzsche, who gave the doctrine its most potent literary and psychological form without being a biologist at all; to its serious opponents (Bagehot, Huxley, Lester Frank Ward, and Darwin himself); to its most disciplined American exponent, William Graham Sumner, whose alibi for domestic economic aggression stopped hard at war and empire; to its popularization by naturalist novelists Jack London and Frank Norris; to the self-contradicting careers of Andrew Carnegie and John D. Rockefeller, ruthless competitors who preferred monopoly to competition and then recycled the same providential logic into philanthropy; to the doctrine's hardening into American constitutional law via the Fourteenth Amendment and the Lochner decision; and finally to a coda on the Franco-Prussian-War-era depopulation panic and the rise of eugenic anxiety.

Themes

Social Darwinism was less a coherent theory than a family of contradictory slogans held together by rhetorical convenience. Gay is explicit that "the appearance of unanimity" among Social Darwinists was "an illusion, generated by a handful of glib advocates" who papered over real disagreement with short, vague, quotable maxims. He sorts the field into three parties — fundamentalists, liberals, and skeptics — and even within the doctrine's own ranks there was an optimistic (Spencerian) and a pessimistic (Vacher de Lapouge) wing. The doctrine spread not through one theorist's system but through independent popularizers, novelists, and businessmen each reinforcing the same inference — which faintly echoes how a history-machine assembles silently from distributed, uncoordinated components until it looks, in retrospect, like a single movement; the fit is partial, since what assembled here was a contested rhetorical common sense rather than an institutional paradigm, and its incoherence is Gay's whole point.

Nietzsche supplied the psychological radicalization the biologists never intended. He was, Gay stresses, "not a biologist" but a "cultural aristocrat" idolized by bourgeois readers precisely for seeming anti-bourgeois. His claim that "life itself is essentially appropriation, injury, overwhelming of the alien and the weaker" folds domination and cruelty directly into the definition of life via the will to power. This half-resonates with Conatus — both name a drive to persist and expand that is bigger than mere survival — but Nietzsche's version is not neutral about domination: appropriation and injury are constitutive of the drive itself, where Spinoza's striving carries no such built-in aggression against others.

The opposition was serious, cross-national, and included Darwin himself. Bagehot argued struggle could be sublimated into "a government by discussion"; Huxley insisted ethical progress meant "combating," not imitating, the "cosmic process," since nature is "kind to the criminal as to the good citizen"; Ward attacked laissez-faire's non sequitur that natural law forecloses human control. Darwin himself, writing to Lyell just after publishing the Origin, mocked a newspaper's claim that he had proved "'might is right'"; Gay notes wryly that Darwin, like Marx, could disown his own -ism.

Sumner's alibi for aggression was domestic and economic only — it stopped hard at the water's edge. Sumner preached that "the world owes nobody a living" and that before nature's tribunal "a man has no more right to life than a rattlesnake," yet he denounced American imperialism as "a case of genuine political earth hunger" and "pure outrage, cruelty, and aggression," and made his hero not the plutocrat but the overtaxed, unglamorous "Forgotten Man." The same thinker who justified ruthless competition at home refused to let the alibi license war or conquest abroad.

Novelists did the popularizing that professors and pamphleteers could not. Jack London and Frank Norris, both indebted to Zola, dramatized "life as warfare without pity ending only in death" for a mass audience; Gay calls their stories "embodied alibis for literally mortal competition," a more effective vector for the doctrine than Sumner's classroom or Spencer's treatises.

Carnegie and Rockefeller lived the alibi's central contradiction. Both preached merciless competition while building monopolies specifically to escape "cutthroat competition," then redirected the same providential, scientific rhetoric into philanthropy — Carnegie's libraries, Rockefeller's university and foundation — without ever renouncing the worldview that had made them rich. Gay treats this less as hypocrisy than as the alibi absorbing its own contradictions.

The alibi eventually hardened into constitutional law. Company lawyers repurposed the Fourteenth Amendment — designed to protect freed slaves — into a "liberty of contract" shield against labor regulation, reaching its apogee in Lochner v. New York (1905). Holmes's dissent, "The Fourteenth Amendment does not enact Mr. Herbert Spencer's Social Statics," shows an alibi for aggression migrating from rhetoric into durable legal infrastructure — and losing, for the moment, even when a sitting justice named it as ideology rather than law.

A coda ties the struggle-for-existence idiom to a transatlantic demographic panic. After 1870, French anxiety over contraception and depopulation, matched by German fear of Russian birthrates, fed directly into eugenic research (Dugdale's Jukes study) and Theodore Roosevelt's fear of "race suicide" — showing the same idiom migrating from economic combat to biopolitics.

Notable passages

The Nietzsche paragraph is the unit's philosophical center of gravity: it isolates the passage from Beyond Good and Evil that makes aggression definitional to life itself, and frames Nietzsche's bourgeois cult status as ironic given his contempt for bourgeois "conformity, prudence, vulgarity, and materialism."

The long Sumner passage ("Sociology, as Sumner conceived it...") is the starkest statement of Social Darwinist first principles in the unit — nature as niggardly tribunal, altruism pointedly absent from Sumner's list of human motives, and the aphoristic "two men cannot eat the same loaf of bread" doing the work of an entire economic theory.

The Darwin/Lyell passage, with its "sketch map suffers the defects of easy legibility" self-critique, is the pivot where Gay steps back from cataloguing the doctrine to note its incoherence and Darwin's own distance from it; the phrase itself is a productive half-echo of Legibility — Gay is naming exactly the WMP-recognized cost of legibility, that collapsing a messy multinational field into three tidy factions (fundamentalist/liberal/skeptic) buys clarity at the price of the real "crosscurrents" and "unexpected alliances" it erases.

The Carnegie–Rockefeller paragraph crystallizes the volume's alibi-and-paradox structure in miniature: men who invoked Darwinian competition to justify their rise built monopolies specifically to avoid competing, undercutting their own rationale from within.

The Sumner anti-imperialism passage is essential counterweight to the earlier Sumner quote — without it, Sumner reads as a pure apologist for pitiless struggle, when Gay's actual point is that his alibi was self-limiting, aimed at domestic economic rivalry and explicitly withheld from war and conquest.

The Holmes/Lochner passage closes the American strand of the argument by showing the alibi's furthest reach: from magazine slogans and business rhetoric into Supreme Court doctrine, with Holmes's dissent naming the ideological substitution outright even as the majority prevailed.
