Wilhelm Busch and the Cultivation of Cruel Laughter

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Wilhelm Busch, Germany's most popular nineteenth-century humorist and creator of Max und Moritz, is Gay's paradigm case for bourgeois humor as a socially licensed outlet for aggressive and sadistic fantasy, one whose cruelty toward children, animals, wives, the French, and Jews reveals structural anxieties of the age rather than personal pathology.

Gay reconstructs the life and reception of Wilhelm Busch (1832–1908), the illustrator-poet of Max und Moritz and the most widely beloved German humorist of the century, as the fullest case study of "humor as licensed aggression." Busch's enormously popular rhymed fables inflict decapitation, impalement, mutilation, and gratuitous cruelty on children, animals, spouses, and enemies, and his readers — Freud among them, who called him "our friend Busch" — found this uproarious rather than shocking. Gay reads Busch's biography (a joyless, duty-bound bourgeois upbringing; a retreat into rural solitude; a guarded, sexless private life) against the aggression that saturates his art, and argues that Busch's popularity, not his idiosyncrasy, is the historical point: his readers' fantasy life matched his own, and his work let a repressed, self-disciplined bourgeois public discharge impulses it could not otherwise admit to holding.

Themes

Humor as a socially acceptable outlet for the "buried wishes to surpass and humiliate, to maim and murder" — Gay's opening thesis, illustrated by a hallucinatory summary of Busch's plots (a husband blinded and burned by his own wife, a pedagogue's caning imitated by his pupils on their dogs, a garden nuisance killed by having a blowpipe rammed down his throat). The mechanism is reassurance: "wishing is not doing; fantasy is not reality," so the humor "reenacted the jungle warfare of the unconscious" without, apparently, causing harm.

The contemporary defense — cruelty is funny because it is impossible. Gay stages Friedrich Theodor Vischer's 1880 apologia: violence in Busch reads as comedy only because "the accent must fall on impossibility" — it is too absurd to be believed, so the reader "shudders... and laughs at the artist's crazy idea." Gay partly credits this (Busch's exaggerations are the "extravagant repressed fantasies of the child") but complicates it: he insists Busch's world was not antiseptic — bourgeois households routinely handled butchered fish, slaughtered pigs, and mutilated bodies — so part of the humor's force was the shock of recognition, not pure fantasy.

The convenient Other: Jews and the besieged French. Despite genuine friendships with Jewish intimates (the conductor Hermann Levi, the writer Paul Lindau), Busch casually deployed "the conventional stereotype of 'the Jew': physically and morally repulsive, addicted to money, and very sly," most bluntly in the hook-nosed caricature Schmulchen Schiefelbeiner — a figure Busch used to flatter his own and his readers' self-satisfaction ("that he and his kind were surely better looking than that"). His single foray into wartime nationalism, the cartoon Monsieur Jacques a Paris, gloats without restraint over the starvation of besieged Parisians during the Franco-Prussian siege; Gay judges it among the lowest specimens of German Schadenfreude over the enemy's plight, evidence that "the stimulating, dimly erotic wartime atmosphere had weakened the checks on his raw aggressive impulses." Both cases show aggression normally sublimated into private, apolitical humor flaring outward toward a national or ethnic Other under specific historical pressure (unification, war), then receding.

Cultivation as discipline, and its dialectical double. Busch's own upbringing modeled the book's title concept directly: a stern, undemonstrative father, farming the boy out to relatives at nine, and (per the appended footnote) a household in which "only boys are flogged" and the cane stood as "the supreme educator." Busch's mature verdict — "it is, in fact, necessary to subject the pleasure principle to the reality principle" — reframes his whole career as a machinery for managing, not denying, aggression: the book's closing line calls his work "an extraordinary display of how to manage aggression by dissolving violence in laughter."

Political quietism punctuated by aggression. Busch professed contempt for political activism ("happiness... lies in his own head, in the harmonious cultivation of his personal capacities") and mocked his own countrymen's indifference to Bismarck's fall in Eduards Traum. Yet he shared, unreflectively, in the near-universal German cult of Bismarck as "our hero" and "the great pilot," illustrating how bourgeois political psychology of the era combined private disengagement with an uncritical, almost apolitical nationalism.

Notable passages

The opening catalogue of plots (the drunken wife, the caning pedagogue, the blinded husband, the killer with the blowpipe) establishes, before any argument is made, the sheer density of licensed cruelty in Busch's ordinary output. The direct thesis statement — humor securing a "socially acceptable outlet" for wishes to "maim and murder" — is the interpretive key Gay applies throughout. The Schmulchen Schiefelbeiner passage and the Monsieur Jacques a Paris passage together carry the chapter's treatment of Gay's "convenient Other": both show respectable affection for individuals coexisting with structural contempt for the category (Jews, the French) once a socially sanctioned occasion (a caricature commission, a war) presents itself. Vischer's "accent must fall on impossibility" is the period's own theory of why the cruelty didn't count as cruelty — which Gay accepts only partway. The closing passage on the reality principle supplies the chapter's resolution: Busch's laughter is presented not as an escape from bourgeois discipline but as its necessary safety valve.

World Machines resonance

Busch's humor functions here as legitimacy and control infrastructure in miniature: a mass-consumed, low-cost mechanism by which the nineteenth-century bourgeois order (part of the long dominance of the modernity-machine) metabolized aggression that its own disciplinary demands (the cane, duty, self-control) generated, without resorting to institutional violence or destabilizing the social order it depended on. The resonance is partial — this is a psychological/cultural safety valve operating at the level of the individual reader and the printed page, not the measurement-and-registration apparatus (legibility-as-machine-core) the world-machines framework usually means by a machine's control infrastructure — but the underlying function (channel destabilizing impulse into a form that reinforces rather than threatens the prevailing order) is the same logic at a different scale.
