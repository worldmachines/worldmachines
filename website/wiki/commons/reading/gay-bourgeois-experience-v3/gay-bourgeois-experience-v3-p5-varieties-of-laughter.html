Varieties of Laughter

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Peter Gay opens Part Five, "The Bite of Wit," by surveying a century of theorizing about laughter — Hobbes, Baudelaire, Bergson, Meredith, Vischer, Lipps, and Freud's 1905 jokebook — alongside practicing humorists like Lewis Carroll, Edward Lear, and W. S. Gilbert, to argue that Victorian humor in all its variety was a socially licensed outlet for aggression.

Opening Part Five ("The Bite of Wit") of The Cultivation of Hatred, Gay surveys nineteenth-century theorists of laughter — Hobbes, Baudelaire, Bergson, Meredith, Vischer, Lipps, and finally Freud's 1905 Jokes and Their Relation to the Unconscious — alongside practicing humorists (Lewis Carroll, Edward Lear, Christian Morgenstern, Mark Twain, W. S. Gilbert) to argue that however innocent or playful humor looks, its animating force across the Victorian era was aggression: boasting, self-punishing, or aimed outward at a chosen target, always shielded by the pretense that "it's only a joke."

Themes

Humor as licensed aggression. Gay opens from ordinary language — humor is already spoken of in terms of "biting, slashing, cutting" — and builds outward through Hobbes's theory of laughter as "sudden glory" arising from comparative superiority, then Baudelaire's more feverish gloss: laughter as a "satanic idea," a badge of rebellion against divine order that simultaneously betrays the laugher's own insecurity. Wit, on this account, is boasting and self-defense at once.

The overdetermined, Janus-faced psychology of wit. Humor does multiple, often contradictory psychic work in the same act: releasing tension, indulging regression from "punitive superego," or performing "ferocious self-criticism." Crucially, joking is a transaction between teller and audience that "ingratiates the teller with a chosen audience" while, "at the same time and by the same means," it "stigmatizes others as outsiders to be disliked or despised" — humor manufactures in-groups and out-groups by the same stroke.

Innocent play against a violent undertow. Gay concedes real innocent humor exists — children's jokes, punning, Jean Paul's claim that "freedom generates jokes and jokes generate freedom" — but insists "the boundaries between playfulness and aggression are porous and indeterminate." His proof texts are the great Victorian nonsense-makers: Lewis Carroll and Edward Lear's whimsical verse, which "deposited in their work the tensions of barely controlled murderous feelings," most starkly in Carroll's juvenile poem where a boy baits his fishhook with his own younger brother.

W. S. Gilbert as extended case study. The Bab Ballads — cannibalism ("The Yarn of the 'Nancy Bell'"), a besotted executioner's fiancée, a boy who tortures his family and pokers his father, an ogre who eats good children out of love — are read as comic license for material that would otherwise be unspeakable. Gay's clinching evidence is biographical: a self-portrait Gilbert scribbled late in life, annotated with confessions ("I hate my fellow-man," "I loathe everybody," "I like pinching little babies") that strip away the comic mask entirely.

A century of theorizing the joke. Gay marches through the era's "theory industry": Bain's laughter as "degradation of some person or interest possessing dignity"; Meredith's insistence that wit is "warlike," a Toledo blade that needs "an adversary"; Vischer's more forgiving view that self-directed comic aggression is a form of self-knowledge; Lipps's claim that Aristophanic comedy is the "highest humor"; and Bergson's "momentary anesthesia of the heart," in which laughter becomes a social "corrective" — punishment dressed as amusement.

Freud's economic closing argument. The chapter ends by folding Freud's 1905 Jokes into the volume's larger drive-theory arc: "joke work" mirrors "dream work"; "tendentious" jokes split into obscene and hostile categories that anticipate the 1920s theory of Eros versus aggression; and jokes work economically, discharging the psychic energy that ordinary social repression continuously consumes, so that "the joke represents a rebellion against ... authority, a liberation from its pressure."

A loose resonance with world-machines. The chapter's real argument is that Victorian society did not merely suppress aggression but built a licensed, low-stakes institution — humor — for discharging it without disturbing the underlying order; Freud's point that repression's maintenance costs continuous psychic "expenditure," which jokes economize, reads almost like a maintenance budget for a control infrastructure. The fit is partial: this is a psychological safety valve rather than the technological/institutional infrastructure the term usually names, and its effect is to stabilize the existing order rather than to resist capture — closer to a captured, tolerated outlet than to genuine capture-resistance. Still, the pattern (legitimized channels that bleed off pressure a system cannot otherwise absorb) is one the World Machines frame recognizes.

Notable passages

The opening paragraph states the chapter's thesis directly: joking, punning, and comedy are grounded in the same impulses as "warfare between men and women," all exercising and controlling aggression (c1). Gay then traces the "boasting" theory of laughter from Hobbes's "sudden glory" through Baudelaire's more anxious, quasi-religious reading of laughter as satanic self-assertion that both storms the heavens and betrays weakness (c2). A third passage uses Bergson's dictum that "our laughter is always the laughter of a group" to explain why German scatological humor didn't travel to England, while a rare few humorists — Mark Twain chief among them — crossed every border (c3). Gilbert's case is anchored by the passage describing his scribbled self-portrait, ringed with confessions of misanthropy, which Gay reads as unmasking the aggression beneath his "amusing guise" (c5). Freud's contribution is anchored twice: once via the passage tying his 1905 joke categories (obscene/hostile) directly to the later drive theory of life and death (c4), and once via Bergson's parallel claim that laughter functions as a social "corrective" through which "society avenges itself for the liberty one has taken with it" (c6).
