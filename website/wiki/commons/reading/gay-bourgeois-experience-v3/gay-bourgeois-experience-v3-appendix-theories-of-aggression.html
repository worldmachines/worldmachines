Appendix: Theories of Aggression

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay closes The Cultivation of Hatred with a theoretical appendix surveying twentieth-century debates over the origin of human aggression—ethologists' innate drive, environmentalists' oversocialized man, and Freud's own shifting positions culminating in the Eros/Thanatos dualism—before defending aggression's positive, mastery-oriented dimension as the drive behind civilized construction.

In this closing appendix, Gay steps back from historical narrative to lay out the theoretical scaffolding behind the whole volume: the twentieth-century argument, chiefly among ethologists and psychoanalysts, over whether aggression is an innate drive or a purely learned response, and how Freud's own thinking on the question evolved and ultimately fractured his followers. The appendix ends by rehabilitating aggression's constructive face—the "drive toward mastery"—as the psychic fuel behind civilized work, tying back to the book's thesis that the nineteenth century both destroyed and built.

Themes

The innate-drive camp and its critics. Gay frames the debate as a stand-off between ethologists (Konrad Lorenz and successors like Irenäus Eibl-Eibesfeldt), who posit an innate aggressive drive with adaptive evolutionary value that must be discharged or it will "raise fatal mischief," and environmentalist critics who suspect this biology is cover for reactionary laissez-faire politics and who instead stress the child's malleability under family, school, and class. Gay sides with neither extreme: the environmentalists rightly show that expressions of aggression are learned and historically variable, but their picture lapses into what sociologist Dennis Wrong called "the oversocialized conception of man," ignoring a "stubborn innate core" that resists outside pressure. For a historian, Gay says, the real task is to specify the shifting balance between plasticity and rigidity in any given period—a methodological statement that frames the whole book's project.

Freud's aggression was never a settled doctrine. Gay traces Freud's aggression theory from its earliest, implicit form—embedded in infantile sexuality, the abandoned seduction theory, and above all the Oedipus complex's "tense interplay of opposites" (the borrowed term "ambivalence")—through to the 1920 postulate of Eros and Thanatos as two symmetrical, mythic drives locked in permanent combat. Gay is notably skeptical of this late dualism, calling it "a triumph of theoretical clarification" bought at "a risky distance from the clinical terrain," and notes that most analysts besides Melanie Klein's circle discarded the death instinct as biologically ungrounded while keeping the underlying idea of conflicting drives (David Rapaport's line that Thanatos is "dead as a doornail" while the origin of aggression remains "unsolved" is the appendix's driest joke).

A third, pluralist school treats aggression as many things, not one. Gay gives extended space to Leo Stone's 1971 argument that aggression is not a unitary drive at all but "the aggregate of diverse acts, having diverse origins," bound only loosely by their impact on objects—hunger-driven, narcissistic, sexual, or simply pleasurable in the exercise of bodily power. Gay endorses this pluralism as congenial to historians, who are "professionally committed... to celebrating the uniqueness of each event," while still defending his own use of the general term "aggression" across the book because its manifestations bear "significant family resemblances."

Aggression has a positive face: the drive to master. The appendix's real payload is its rehabilitation of Freud's early, later-abandoned notion of a Bemächtigungstrieb ("drive toward mastery")—an urge independent of sexuality, aimed not at harming objects but at converting passivity into activity (the child who plays dentist after suffering at one). Gay draws on Ives Hendrick's "instinct to master" to argue that this same aggressive energy, redirected, is "the fuel for essential civilized and civilizing work": tool-invention, communications, trade techniques, and distributive institutions are all, in Gay's words, a kind of "attack" on the world, aimed at reducing pain and increasing pleasure "on the solid ground of reality rather than in the cloud land of fantasy." This inverts the appendix's opening pessimism—aggression is not only what must be tamed but also the raw material civilizations mine to build themselves.

The volume's thesis, stated plainly. Gay closes by naming what the whole book has been arguing: the nineteenth century was an age of "aggressive destruction and construction both," in which respectable bourgeois culture worked harder than any before it to cultivate hatred—"and needed to."

Notable passages

The Lorenz/Eibl-Eibesfeldt summary and Gay's brisk dismantling of environmentalist "oversocialized" man set up the appendix's refusal of either determinism. The passage on Freud reading Darwin as "a cautionary tale far more than a celebration" sharpens the contrast with Lorenz's more sanguine ethology. The 1920 Eros/Thanatos passage is Gay's clearest statement of why most Freudians balked at the death drive even while keeping drive-conflict as a working model. Leo Stone's pluralist catalogue—hoodlum, overprotective mother, Don Juan, someone who "deliberately omits to greet an acquaintance"—does real argumentative work by showing how heterogeneous "aggression" can be. The tools-and-institutions passage is the appendix's hinge, converting Freud's speculative drive theory into a psychological account of nineteenth-century infrastructure-building; it resonates with the world-machines idea of a machine as interlocking institutions, technologies, and practices that channel a society's energies at scale, though Gay's frame is a psychoanalytic drive (mastery) rather than an institutional-legibility mechanism—the resonance is about what fuels infrastructure-building, not how it is administered. The closing sentence is the flattest and most consequential: it is the thesis of the entire volume, stated without ornament.
