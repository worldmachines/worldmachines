In Search of Civilized Rationales: The Uneasy Legitimacy of Punishment

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Charles Holmes's furious 1865 letter after Lincoln's assassination frames Gay's opening survey of nineteenth-century penal theory, in which retribution, deterrence, and rehabilitation each function as more or less candid alibis for an appetite for revenge that reformers and hardliners alike conceded was built into human nature.

This unit opens Part Two ("Pathologies") with the private letters of Charles Holmes, an obscure northeastern manufacturer and Civil War veteran who erupted into vindictive rage on hearing of Lincoln's assassination in April 1865, then caught himself and returned to his ordinary courtship correspondence — a vignette staging the volume's central problem: how self-disciplined, respectable bourgeois experienced sudden aggression as both alien to their character and intensely gratifying. From there the unit becomes the chapter's first section proper, "In Search of Civilized Rationales," a comparative survey (Britain, France, Germany, the United States) of nineteenth-century arguments over punishment, showing how the three standard rationales — retribution, deterrence, rehabilitation — all served, in different measures, as legitimating covers for a lust for revenge that reformers and rigorists alike agreed was inborn. The disagreement was only over whether the state should master that appetite or indulge it.

Themes

  • The "license" mechanism. Bourgeois self-control could suspend itself once a person felt licensed by moral certainty. Holmes's grief over Lincoln converts pity into "a stranger... dark stem relentless and implacable" precisely because he is sure he is in the right; Gay generalizes this into "a license for a punitiveness so extreme... it seems just a trifle mad," previewing the volume's recurring argument that righteous cause is the century's most common alibi for aggression.

  • Punishment as alibi for revenge. The bulk of the unit tracks rival nineteenth-century rationales for punishment — retribution (Kant, James Fitzjames Stephen), deterrence, rehabilitation — and Gay's recurring move is to show reformers and rigorists secretly agreeing on the psychological bedrock (an ineradicable "lust for revenge") while disagreeing only about whether law should discipline or gratify it. The section's thesis is explicit: punishments were "only too often an alibi for unacceptable, even deranged vengeance."

  • Class as a filter on aggression theory. Ernest Bertrand's statistical comparison of French classes, and the rising list of British capital crimes (from roughly sixty items to some 220 by 1820, overwhelmingly protecting property), show a bourgeoisie theorizing crime in ways that flatter its own self-image: impulsive violence assigned to the poor, property offenses policed most fiercely, even as jury nullification by "good bourgeois" cuts against their own class interest.

  • Freud's Totem and Taboo as the century's founding myth. Gay reads Freud's speculative parricide-and-guilt narrative, written "just before the First World War," as the modern, secular descendant of Genesis and the Greek tragic myths — all three enact the same "majestic circular sequence: violation of order, meting out of punishment, restoration of order," and all three make punishment and self-restraint the price of civilization itself.

  • Manliness deployed against reform. Reform gets gendered: Macaulay worries about "effeminate feeling" softening juries and Parliament, Carlyle mocks philanthropists who would "cure a world's woes by rose-water," Bismarck calls abolitionists "sickly," so that toughness on crime becomes a performance of masculine virtue rather than an argument to be won or lost on its merits — a direct instance of Gay's manliness-as-alibi theme.

  • The Enlightenment inheritance and its afterlife as a legibility project. Beccaria and Montesquieu's demand for calibrated, "proportion[ate]" punishment, married to the era's new "science of man" (sociology, criminology — the word popularized by Topinard in the 1870s), gives nineteenth-century penal reform its self-image as rational administration rather than vengeance. This resonates, loosely, with legibility-as-machine-core: an attempt to render an inherently variable, personal passion (revenge) measurable and codified as proportionate law — though Gay's domain is jurisprudence and moral psychology, not the financial and administrative record-keeping (bookkeeping, cadastres) that page's cases are drawn from.

Notable passages

The unit opens on Holmes's letter to his fiancée Annie, in which pity gives way, in his own words, to a "stranger there dark stem relentless and implacable" whom he welcomes "with all the ardor... that a Lover might his chosen one" (c1) — the emotional data point the whole chapter reasons from. Gay's own gloss on the episode names the mechanism directly: Holmes could let hostility into awareness "because he was certain that he was right," and this certainty "constituted a license for a punitiveness so extreme, so uncontrolled, that it seems just a trifle mad" (c2). The chapter then widens from one man's letter to a century's jurisprudence, landing on its clearest thesis statement — that punishments "were only too often an alibi for unacceptable, even deranged vengeance" even where they "could not eradicate" their own "claims to legitimacy" (c3). Gay closes the historical arc of founding myths with Freud's Totem and Taboo, in which the primal parricide leaves the sons imprisoned by their own guilt, so that "constraining civilization is at once a punishment and the necessary condition for its continued existence" (c4). Along the way, the gendered register of the debate surfaces in Macaulay's and Carlyle's shared anxiety about "effeminate" reform (c5), and the century's most unguarded admission of what punishment is really for comes from James Fitzjames Stephen, for whom the law should "satisfy... the feeling of hatred" so that "criminals should be hated, and their punishment should express that hatred" — in Gay's judgment, "the frankest justification that the century produced for legalized aggression against malefactors" (c6).
