The Long Birth of Political Culture

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay traces the halting, century-long invention of “legitimate opposition” — the sublimation of raw contests for power into rule-governed party politics in Britain, revolutionary and post-revolutionary France, and the United States.

Gay traces how the naked, often lethal contest for power — dynastic plots, armed noble factions, favorites clawing for a ruler's ear — was gradually converted into a rule-governed institution of “legitimate opposition,” a process he frames as the sublimation of aggression into parliamentary and electoral form. The chapter runs from the 1826 coinage of “His Majesty's Opposition” in the British Commons, back through eighteenth-century anti-faction anxiety (Bolingbroke, Madison, Hamilton), through Hume and Burke's rehabilitation of party, into the French Revolution's ambiguous double role as both midwife and saboteur of political culture, Napoleon's antipolitical mastery, and the diverging nineteenth-century paths by which the United States, Britain, and France built — and in France's case repeatedly lost and rebuilt — a durable, two-sided contest for power.

Themes

  • Aggression sublimated into loyal opposition. Palmerston's 1852 reminder to the Commons that “we who are Gentlemen on this side of the House should remember that we are dealing with Gentlemen on the other side” is, for Gay, politics functioning exactly as designed: rivalry retained, its capacity to maim removed. Disraeli's praise of a “wholesome” constitutional Opposition and Hobhouse's throwaway coinage of “His Majesty's Opposition” show the idea becoming so naturalized its origin as a joke was forgotten.

  • From slandering faction to defining party. Before opposition could be legitimate, faction had to stop being a synonym for treason. Bolingbroke's self-serving fantasy of a patriot king “floating above factions” fed directly into Madison and Hamilton's Federalist-era dread of faction as a disease bound to destroy liberty — a properly eighteenth-century assumption that every political problem has one correct answer. Hume softened the temperature; Burke completed the turn by defining a party as “a body of men united, for promoting by their joint endeavors the national interest, upon some particular principle,” explicitly “without resorting to proscriptions.” No proscriptions is the hinge of the whole chapter: it is the rule that converts a power struggle into a political system.

  • The French Revolution as poisoned civic education. The Revolution put the ordinary citizen on the political stage for the first time — an “unprecedented political education” driven by pamphlets, clubs, and the revolutionary journées — but the same actors who had broadened the right to aggression “now monopolized it.” Dissent became treason, taste became a loyalty test (what one read, wore, or applauded), and Robespierre and Marat used “faction” as an epithet as freely as the Old Regime had used treason. Gay's verdict: the Revolution was “the supreme agent of political education” that “also drastically subverted that education.”

  • Napoleon as the great antipolitician. Bonaparte did not merely rule without opposition; he retracted the very possibility of it, restricting political voice to what he could “sponsor or control.” He is the negative proof of the chapter's thesis — what remains when a society still concentrates aggression in one will rather than distributing it through rule-bound contest.

  • National divergence: dogma yielding to experience. The United States resolved the tension “slowly and painfully, in favor of experience” despite the Founders' anti-party dogma and the demagogic Alien and Sedition Acts. Britain, by contrast, “acquired the two-party system in a fit of absence of mind,” with no comparable ideological crisis. France's path was the most tortuous: Restoration censorship and property-qualified suffrage, the 1830 collapse of Charles X, the disappointing July Monarchy, and finally the 1848–1851 cycle from republic to Louis Napoleon's coup — a record of “a dozen constitutions, two republics, two royal dynasties, and two empires” in sixty years.

  • Bourgeois class fracture inside the political public. Even where a bourgeois political class displaced the nobility (as under the July Monarchy), it was not unified: Marx's split between the ruling “finance aristocracy” and the oppositional “industrial bourgeoisie” shows that the political public opposition depends on was itself internally riven, a caution against treating “the bourgeoisie” as a single actor.

For World Machines, this is a legibility story at the level of political conflict itself: what gets built here is infrastructure for making power contests measurable, rule-bound, and non-lethal — the same impulse legibility-as-machine-core names for cadastres and timetables, applied instead to the transfer of power. France's repeated relapse — old absolutist legitimacy dead, new parliamentary legitimacy not yet secure enough to survive a Bonaparte — reads as a textbook gramsci-gap, recurring three times in one lifetime rather than once. Britain's accidental, undramatic acquisition of a two-party system (“a fit of absence of mind”) loosely resonates with the history-machine pattern of a paradigm assembling silently from independent components until it lurches into visibility, though Gay's is a much smaller and more secular claim than Lohse's.

Notable passages

The opening paragraph sets the thesis in miniature: Palmerston's generous observation about “Gentlemen on the other side” is offered as politics-as-aggression-sublimated in its purest, most self-congratulatory form. Burke's definition of party — “without resorting to proscriptions” — is the chapter's conceptual keystone, marking the point where faction stops being an accusation and becomes a description of ordinary political life. The passage on revolutionary denunciation (“what newspaper one read, what clothes one wore”) shows the same energy that built legitimate opposition also capable of curdling into totalizing suspicion when the rule against proscriptions is dropped. Napoleon's studied restriction of “the only public voice that really counted” to his own is the chapter's clearest negative case. The British paragraph is included for its understatement — a durable two-party system arriving with no fanfare, “even before the Reform Act of 1832.” The closing passage on French constitutional churn (“a dozen constitutions, two republics, two royal dynasties, and two empires”) is the chapter's ledger of how precarious this infrastructure remained even in the country that had done the most to invent it.
