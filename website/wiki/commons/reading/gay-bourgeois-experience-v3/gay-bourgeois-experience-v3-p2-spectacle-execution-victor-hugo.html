Execution as Spectacle, and the Long War over the Gallows

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Public executions curdled from solemn communal ritual into disorderly carnival entertainment across the nineteenth century, and the resulting bourgeois horror at the spectacle fed a decades-long, cross-national contest over the death penalty in which Victor Hugo became the most tireless and effective abolitionist voice.

This unit traces how the nineteenth-century hanging or guillotining, meant as a solemn purgative ritual, degenerated in practice into a carnival of drunkenness, hawking, pickpocketing, and mixed-class voyeurism, and how eyewitness horror at that spectacle — recorded by Karl von Holtei, Byron, Thackeray, and Dickens — fed a slow, uneven, nation-by-nation retreat from public execution. It then follows Victor Hugo's half-century abolitionist crusade, rooted in an adolescent memory of a branding, through his novel Le Dernier Jour d'un condamne and his later political manifestos, before closing on the deeper argument: the state's post-Revolutionary monopoly on legitimate violence left nineteenth-century governments too anxious about "the dangerous classes" to loosen their grip on the hangman, even as bourgeois moral sensibility pushed the other way.

Themes

Execution as carnival, not catechism. Gay opens by contrasting the intended function of a hanging — "a terrifying warning" and "a solemn ritual in which a community made itself whole again by purging itself of a transgressor" — with its actual, degraded form: thousands-strong mobs, drunkenness, fistfights, hawkers selling invented last-words poems, pickpockets working the crowd. Crucially, this was not only a lower-class entertainment; "a sizable smattering of good bourgeois and sophisticated aristocrats also came to watch," renting windows overlooking the scaffold rather than mingling with the mob below — a class-stratified but shared appetite for the spectacle.

Witness testimony as evidence of aggression's erotic admixture. Gay reads eyewitness accounts as data for his psychoanalytic thesis that cruelty and eros intermingle. Karl von Holtei's account of a 1815-ish double hanging fuses horror, adolescent sexual excitement (his fixation on a condemned woman's "lascivious charms"), and a grotesque incongruous detail — a spectator calmly eating buttered bread as the wheel breaks bones. Byron's trembling at a Roman guillotining ("I would have saved them if I could") supplies the counter-current: the "humane impulse, conquering the aggression liberated by the sight of bloodshed," which Gay treats as the emotional seed of the abolition movement.

Journalism turns private revulsion into public campaign. Thackeray's and Dickens's 1840 dispatches on the Courvoisier hanging at Newgate reached a much wider public than Byron's letter or Holtei's memoir. Both men record shame at their own "brutal curiosity," but Dickens's focus is more social than personal: his disgust is trained on the crowd itself, which showed "no token... of any emotion suitable to the occasion," only "ribaldry, debauchery, levity, drunkenness." Gay flags Dickens's later retreat to opposing only public hangings (not capital punishment itself) as emblematic of a century "eager for a new humanitarianism yet reluctant to dismiss the old severity and the old enjoyments."

National divergence in managing the same anxiety. Britain abolished public hangings outright in 1868; France kept the guillotine in public view but eventually relocated it to a less accessible part of Paris; the German Empire moved execution behind prison walls entirely, substituting a centrally broadcast administrative announcement (church-bell tolls, placards) for the crowd itself. Gay treats these as different technical solutions to one shared bourgeois problem: how to keep the deterrent and the state's authority intact while disowning the disreputable spectacle.

Victor Hugo's crusade, from a childhood memory to a legal-philosophical manifesto. Hugo traced his lifelong campaign to a scene he witnessed as a teenager: a girl branded on the shoulder for petty theft, whose scream he says he "will always have in my soul." That memory, in his own account, converted latent horror into a fifty-year vocation — "I left the place determined... to battle against the evil actions of the law." His 1829 novella Le Dernier Jour d'un condamné, an interior monologue of a condemned man's last day, became an internationally influential abolitionist text (Gay notes its hold on Dostoevsky, Zola, and its provocation of Carlyle's disapproval), and its 1832 preface makes the political intent explicit, culminating in Hugo's declaration that the old order rested on "three pillars, the priest, the king, the hangman" and that it is now time for the third to fall.

The deeper argument: the state's monopoly on aggression, defended out of anxiety, not confidence. The unit's final movement reframes the whole debate structurally. Post-Revolutionary state-building had given nineteenth-century governments a near-total monopoly on legitimate violence — the sole right to prosecute, sentence, and punish — reinforced by a bourgeois "cultural superego" (internalized guilt easing the state's task) and by reform rhetoric blaming crime on social conditions. But governments never felt secure enough to relax that monopoly: the specter of "the dangerous classes" — a fable about turbulent migrant and proletarian masses "given to strikes, pillage, rapine, and murder" that took hold in 1830s France — kept legislators in a defensive crouch, pitted against a countervailing bourgeois "spirit of moral sensibility and fellow feeling." The death penalty, Gay argues, "put the bourgeois conscience to work" as almost nothing but slavery had.

This last movement resonates with the World Machines idea of a civilization's legibility-as-machine-core and modernity-machine project: the shift from an unruly, crowd-witnessed execution to a centrally administered, procedurally uniform one (walled off in Germany, relocated in France, abolished-in-public in Britain) is the state consolidating administrative legibility over violence precisely by making the act itself less publicly visible — legibility to the bureaucracy purchased through opacity to the crowd, an inversion worth flagging rather than a clean match.

Notable passages

The opening paragraph on the carnivalization of hangings sets Gay's whole argument: an institution built for solemn deterrence collapsing into disorderly entertainment, with the affluent renting windows rather than abstaining. The Holtei passage is the unit's clearest illustration of Gay's psychoanalytic thesis about the "explosive admixture of erotic impulses in aggressive behavior," pairing adolescent sexual fixation with the surreal, deadpan detail of the woman eating buttered bread mid-execution. Dickens's account of the Newgate crowd supplies the moral-observer counterpoint that fed the reform movement, while his later retreat to opposing only public executions captures the century's ambivalence in miniature. Hugo's account of the branding scene is the unit's origin story for the whole abolition movement, and his "three pillars" declaration is its most compressed political statement — hangman, king, and priest as one collapsing edifice. The closing paragraph on the state's monopoly on aggression is the unit's structural payoff: it explains why the debate the earlier passages dramatize could run for a hundred years without resolution.
