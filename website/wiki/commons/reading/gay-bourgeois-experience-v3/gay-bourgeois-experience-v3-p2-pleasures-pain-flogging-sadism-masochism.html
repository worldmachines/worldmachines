Flogging, Its Alibis, and the Naming of Sadism and Masochism

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Nineteenth-century flogging in home and school was sustained by respectable alibis (duty, manliness, biblical warrant, utilitarian calculation) even as reform slowly narrowed it in law, while the same erotic charge that flogging carried surfaced openly when Krafft-Ebing named sadism and masochism after Sade and Sacher-Masoch and Freud identified the two as one drive turned outward or inward.

This unit opens Gay's chapter "The Pleasures of Pain" by tracing the long, uneven nineteenth-century retreat of flogging as a domestic and pedagogical institution in Germany, Britain, and the United States — a retreat sustained by reformers (Montaigne's heirs, Horace Mann, Henry Salt, Eduard Sack) against teachers and officials who defended caning with an arsenal of respectable rationales — and then follows the same erotic charge underground into psychiatry's coining of "sadism" and "masochism," via the biographies of Sade and Sacher-Masoch, the schoolboy testimony of Roger Fry, and Freud's 1905 claim that the two perversions are a single drive facing outward or inward.

Themes

Flogging survived on alibis, not on argument. Gay catalogues the rhetorical cover that let corporal punishment persist long after its cruelty was visible: the flogger's platitude "this hurts me more than it does you" (H. H. Lincoln), the biblical "spare the rod," Julius Beeger's appeal to endangered manliness against a "silken Zeitgeist" of "effeminization," and, most starkly, the Boston School Committee's frank Benthamite calculus that "the greatest good of the greatest number" required continuing to beat children lest "ungoverned, unrestrained, wilful boys, become turbulent, violent, and vicious men." Each alibi converts an aggressive appetite into a disagreeable duty performed for the victim's own good.

Reform proceeded by law and regulation, not abolition. Rather than vanishing, flogging was slowly re-legislated: Prussia's 1794 code granted fathers, masters, and teachers a right to "moderate" chastisement; a century later Saxony permitted the cane only after warnings and milder penalties had failed, and Bavaria (1909) and Prussia (1909) confined it to the youngest grades and required a headmaster's sign-off. The Habsburg empire abolished corporal punishment in 1848 and then restored it, with the stroke count for recidivists spelled out, only four years later. The pattern is one of increasingly narrow, textually explicit rules governing a practice that opinion could not yet outlaw outright — the state comparing its own restraint against neighbors' (Morrill Wyman's 1867 survey of ambassadors) as a mark of civilizational progress. This turn from a father's or master's arbitrary prerogative to a codified, procedurally hedged, cross-nationally benchmarked practice is a small, concrete instance of a society trying to render a channel of violence auditable and comparable rather than eliminating it — a modest echo of legibility-as-machine-core's project of making an unruly practice measurable and rule-bound, though here the project stalls well short of full abolition and the class exemption for officers and landowners shows the rules were never neutral.

Flogging as institution and erotic theater. The English public school made caning a ritualized, near-ceremonial practice — Sneyd-Kynnersley's Monday assembly, the black-draped block, the head boys holding the culprit down — that Gay reads as both a disciplinary technology and (per Roger Fry's testimony) an occasion in which the headmaster's own pleasure was visible. Parents rarely objected; sending boys away to have their spirits "broken" was often the point.

From flogging to diagnosis. Krafft-Ebing's coinages "sadism" and "masochism" (after the Marquis de Sade and Leopold von Sacher-Masoch, "neither... a bourgeois") gave the erotics of flogging a clinical vocabulary. Rousseau's Confessions — where an eleven-year-old's caning by a woman in charge of him becomes desired rather than dreaded — is Gay's canonical case of the pattern later generalized as "the English vice." Freud's 1905 observation that "a sadist is always a masochist, too, at the same time" reframes both as the same aggressive drive, sexualized and directed outward or inward, which Gay treats as the paradox at the heart of the chapter: a relationship in which pain gives pleasure.

Ambivalence as substrate. Gay closes the arc by reaching past clinical sadomasochism to ordinary ambivalence — La Rochefoucauld's aphorism about pleasure in a friend's misfortune, Dostoevsky's "strange inward glow of satisfaction" at others' disasters — suggesting the same aggressive undercurrent runs, in milder form, through love itself.

Notable passages

The Boston School Committee's 1867 reply to petitioners (including Harvard's president and Longfellow) is the clearest specimen of institutional alibi-building: corporal punishment is reluctantly continued because "the greatest good of the greatest number" demands it, and its "abolishment would bring great and lamentable evils." Against this stands michel-de-montaigne's much older dissent, quoted by Gay via Henry Salt's tradition of critics: Montaigne argued boys should be raised "lusty and vigorous" without the "panacea of flogging," diagnosing that "a show of strength is all too often a symptom of weakness." Roger Fry's eyewitness account of a flogging at Sneyd-Kynnersley's school — the black-draped block, the swishing "given with the master's full strength" until "the wretched boy's bottom was a mass of blood" — is the chapter's most visceral evidence that the practice was as much spectacle as correction. Krafft-Ebing's naming of sadism and masochism after Sade and Sacher-Masoch marks the moment flogging's erotics acquired a clinical vocabulary, and Freud's 1905 line that the sadist "is always a masochist, too" supplies the theoretical closure. Finally, jean-jacques-rousseau's own recollection in the Confessions — an eleven-year-old discovering that punishment could be wanted rather than feared — is Gay's originary case for the masochism that later English schoolboys, in his telling, relived.
