Sadism, Rape, and the Reclassification of Suicide

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay reads bourgeois violence turned outward as wife-beating and rape and turned inward as suicide through the same sadistic/masochistic economy that Krafft-Ebing and Freud clinicalized, then traces suicide's long reclassification from theological sin through criminal offense to psychiatric symptom and, with Durkheim and Masaryk, a contested sociological “social fact.”

Gay treats sadism and masochism — clinicalized by Krafft-Ebing’s Psychopathia Sexualis (1886) and reworked by Freud’s Three Essays (1905) into ordinary neurotic conflict rather than a separate species of pervert — as the shared psychological root of two “cultural symptoms of pathological aggression”: violence directed outward at women (wife-beating, rape) and violence directed inward at the self (suicide). He marshals statistical, legal, literary, and journalistic evidence that the respectable bourgeoisie’s self-image as non-violent was false, then follows suicide’s migration from theological sin and criminal offense to psychiatric symptom and, with Durkheim and Masaryk, a sociological “moral statistics” project that never fully sheds its moralizing undertow.

Themes

Sadism and masochism as the shared root of outward and inward aggression. Krafft-Ebing’s clinical taxonomy and Freud’s insistence that sadists and masochists are “neurotics who exhibit in their florid behavior the sexual conflicts all ‘normal’ humans conceal in their unconscious” give Gay his organizing frame: violence against women and violence against the self are two expressions of one aggressive-sexual economy, not separate pathologies.

Bourgeois self-exculpation collapses under its own evidence. The middle classes insisted crimes against women were confined to “jaded aristocrats” and “coarse laborers,” quoting J. W. Kaye’s 1856 claim that “men of education and refinement… do not strike women.” Execution records (127 of 480 executed English murderers, 1837–1901, had killed wives or mistresses), a Waldheim prison study turning up a teacher, two bakers, and a cheese-dairy owner among convicted rapists, and Jack the Ripper’s probable respectability all refute the alibi. The American South’s parallel racialized version — rape as “almost by definition” committed by black men on white women — shows the same exculpatory logic recruiting a different convenient Other.

Rape as power, not desire. Gay’s sharpest analytic claim is that rape is not sexuality’s twin but its servant: men with easy sexual outlets (mistresses, servants, colonial subjects) still raped, because rape delivers “a rabid display of raw power and towering rage” rather than relief from “domestic frustration,” which he calls “that convenient and plausible alibi” — the least credible of the excuses offered for it.

Fiction stages the ambiguity the law couldn’t resolve. Kleist’s Die Marquise von O—, Hardy’s Tess, Ebner-Eschenbach’s Unsühnbar, and Galsworthy’s The Man of Property form a graduated sequence — from a rapist redeemed by marriage, through deliberately misty consent, to Soames Forsyte’s flatly narrated marital rape — evidence that respectable culture could name the act only by degrees.

The press turns rape trials into class theater. The Times’s coverage of the Solomons/Harnsworth case and the Rev. Johnstone prosecution shows magistrates openly sympathetic to servant-class victims, yet wealth still bought delay, flight, or reasonable doubt, while poorer defendants like Job Lawrence and David Harrington met harsh, swift punishment — trials that drew crowds “almost as good as a hanging.”

Suicide’s jurisdiction migrates from church to clinic to statistics table. Augustine’s “he who kills himself is a homicide” and the French code of 1670 (mock trial, forfeited property, the corpse dragged and hanged) gave way at mid-century to the psychiatric reflex that suicide proves insanity (Falret, Esquirol), and then to Durkheim’s and Masaryk’s sociology of “moral statistics.” Each regime claims the same act as its object without cleanly displacing the last — churches “mounted counterattacks” late in the century even as Durkheim published Le Suicide (1897), and denominational writers like Baer and Rost blamed secularism and “the modern Jewish world view” in the same years Durkheim claimed a value-free science. This incomplete, overlapping handover of authority over one act — theological, then juridical, then psychiatric, then statistical, none of them fully retiring the last — is a domain-scale echo of the gramsci-gap pattern WMP uses for machine-level transitions, where old legitimacy doesn’t die outright and the new authority never fully secures the field either; it is also a clean instance of what michel-foucault’s account of the medicalization of deviance describes: successive power/knowledge regimes claiming the same “case” without ever fully replacing the last.

Moral statistics as a small legibility project. Victorian “moral statisticians,” criminologists tallying rape prosecutions by class and literacy, and Durkheim’s suicide tables all convert scattered, morally coded private acts into countable, comparable population-level phenomena — a miniature instance of what WMP calls legibility-as-machine-core: rendering something illegible (motive, sin, passion) into a measurable social fact that can be governed. Gay is careful to undercut the analogy himself: this “science” is compromised throughout by the moralizing agendas of its own practitioners, so the legibility project is never as neutral as its proponents claimed.

Notable passages

The unit opens by naming its interpretive key: Krafft-Ebing and Freud reframe sadism/masochism as ordinary neurotic conflict, which licenses Gay to read violence against women and violence against the self as two faces of one pathology (c1). The long survey of execution statistics, prison records, and Jack the Ripper (c2) does the empirical work of stripping the bourgeoisie of its alibi that such violence was a lower-class or aristocratic vice. The unit’s most condensed thesis statement — that rape is power’s instrument, not desire’s overflow, and that “domestic frustration” is the least credible excuse offered for it (c3) — is the claim the rest of the unit’s rape material substantiates. The pivot to suicide begins by establishing the punitive old regime it will be defined against: Augustinian sin, criminal corpse-desecration, state “counteraggression” against self-aggression (c4). The unit closes on Durkheim’s declaration of scientific faith (c5), which both completes suicide’s reclassification as a sociological object and, in its evangelical register (“beware of treating it as an enemy!”), betrays how incompletely that reclassification escaped the moralizing it claimed to replace.
