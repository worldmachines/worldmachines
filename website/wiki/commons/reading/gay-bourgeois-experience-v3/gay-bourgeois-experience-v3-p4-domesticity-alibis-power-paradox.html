Domesticity: Womanliness Defined

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Nineteenth-century bourgeois ideology naturalized a rigid manliness/womanliness polarity into two mutually reinforcing alibis for reserving overt aggression to men — separate-spheres naturalism and the paradox of the domestically "powerful, weaker sex" — against a running current of dissent from Wollstonecraft to Mill.

Opening Part Four ("The Powerful, Weaker Sex") and its first section, this unit lays out the century's dominant thesis — that the sexes differ as radically in mind as in body — and the two "alibis for the male monopoly on aggression" this thesis supplied: separate-spheres naturalism (manliness is defined against, and requires, a contrasting womanliness) and the paradox that woman, though excluded from public power, secretly rules through domestic influence and therefore needs none. Gay marshals a chorus of exponents — Fontane, Jahn, Hammond, Blanc, Guenther, Hegel, Tennyson, Bushnell, Ruskin, Proudhon, Maudsley — against a thinner line of dissenters — Wollstonecraft, Stendhal, Mill, Herbert Spencer, Frances Wright — before concluding that both alibis, for all their apparent opposition, served the identical function of keeping women off "the battlegrounds of scholarship, business, and politics."

Themes

Manliness is parasitic on a contrasting womanliness, and the pairing licenses aggression for men alone. Gay opens with Fontane's Effi Briest ("Women womanly, men manly") and Turnvater Jahn's near-identical formula to show the dichotomy was a cultural commonplace by the 1890s, then traces it back through the century via James Henry Hammond ("Women were meant to breed—men to do the work of the world"), Charles Blanc's gendering of design and color in painting, and the zoologist Guenther's evolutionary romance of Adam and Eve. The point of the whole catalogue, Gay insists, is that it "quite explicitly reserve[s] the privilege of aggression for the manly male, because he is male."

Philosophy supplied the doctrine its most respectable seal. Hegel's brief remarks on women in the Philosophie des Rechts are read as exemplary: man "has to take over struggle, enmity, hatred," while woman "simply unfolds like a flower, without struggle and without resistance" and "thrones in the family." Gay's gloss is blunt — this is the philosophical warrant for the claim that "the male alone... had earned the right to make overt aggression, constructive or destructive, his business." Tennyson's The Princess ("Man is the hunter; woman is his game"... "man for the field and woman for the hearth") supplies the doctrine's most quoted, most naturalized form, closed off from rational challenge — "with nature so legible, the moral and scientific case against subversive feminists seemed self-evident" — so that only "a rare rebel like John Stuart Mill" thought to answer assertion with argument.

A second, seemingly opposite alibi: women already secretly rule the world. Against the separate-spheres argument, Gay sets its ideological twin — the "oxymoron" of "the power of the powerless," traced from Ecclesiasticus and the Church Fathers through William Ross Wallace's cradle-rocking couplet and Thomas Moore's "'Tis woman, woman, rules us still," to Hegel's darker version: femininity as the state's "eternal irony," secretly "perverting" public purpose into private, domestic ends. The Reverend Horace Bushnell and the Reverend John Burgon extend the same move into open anti-suffrage argument — woman already "governs both the governors of the state and the people besides," so she needs no vote — while Catharine Beecher, a woman, supplies its most "pathetic" endorsement: ambition would strip away every "sacred protection" her dependency currently earns her.

The two alibis are functionally identical, not really in tension. Gay's synthesis is explicit: both propositions are "scornful idealizations" that "wrapped contempt in lyrical, improbable caricatures," differing only in that "the first saw woman as omnipotent mother; the second, as perpetual child." Ruskin's "Of Queens' Gardens" is the fullest statement of the second, "perpetual child" mode — woman is not inferior, only confined to "rule, not... battle," shielded by man from all "peril and trial," required to be "incapable of error" precisely because she is denied any arena in which to err.

Beneath the idealization, an anxious, half-conscious fear of woman. Gay reads the hyperbole psychoanalytically: the recurring image of women as man-eating, castrating, "hermaphrodite" threats to the sexual order (the epithets "hens that crow," "hommes-femmes") betrays a "buried dependence on women, beginning with their mothers," not mere hypocrisy. Feminists agitating for rights become a convenient object onto which this deeper anxiety is displaced — a domestic-scale instance of the volume's broader argument about aggression needing a legitimizing target.

Dissent ran the whole length of the century but did not prevail. Stendhal (1822) blamed men's "despotism" for women's enforced stupidity; Wollstonecraft's Vindication (1792) denied there is "sex in souls"; Mill, Herbert Spencer ("who shall say what her sphere is?"), and the rebellious Frances Wright agreed. Against them stood Henry Maudsley's 1874 counter-dogma ("there is sex in mind as distinctly as there is sex in body") and Proudhon's outright misogyny — and, Gay notes drily, "his side had the upper hand."

The passage where nature is called "legible" to settle a social hierarchy without argument is a loose, worth-naming echo of legibility-as-machine-core: not Scott's state apparatus rendering territory countable, but the same underlying move — treating a contingent social order as self-evidently read off of nature so that no counter-argument need be entertained. The fit is partial: here legibility legitimates a private, familial hierarchy rather than administrative control, but the rhetorical function (foreclosing debate by declaring the order obvious) rhymes.

Notable passages

The Tennyson/Mill paragraph is the unit's thesis statement in miniature: it names "these two alibis for the male monopoly on aggression" outright, stages the naturalized case (the hunter/game metaphor, the marching quatrain of antitheses), and marks Mill as the rare figure who treated it as an assertion rather than an argument.

The Hegel paragraph does the same philosophical work at higher altitude, moving directly from "woman's destiny" as harmonious non-struggle to the flat conclusion that only the male has "earned the right" to aggression.

The Wallace/Moore/Hegel paragraph carries the counter-alibi at its most compact — the cradle-rocking couplet next to Hegel's harsher reading of domestic influence as an "eternal irony" subverting the state, showing the same claim could be flattering or accusatory depending on the speaker's aim.

Ruskin's "Of Queens' Gardens" passage is quoted at length because it is the era's most popular, most fully worked statement of separate-but-not-inferior — man gets "the failure, the offence, the inevitable error," woman gets "rule" without "contest."

Gay's own synthesis ("the first saw woman as omnipotent mother; the second, as perpetual child") is the hinge of the whole unit, converting two ostensibly opposed clichés into one explanatory move.

Wollstonecraft's "there is no 'sex in souls'" closes the unit's dissenting thread, anchoring almost a century of resistance to the doctrine the rest of the unit documents.
