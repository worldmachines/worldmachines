The Franchise as a Referendum on Human Nature

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Across nineteenth-century Britain, France, the United States, Belgium, and the German states, the century-long argument over who should get the vote was, at its most serious level, an argument about human nature — whether ordinary people were rational enough to be trusted with political power or driven by passions and self-interest that made property qualifications, indirect elections, and other engineered restrictions necessary.

This unit opens Gay's chapter "Human Nature in Politics" with the nineteenth century's central constitutional argument: who deserves the vote. Gay frames the whole debate through Graham Wallas's 1908 Human Nature in Politics and argues that beneath the tactical maneuvering of parties trying to rig electorates in their own favor, the serious participants — Tocqueville, Bagehot, Guizot, Constant, Taine, Sybel — were really debating a psychological question inherited from the Enlightenment: can reason govern self-assertion, or does raw human nature (passion, self-interest, submissiveness, the capacity for violence) set a hard limit on who can safely hold power? The unit tours Britain, France, the United States, Belgium, Prussia, and the Frankfurt parliament of 1848 to show the same argument recurring with local variations, and ends in 1862 with Heinrich von Sybel still insisting that equal political rights are "folly" until "education and ethics" are equally developed among all men.

Themes

The Enlightenment's optimistic premise curdles into Victorian caution. The philosophes, committed to Locke's blank slate over Plato's metals-of-the-soul myth, believed education could make anyone a rational citizen; this made the size of the electorate and the size of the educated public a single question. The nineteenth century inherited that premise but grew far more skeptical about how fast, or how far, education could actually stretch — Gay's chapter watches liberal after liberal invent reasons why now is not yet the moment for universal suffrage.

The real fault line is liberals versus democrats, not conservatives versus radicals. Absolutists were dismissed as beneath serious argument. The interesting combat, Gay insists, was between people who agreed constitutional government was desirable but disagreed on who was fit to participate in it — Guizot, Constant, and Taine on one side insisting competence is scarce and unevenly distributed, unnamed Frankfurt democrats on the other insisting "independence of mind" matters more than property.

Property and "competence" tests functioned as alibis for excluding the many while preserving the rhetoric of principle. Blackstone's formula — that only those with "a will of their own" deserve a vote — recurs across borders (Save's Sweden, Kjellen's warning against "the order of nature," Lowe's "order of Providence"). Nobody thought it worth arguing about excluding women, servants, paupers, or children; the controversy was only ever about which men qualified. Prussia's three-tier tax-weighted electoral law is the unit's starkest case: an ostensibly neutral formula (equal tax shares, equal voting weight) engineered to entrench the Junker aristocracy, later gamed by the new industrial bourgeoisie it was designed to exclude.

Fear of "the masses" is the affective constant beneath all the theorizing — canaille, the "swinish multitude," Guizot's "idolatrous cult" of democracy, Sybel's working class that "obeys its passions and is easily seduced." This is the same anxiety that, transposed a lifetime later into aesthetics and social theory, becomes Ortega y Gasset's diagnosis of the mass-man in ortega-revolt-of-the-masses and John Carey's argument in carey-intellectuals-and-masses that "the mass" is itself an invented category defending an elite's self-image — the resonance is loose (Gay's subjects fear the masses' political judgment, Ortega and Carey their cultural and intellectual claims) but the underlying gesture, treating a whole class of people as constitutionally incapable of self-government, is the same one.

Universal suffrage's own paradox: it could produce Caesarism rather than democracy. The chapter's frame sentence names "manipulators of the Caesarist persuasion" as the only political actors untroubled by the franchise question, and Frankfurt delegates explicitly worried that a broad electorate, disconnected from real political judgment, is exactly the raw material a demagogue needs — a fear vindicated, in Gay's telling, by Louis Napoleon's use of the 1848 French suffrage expansion. This reads as a rearguard-era "morbid symptom" in the sense of Gramsci's gramsci-gap: the aristocratic/property-based theory of political legitimacy is visibly dying across every state surveyed here, but no participant yet trusts the democratic alternative to replace it cleanly, so the interregnum fills with elaborate compromise machinery (indirect elections, weighted tax classes, competence tests) rather than a clean transition — though Gay's frame is a single recurring argument sustained across a century, not Gramsci's collapse-and-rebuilding of a whole order.

Notable passages

The opening paragraph sets the terms: for the era's best minds, "views on the vote were views on man's capacity for autonomy and for harnessing aggression," tying the suffrage question directly to the volume's larger argument about aggression and its cultivation. The unit's clearest statement of the liberal-versus-democrat stakes asks whether "day laborers and peasants doomed to lives of unending drudgery" could really be expected to rise to "rational creatures capable of rising above willful self-interest." The Prussian three-class law shows the mechanism by which an ostensibly procedural rule (tax-weighted voting) does the work of naked class exclusion. Guizot's psychological argument — that self-examination reveals "an incessant war between good and bad inclinations," making democracy "the unleashing of all human nature" — states in miniature the Freudian framework Gay brings to the whole volume: politics as a site where instinct must be managed, not simply expressed. Sybel's 1862 formula, closing the unit, shows the argument still unresolved on the eve of Bismarck's empire, warning that premature equality yields only "the wretched alternation of anarchy and dictatorship." And the Belgian liberal Lebeau's aside — "the more we lower the property tests, the more aristocratic the elections we will have" — captures the paradox that haunts every reform effort in the unit: broadening formal access without broadening real independence just hands power to whoever already controls the newly enfranchised.
