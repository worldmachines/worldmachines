A Time for Tentativeness

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Between the 1850s and the 1900s, tentative literary and personal admissions that women deserved a fuller life collided across Britain, France, Germany, and the United States with a tenacious appeal to "Nature" that kept male supremacy and the doctrine of separate spheres intact, eroding only around the edges.

The unit surveys the slow, uneven, and contested mid-to-late-nineteenth-century shift in bourgeois attitudes toward women's place, moving across Britain, France, Germany, and the United States from the 1850s to the 1910s. Gay reads a scatter of literary fictions, private letters, feminist pamphlets, and anti-feminist tracts as evidence that "accepted generalities about women started to crumble after the 1850s" but "crumbled around the edges first": a rival ideal of the competent, partnering wife was emerging, but the doctrine of male supremacy and domestic confinement retained enormous vitality, continually re-armed by an appeal to "Nature" as an unanswerable alibi, and reinforced from unexpected quarters, including the political left.

Themes

  • "Nature" is the alibi that keeps male supremacy standing even as its old certainties erode. From Jules Simon's insistence that "the woman who becomes a worker is no longer a woman" to Herbert Asquith's parliamentary invocation of "those indelible differences of faculty and function by which Nature herself has given diversity," to Kaiser Wilhelm II's 1910 counsel that German women's task lies "in quiet work in the house," the same rhetorical move recurs: dressing a preference for the status quo as an observation about nature that only a "fanatic" would dispute. Gay treats this as the era's master alibi for continued aggression against women's claims — the same logic he elsewhere calls the sturdy alibi of nature, "an inexhaustible debater's resource."
  • The alibi escalates into pseudo-science. Paul Julius Moebius's 1900 pamphlet on the "physiological feeblemindedness of women" (Uber den physiologischen Schwachsinn des Weibes) pushes the nature-alibi to its most literal extreme — smaller, lighter brains as proof of intellectual inferiority — and its commercial success (eight editions by 1906) shows how eagerly this argument was consumed once it wore the costume of anatomy rather than mere custom.
  • Literary fiction and private correspondence register the shift before institutions do. Thomas Hughes's Tom Brown at Oxford (1861) stages Mary Brown's rebuttal of the doctrine that women must be shielded from the world — "tentative and inconsistent yet pregnant with the future," in Gay's phrase — while Elizabeth Barrett's 1845 letters to Robert Browning describe courtship itself as an asymmetric contest, "the growth of power on one side" against "the struggle against it... on the other." Both anticipate arguments the public sphere would not catch up to for decades.
  • The myth of woman's "secret empire" functioned as a compensatory alibi, not a concession. J. M. Barrie's What Every Woman Knows (1908) and a run of fictional henpecked husbands (Mrs. Proudie, Frau Jenny Treibel, George Pontifex's wife) let bourgeois men imagine that women already held real power backstage — Maggie Wylie's "Every woman knows that" — which Gay reads as a psychologically convenient fiction that discouraged formal reform precisely because it made subordination feel survivable, even secretly advantageous, without changing the structure that produced it.
  • Resistance to women's rights was not only conservative or religious — it also came from the political left. French Socialists and Radicals (Michelet, Leon Richer, Georges Clemenceau) opposed the female vote on the ground that women were "impregnated with the sacristy" and would vote as their priests directed, turning anticlerical politics into an argument against suffrage. This complicates any simple mapping of reaction onto religion or conservatism alone — the convenient rationale recurs even inside a self-consciously progressive politics.
  • National variation in how far, and how coherently, the old dispensation was challenged. Britain and the United States are described as furthest along despite continued resistance (Asquith still leans on "Nature" in 1892); France is culturally torn — a strong feminist tradition (Deraismes, Auclert) coexists with a robust domestic ideology of the foyer and reports (from foreign observers) of a French "power behind the throne"; Germany's organized women's movement is latest and most modest, its leading feminists asking only for better preparation for wifehood, with the Kaiser and Moebius giving the doctrine of separate spheres its bluntest, most biologized defenders.

Notable passages

Hughes's Mary Brown answers her husband's chivalrous wish to shield her from hardship by demanding to be put "on your own level," concluding that women are "not meant to sit in fine silks, and look pretty, and spend money" — a lame, incoherent peroration by Gay's own account, but one he reads as evidence the doctrine of domesticity was already under strain by 1861.

Elizabeth Barrett Browning's letters recast romantic love as covert warfare: "what most people call love is really a kind of warfare, with one side enjoying all the strategic advantages," and men's affection curdles into "the tone of right & might" the moment it is secured — the unit's clearest statement of gender relations as a site of structured, asymmetric aggression, years before organized feminism existed to name it.

Jules Simon's approving citation of Michelet — "the woman who becomes a worker is no longer a woman" — shows the nature-alibi operating with the prestige of both statesmanship and literary eloquence at once, licensing the same conclusion (women belong in the home) from two authorities that otherwise had little in common.

Moebius's clinical verdict that "woman is a being halfway between child and man, and mentally she is so, too" marks the alibi's most extreme form in the unit: a full biological determinism marketed successfully to a wide public.

Barrie's Maggie Wylie, rewriting her husband's speeches while letting him take the credit, delivers the unit's central irony — "Every man who is high up loves to think that he has done it all himself; and the wife smiles, and lets it go at that... Every woman knows that" — a comic version of the same substitution of compensatory myth for actual equality that runs through the whole unit.

The French Radical Georges Clemenceau's charge that women are "completely impregnated with the sacristy" shows anti-clerical, ostensibly progressive politics recruiting the same essentializing move used elsewhere by clergy and conservatives, just aimed at a different fear.

The unit's recurring image of ideas that "crumbled around the edges first" — old certainties intact at the core, fraying only at the margins, decades before any decisive break — resonates with gramsci-gap: a legitimacy in visible decline whose successor has not yet displaced it, producing exactly the "tentative and inconsistent" mixture of signals Gay documents here.
