August 4, 1914: The Collapse of the Bourgeois Century's Cultivation of Aggression

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Peter Gay's epilogue reads the outbreak of the First World War as the collapse of a century-long, always-partial bourgeois project of restraining aggression, driven above all by nationalism, "the alibi of alibis," which by August 1914 overrode class solidarity, cosmopolitan sentiment, and economic interdependence — a collapse Gay insists was contingent, not fated.

Peter Gay closes The Cultivation of Hatred with a reading of the July Crisis and the outbreak of the First World War not as a bolt from the blue but as the collapse of a century-long, always-partial bourgeois project of restraining aggression — a collapse driven by nationalism, "the alibi of alibis," which by August 1914 had overridden class solidarity, religious universalism, and the economic interweaving of nations that had made war seem irrational to contemporaries like William Graham Sumner. Gay insists the outcome was contingent, not fated: diplomatic blunders, misread signals, and "sheer stupidity" did as much work as the "greed of imperialists" or the "swagger of emperors."

Themes

Nationalism as the supreme, all-devouring alibi. Gay traces nationalism from a mild, near-benign "sticking out of elbows" (his example: Norway's cultural self-assertion against Denmark and Sweden) through its Enlightenment-era rival, cosmopolitanism (Voltaire's "fatherland" of the sophisticated and irreligious; Schiller's "Ode to Joy"), to its transformation — via the trauma of Napoleonic conquest — into a "militant retort to severe narcissistic injuries," and finally into the late-century "alibi of alibis" that in 1914 dissolved every competing loyalty, including the international socialist movement's pledge to resist a war fought for capitalists. nationalism counter-enlightenment-and-nationalism

Contagious hatred and popular demand for war. War fever came from below as much as above: crowds cheering Wilhelm II's mobilization order, Sazonov warning of revolution in Russia if the government failed to back Serbia, "law-abiding citizens" harassing stranded foreigners and hounding pacifists. Gay reads this as evidence that the alibi for aggression was not elite manipulation alone but something the bourgeois public actively craved.

The convenient Other turned against the near-neighbor. The intensity of German hatred fell hardest not on the culturally distant enemy but on Britain — "perfidious Albion," the nation "they had most admired" — which Gay glosses via Freud's "narcissism of small differences": likeness, not difference, licenses the sharpest hostility. The same logic drove citizens to rebaptize family names that might mark them as the Wicked Other.

Even the theorist of aggression was not immune. Freud's own psychoanalytic circle succumbed to war euphoria in 1914 — Karl Abraham and Max Eitingon crowing over "dazzling news," Freud himself invoking "our" war loan — before growing appalled by late 1915. Gay uses this lapse to argue that intellectual mastery of a drive does not immunize its possessor against the drive itself.

The liberal temper: hard-won, easily lost. Gay names the bourgeois disposition capable of checking aggression — tolerance for ambiguity, for "the many shades of gray," a "gift for testing and accepting reality" — and stresses its fragility: "not an attitude easily acquired," and (via Hobson) the first thing the jingo loses is his sense of humor. Chauvinism's triumph in 1914 is this temper's defeat, not evidence it never existed.

Contingency over fatalism. Gay explicitly resists reading 1914 as a "predictable calamity" written in the stars, attributing the outcome to accumulated misjudgment and momentum as much as to structural forces — preserving agency and blame rather than treating the war as historically overdetermined.

Notable passages

The opening paragraph frames August 4 as the day that "doomed the great compromises that had largely held national antagonisms in check through the long nineteenth century" — the thesis sentence for the epilogue and for the volume's argument that the century's aggression was never eliminated, only cultivated and contained.

The nationalism-as-alibi passage is the volume's summary claim in miniature: of all the alibis for aggression Gay has catalogued across the book (competition, the convenient Other, manliness), nationalism proves the master alibi because it can absorb and override every other social identity at once.

The Anglo-German hatred passage ("Gott strafe England," Lissauer's "Hymn of Hate") is Gay's clearest illustration that the Other need not be the most different — it can be the most admired, which if anything sharpens the aggression via the narcissism-of-small-differences logic.

The Freud vignette is a small, almost embarrassed aside, but it is load-bearing: it is Gay's own evidence, drawn from Freud's circle and voice, that the will to hatred was near-universal even among the analysts of the unconscious.

The liberal-temper passage is the closest thing in this unit to a positive counter-thesis to the book's title — the trait that cultivated restraint rather than hatred — which Gay treats as real, valuable, and always precarious.

The closing contingency passage is Gay's historiographical signature: refusing to let the war read backward as inevitable protects the moral stakes of the choices bourgeois leaders and publics made in July 1914.

World Machines resonances

Read against WMP's framework, this epilogue is an anatomy of a Dusk collapse compressed into weeks rather than a century: the "great compromises" — the diplomatic concert of Europe, the interlaced world economy, internationalist socialism, cosmopolitan sentiment — function as the legitimating infrastructure of an order, and their sudden failure under the "alibi of alibis" resembles a gramsci-gap: the old accommodation (cosmopolitan and internationalist loyalty) dies before anything is ready to replace it, and what fills the gap looks like the "morbid symptoms" the concept predicts — jingoism, atrocity propaganda, chauvinist regression even among trained psychoanalysts. The fit is loose rather than structural, though: Gay's account is psychological and moral (a "liberal temper" losing a fight against "primitive demands"), not an account of institutions or technologies decaying, and he explicitly rejects the kind of inevitabilist reading that a clean world-machines Dusk narrative might invite — for Gay, contingency and "sheer stupidity" mattered as much as any structural unraveling.
