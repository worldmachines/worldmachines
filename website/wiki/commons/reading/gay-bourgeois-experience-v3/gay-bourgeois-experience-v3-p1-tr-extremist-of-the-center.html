T.R.: Extremist of the Center

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Theodore Roosevelt incarnates, in a single commanding personage, the nineteenth-century alibis for aggression — competition, racial hierarchy, and manliness — while also showing how bourgeois society could bridle that same aggression into a workable instrument of democratic politics.

Gay reads Theodore Roosevelt as the nineteenth century's alibis for aggression made flesh: a single man who exhibited, almost to the point of caricature, the lust for competition, the racial attitudes, and the cult of manliness that his contemporaries used to license hostility — and who simultaneously showed how that aggression could be tamed into a legitimate engine of democratic government. The chapter moves from T.R.'s childhood asthma and his lifelong "counterphobic" conversion of passivity into activity, through his self-conscious performance of manliness and his idiosyncratic, half-sophisticated Social Darwinism, to his racial nationalism and his mature self-image as the "extremist of the center" — a man at war on every front who nonetheless, on balance, harnessed rather than unleashed his aggressive energies into the Square Deal and federal regulation.

Themes

Aggression incarnated in one man. Gay opens by generalizing his own method: alibis for aggression are "collective cultural phenomena" that normally live in the public domain, but occasionally condense into "a single commanding personage." T.R. is offered as the purest case — H. G. Wells's line that no president ever so reflected the quality of his time is doing real argumentative work here, licensing Gay to read one biography as a diagnostic instrument for reading an entire class and era.

Manliness as counterphobic engine. T.R.'s signature alibi is manliness, and Gay roots it explicitly in psychobiography: a sickly, asthmatic boy, humiliated by stronger boys on a stagecoach, converts "pathetic wishing for strength" into its "cultivation" — boxing lessons, ranching, big-game hunting, the "strenuous life" as doctrine. Gay names the mechanism as psychoanalytic counterphobic behavior: acting as though unafraid until the fear recedes from awareness, never quite from the psyche. The famous motto and the University of Berlin speech about "manliness of fibre" eroding under comfort are treated not as ideology first and personality second, but the reverse.

A felt rather than argued Social Darwinism. T.R. absorbed competitive struggle as a moral fact of life without adopting the crude survival-of-the-fittest doctrine wholesale — Gay credits him with "repeated attacks of good sense," noting his skepticism of Benjamin Kidd and his view that "in civilized societies the rivalry of natural selection works against progress." The alibi operates for him less as explicit theory than as temperament: a readiness to see all of life, politics included, as a species of warfare, expressed via the Hobbesian "power after power" and the boxing-ring metaphor for entering politics.

The convenient Other: race and nation. T.R.'s nationalism (The Winning of the West) and his racial hierarchy — contempt for Latin Americans as "Dagos," casual assertion that Blacks were "altogether inferior," and above all his "race suicide" panic over birth control among the "old native American stock" — supply the chapter's clearest instance of Gay's convenient-Other alibi, race and nation licensing what would otherwise be naked aggression. Gay is careful to note the inconsistencies (defending Japanese immigrants, dining with Booker T. Washington, then never repeating that "blunder") rather than flattening T.R. into a simple bigot; the ambivalence is itself part of the portrait.

Sublimation into words, and the taming of aggression into political culture. Most of T.R.'s "killings," Gay notes drily, were rhetorical — an avalanche of letters, articles, and books that let him discharge hostility ("scoundrelism" against Rockefeller and Harriman) without physical violence. This shades into the chapter's larger argument: T.R.'s mature self-understanding as the "supreme extremist of the center," at war simultaneously on opposite fronts (against plutocrats and against radicals, against jingoes and against pacifists), converts aggression into a stabilizing political posture rather than a revolutionary or demagogic one. The Square Deal, trust-busting, and federal intervention in labor disputes are offered as the practical, prudent residue of a temperament that could easily have curdled into Caesarism — Gay's closing judgment is that T.R.'s career shows "vital energies harnessed rather than let off the leash." This is a small, personalized instance of a civilization building institutional channels for aggression — a bourgeois order legitimizing and routing hostility into administrable form rather than either suppressing or unleashing it; it resonates with legibility-as-machine-core insofar as regulatory capture of "malefactors of great wealth" (Northern Securities, meat-packing, railroads) is precisely the state rendering concentrated industrial power visible and governable — though Gay's register is psychological and moral rather than the cartographic/statistical legibility techniques (cadastres, censuses) the concept was built from.

Notable passages

The opening paragraph sets Gay's method in miniature: alibis for aggression are collective, but T.R. lets Gay read the collective phenomenon through one "classic symptom of bourgeois aggression at work." The counterphobic passage — "Spurred on by... his father's approval, he lifted himself by his emotional bootstraps" — is the psychoanalytic hinge of the whole chapter, explaining the strenuous-life doctrine as a lifelong management of remembered helplessness rather than free-standing philosophy. The Berlin-speech quotation on "manliness of fibre" shows the doctrine at its most explicit and public. The race-suicide passage is the chapter's starkest convenient-Other material, pairing eugenic panic with an almost domestic anecdote (admiring a family with seventeen children) that makes the ideology feel lived rather than abstract. The "extremist of the center" passage and the closing paragraph on the Square Deal together carry Gay's ultimate verdict: aggression pitted against aggression, producing a workable — if noisy — political center rather than either paralysis or demagogic rupture.
