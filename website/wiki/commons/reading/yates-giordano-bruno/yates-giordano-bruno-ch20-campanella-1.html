Campanella as Bruno's successor: the Calabrian revolt and the City of the Sun

Commons · Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Yates presents Tommaso Campanella as Bruno's southern-Dominican successor, argues that the Calabrian revolt of 1599 enacted a Brunian programme of magical reform, and reads the City of the Sun as a Hermetic city modelled on Picatrix's Adocentyn and the Asclepius prophecy.

The opening of Yates's chapter on Campanella uses the younger Calabrian Dominican to shed retrospective light on giordano-bruno. She sketches Campanella's three periods (heretical friar and revolutionary, Neapolitan prisoner-writer, Parisian prophet of the French monarchy), lines up the parallel biographies (Dominican vows, heresy trials, Padua, Roman Inquisition prisons), and argues that the 1599 Calabrian revolt looks like Bruno's reform programme put into action. She then describes the City of the Sun in detail and argues that its deepest source is Hermetic: the magical city of Adocentyn in the Picatrix and the Asclepius prophecy of a City of the Egyptian gods. The unit ends by relating the Solarians' ambivalent Copernicanism to Bruno's Cena.

Themes

Campanella as a Magus with a mission, luckier and more cunning than Bruno. Both are magician-philosophers descended from Ficino, both universal reformers. Yates credits Campanella with a savoir-faire, or cunning, that Bruno lacked, which let him shift the reform from revolution to orthodox channels (Spain, the Papacy, then France) and survive. She says he was willing to dissimulate, and he simulated madness to escape burning.

Parallel lives, and a possible transmission. Yates lays out near-synchronous dates (Dominican entry 1563 vs 1582, flight and heresy proceedings 1576 vs 1589, Padua just after Bruno left, Roman prisons at the same time). She argues that a torch may have passed from Bruno to Campanella, citing phrases in later Campanella letters reminiscent of the Cena, but she hedges: Pucci's influence, a wider fermenting discontent among southern Dominicans, and fin de siecle expectations of reform are alternative or contributing causes.

The revolt as Bruno's programme in action. Yates says nobody has connected Amabile's documents on the Calabrian revolt with Bruno, but that it now leaps to the eye. The revolt rested on portents (the descent of the sun, the numerology of 1600), on natural religion and ethics, and on Campanella's inspired personality, with almost no practical preparation. She reads Bruno's execution in February 1600 as possibly a warning timed with the suppression of the revolt, and sees in both fates the suppression in Italy of Renaissance forces that migrated elsewhere in the seventeenth century.

The City of the Sun as a magical, astrological model of the world. Seven planetary circles, a round temple with globes and planet lamps, and walls that picture all nature and its stellar correspondences. The ruler, Sun (Metaphysics), is priest-king, aided by Power, Wisdom and Love. Yates insists it is not a modern blueprint of a well-governed state: breeding is astrological, and the city is arranged to be right with the stars. Its Magi include Christ and the apostles. The wall-as-encyclopedia, in which children learn every art and science from pictures, is a total legible model of the cosmos, which loosely rhymes with the legibility concerns of the modernity-machine, though here legibility is magical and astral rather than administrative or statistical.

Hermetic source over Utopian sources. Yates grants Plato's Republic and suggests thomas-more's more-utopia as secondary (the traveller-discovers-a-city fiction). She argues the primary layer is Hermetic: Adocentyn in the Picatrix (four gates, planetary lighthouse, ordering images, tree of generation, temple of the Sun) and the Asclepius prophecy of a City towards the setting sun. Solarians are, in her reading, Hermetic pseudo-Egyptians, and the Sun priest is Hermes Trismegistus's priest-philosopher-king. Campanella's version is Christianised: natural law needs only the sacraments.

Copernicus as portent. The Solarians admire Copernicus but are undecided, and move quickly from astronomy to portents and a dislike of Aristotle the pedant. Yates sees the atmosphere of Bruno's Cena, though the Solarians' millenarianism is unlike Bruno.

Notable passages

The framing paragraph (c1) states the whole comparative method: Campanella's notions and career resemble Bruno's with variations, and the differences explain Bruno. The revolt passage (c2) is Yates's boldest interpretive claim, hedged in the surrounding text. The death-date passage (c3) ties Bruno's execution to the suppression of the revolt. The description of the Sun priest and his collaborators (c4) carries her insistence that astrology, not modern eugenics or politics, structures the city. The Adocentyn comparison (c5) and the Egyptian-layer argument (c6) give her Hermetic thesis on the source. The final Copernican passage (c7) connects the City back to the Cena and Bruno's return to Egyptianism, hedged with 'something like'.
