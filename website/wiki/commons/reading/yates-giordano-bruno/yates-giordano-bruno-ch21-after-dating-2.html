Rosicrucians and Kircher: Hermetism after the dating

Commons · Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Yates argues that the Rosicrucian manifestoes (1614-16) and Athanasius Kircher's Oedipus Aegyptiacus show Renaissance Hermetism surviving Casaubon's dating by going underground into reform-minded esoteric circles and into Jesuit Egyptology, with Bruno and Campanella as possible, hedged, sources of the Rosicrucian reform impulse.

Yates treats two survivals of the Hermetic-Cabalist tradition that simply ignore Casaubon's dating of the Hermetica. The first is the Rosicrucian movement, whose 1614 Cassel manifesto yokes the Fama Fraternitatis to a German translation of Boccalini's satirical "General Reformation"; she reads the pairing as a hint of continuity with Bruno's magical reform mission and, more securely, with Campanella through Andreae and Wense. She then speculates, very tentatively, about a stream running from Bruno's Elizabethan court Hermetism through Ashmole to Freemasonry (and Mozart's Magic Flute). The second is the Jesuit Athanasius Kircher, whose Oedipus Aegyptiacus (1652) keeps Hermes Trismegistus as a real ancient Egyptian author, reads hieroglyphs through Ficino's Hermetic texts, and draws a careful line between acceptable natural magic and condemned diabolic magic.

Themes

Hermetism goes underground rather than dying. Yates cites Garin's phrase for the esoteric channels within which Hermetism continued after "Hermes Trismegistus" was dated. The Rosicrucians, the possible Masonic stream and Kircher's Jesuit Egyptology are presented as such channels. Her claims are hedged throughout ("perhaps", "may", "a not impossible supposition"), and she says the Rosicrucian sequence "does not prove anything; it is merely suggestive." This fits the WMP interest in a misdated founding corpus whose worldview outlives its philological refutation, though Yates describes an intellectual afterlife, not a machine.

General reformation as a Hermetic hope. The Rosicrucians expect a general reformation of divine and human things, which Yates compares with Bruno's magical reform. Boccalini's version is a disillusioned satire in which the reformers end up regulating the price of cabbages; the Fama is naive and hopeful. She dates the sequence: Henri IV assassinated 1610, Boccalini published 1612-13 and dead 1613, the Fama 1614. The Rosicrucians are Lutheran, alchemical and eirenist, continuing the liberal, tolerant tendencies of sixteenth-century religious Hermetism, and perhaps hoped to avert the Thirty Years War by magic. A reform program pinned on a monarch and then broken by events loosely resonates with giordano-bruno's failed reform program, and with the Gramsci-gap idea of hope surviving the death of its vehicle, though Yates never frames it that way.

Bruno versus the Rosicrucians (as represented by Fludd). Yates lists differences: Bruno is less Christian and less alchemical, and not Fludd's geocentric. The Rosicrucians look like a return to Renaissance origins, preserved in Lutheran Wittenberg circles, not a continuation of the recent Bruno and Campanella developments. Campanella's link is firm (Tobias Adami at Tuebingen, Andreae, Wense, the naming of Andreae's union as a City of the Sun); the Bruno link stays conjectural, via the possible Giordanisti.

Kircher: Egyptian hieroglyphs need Hermes. Yates argues that Renaissance hieroglyphic interpretation depends on believing in Hermes Trismegistus as an ancient Egyptian priest, so Kircher, dating him in Abraham's time, cannot abandon him. Kircher reads Corpus Hermeticum IV and V (in Ficino's Latin) as hidden in the hieroglyphs, and sees Egyptian monuments as carrying Ficinian Hermetism, a late climax of the hieroglyphic tradition that Yates says will soon be superseded when the true nature of hieroglyphs is discovered. The obelisk interpretation ties the crux ansata (Ficino's De vita coelitus comparanda) to the Christian cross, and his Isis and Osiris exposition combines Egyptian immanence with Pseudo-Dionysian light mysticism, what would be called panpsychism in Bruno. His Heliopolis as civitas Solis supports Yates's view that Campanella's City of the Sun was ultimately Egyptian.

Kircher on magic: a careful Hermetist-Cabalist. He quotes the Asclepius idol-making passage twice, first neutrally as archaeology, then with the marginal "Trismegisti impia doctrina". He allows a natural Magia lucis et umbrae, syncretises Cabala and Hermetism after Pico but condemns Cabalist magic. Yates contrasts Bruno's Egyptianism, "demonic and revolutionary", with Kircher's, where Christianity is supreme; she suggests Patrizi's advice to the Jesuits to take up Hermetism was not misplaced.

Freemasonry and Mozart (speculative). Yates asks whether a courtly Elizabethan stream from Bruno's royalist, chivalric Hermetism, crossed with imported Rosicrucian ideas through Fludd, Vaughan and Ashmole, contributed to Freemasonry, and reads the Magic Flute as a translation of the good Egyptian religion into music. She labels this tentative, and it should be carried with that label.

Notable passages

The passage linking the Fama to Bruno's reform (c1) and the Bruno-versus-Fludd comparison (c2) set out her cautious case, while c3 gives her conclusion that Campanella reinforces, not cancels, the Bruno suggestion. The Kircher passages (c4, c5, c6) carry her positive argument about why hieroglyph-reading required Hermes to remain ancient, how Kircher handles the Asclepius magic, and how his Egyptianism differs from Bruno's. Relevant for wider context: modernity-machine readings treat the dating as a turning point; this unit shows the dated text's worldview persisting in institutions and scholarship (a Jesuit archaeology, secret societies) for a further century or more, with the qualification that Yates presents Kircher's work as a late, soon-to-be-superseded flowering.
