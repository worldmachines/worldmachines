Ficino's Natural Magic: the Libri de Vita, Plotinus, and the Spiritus Mundi

Commons · Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Yates argues that Ficino's De vita coelitus comparanda, presented as medicine and as a commentary on Plotinus, is really a Neoplatonic justification of the talismanic magic of the Asclepius, powered by a theory of spiritus that Ficino probably drew from the Picatrix.

Yates opens Chapter IV by reading Ficino's Libri de Vita (1489) first as an ordinary medical treatise, saturated with the astrological assumptions any Renaissance physician took for granted, and then as a vehicle for a subtle talismanic magic he was nervous about. She works through the metaphysical preamble to the third book, De vita coelitus comparanda, and argues that its stated debt to Plotinus (Ennead IV, 3, xi) is a means of rehabilitating the Egyptian magic of the Asclepius on Neoplatonic grounds; by the end of the book Ficino says Plotinus was merely repeating Hermes Trismegistus. She then turns to the mechanism, the spiritus mundi, and suggests, with hedging, that Ficino's clear-cut theory of it came from the Picatrix rather than from Plotinus.

Themes

Medicine as cover and carrier for magic. The books are for melancholy students under Saturn, who are told to favour the objects of Sol, Jupiter and Venus (the "Three Graces"), with gold, green, flowers, scents and diet. Yates notes that much of this is normal medicine, but that Ficino was also offering talismans and warned that astronomical images may be omitted. Her wry aside about an expensive psychiatrist is her own gloss, not Ficino's.

A three-tier cosmos as the theory of magic. Intellect (Ideas), soul of the world (seminal reasons), body of the world (material forms). Degenerate material forms can be re-formed by manipulating the higher images on which they depend, in the "middle place". Yates follows Garin: the priestly Magus keeps the circuit between the divine world, the world soul and the sensible world running, playing a semi-divine role. Gombrich's point about ancient images (an ancient image of Justice contains an echo of the Idea) explains why an ancient star image would carry power.

Reinterpreting the Asclepius through Plotinus. Yates offers a reconstruction rather than a certainty: Ficino once followed Aquinas in condemning the Asclepius's statue-animating magic as demonic; reading Plotinus, he concluded that Hermes drew on the nature of the world, not on demons, even if wicked Egyptian priests had used demons. This lets Christian Neoplatonism absorb Hermetic magic, at the price of what she calls much ingenious evasion of authoritative Christian pronouncements; the Origen/Celsus passage is a possible encouragement that turns out to be less helpful in context.

Spiritus as the channel. Between the soul and body of the world lies a fine, airy, warm spiritus mundi through which stellar influence reaches man, who drinks it through his own spirit, especially via the rays of Sun and Jupiter. Animals, plants, foods, scents and colours are chosen to attract a planet's spiritus. Yates says she could not find this clear-cut doctrine in Plotinus, and that the Picatrix (series intellectus, spiritus, materia; talismans as stores of a star's spiritus, with lists of images) is where Ficino could have found it. She stays tentative: "could have", "may have used".

Resonance with WMP (partial). The Magus as a figure who operates on the world by understanding its hidden structure fits the project's interest in the will to operate as a precursor of the scientific stance behind the modernity-machine; but Ficino's operation is sympathetic and hierarchical (reflection of Ideas downward), not the legibility-by-measurement of legibility-as-machine-core, so the rhyme is loose. Yates herself does not make this argument in this unit.

Notable passages

The opening of the chapter frames the Libri de Vita as normal medicine that nevertheless contains talismans (c1). The central argumentative move is that the commentary on Plotinus "becomes, by devious ways, a justification for the use of talismans" and is really a commentary on the Asclepius (c2). Garin and Gombrich supply the account of the Magus and of ancient images (c3). Yates's reading of Ficino's change of mind about Aquinas and demons gives the intellectual motive (c4). The spiritus doctrine (c5) and its probable source in the Picatrix (c6) supply the mechanism.
