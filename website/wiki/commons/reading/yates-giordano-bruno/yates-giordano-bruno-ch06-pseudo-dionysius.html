Pseudo-Dionysius as the Christian frame of Renaissance magic

Commons · Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Yates argues that Pseudo-Dionysius's angelic hierarchies and negative theology gave Ficino and Pico the religious and cosmological frame into which natural (Spiritus) magic and practical Cabala fit, blurring the line between astrologising mysticism and mysticising astrology, with Light and Love as continuous currents linking Magus and angelic orders.

Yates treats Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, accepted in the Renaissance as a contemporary of St. Paul, as the Christian ally of Ficino and Pico and as the supplier of the religious and cosmological setting in which the two magics operate. She lays out Ficino's cosmic order from the four elements up through the planets, eighth sphere, crystalline sphere and Empyrean, with the nine angelic orders (as reshaped by Aquinas and Dante); shows Pico in the Heptaplus mapping the three hierarchies onto the Cabalists' three worlds; sets out the negative theology and its confirmation by the Hermetic "No Name yet All Names"; and closes with Light and Love as continuous currents between the Magus and the divine. The chapter, she says, is not about the two magics as such but about the framework in which religion and magic are tenuously related.

Themes

Misdating again. Dionysius was not the Areopagite but an unknown Neoplatonically influenced writer, roughly of the same period as the misdated prisci theologi. Yates, citing R. Roques, notes possible Hermetic-gnostic parallels in the hierarchies, so the misdating error recurs at a second point of the Renaissance synthesis. This resonates loosely with the WMP interest in a civilisation's founding texts being misdated (see modernity-machine), though here the misdating is a bridge-building device rather than a later philological rupture.

Angelic continuation of Spiritus magic. Ficino's hierarchies "drink" from the Trinity in a way that echoes the Spiritus poured through the stars in the De vita coelitus comparanda; Ficino's magic aims no higher than the planets and the Sun but has an angelic continuation beyond. Yates hedges: Ficino, "or so I believe", would not have tried to operate with angels.

Pico versus Ficino: operating with angels. Yates argues that Pico, through practical Cabala, had a means of reaching and operating with the angelic world that Ficino lacked. She notes Pico "astrologises" the hierarchies more strictly than Ficino, a tendency absent in Dionysius himself. She carefully hedges the Sephiroth correlation (Garin's suggestion; not found in Pico; found in Fludd and Kircher later). This is the pivot from contemplative to operative religion, the "will to operate" theme that Yates later links to science.

Negative theology. Dionysius's via negativa (through the Cloud of Unknowing and Cusanus) reaches Ficino, who reads Dionysius's God-with-no-name and all-names as confirmed by the Asclepius. The Cabalistic Ensoph and Keter parallel this. Pico's synthesis, Yates thinks, is made at a mystical level in which all names are at bottom the No Name.

Light and Love as continuous currents. The gradations of light (splendor, candor, lumen, perhaps lux), Pico's gradations of heat, and Eros in the hierarchies, which Ficino deforms into "the magical theme of universal sympathy", make it hard to say when supercelestial becomes celestial and terrestrial, or when erotic magic becomes participation in divine love. This resembles WMP interest in continuities that make categories blur, though for Yates it is a religious and poetic experience, not an infrastructure.

Gnosis, optimist and dualist. Yates argues that Ficino and Pico correctly, if on a wrong chronology, perceived affinities between Hermetic, Dionysian and Cabalist schemes, all related to a religion of the world. Ficino misread dualism (the Ormuzd/Ahriman example); the Asclepius, Cabala and Dionysius are optimist. She restricts "gnosticism" to a non-Manichaean sense.

Notable passages

The curated passages cover the angelic continuation of Ficino's Spiritus magic (c1), Pico's Cabala-based access to the angels (c2), Pico's astrologising of the hierarchies (c3), the negative theology and Hermes's confirmation (c4), the chapter's framing of mysticising astrology and light drunk through Spiritus (c5), and the optimist/dualist gnosis and the comparative-religion claim (c6). Watch attributions: the Dionysian and Hermetic quotations are the sources Yates expounds, whereas the hedged claims about what Ficino or Pico would have done are her own interpretation.
