Optimist gnosis, the Asclepius, and the rehabilitation of Egyptian religion

Commons · Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Yates completes her tour of the Ficinian Hermetica with the optimist-gnosis treatises XI and XII (the world reflected in the mind, a living moving earth), then the Asclepius with its god-making statues and Lament, and argues that Ficino's readers would have rehabilitated this Egyptian religion, and with it astral magic, as pious and prophetic of Christianity, as the Siena pavement shows.

Yates summarises Corpus Hermeticum XI (the Mind to Hermes) and XII (Hermes to Tat on the Common Intellect), both of the optimist type of gnosis, then gives a long digest of the Asclepius (the Sermo Perfectus): the temple setting, man as magnum miraculum, the hierarchy of gods, the making of gods from statues, and the Lament (Apocalypse) on the passing of Egypt. She then turns to her own argument. Since the commentary once attributed to Ficino was by Lefevre d'Etaples, Ficino's real view of the Asclepius must be read through the argumentum to his Pimander. She argues that, in the light of the Corpus, Ficino's readers would have seen Augustine as mistaken about the Lament and would have accepted the Asclepius's cult, including astral magic, as a legitimate pious practice. The unit closes on the Siena cathedral pavement, where Hermes Trismegistus stands with the Sibyls as a Gentile prophet of Christianity, "contemporaneous" with Moses.

Themes

Reflection of the universe in the mind. In CH XI the gnosis is to make oneself equal to God by holding all times, places and qualities in thought: "the like is not intelligible save to the like." Yates contrasts this with the pessimist gnosis of the earlier Tat treatise, where matter was evil and regeneration meant escape. Here the world is good because it is full of God. She notes that world-reflection in the mind belongs to both types, with a different emphasis: ethical release in one, a "kind of pantheism" in the other. She adds that a Renaissance enthusiast who believed all the texts came from one most ancient Egyptian would have blurred these distinctions.

A living, moving earth. CH XII holds that nothing in the world is dead, that death is only dissolution of a mixture, and that the earth is not immobile but both stable and subject to many movements. Yates restates that "nothing can die and everything moves, including the earth", and calls this the ideal philosophy for Man as Magus. She does not yet draw the Copernican consequence here; that comes later in the book. Dead matter does not exist in this scheme, since the energies operating in matter are parts of God, which is a very different cosmos from a mechanical one.

Man as divine and double; the making of gods. The Asclepius presents man as a magnum miraculum, double in nature (divine intellect, elemental body), placed to govern terrestrial things. Because man "makes gods" by drawing celestial virtue into statues through herbs, stones, aromatics, hymns and rites, the text supplies the doctrinal charter for sympathetic magic. Yates gives this passage as the hinge to Ficinian magic.

The Lament as decline narrative and restoration. The Lament describes the departure of the gods, the collapse of piety, laws forbidding the religion of the mind, cosmic disorder, and finally a cleansing and rebirth of the world by the will of God. Yates observes that it identifies the decay of the religion of the world with the decay of ethics, and its restoration with moral restoration. She argues that, once Hermes was thought to prophesy Christ, the Lament could be read as an injunction to infuse a decayed Christianity with Egyptian piety, not (as Augustine read it) as a devil-inspired prophecy of Christianity abolishing idolatry. She says the author may have been influenced by Hebrew prophecy.

Yates's central claim: rehabilitation of the Asclepius. She writes that this rehabilitation is, "I believe, one of the chief factors in the Renaissance revival of magic", and that the Asclepius must be read in the context of Ficino's Pimander and its pious commentary. She is careful to dissociate Ficino from Lefevre d'Etaples's disapproval of the god-making passage.

Resonances. The Lament's arc (golden-age religion, decay of the sacred, disorder, violent renewal) rhymes with the cyclical Dawn, Day, Dusk framing of world-machines, though the Hermetic text is a theological and moral cycle with divine intervention, where WMP's cycles are infrastructural and non-teleological. The Siena pavement is a case where a text mis-dated as older than Moses and Plato is embedded in a cathedral floor, an early instance of the misdated-founding-text theme that Yates's book builds toward (the Casaubon dating is not in this unit).

Notable passages

The CH XI comparison of the two gnoses is the unit's analytical core on the mind and world. The god-making passage and the Lament are the primary texts Yates marshals (Hermetic voice, not Yates's own view). Her own argument is concentrated in the paragraphs on Ficino's argumentum, on astral magic as the cult that goes with the religion of the mind, and on the changed attitude to the Lament. The Siena description (the inscription "Take up thy letters and laws O Egyptians", read as Moses supplicating the Egyptian lawgiver to revive piety and morality) shows the Renaissance status of Hermes in visual form; note her hedges ("perhaps intended", "if this figure is indeed intended to be Moses").
