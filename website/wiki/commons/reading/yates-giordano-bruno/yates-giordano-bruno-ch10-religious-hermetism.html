Religious Hermetism without magic: the Christian Hermes and the search for toleration

Commons · Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Yates surveys sixteenth-century religious Hermetism, a Christianised Hermes with the Asclepius's magic excised (France, Mornay, Rosseli, Patrizi, More's Utopia, Dee), as a tolerance-seeking palliative for the wars of religion and as the foil against which Bruno's magical, non-Christian Hermetism must be read.

Yates argues that alongside Ficino's magical current there ran a purely religious and philosophical use of the Hermetica, in which the magic of the Asclepius was disowned (as bad magic, or as an interpolation by the Latin translator Apuleius) so that Hermes could be admired as a near-Christian prisca theologia authority. She traces it mainly through France (Lefevre d'Etaples, Champier, Turnebus, Foix de Candale, Pontus de Tyard, Du Plessis Mornay), then a Capuchin commentator (Hannibal Rosseli) and the Italian Francesco Patrizi, who wanted the Pope to make Hermetism the intellectual weapon of the Counter Reformation. She links the movement to Erasmian tolerance, and reads More's Utopia as anticipating it. England, she says, was isolated from this current, with John Dee and Sidney's circle as exceptions. The chapter is explicitly preparation for Bruno, whose Hermetism abandoned the Christian interpretation on which the pious versions rested.

Themes

Magic subtracted, Christianity added. The device is to keep the Christian-looking Hermes (Genesis, the Word as Son of God, the regeneration of Corpus Hermeticum XIII) and discard the idol-making passage. Champier first proposed that Apuleius inserted it, an idea later French writers repeated. Yates notes a paradox: Lefevre banned the Asclepius's magic yet printed with it Lazzarelli's Crater Hermetis, which she describes as "something like a magical interpretation of the psychology of religious experience". She also suggests that blaming Apuleius may have attracted magicians to him, and she names Bruno as the modern magician who could have taken The Golden Ass as a model.

Dating pushed back as piety rises. Yates observes a tendency "by which the holier and more Christian Hermes Trismegistus becomes, the more his date is pushed back" (Vergerius's preface to Turnebus's 1554 edition puts him before Moses). She also notes that Casaubon's 1614 dating first assumed the Hermetica were Christian forgeries, so deeply had the Christian reading penetrated. Modern scholars, by contrast, see them as Hellenistic gnosticism with little Christian influence. This is a historian's point about a misdated founding text; it fits WMP's interest in misdated foundations and philology as a legibility event (modernity-machine, Legibility), though Yates herself does not frame it in those terms.

Hermetism as a way above the religious wars. Mornay (Protestant, 1581, Antwerp) and Rosseli (Catholic Capuchin, published in tolerant Poland) are presented as men turning to the Hermetic religion of the world to escape fanatical force on both sides. Yates connects this to William of Orange's Antwerp experiment and to the Erasmian tradition, which "joins the Hermetic-Cabalist tradition" at this point. Henri III's politique Catholicism and Bruno's supposed political mission to England belong to the same setting (her own 1939-40 argument). Mornay's Hermetism-plus-Cabala is emphatically mystical and theological, not Magia or practical Cabala. Mornay also says all magic is wrong and vain.

Court magic in the French background. Yates admits the magical current stayed strong in France (Gohorry's medical-magical Academy). She suggests Catherine de' Medici's Ballet comique of 1581 may have been for her "an extended and complicated talisman", with Baif's Academy of Poetry and Music showing how thin the line between magic and art or religion is. She flags this as possible, not proven.

Patrizi: the Hermetic Counter Reformation. Patrizi's Nova de universis philosophia (1591) petitions Gregory XIV to teach the Hermetica instead of Aristotle and win back Protestants by philosophy rather than censures or arms. Yates stresses that it belongs to the Ficinian, magic-tolerant Italian tradition (prisca magia as true religion, a light series, universal animation, but no Copernicus). It was condemned after Patrizi's call to Rome in 1592, and he was effectively silenced. The story shows the confusion of the period, before Del Rio's position on magic was published.

More, England and Dee. Yates suggests Hermetic influence in the religion of the wisest Utopians and reads More's toleration decree as a statement of the principles later religious Hermetism turned to. Elizabethan Puritan Anglicanism lacked prisca theologia; Hermetism survived only privately, in Sidney's circle and in Dee, a Christian Magus and genuine mathematician of the Copernican theory, whose diary shows his spiritual home in religious Hermetism through Rosseli. Compare thomas-more and more-utopia, and giordano-bruno. Bruno's Hermetism is a "yet another brand" Sidney had to weigh.

Bruno's rupture (foreshadowed). Yates says Bruno took "the appalling step of abandoning the Christian interpretation of Hermetism", so his fate was worse than Patrizi's. The resonance with WMP is loose: the chapter shows a golden-age/prisca worldview being used as a peace technology, and Bruno as its terminal, heterodox form, though Yates is not offering a machine-cycle theory.

Notable passages

The opening paragraph states the thesis of religious Hermetism. The Casaubon paragraph gives the irony that the first critical dating assumed Christian forgery. The Mornay/Antwerp paragraph links Hermetism, toleration and Erasmianism. The Patrizi dedication and Yates's gloss show the movement placed in the Counter Reformation. The passage on Bruno's abandonment of the Christian reading is the hinge to the next chapters. The Thomas More conclusion frames Utopian toleration as the anticipated palliative.
