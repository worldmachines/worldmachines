Giordano Bruno's first Paris visit: the memory arts as Hermetic magic

Commons · Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Yates presents Bruno's Paris period (1581-83) through his two magic-memory books, De umbris idearum and Cantus Circaeus, arguing that they show him already a Hermetic Magus who pushes Ficino's talismanic magic and Agrippa's images to an anti-Christian 'Egyptian' extreme, and speculating that Henri III's protection sent him to England.

Yates opens Chapter XI with Bruno's biography (born Nola 1548, Dominican, flight for heresy in 1576, Geneva, Toulouse, Paris in late 1581) and then argues that the two mnemonic books he published in Paris in 1582, the De umbris idearum and the Cantus Circaeus, are not mere mnemotechnics but works of magic. She sketches the classical art of memory (places and images, per the Ad Herennium) and its Renaissance transformation into a Neoplatonic and Hermetic method of imprinting archetypal celestial images on the mind. She then walks through the De umbris (its Hermes dialogue, thirty-fold structure, 150 talismanic images drawn largely from Agrippa) and the Cantus Circaeus (Circe's planetary incantations, again Agrippan). She closes on Bruno's relation to Henri III and the possibility that the French king's protection lies behind his English sojourn. See also giordano-bruno.

Themes

Memory as Hermetic self-transformation. Yates's central claim (hedged: 'I believe') is that Renaissance magic memory descends from the Hermetic experience of reflecting the universe in the mind. Talismanic or archetypal images placed on the memory wheels give the Magus universal knowledge and powers. She ties this to Corpus Hermeticum XI ('make yourself equal to God') and to the gnostic goal of becoming the Aion.

Bruno as a radicalised Ficino. The De umbris draws on Ficino's De vita coelitus comparanda but without Ficino's 'Christian inhibitions'. Bruno believes Hermetic Egyptianism better than Christianity, and so, in Yates's reading, moves back toward darker mediaeval necromancy while keeping Ficino's 'Plotinising' of images. Her suggestion that Bruno's 'shadows of ideas' are the celestial magic images is offered as extraordinary but probable, and she says Ficino's 'shadows' may sometimes mean the same.

Anticipations of the English dialogues. The opening Hermes/Philothimus/Logifer dialogue already contains the cast (sage against pedants), the solar imagery of light against dark creatures, and an allusion to the Lament of the Asclepius. Yates reads that allusion as an anti-Christian claim that false Christian 'Mercuries' suppressed the better Egyptian religion.

Agrippa as source-book. The 36 decan images, the planetary images and lunar mansions in the De umbris, and the planetary incantations in the Cantus Circaeus are shown to derive from Agrippa's De occulta philosophia, with variations. Only the 36 images of the twelve houses are apparently Bruno's own invention.

Barbaric versus Ficinian magic. Comparing Circe's 'barbara & arcana carmina' with Ficino's elegant Orphic hymns (and Diacceto's solar rite), Yates calls Bruno's magic 'reactionary and barbaric', possibly deliberately, to get a stronger magic. She also finds a moral and political reform theme in Circe's magic (Astraea, the cock as French Monarchy), anticipating the Spaccio, and links it to the court Ballet comique de la reine.

Patronage and mission. Bruno's account of Henri III is known only from his testimony to the Venetian inquisitors. Yates judges his claim to have proved the art non-magical untrue, and argues on circumstantial grounds that a letter of introduction to the ambassador Castelnau was real. She raises the possibility, hedged as 'possible', that Henri sent him into England sub rosa. The French Catholic Hermetists of the previous chapter, who kept Hermetism apart from magic, form the contrasting background.

Notable passages

The memory-art passage (c1) is the interpretive key: classical mnemonics becomes a way of tuning the Magus to cosmic powers. The Hermes dialogue passage (c2) establishes Bruno's Egyptian, anti-Christian Hermetism at the very start of his publishing career. The talisman-and-Corpus-Hermeticum passage (c3) links the celestial images to the gnostic ideal of becoming equal to God; the quoted CH XI text there is the Hermetica speaking, while the framing ("I believe") is Yates's inference. The Circe passage (c4) shows Yates reasoning tentatively about why incantations precede an art of memory. The last two passages (c5, c6) cover Henri III and the ambassador, showing her method of weighing Bruno's self-serving testimony against court context. WMP resonance is faint here: the memory system is an attempt to make the cosmos inwardly legible and operable, a Hermetic cousin of the modern legibility drive, though it is magical and archetypal where legibility is bureaucratic and quantitative.
