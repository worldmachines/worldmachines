Bruno's English campaign opens: the Thirty Seals, Oxford, and Abbot's Ficino

Commons · Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Yates opens her account of Bruno in England by treating the Explicatio Triginta Sigillorum as a Hermetic-magical memory work, then uses George Abbot's 1604 report of Bruno's Oxford lectures (Copernicus delivered out of Ficino's De vita coelitus comparanda) to argue that Bruno's Copernicanism was already a Ficinian, religiously charged astral magic, and announces that the Italian dialogues serve a Hermetic 'Egyptian' reform.

Yates begins Chapter XII by describing the volume with which Bruno opened his campaign in England: a reprint of the memory art from the Cantus Circaeus plus the Explicatio Triginta Sigillorum and Sigillus Sigillorum, dedicated to the French ambassador and addressed also to the Vice Chancellor and doctors of Oxford. She reads it as continuous with the Paris memory books: memory as an instrument for forming a Magus, combined with Magia, Cabala and Lullism. She then turns to Bruno's 1583 Oxford visit during the entertainment of the Polish prince Alasco, setting Bruno's own boastful account in the Cena de le ceneri against a newly found Oxford witness, George Abbot's 1604 anti-Catholic tract (found by Robert McNulty, 1960), which says Bruno lectured on Copernicus and that his lectures were taken almost verbatim from Ficino's De vita coelitus comparanda. Yates accepts this, argues Abbot must have seen a religious meaning in Bruno's 'Ficinian Copernicanism', and closes by announcing her programme: Bruno's moral and philosophical dialogues are part of a Hermetic religious mission, an expansion of Ficino's magic into a full restoration of the magical religion of the pseudo-Egyptians of the Asclepius, to be shown first in the Spaccio and then the Cena.

Themes

Memory as the training of a Magus. Yates calls the volume 'a further development of the exploration of memory as a major instrument in the formation of a Magus'. The 'thirty' grouping of seals places it in the mystico-magical world of the De umbris idearum, and the title-page promises 'logic, metaphysics, Cabala, natural magic, arts long and short'. Her judgement is that Bruno's memory system is a magical, not merely mnemonic, technology, which is the same reading of Bruno as operator that runs through the book.

Copernicus inside astral magic. The key finding is Abbot's testimony, which Yates calls 'a priceless piece of information': at Oxford Bruno announced the earth's motion in the same lectures that drew on Ficino's sun-worship and astral magic. This supports her larger claim that Bruno's heliocentrism is a Hermetic gesture rather than a mathematical astronomy. She hedges about the Oxford audience ('Do they understand what it is all about? Perhaps not'), and notes that the word 'juggler' hints at the magician.

Abbot's motive and religious meaning. Yates argues that the Bruno digression is a flimsy pretext in Abbot's argument against Bristow and Hill (Catholic claims to miracles, unity, and visions), so Abbot must have known something of the Explicatio, in which Bruno defends a 'good' magical religion based on Agrippa. On her inference, Abbot read the Ficinian Copernicanism as religiously significant, which is an interpretation and not a fact stated by Abbot.

Bruno on Oxford. Bruno's own accounts (the fifteen syllogisms in the Cena; the qualified praise of the pre-Reformation friars in De la causa) are treated as complaints against 'grammarian pedants', the Ciceronians who replaced the metaphysical schoolmen. Yates finds the apology 'really apologises for nothing'.

A programme for the chapter. The Italian dialogues are usually classed as moral and philosophical; Yates proposes to show them as a Hermetic mission, with the Spaccio della bestia trionfante carrying the full 'Egyptian' message and the Cena translating it into Copernican terms.

Resonances with World Machines

The Oxford scene is a small case of giordano-bruno as a terminal figure of the Hermetic wing of the Renaissance, a magus operating on the world through memory and image, unheard by academics whose Ciceronian learning belongs to the humanist side of what WMP would call the modernity-machine's construction. It also resonates loosely with legibility-as-machine-core, though this is a stretch: Bruno's memory system is an effort to make the world internally readable and manipulable, but as magic and not as administrative or statistical legibility. Yates herself makes no such connection.

Notable passages

The memory volume (c1). Yates frames the Explicatio Triginta Sigillorum as memory-for-a-Magus, continuous with the Paris books and aimed at winning an academic position as he had with Henri III.

Title-page and combination (c2). The Oxford doctors would have seen at once that the book concerns 'extremely obscure kind of magic', a 'complex combination of Magia and Cabala with Lullism and the art of memory'.

Abbot's report (c3). The heart of the unit: Abbot's account of Bruno stripping up his sleeves, talking of 'chentrum & chirculus & circumferenchia', setting forth Copernicus, and being caught, by 'a graue man' fetching his Ficino, reading out De vita coelitus comparanda. Yates's commentary follows immediately.

Why Abbot cared (c4). Yates's argument that the pretext is too flimsy, so Abbot knew the Explicatio and its Agrippan defence of 'good' magical religion, and that Bruno's abuse of Oxford in the Cena sits behind the tract.

Yates's verdict (c5). Allowing for Abbot's animus, she accepts the testimony that Bruno associated Copernicus with the De vita coelitus comparanda, and infers Abbot saw a religious meaning in it.

The programme of the chapter (c6). The dialogues are Hermetic religious mission; Ficino's magic expanded into a full restoration of the Asclepius's Egyptian religion.
