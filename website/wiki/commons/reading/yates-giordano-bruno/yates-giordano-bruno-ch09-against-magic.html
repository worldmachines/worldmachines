Opposition to the Magus: theological objections and the humanist tradition

Commons · Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Yates argues that Renaissance magic met sustained theological opposition from Catholics and Protestants, and that Latin humanism, with its correct chronology and philological realism, was a force against the Magus, culminating in Erasmian Oxford's hostile reception of Bruno as a Ficinian Magus.

Yates surveys two lines of resistance to the Ficino-Pico magic. First, she sketches theological objections, drawing on D. P. Walker and Thorndike: Giovanni Francesco Pico, Johann Wier, Erastus (Protestant) and Martin Del Rio (Catholic Jesuit) all reject talismans and the pagan prisca theologia, and Del Rio condemns Agrippa. Second, she defines the Latin humanist tradition (Petrarch, Valla, Poggio) as an experience of a different order from the recovery of the Greek and Hermetic texts, and argues that its correct chronology, literary bent and historical realism make it uncongenial to magic. The chapter ends by tracing this through Erasmus, English Reformation book-burning, and Bruno's collision with Erasmian Oxford in 1583.

Themes

Magic was a religious issue, so opposition was religious. The Magi claimed piety and natural rather than demonic magic, but critics asked when an angel is not an angel but a demon. Protestants (Wier, Erastus) wanted religion cleared of magic entirely and read the prisca theologia as Egyptian idolatry; the Counter-Reformation (Del Rio) rejected both Ficino's talismans and Pico's practical Cabala, so Alexander VI's tolerance was not endorsed.

Two Renaissance recoveries, two chronologies. The Latin humanist knows the true date of the civilisation he wants to return to (Ciceronian Rome) and carries that realism into textual scholarship (Valla on the Ad Herennium). Ficino, by contrast, is unfailingly gullible about Hellenistic texts taken as prisca theologia. This is the book's central misdating thesis seen from the other side: the philological habit that would eventually expose the Hermetica was present in humanism but not in the Magi. It loosely rhymes with the theme of misdated founding texts and philology as a legibility event in the modernity-machine, though Yates makes no such claim and here only sets up the contrast that Casaubon later resolves.

Different aims, different dignity of man. Poggio's dignity is social Roman grandeur; Pico's is Man as Magus with divine creative power. Humanists call the Middle Ages barbarous; the Magus finds a golden chain of pia philosophia running through scholasticism. Pico's letter to Ermolao Barbaro expresses this, and Yates notes the irony that better philology might have prevented the Magi from becoming Magi.

Infiltration: the hieroglyph. The two traditions mix in Horapollo's Hieroglyphica and the emblem: a Hellenistic misunderstanding of Egyptian writing that is not magical but popular with humanists. Erasmus values hieroglyphs as a universal visual language, a perfectly rational use of Egyptianism.

Erasmus as religious humanism against magic. Pious, literary, uninterested in metaphysics or natural philosophy, Erasmus proposes a return to Christian sources through printing the New Testament and the Fathers (his own prisca theologia). Yates suggests, with hedges, that his anger at "Termaximus" and his doubts about the Chaldean Oracles, Hermetica, Cabala and Dionysius fit this. The printing angle touches elizabeth-eisenstein's themes, though Yates only mentions printing as Erasmus's tool.

Reformation destruction and Bruno at Oxford. Yates asks, without answering, whether the Renaissance revival of magic fed the suspicion of all philosophy as magic behind the Edwardian burning of mathematical books. Bruno's complaint about Oxford pedants is that they are Erasmian humanists; McNulty's discovery that he quoted Ficino's De vita coelitus comparanda at Oxford shows he came as a Ficinian Magus. giordano-bruno is thus set up as a Hermetic Magus whose mission was rejected in Protestant England and ended at the stake in Rome.

Notable passages

The opening survey (c1) and the Del Rio paragraph (c2) give the theological map. The Latin-humanist chronology paragraph (c3) is the chapter's hinge argument on dating. The Pico letter and Yates's gloss (c4) express the contrast of aims. The Erasmus section (c5) shows religious humanism as anti-Magus, and the closing Oxford passage (c6) links the whole chapter to Bruno.
