Campanella's Hermetic magic, animism and theology set beside Bruno

Commons · Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Yates completes her Campanella chapter by comparing the Spaccio with the City of the Sun, arguing that Campanella's magic, animism, theology and even his Copernican language descend from Ficino's Hermetic magic, and that his later modified Hermetism makes Bruno's Egyptian mission look less improbable.

Yates closes her treatment of Campanella by first comparing the reform of the heavens in Bruno's Spaccio with the Città del Sole (Christ as Magus, a sun-centred reform, planetary virtues, ethics aimed at social utility), then arguing that Campanella, unlike the way he is usually read, was thoroughly versed in Ficino's astral magic and knew its Hermetic source. She documents his actual magical practice for Urban VIII (1628) and for himself (1639), and reads the City of the Sun as a state in which a priesthood of Magi permanently does what the private ritual does once. The unit then takes up how Campanella squared magic with Catholicism (Pseudo-Dionysian angelic hierarchies, a projected new Dominican Summa, Renaissance Thomism), his Hermetic animism, the primacy of Hermetism over Telesio and Porta, echoes of Bruno's infinity and Copernicanism, and the likelihood that his surviving works are toned-down versions of an earlier, more Brunian and demonic programme. She ends by suggesting Bruno may have hoped to do for Clement VIII what Campanella did for Urban VIII.

Themes

Concordance of the Spaccio and the City of the Sun. Despite very different literary forms, Yates finds a deeper agreement: a sun-centred reform, beneficent planetary influences, virtue triumphing over vice, ethics directed at social utility. This supports her larger claim that Bruno's mission was a Hermetic reform, and that Campanella's revolt had comparable aims. Yates hedges lightly (it 'can thus be seen as not altogether dissimilar').

Campanella as heir of Ficino's magic (via D. P. Walker). In the Metaphysica he summarises the De vita coelitus comparanda and traces 'all this doctrine' to Hermes Trismegistus, quoting the Asclepius on animating idols. Yates leans on Walker, the only scholar she credits with noticing the point. Her own inference is that the City of the Sun is Adocentyn (from the Picatrix) made real: a state permanently preserved from bad celestial influence by a priesthood that performs astral magic constantly, where the 1628 ritual was a private, one-off substitute for failing heavens.

Making magic Catholic. Continuity between the stars and the Pseudo-Dionysian angelic hierarchies lets the cult of the stars lead upward to angels and God, as in Ficino. Campanella, however, largely lacks the Cabalist branch (Pico's Magia cum Cabala), just as Bruno subordinated Cabala to Egyptianism. Unlike Bruno, Campanella builds a full theology: a new Dominican Summa in which Grace is a kind of Magia Divina following from Magia Naturalis, resting on animist rather than Aristotelian philosophy. Yates presents this as the culmination of a Renaissance Thomism whose history is unwritten, and says its thesis is her belief rather than settled fact.

Animism and magic are Hermetic in origin. Against the standard story (Telesio and Porta as the chief influences), Yates argues from Campanella's own citations that the world-as-living-animal and the power to make gods both come first from Trismegistus, with Telesio and Porta secondary. This bears on her wider thesis that Renaissance animism, the basis of magic, is ultimately Hermetic. The world-machines-style reading is that a worldview's operative premise (a live, ensouled cosmos) sits beneath the technique built on it; note though that Yates is tracing intellectual sources, not infrastructure.

Echoes of Bruno, and Campanella's caution. Campanella's passage on thinking beyond the sun into infinite worlds recalls giordano-bruno. He names Bruno only once, in the 1622 apology for Galileo, and there as heretical, while using a Cena-like language of a 'secol novo'. Yates suggests he deliberately distanced himself, and that his earliest, unexpurgated views may have been closer to a full Egyptian reform with demonic magic. His surviving Theologia covers a milder, Christianised Hermetism, which in turn makes Bruno's appeal to the papacy look less wild.

Notable passages

The opening comparison of the Spaccio and the City (c1) is the fullest statement of their shared programme. The Urban VIII rite and the Adocentyn reading (c2) carry Yates's central interpretive move about Campanella's ideal state. The theology passage (c3) gives her account of the projected new Summa and the Renaissance Thomism it culminates. The animism passage (c4) and the Asclepius/Porta quotation with its Neoplatonic theurgy and the natural-to-divine magic ladder (c5) show her argument that Hermes stands behind Campanella's magic. The closing pages (c6) offer her judgement that Campanella softened an earlier, more extreme programme and that this shows Bruno's mission was not so improbably wild.
