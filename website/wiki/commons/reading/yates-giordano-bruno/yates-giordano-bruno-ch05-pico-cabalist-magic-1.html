Pico's Marriage of Natural Magic and Practical Cabala

Commons · Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Yates argues that Pico della Mirandola, drawing on Ficino's natural magic, added practical Cabala as a higher, Hebrew-language spiritual magic, and in his magical Conclusiones, Apology and Oration on the Dignity of Man declared natural magic (the 'marriage' of earth and heaven) weak and ineffective without it, thereby founding the Hermetic-Cabalist tradition.

Yates opens Chapter V by presenting Pico as the man who took Ficino's magia naturalis, embraced it more openly, and then capped it with a second, more ambitious magic: practical Cabala, which invokes angels, the sephiroth and God through the power of Hebrew. She sets out three limited aims: to establish what Pico means by magia, to show that he distinguishes theoretical from practical Cabala, and to show that he thinks natural magic is a weak force without the latter. She then works through selected Conclusiones Magicae (the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 5th, 13th, 24th on natural magic; the Orphic conclusions; the 15th, 21st and 22nd on Cabala), the Apology, and the Oration. She explicitly disclaims knowing Pico's actual Cabalist procedures, which she leaves to Hebrew specialists.

Themes

Two magics, one union. Natural magic works by the spiritus mundi and sympathies; practical Cabala reaches beyond the cosmos to angelic and divine powers. Yates argues that Pico is the founder who first united the Hermetic and Cabalist types of magic, and that this Hermetic-Cabalist tradition had momentous consequences, both mystical (numerological and harmonic speculation absorbing Pythagoreanism) and magical.

Symmetry of Hermes and Moses. The Renaissance mind, she says, loved parallels: Hermes Trismegistus, the Egyptian Moses and author of the Pimander, matches Moses and the Cabala, a secret doctrine supposedly handed down orally from Moses. She hedges that Cabala is 'not, I believe, ever called a prisca theologia', since that term belongs to Gentile sources. Both traditions rest on creation by the Word, which Lactantius helped to cement.

What Pico's natural magic is. Pico condemns 'modern magic' (Conclusion 1) yet allows natural magic (2). Yates reads Conclusions 3, 5 and 13 ('marrying the world') as the linking of earth and heaven by sympathetic magic, and, in her own inference (she hedges with 'I think', 'or so I would think'), as including Ficinian talismans. The 24th conclusion gives magical characters and figures more power than material qualities. His Orphic conclusions insist that the gods' names are natural, not demonic, virtues. She concludes his natural magic is probably the same as Ficino's, but explicitly non-demonic.

The Magus as Hermetic figure. The Oration opens with the Asclepius, 'Magnum, o Asclepi, miraculum est homo', which Yates reads as Pico boldly declaring what Ficino muffled: that the magic of the Asclepius is his real subject. The Magus 'marries earth to heaven' as the peasant marries vine to elm. This makes the Oration 'the great charter of Renaissance Magic'. It resonates loosely with the World Machines theme of magic as a will to operate on the world before science, though Yates makes no such claim in this unit and the magic here is explicitly theological.

Ficino outbid. Conclusions 15, 21 and 22 make Cabala necessary for any efficacious magic. Yates reads this as an implicit guarantee that Ficino's magic was non-demonic, because he was too pious to use demons and too ignorant of Hebrew for angelic magic: harmless but weak.

Notable passages

The opening framing (c1) defines practical Cabala as an ambitious spiritual magic inseparable from religion. The Hermes-Moses-Word passage (c2) shows why the two traditions fused. The reading of the 'marrying the world' conclusions (c3) and of the characters and figures (c4) gives Yates's account of Pico's natural magic. The Oration passage (c5) ties it to the Asclepius. The closing passage (c6) delivers the 15th, 21st and 22nd conclusions and the verdict on Ficino.
