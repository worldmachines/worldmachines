The Anxious Bourgeois and the Making of “Victorian”

Commons · Peter Gay · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Victorian bourgeois selfhood rested on managed anxiety and a disciplined “will”—expressed through neurasthenia, a tested self-made-man myth, and an idealized family portrait—while the word “Victorian” itself flipped from an honorific into the insult that antibourgeois writers like Zola, Shaw, Strindberg, and Ibsen were about to weaponize.

Closing out the introduction before Vol. V turns to taste and the arts, this unit argues that Victorian bourgeois selfhood was built on the psychic tax of progress—managed anxiety, a disciplined “will,” a tested myth of self-made mobility, and a self-idealizing family portrait—and then pins down “Victorian” itself as a word whose meaning inverted from compliment to insult, ending on the cross-national antibourgeois campaign the book's next chapter will examine.

Themes

Anxiety as the tax on progress. Gay opens with the claim that “all change is traumatic, even change for the better”: nineteenth-century gains in mobility, industry, and urbanization generated a chronic, often repressed unease. Physicians and moralists medicalized this unease as “nerves” or, in George Beard's coinage, “neurasthenia”—“the cry of the system struggling with its environment”—and even painters like van Gogh absorbed the diagnosis (“We are all of us more or less neurotic”). Freud's contribution, in Gay's telling, was not to invent this anxiety but to redirect it away from moralized “maladies” like masturbation and toward a more clinical account.

The disciplinary response: routine, timetable, iron cage. Confronted with “the industrial maelstrom,” Victorians—entrepreneurs especially—answered with “obsessive routines: a nail-biting devotion to numbers, a surrender to timetables, a fussy punctuality,” which Gay glosses via Max Weber's “iron cage” of capitalist discipline. The same cause produced its opposite too: “restlessness as much as rigidity.”

The self-made man, tested against the record. Gay treats the Horatio Alger mythology of upward mobility as “self-deluding fiction” next to “tough-minded reportage,” but he does not dismiss mobility outright: Andrew Carnegie, the Schneider brothers, Adolphe Thiers, Karl May, and Hans Christian Andersen are real cases of climbers, set against the German distinction between the property-owning Besitzbürgertum and the cultivated Bildungsbürgertum, and against the “snobs” who policed the line between old and new status.

The family as self-idealization, and its critics. The bourgeois family is presented as the era's most cherished emblem—haven, moral nursery, discreet vessel for legitimate sexuality—rendered in flawless sentimental genre painting. Gay immediately qualifies this: the same institution “could become a prison house,” and he credits both the hagiographers and the debunkers (implicitly Marx, Engels, and Zola, whose Pot-Bouille stages bourgeois adultery) with being “both right—in part.”

The will as virtue and danger. Victorian pedagogy centered on taming “the will's raw, blind urges.” Henry Maudsley's formula—not the will suppressed but the “well-fashioned will”—organizes Gay's account of contested cases (Rockefeller's fortune-building, strikes, imperialism) where Victorians agreed on the need for discipline but disagreed, along political lines, about what counted as its abuse.

Freud's own bourgeois psychology. Gay quotes a private 1883 letter from Freud to his fiancée Martha Bernays contrasting bourgeois “refinement”—won through renunciation, the deliberate avoidance of suffering over the pursuit of pleasure—against the unrestrained “people.” This is offered as Gay's clearest first-person evidence that bourgeois self-control was an internalized economy of pleasure and pain, not mere hypocrisy.

Defining “Victorian,” and its inversion. Gay traces the word from its 1839 coinage as a domestic honorific to its 1870s consolidation as an age-name, then to its later capture by critics who turned bourgeois self-praise (good sense, practicality, ready tears, regularity) into insult (dullness, vulgarity, sentimentality, slavish scheduling). He extends “Victorian” past Britain to a cosmopolitan bourgeois culture of taste, exhibitions, and shared anxieties (the transnational panic over masturbation is one example), with the 1851 Great Exhibition—read through Charlotte Brontë's awed letters home—as its quintessential expression of “material inventiveness” assisting “spirituality.”

Setting up the bourgeoisophobes. The unit ends by naming the writers—Zola, Shaw, Strindberg, Ibsen—whose post-1870s “antagonistic propaganda” against “the comfortable classes” Gay says “sang a single tune” across national borders, and which modernist artists would soon adopt wholesale. This is the direct setup for Gay's bourgeoisophobe theme proper.

Notable passages

The iron-cage passage (c2) is the unit's clearest bridge from private nervous complaint to public disciplinary regime: Weber's phrase names the same order-imposing impulse—timetables, punctuality, “a nail-biting devotion to numbers”—that World Machines calls legibility, the modernity-machine's core optimization for a measurable, orderable world; Gay's frame is psychological and moral where WMP's is institutional and infrastructural, but the phenomenon described (disciplinary routine as the price of scale) is the same one.

The closing passage (c6), naming Zola, Shaw, Strindberg, and Ibsen as a cross-national front of antibourgeois writers, is the direct antecedent of Gay's “bourgeoisophobe” category flagged in this source's brief; it resonates with carey-intellectuals-and-masses's account of modernist intelligentsia turning contempt for a rising public into a defensive myth, though Gay's antagonists here are novelists and playwrights denouncing a class rather than Carey's intelligentsia defending status against a mass readership—a related but not identical grievance. The family passage (c3), read alongside Gay's nod to Marx, Engels, and Zola as partial truth-tellers about bourgeois hypocrisy, also touches kulturkritik's self-reflexive critique-of-culture-from-within-culture, though only lightly: Gay is narrating the critique, not performing it.

The Maudsley (c4) and Freud (c5) passages together carry Gay's psychological argument proper: the “well-fashioned will” and Freud's private economy of renunciation-versus-pleasure are the conceptual hinge the book will later apply to taste itself—expect this same logic of channeled, sublimated energy to reappear when Gay turns to bourgeois responses to art.
