A Profusion of Alternatives: Modernism's Riotous Debut and Bourgeois Patronage

Commons · Peter Gay · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Opening Chapter Six, Gay charts modernism's explosive proliferation across painting, music, architecture, and the novel after 1880 and argues that, despite near-universal bourgeois unease and strategies of co-option, it was middle-class patronage rather than a self-sufficient avant-garde that carried modernism to triumph.

Opening Chapter Six, Gay surveys the sudden multiplication of modernist styles in painting, poetry, sculpture, architecture, music, and eventually the stream-of-consciousness novel from roughly 1880 to the eve of the First World War, tracing the bourgeoisie's mixed response — riots, hissing, defensive philistinism, co-optive misreading, and, ultimately, indispensable patronage — to the question of what to do with an art that no longer wanted to please.

Themes

A profusion, not a single revolution. Gay opens by describing how "alternatives for gratifying the bourgeoisie’s cultural appetites multiplied with exhilarating and alarming speed" after 1880, sweeping through the visual arts and eventually reaching the novel, which by 1914 had moved from Zola's "candid renderings of sexual and sordid moments" to mapping "stream of consciousness." Rather than a single coordinated movement, modernism appears as a crowd of competing isms — "Realist, Symbolist, Expressionist, Vorticist" — "crowding one another early in the twentieth century."

Even the canonical landmarks provoked riots, not applause. Gay anchors modernism's "undisputed classics" to 1907–1913 — Picasso's Demoiselles d'Avignon, Stravinsky's Rite of Spring, Duchamp's Nude Descending a Staircase, Gropius's Fagus factory — and notes that "All but the Gropius provoked vocal, at times venomous resistance," from the Rite of Spring's Parisian fracas to Schoenberg's routine hissing in Vienna's concert halls. That modernism clusters chronologically but resists a single formal definition is itself part of Gay's point.

Bourgeois anxiety about the arts predates modernism's canonical dates. Gay reaches back to Emerson's 1860 image of cultural "literalists" unsettled by wit, and to Frederic Harrison's 1880s assault on the "discordant hubbub of modern Picture Exhibitions" as the "root of decadence in art," to show that defensive unease was already in place decades before Picasso or Stravinsky, and that it was remarkably uniform across genre and country even as intensity varied — Emerson's plural "earthquakes" is Gay's own governing metaphor for the phenomenon.

Some modernists arrived by accident, not conviction. The architect C. F. Annesley Voysey, aligned with the Arts and Crafts reaction against ornament, insisted late in life that he had never striven for novelty and "had always believed in dignity and individualism." Gay generalizes from Voysey to Corot and Brahms: transitional figures who looked backward yet, in his phrase, ended up "paving the way to the future" — modernism conscripted reluctant soldiers as well as willing volunteers.

Modernism as a claim to authenticity, rooted in sexual and aggressive drives. Gay reads the movement's self-image as "a call to authenticity," "a crusade in behalf of sincerity" against an academicism that covered up eroticism as "socially acceptable pornography." He uses Liebermann's frankly sexual metaphor for Manet's sketches — "the artist celebrates his wedding night with his work" — alongside Schoenberg's and Kandinsky's claims to work as passive recipients of interior "dictation," as modernists' own testimony to art's unconscious, libidinal sources.

The pleasure wars barely touched the majority of the bourgeoisie. Gay separates "an active minority engrossed in the drama of high culture" from the majority who kept breeding pigeons, organizing Sunday picnics, and reading serialized family weeklies, shaking their heads at scandalous exhibition reports without engaging further. This is a recurring caution in Gay's volume: the bourgeoisie was "a deeply cleft class," not a uniform bloc arrayed on either side of the pleasure wars.

Co-option: taming modernism by misreading it. Gay's sharpest analytic move catalogues how conservative taste absorbed the shock of the new by domesticating it: Degas's "sweating, awkwardly posed dancers" reproduced as decorative prettiness, Brahms's First Symphony reassured by the cliché that it was "Beethoven’s Tenth," Winslow Homer's paintings "vulgarized into calendar art." Reproduction itself functions, in this account, as a technology for defanging: the image that unsettled in the gallery could be tamed in the parlor.

The section's closing paradox: bourgeois enemies were modernism's patrons. Gay undercuts any clean avant-garde-versus-philistine story with his final move: "it was the middle classes that nourished their sworn enemies," since "avant-gardes could not have made their way without massive bourgeois patronage." The same class that rioted at the Rite of Spring and vulgarized Degas also supplied the money, audiences, and institutional space modernism needed to survive — new money and old alike, since even "inconspicuous consumption could be the most conspicuous consumption of all."

Notable passages

The opening paragraph states the section's stakes directly: an accelerating "profusion" of cultural alternatives that leaves middle-class audiences facing a question of response that "became increasingly acute" (c1). The landmarks-and-riots paragraph dates modernism's undisputed classics and the "venomous resistance" most of them provoked, with the Rite of Spring premiere as the paradigm case (c2). The reflection on Voysey, Corot, and Brahms captures Gay's recurring point that modernism absorbed reluctant recruits who were, in retrospect, "paving the way to the future" without meaning to (c3). The "call to authenticity" passage states Gay's thesis about modernism's self-understanding as a crusade for sincerity against academic dishonesty (c4). The co-option passage catalogues, with unusual density of example, how popular reproduction drew the sting from Degas, Brahms, and Winslow Homer alike (c5). And the closing paragraph delivers Gay's structural payoff: whatever their professed contempt, the bourgeoisie were modernism's indispensable patrons (c6).

The chapter's governing image — a "profusion of alternatives," isms "crowding one another" for space — rhymes with the divergence-machine's logic of proliferation past any single legible order, though Gay's register is aesthetic taste and patronage rather than institutions or technology, and the isms he lists compete for standing inside one contested field rather than diverging into separate, non-communicating domains. The pleasure wars as Gay stages them here — academic authority losing its grip on legitimacy while no successor idiom yet commands consent, producing riots, hissing, and Emerson's "earthquakes" of unease before any new consensus stabilizes — resonate with a gramsci-gap in miniature: an aesthetic interregnum rather than a civilizational one, and a far shorter, more reversible one. And the closing paradox, that a "sworn enemies" relationship coexisted with total financial dependence, sits close to carey-intellectuals-and-masses: Carey reads modernism as the intelligentsia's defensive contempt for a new mass public, but Gay's bourgeoisie is simultaneously the object of that contempt and the source of the money and institutional standing modernism needed — a messier, more mutually implicated relationship than a clean opposition between an intelligentsia and the mass it despises.
