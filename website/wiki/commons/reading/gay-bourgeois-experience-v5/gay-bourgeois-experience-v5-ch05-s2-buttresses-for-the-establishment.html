Buttresses for the Establishment

Commons · Peter Gay · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Three wealthy bourgeois donors—the Amsterdam coal merchant Carel Fodor, the Hamburg-bound wool merchant Gustav Schwabe, and the Berlin banker Joachim Wagener—channel philanthropic energy equal to any avant-garde patron's into founding public museums that enshrine safe, academic, riskless taste rather than challenge it.

Gay profiles three wealthy bourgeois donors—the Amsterdam coal merchant Carel Joseph Fodor, the German-born, London-based wool merchant Gustav Christian Schwabe, and the Berlin banker Joachim Heinrich Wilhelm Wagener—who converted private fortunes into the founding collections of major public museums (the Fodor Museum in Amsterdam, the enlarged Hamburg Kunsthalle, and Berlin’s National-Galerie), showing that the acquisitive energy fueling bourgeois patronage elsewhere in the book could just as readily entrench safe, academic, “riskless” taste as the public standard.

Themes

Movers without being shakers. Gay opens by distinguishing energy from direction: these donors, he writes, were “summoning up as much energy as did the enthusiasts for artistic innovation” yet spent it to sustain “established styles” rather than challenge them—a deliberate counterweight, within the book’s larger argument, to the innovators and speculators profiled elsewhere.

The riskless collection as institutional genre. All three collections share a family resemblance: competent, finished, pleasing, and carefully purged of anything that could disturb a middlebrow public. Fodor bought French works that were “picturesque, entertaining, beautifully done,” with Ingres and Scheffer but “no Daumiers,” and the sole “Delacroix” in his cabinet was an obscure watercolorist who merely shared the name. Schwabe’s bequest to Hamburg was inspected on-site by a painter who reported “not one undraped figure in the whole collection,” a gallery of “paintings meant to entertain rather than shock.” Gay builds the Fodor and Schwabe paragraphs identically: an inventory of respectable names, closed by the pointed absence of anything genuinely disruptive.

Private fortune becomes public monument. Each case traces the same arc from personal accumulation to permanent civic fixture: Fodor’s testament turns three Amsterdam houses and a lifetime’s purchases into a public gallery; Schwabe’s gift forces the physical enlargement of the Hamburg Kunsthalle; Wagener’s 262 paintings, willed to the Prussian crown, become the founding core of the Berlin National-Galerie, its building not finished until fifteen years after his death and the founder’s own name eventually dropped “to encourage others to donate.” The political economy of art here is institutional as much as commercial—collecting as the mechanism that builds the very museums that will later canonize, or fail to canonize, new art.

Wagener’s psychology: exacting patron, abject subject. The longest and most personal portrait in the unit is Wagener’s, and Gay uses it to dramatize taste’s mixed, sometimes contradictory feelings. With painters he was possessive and impatient—waiting, he told Karl Friedrich Lessing, “like a child for the Christmas tree”—rejecting Hermann Anton Stilke’s commissioned sketches twice and returning a finished Alfred Rethel outright; with dealers he haggled over prices he called ruinous. Yet in his 1859 testament to the future King Wilhelm I, this same demanding businessman turns “stiff” and “self-abasing,” asking to “beg humbly for its gracious acceptance in the name of art” and leaving the collection’s fate to royal “All-highest discretion.” Gay reads the shift as class psychology: a man of “worldly success and bourgeois dignity” who had “not yet overcome his awe before majesty.”

Patronage entangled with nationalism. Wagener’s gift is accepted as evidence of “warm love of country” and “high-minded patriotism,” and Gay closes the anecdote flatly: “In an age of nationalism, art was rarely untouched by politics.” The unit’s geography reinforces the point: commercial wealth—coal in Amsterdam, wool traded from London, banking in Berlin—flows upward into state-adjacent or crown-sanctioned institutions rather than staying purely private or purely commercial.

Notable passages

The opening “movers were shakers” framing (c1) states the section’s organizing contrast before any of the three biographies begins. The Fodor paragraphs (c2, c3) show Gay’s method of close description building toward a verdict: Christus consolator’s content—a Christ freeing the enslaved and the suffering—is “politically progressive,” its academic finish “aesthetically conservative,” and the collection as a whole is defined as much by its exclusions (no Daumier, no real Delacroix) as by its acquisitions. The Schwabe passage (c4) turns an inspector’s incidental remark—no undraped figures—into the collection’s defining fact. The Wagener material (c5, c6) carries the unit’s Freudian undertow: the same man who dictated terms to painters becomes childlike before the Prussian crown, and the founding of the National-Galerie itself unfolds slowly and anonymously, the founder’s name deliberately erased.

This slow, uncoordinated assembly of Europe’s national galleries—one bequest here, one enlarged building there, spread across decades and three different cities—resonates with history-machine: no single “great man” founding gesture, but independent, unconnected acts of bourgeois philanthropy in Amsterdam, Hamburg, and Berlin that only in retrospect add up to the modern public art museum as an institution. The riskless taste these men enshrined—competent, unshocking, “meant to entertain rather than shock”—is the material and institutional backing for exactly the culture the modernist intelligentsia in carey-intellectuals-and-masses would later hold in contempt; the resonance runs in the opposite direction from Carey’s argument, though, since here it is the wealthy bourgeoisie itself, not a threatened intelligentsia, doing the choosing and the entrenching.
