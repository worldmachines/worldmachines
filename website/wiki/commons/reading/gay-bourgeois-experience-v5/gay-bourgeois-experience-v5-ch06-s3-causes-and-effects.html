Causes and Effects: Money, Cities, Faith, and Politics Behind Modernism's Rise

Commons · Peter Gay · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Bourgeois affluence, urbanization, quasi-religious fervor, and political polarization converge, atop a deeper substrate of ambivalent psychic adjustment, to explain why modernist challenges proliferated in the late nineteenth century.

Having sketched modernism's history, Gay turns to explanation: why did modernist challenges to bourgeois taste proliferate specifically in the closing decades of the nineteenth century? His answer is deliberately multi-causal — rising bourgeois disposable income, the concentration of artists and audiences in booming cities, the quasi-religious intensity some avant-gardes borrowed from mysticism, and the pull of avant-garde politics toward ideological extremes, epitomized by the Dreyfus affair's fracturing of French artistic circles — all resting on a Freudian substrate of ambivalence: modernity's pervasive, unavoidable pressure to adjust produced both exhilaration and anxiety at once.

Themes

Loose cash and shrinking families freed bourgeois income for culture. Gay grounds the "loose cash" thesis in hard numbers: French per capita income nearly tripling between 1825 and 1914, professional incomes like physicians' doubling, and a falling birthrate (31.4 to 18.8 per thousand) that left middle-class households more disposable income for "wall decorations, a small private library, or an occasional concert." He qualifies the claim carefully: this affluence was unevenly distributed, and poverty and status-anxiety persisted lower down and even within the middle class itself.

Modernism was structurally an urban phenomenon — inspiration and marketplace at once. Cities supplied both the avant-garde's subject matter (Impressionist railway stations and boulevards, Seurat's park promenaders, Kirchner's strollers) and its institutional infrastructure — theaters, dealers, publishers, cafés for the like-minded. Gay names the exceptions (Worpswede, Eugeni Güell's Parc Güell, Emil Nolde's Schleswig farmhouse) precisely to show how strong the urban rule otherwise was.

The "pleasure wars" took on real religious intensity, and not always metaphorically. Alongside Christian Science's and Theosophy's late-century vogue as pseudo-scientific substitute faiths, modernist painters and poets — Kandinsky, Mondrian, Yeats — turned to occult creeds to re-enchant a world they saw as fallen into "seductive materialism." Gay flags the irony pointedly: if Enlightenment secularism is modernity's defining trait, some of modernism's most fervent "inner necessity" votaries were, in that sense, not moderns at all.

Avant-garde politics gravitated to the extremes rather than the bourgeois center. Anarchism (Pissarro and his circle), Futurist "cultural politics" (Marinetti's Venice leaflet stunt), and general bourgeoisophobic contempt pushed many avant-gardes toward the political margins, left or right, rather than toward "boring middle-class parties" — a pattern contemporaries like G. K. Chesterton and Theodore Roosevelt noticed and worried over.

The Dreyfus affair fractured avant-garde solidarity along the fault line of prejudice. Gay uses individual case histories — Renoir's crude anti-Semitism, Degas's descent into virulent Jew-hatred despite decades of friendship with Jewish patrons and colleagues, Monet's enthusiastic Dreyfusard support of Zola, Pissarro's position as both Jew and anarchist — to show that even ostensibly apolitical modernist circles could not escape mass politics.

Not every artist was a zealot — market capitulation was its own available response. The architects Gilbert Scott and P. J. H. Cuypers each abandoned a preferred style (Gothic) for the one that would win the commission (neoclassical, then Renaissance), which Gay offers as counter-evidence to any picture of universal artistic principle — a contrast to true believers like Pugin.

Gay's own synthesis: modernism answered a general, ambivalent pressure to adjust. His closing move ties money, urbanization, ideology, and politics together as symptoms of "pervasive change" that produced simultaneous "elevated levels of gratification and anxiety" — a characteristically Freudian reading in which bourgeois flight to or from the new is one and the same adaptive response to a world in which, as Gay puts it, nothing seemed secure.

Notable passages

The economic passage (c1) is Gay's evidentiary backbone for the "loose cash" claim: precise per capita income figures and a collapsing birthrate, rather than mere assertion, establish money as modernism's necessary if not sufficient condition.

The urbanization passage (c2) states plainly that most modernists were "men and women of the city," with named rural exceptions doing double duty as proof of the rule.

The Theosophy passage (c3) carries the chapter's sharpest irony — Kandinsky and Yeats reaching for mystical "inner necessity" against Enlightenment secularism — and gestures at the same self-reflexive cultural anxiety that kulturkritik names: a critique of one's own culture that borrows the authority of the thing (here, science and religion both) it claims to transcend.

The politics passage (c4) and the Degas/Dreyfus passage (c5) together carry Gay's case that bourgeoisophobia was never purely aesthetic — it bled into anarchism, Futurist theater, and eventually into the ugliest prejudices of its own adherents, complicating any tidy story of avant-garde virtue against bourgeois vice. The Futurists' museum-burning rhetoric and their hunger for a large public that mostly ignored them resonates with carey-intellectuals-and-masses — Carey's thesis that modernism's stance toward a mass public was itself constitutive of the movement, though Gay's Futurists actively courted spectacle and publicity where Carey's modernists more often recoiled into deliberate difficulty; both agree the "public" being addressed or scorned was as much rhetorical construction as counted audience, which also brushes against the-mass-as-fiction.

The closing synthesis (c6) is Gay's own thesis sentence for the chapter, and the clearest statement in this unit of his Freudian method: mixed feelings, not one-sided hostility or enthusiasm, as the true bourgeois response to modernism. The world "in which nothing seemed secure" that Gay describes only loosely rhymes with the World Machines Project's gramsci-gap — Gay's is a diffuse cultural-psychological anxiety internal to a single era's Day, not the specific interregnum between a dying and an unborn world machine, but both name a historical moment when old certainties visibly fail to reassure without yet being replaced.
