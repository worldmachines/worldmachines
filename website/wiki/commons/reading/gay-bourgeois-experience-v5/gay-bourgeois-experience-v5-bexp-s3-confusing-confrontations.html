Confusing Confrontations: How “the Bourgeois” Became a Screen for the Fight Over Taste

Commons · Peter Gay · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Victorian taste battles crossed every expected line—rebels admired academics, secessionists borrowed styles from the past—and Gay argues that denouncing “the bourgeoisie” was really a screen for the avant-garde's fight over economic survival and access to legitimizing critics and museums, a case he makes by favoring Thomas Mann's ambivalent Tonio Kröger and Baudelaire's own defense of the middle class over Flaubert's one-note contempt.

Gay surveys the tangled, cross-cutting alliances of the Victorian taste wars—rebels admiring academics, secessionists borrowing styles from the deep past—to argue that denunciations of “the bourgeoisie” were less an accurate diagnosis than a rhetorical screen for the avant-garde's real fight for economic survival against the dealers, critics, and museum directors who controlled access to legitimacy; he then turns to Thomas Mann's Tonio Kröger and Baudelaire's own defense of the middle class to correct the “bourgeoisophobe” myth that Flaubert helped fix in place.

Themes

  • Victorian taste alliances crossed every expected line. Van Gogh professed “an absolutely unlimited admiration for Meissonier”; the Nazarenes revolutionized German painting by imitating early Italian art; the Pre-Raphaelites embraced a distant past style to secure art's future honesty and seriousness; Brahms, dismissed by Wagnerites as out of date, was heard by his first listeners as “difficult, unduly modernist”. No stable mapping from stylistic radicalism to political radicalism survives contact with the record.

  • Denouncing “the bourgeoisie” functioned as a screen for a fight that was really about access and survival. Gay's central move: avant-garde manifestos aimed their fire at bourgeois philistinism, but denunciations of the bourgeoisie actually functioned as a screen concealing the anti-academics’ true target. Rebels needed a place among the dealers, bureaucrats, critics, and museum directors who legitimized art and controlled the paying public's taste — the issue was one of aesthetic conscience, but of economic survival no less.

  • Canons were flexible, not fixed, so the avant-garde's task was arduous but not hopeless. Official Salons eventually absorbed Corot's landscapes, Couture's proto-Impressionist brushwork, and the Barbizon painters (Théodore Rousseau's canvas fetched 35,000 francs six years after his death). Léonce Bénédite's 1897 attempt to define the Impressionists positively could only land on negative, reactive qualities — autonomy, impatience, resistance to official taste — which is why the Impressionists called their own shows Salons of the Independents.

  • Taste was radically geographic. The same artists were received completely differently city to city: Beethoven's staggered conquest of the German-speaking countries, then England, then a reluctant France; Constable prized more by Delacroix than by the Royal Academy; Courbet better received in Munich than Paris; Mackintosh embraced by the Vienna Secession over his native Glasgow. Some tastes (German scatological humor, a preoccupation with profundity and death) simply didn't export across borders.

  • Gay locates the real diagnosis of bourgeois/avant-garde relations in Thomas Mann, not Flaubert. Tonio Kröger's self-description — “What resulted was this: a bourgeois who got lost in art, a bohemian homesick for his respectable upbringing, an artist with a bad conscience.” — models ambivalence rather than contempt, and Gay treats this 1903 novella as more useful to the historian of Victorian culture than Flaubert's single-minded acrimony. This is Gay's Freudian reading of taste in miniature: mixed feelings, not clean hostility, as the real psychological condition. It resonates with carey-intellectuals-and-masses — Carey also traces modernist contempt for a mass bourgeois public to intelligentsia self-defense, but where Carey's intellectuals hold that contempt as settled doctrine, Gay's Kröger (and Gay's own argument) insists the contempt was never uncomplicated even among the bourgeoisophobes themselves; the resonance is real but the two accounts pull in partly opposite directions.

  • Flaubert is held responsible for a durable myth, and Gay convicts him by his own admired witness. Baudelaire's Salon of 1845 called the epithet “the bourgeois” “impertinent” and said it should be erased from the dictionary of criticism, “especially since there are so many bourgeois among artists.” — a critique of the critics' own vocabulary that fits kulturkritik's self-reflexive mode. His 1846 Salon went further, dedicating itself to the bourgeoisie and addressing it directly: “you have founded collections, museums, galleries.” and made once-monopolized treasures “you have made accessible to the masses.” Gay's payoff line — the bourgeois as a possible backer rather than the sworn adversary of unconventional, even modernist, taste — is the unit's thesis. It answers the “screen” identified above directly: the invented, denounced “bourgeois” enemy resembles the kind of rhetorically constructed category the-mass-as-fiction describes, though the sleight of hand runs the other way here — not inventing a contemptible mass, but scapegoating a class that, on Baudelaire's own evidence, actually built the museums.

Notable passages

  • The opening paragraph on crossed battle lines (Van Gogh/Meissonier, the Nazarenes, the Pre-Raphaelites, Wagner/Brahms) sets up the unit's governing paradox: no reliable map from stylistic radicalism to political radicalism.

  • The “screen concealing the anti-academics' true target” passage is Gay's clearest statement that the fight over “bourgeois taste” was really a fight over legitimizing institutions and economic survival.

  • The geography-of-taste passage (Beethoven, Constable/Delacroix, Courbet in Munich, Mackintosh in Vienna) is Gay's evidentiary base for treating taste as contingent on place rather than fixed by aesthetic law.

  • Tonio Kröger's self-diagnosis is the passage Gay explicitly prefers to Flaubert as a historical document of the bourgeois/artist relationship.

  • Baudelaire's Salon writings — both the 1845 attack on the word “bourgeois” and the 1846 dedication — are the best direct evidence Gay marshals for reversing the received wisdom that the bourgeoisie was simply the enemy of taste, and the passage the unit's own thesis (“the bourgeois as a possible backer rather than the sworn adversary”) rests on.
