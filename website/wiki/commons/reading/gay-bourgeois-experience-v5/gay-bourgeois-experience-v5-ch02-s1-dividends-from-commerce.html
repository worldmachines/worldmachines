Dividends from Commerce

Commons · Peter Gay · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Manchester's Victorian merchants and manufacturers — many of them German-Jewish immigrants like Charles Hallé's patrons — built the city's musical and civic culture as private, profit-seeking or philanthropic ventures independent of court, state, or aristocratic sponsorship, until early-twentieth-century bourgeoisophobes began deriding the resulting consensus as philistine.

Opening Chapter Two, Gay sets out his "geography of taste" thesis — that the nineteenth-century bourgeoisie was one class with a dozen local faces — and then spends this section proving it through Manchester: a commercial and industrial city whose merchants and manufacturers, without court, aristocracy, or state to lean on, built an entire apparatus of high culture (the Hallé orchestra, a conservatory, a university, libraries, an art museum) out of their own money and initiative, turning trade into taste even as critics inside and outside the city kept insisting commerce and culture were incompatible.

Themes

Taste has a geography, and it is not accidental. Gay opens the chapter insisting that Parisian, Berlin, Manchester, and Munich bourgeois cultivated art differently not by whim but because "taste was embedded in cultural habits, political institutions, and attitudes to authority that left their imprint." Manchester — commercial, self-governing, without resident aristocracy or court — is his first case, and its contrast case, named but deferred here, is Munich, a city of court and state patronage ("this was not the kind of speech they were giving in Munich").

Commerce paid taste's way in directly, through patronage. Charles Hallé's move from revolution-wracked Paris to Manchester in 1848 was engineered by Hermann Leo and a circle of "cultured capitalists," many of them assimilated German-Jewish manufacturers (the Schwabes, later Henry Simon and Gustav Behrens), who wanted a professional musician to "stir the dormant taste for the art." The orchestra Hallé eventually built was not civic charity handed down from above; it was demanded and financed by a self-conscious merchant elite acting on its own behalf — the literal "dividends from commerce" of the section title.

High culture was run as a business, not dispensed as philanthropy. Gay is emphatic that the Hallé orchestra was a private, profit-seeking venture mounted at its owner's "own risk and peril,' not a charity: after a token profit in its first season it was clearing over £2,000 a year by 1866. Ticket prices were graduated (2s. 6d. reserved, a shilling unreserved) so that "high culture in Manchester was slouching toward democracy" without ceasing to be a paying concern — Gay's political economy of art in miniature: prices, seasons, subscriptions, profit and access all negotiated at once.

Manchester's cultural institutions assembled themselves, piecemeal and privately, with no central design. The Portico Library, the Athenaeum, the Manchester Statistical Society, Owens College (later Victoria University), the John Rylands Library, the Royal Manchester College of Music, the Whitworth Art Gallery — each arose from an individual merchant's legacy or a committee of subscribers, never from crown or government initiative. Gay quotes Tocqueville's 1835 verdict that Manchester's "exterior appearance... attests the individual powers of man, nothing the directing power of society," and Oliver Elton's later gloss, "private enterprise and patriotism," as the closest thing the city had to a founding creed.

Manchester's bourgeois told themselves a grand historical story to dignify trade. At the Rylands Library's opening, the Reverend Fairbairn linked Manchester to Alexandria, Florence, and Venice — cities "whose kings were merchants, and whose merchants were kings" — to argue that commerce, not court or church, had always been culture's real patron. This is the geography-of-taste thesis turned into bourgeois self-mythology: legitimating money-making by annexing it to a lineage of civilization-building.

Art lagged behind music, and exposed the limits of bourgeois taste-making. Manchester's early picture collectors were repeatedly gulled by "Picture Jockeys" selling fake Old Masters; the city's museum (the Whitworth, founded only in 1889 after Joseph Whitworth's death) suffered from "crippling uncertainty of touch" and amateurish governance. Where music had Hallé as a single expert intermediary training taste over decades, art collecting had no such figure, and the results were correspondingly shakier — a caution against reading Gay's Manchester as an uncomplicated success story.

The bourgeoisophobe turn arrives at the end, dated precisely. In 1905 the critic Gerald Cumberland attacked the Hallé's beloved standbys — the Messiah, Elijah — as ossified: "They are no longer musical works; they have become religious services," tainted by an "authentic spirit of Philistinism." Gay marks this as the moment the fifty-year consensus Hallé had built started to look, to a new generation, like exactly the philistinism the bourgeoisophobes (Flaubert's heirs) loved to mock — even though Gay's own account up to that point reads as a rebuttal of the philistine stereotype.

Notable passages

The chapter's opening paragraph is Gay's thesis statement for the whole geography-of-taste argument, insisting that a shared bourgeois "intellectual and emotional style" nonetheless diverged sharply by city because it was "embedded in cultural habits, political institutions, and attitudes to authority." The Leo–Hallé recruitment passage supplies the section's title in miniature: a merchant patron courting an artist with flattery and material inducement, framed by Gay as commerce actively buying its way into taste rather than merely tolerating it. The passage on the Hallé orchestra's finances is Gay's clearest statement of political economy: profit and pedagogy pursued as one project, with ticket pricing calibrated to widen access without erasing the enterprise's commercial nature. Fairbairn's Rylands Library oration, comparing Manchester to Alexandria, Florence, and Venice, is the best evidence Gay marshals for bourgeois self-understanding — trade dignified as civilization's oldest patron. The Tocqueville/Elton passage crystallizes how these institutions actually got built: independently, privately, without a directing authority, each philanthropic act or subscription drive accreting into an institutional landscape nobody planned as a whole — a pattern that resonates with the history-machine idea of a paradigm assembling silently from distributed components until it becomes visible, though here the "paradigm" is a city's cultural infrastructure rather than a civilizational one. Finally, the Cumberland passage is Gay's hinge into the bourgeoisophobe theme this volume is built around: a consensus that functioned as living musical culture curdling, within two generations, into rote "tradition — almost a superstition" that no longer needed to be experienced, only maintained — an instance of institutions passing from functional use into ritual husk that also echoes the decay-sequence (functional practice hollowing into performative and ornamental repetition), and a close cousin of carey-intellectuals-and-masses's argument that literary intelligentsia turned contempt for a newly enfranchised public into a badge of taste, though Cumberland's target is an established bourgeois consensus rather than a newly literate mass readership.
