From Zola to Wilde: The Bourgeoisie's Ambiguous Embrace of the Avant-Garde

Commons · Peter Gay · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay reads the scandals over Manet's Olympia and Oscar Wilde's trials as evidence that the Victorian bourgeoisie's relationship to the avant-garde was fractured and self-contradictory rather than the simple philistine hostility the bourgeoisophobe myth assumes.

Gay pairs two flashpoints in the Victorian pleasure wars — the critical furor over Manet's Olympia (1865) and the trials and imprisonment of Oscar Wilde (1895) — to argue, in a claim he flags outright as "the central issue of this book," that the bourgeoisie's relationship to the avant-garde was never the simple hostility the bourgeoisophobe myth assumes. Academic painters like Cabanel and Bouguereau were pilloried by modernist critics as purveyors of "dead art," yet their most famous canvases were bought not by bourgeois patrons but by Emperor Napoleon III; Manet's own defenders, Ravenel and Zola, could not avoid classing him as a rebellious bourgeois rather than an outsider; and when Wilde was destroyed by the "ghoulish public" his aestheticism had scorned, his most consequential rescuers were respectable, often Jewish, bourgeois patrons rather than fellow avant-gardists.

Themes

Academic "dead art" was cynically formulaic — but its patrons were imperial, not bourgeois. Critics sympathetic to Impressionism accused Cabanel, Bouguereau, and Gérôme of producing technically flawless, dead art to formula for "the rich and powerful." Zola and Huysmans savaged Cabanel's Birth of Venus for its "white and pink almond paste" prettiness and its lack of "muscles, no nerves, no blood." Gay notes pointedly that the reception of such paintings reflected "not... bourgeois but... imperial taste": Napoleon III bought them both, complicating any story that simply pins bad taste on "the bourgeois."

Victorian critical judgment was chronically incoherent, not monolithic. Baudelaire, "that astute reader of contemporary trends," inexplicably preferred the minor illustrator Constantin Guys to his own friend Manet; Shaw dismissed Schubert as "brainless" and Brahms as a mere "sentimental voluptuary"; Henry James, the novelist most attuned to painting as subject matter, was privately indifferent to Manet, Renoir, Monet, Cézanne, van Gogh, and Gauguin, preferring the safely fashionable John Singer Sargent; even Flaubert reversed his own verdict on Stendhal within seven years. Gay marshals this incoherence against any tidy map of bourgeois-versus-avant-garde taste.

The "doctrine of distance" made nudity respectable by mythologizing it — and Olympia broke the rule. Bourgeois viewers could look at erotically charged nude bodies without embarrassment only if the work was properly "dressed" in a title like "Eve," "Spring," or, as with Hiram Powers's wildly popular Greek Slave, a narrative of victimhood safely displaced onto ancient or exotic others. Manet's Olympia violated this convention by presenting a recognizable contemporary Parisienne — modeled by Victorine Meurent — as a self-possessed, unmistakably priced courtesan rather than an allegorical Venus, converting comfortable looking into scandal.

Manet's own defenders still called him bourgeois, undercutting a clean bourgeois/avant-garde opposition. Ravenel praised Olympia as "an armed insurrection in the camp of the bourgeois" and "a splendid antibourgeois manifesto"; Zola read it as pure formalist color-and-mass, a Mondrian avant la lettre. Yet Gay's own verdict on both defenses is that they were "both satisfactory and unsatisfactory": Zola himself "recognized Manet to be a bourgeois, if a rebellious one," disturbing his fellow bourgeois from the inside rather than attacking from outside it — the book's namesake ambiguity in miniature.

Wilde's "Critic as Artist" elevated criticism over creation and beauty over ethics. Wilde argued the line between creative and critical faculty was "entirely arbitrary," that art answers only to beauty and not to ethics or truth, and that criticism "demands infinitely more cultivation than creation." This is itself a turn toward kulturkritik: a self-reflexive move that makes the act of critique, rather than any object criticized, the site of value. The doctrine was not new in itself, Gay notes, but became explosive once fused, at trial, with the public exposure of Wilde's sexuality — two "usually distinct" domains collapsing into one scandal.

The bourgeois public's response to Wilde's fall was itself fractured, not univocal. Newspapers ranged from the gloating St. James Gazette and Westminster Gazette (calling for "a dash of wholesome bigotry") to the neutral Times and Pall Mall Gazette to the openly sympathetic Reynolds's News. Friends split similarly — Shaw declined to sign a clemency petition out of self-protection, while Yeats urged Wilde to stand and fight his "cant-ridden culture." Strikingly, several of Wilde's most consequential supporters — Adela Schuster, an anonymous yachtsman who offered his boat for Wilde's escape — were Jewish, which Gay reads as outsiders recognizing another outsider's plight. This resonates with carey-intellectuals-and-masses and the-mass-as-fiction: like Carey, Gay dissolves a supposedly monolithic public ("the bourgeoisie," standing in for Carey's "the mass") into a scatter of individual, often contradictory, responses. But the resonance is partial and runs the other way: Carey's charge is intelligentsia contempt for an invented mass, whereas Wilde's own dismissal of "the views of ordinary individuals" is the intelligentsia gesture Carey describes, and Gay immediately surrounds it with ordinary bourgeois people who defended Wilde anyway — the fiction being dismantled here is "the bourgeois," not "the mass."

Notable passages

The opening indictment of academic "dead art" (c1) sets up Gay's irony: the reception of Cabanel and Bouguereau reflected imperial rather than bourgeois patronage, undercutting the bourgeoisophobe caricature before the chapter even reaches Manet.

The "doctrine of distance" passage (c2) is the conceptual key to why Olympia scandalized: it names the convention Olympia broke, explaining the mechanism by which mythological titles like "Eve" or the Greek Slave made nude-viewing respectable.

Ravenel's declaration that Olympia was "an armed insurrection in the camp of the bourgeois" (c3) is flagged by Gay himself as touching "the central issue of this book" — the ambiguous bourgeois relation to the avant-garde — making it the unit's thesis statement in miniature.

Gay's own analytic verdict on Zola's formalist rescue of Olympia (c4) is the clearest instance of Gay's authorial voice in the unit: he credits Zola's reading as generous but insists it still classes Manet as an insider-bourgeois rather than an outside attacker, generalizing to the "fruitful incoherence" of Victorian taste and its critics.

Wilde's "Critic as Artist" passage (c5) states the aestheticist doctrine — art answerable only to beauty, not ethics — that made his fall, once his sexuality became public, read as a single scandal rather than two.

The passage on Wilde's supporters (c6) — heavily, strikingly Jewish — closes the unit on Gay's recurring point that "the bourgeois" was never a single verdict-giving body, even in extremis.
