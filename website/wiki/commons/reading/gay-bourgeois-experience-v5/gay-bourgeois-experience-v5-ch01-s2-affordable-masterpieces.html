Affordable Masterpieces: Pricing High Culture for the Bourgeois Market

Commons · Peter Gay · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
A tiered market in reproductions, copies, and differentially priced tickets let bourgeois families across the income scale buy partial access to ‘masterpieces,’ while Gay insists that how much a family could spend on culture tracked its class position far more reliably than its actual taste.

Gay surveys the machinery — household budgets, reproduction technologies, a market in copies, and deliberately tiered ticket prices — that let bourgeois of every income band buy some purchase on “high culture,” and argues throughout that the amount of money a family could spend on art tracked its place in the class hierarchy far more reliably than it tracked the sophistication of its taste.

Themes

  • A culture budget is a small fraction of a small budget. Gay opens with the 1889 accounts of a Berlin civil servant's family: theater, concerts, and reading aloud together cost barely one percent of the father's salary. The point is not stinginess but structure — “So much money had to be spent on so much else” — and it sets the economic floor under everything that follows.

  • Money is not taste. Gay states his governing thesis directly: a manufacturer could spend far more than his bookkeeper on culture, “but this says little about what the manufacturer or the attorney would do with the cash.” Petty and even lower bourgeois could have “a genuine feeling for what they saw, heard, or read” despite having no money at all — a direct rebuttal of the flattened bourgeois-as-philistine caricature that (per the source-specific cautions for this volume) later historians inherited from writers like Flaubert.

  • Reproduction technology as democratizer and threat. Lithography, engraving, photography, and photogravure built an industry that put “masterpieces” within reach of ordinary households, and just as reliably provoked alarm from self-appointed guardians of taste. Barbey d'Aurevilly's attack on photography as a “democracy of the portrait” is Gay's sharpest instance: snobbish, but not wrong that reproduction “cheapened” culture in more than the price sense.

  • Copying old masters: training ground and battlefield. Copying in the Louvre was simultaneously an academic training exercise (Manet, Courbet, Berthe Morisot, Cézanne), a paid commission for private collectors like Thiers and Count Schack, and a target of bourgeoisophobe scorn: Duranty and Pissarro both fantasized, in print, about burning the Louvre's “catacomb” of copied masters to the ground.

  • Differential pricing as a technology of inclusion — and stratification. Munich's opera and concert houses built explicit price ladders for Tristan und Isolde, the Flying Dutchman, and the new Philharmonic, scaled down to students and “poverty-stricken Wagnerites” by name. This is Gay's political economy of art at its most literal: institutions engineering access without dissolving the class distinctions the price bands encode. It half-resonates with legibility — a market rendering its public legible by income and profession, sortable into visible strata — though this is commercial price discrimination rather than the state's cadastral or statistical legibility that concept usually names.

  • The Mittelstand and its pedagogues. The petty bourgeoisie's cultural aspirations were genuinely throttled by time and money, and its favored family weeklies (Gartenlaube, Daheim) channeled taste toward sentimental anecdote and nationalist self-congratulation — crowing that Germany had “struck like a bomb” at the 1878 Paris World's Fair. The pedagogues' anxious mission to police “the level of German culture” is itself a case of kulturkritik, cultural critique that is as much about self-definition against a feared mass audience as about the art it claims to judge.

  • Bourgeoisophobe contempt for the “gros public.” Berlioz's and Joachim's private scorn for a “public en gros” that wanted only “relaxing,” “colorless” music, free of “unexpected effects,” rehearses — decades before modernism proper — the intelligentsia's defensive contempt for a mass audience that carey-intellectuals-and-masses describes; the parallel is not exact, since Gay's targets are still working within bourgeois institutions (the opera house, the salon) rather than fleeing them into an avant-garde counter-culture. “The public en gros” is also a case of the-mass-as-fiction: a homogenizing category that erases the earnest, untrained-but-authentic responses (Behncke's honeymoon diary, elsewhere in this unit) that Gay has just spent pages insisting were real.

Notable passages

The 1889 Berlin family budget is the unit's anchor fact: theater, concerts, and a once-a-year party added up to barely more than one percent of a comfortable civil servant's salary, and Gay uses it to insist that “So tight a budget left very little, if anything, for art” well before any question of taste arises.

Gay's explicit statement that “it would be naive to equate money with taste” is the interpretive hinge of the whole passage: it reframes everything else — cheap reproductions, copying, tiered tickets — as evidence about access rather than evidence about discernment.

Barbey d'Aurevilly's 1867 broadside against photography as “that democracy of the portrait, that equality before the object, brutal and mendacious” captures the elite anxiety that cheap reproduction was corroding taste, which Gay partly credits (“photography 'cheapened' culture”) even while calling the sentiment snobbish.

Duranty's mock confession that he would “set fire” to the Louvre's “catacomb,” echoed by Pissarro's wish to burn the “necropolises of art,” dramatizes the bourgeoisophobe wing turning against the very copying practice that trained the Impressionists — reverence and revolt sharing the same institution.

The Munich ticket ladder — 15 gulden boxes for the Tristan premiere against a 24-kronen “Galerie” seat, and a 1-mark ceiling at the new Philharmonic — is Gay's most concrete demonstration of the political economy of art extending downward by design, city by city, seat by seat.

Berlioz's report of Boieldieu's advice to write for the “gros public” that wants music demanding “neither major talents nor close attention,” and Joachim's private complaint to Brahms about a public “addicted to entertainment” with “crude expectations,” together preview the modernist-versus-middlebrow rift that organizes the rest of the volume.
