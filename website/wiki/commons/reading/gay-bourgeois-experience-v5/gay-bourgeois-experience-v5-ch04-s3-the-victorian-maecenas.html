The Victorian Maecenas

Commons · Peter Gay · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Peter Gay traces how Victorian-era bourgeois patronage of individual artists shaded into a broader pattern among wealthy collectors of an "education to modernism," moving from safe academic taste toward the avant-garde, even as other equally rich collectors never made that conversion.

Peter Gay traces how the old-fashioned figure of the Maecenas — the patron who discovers and fosters raw genius — survived into the bourgeois Victorian decades even as dealers rose as the dominant culture brokers, and shows how patronage shaded, almost imperceptibly, into the parallel bourgeois practice of collecting: private buyers who began as customers of academic or Old Master painting and ended, in a recurring "education to modernism," as backers and even co-creators of the avant-garde.

Themes

Patronage persisted, but on altered terms. Aristocratic models (Sir George Beaumont, Count Schack) found "bourgeois imitators" — Fürstenberg and Larsson, Reed and the American landscapists, von Meck and Tchaikovsky, Fiedler and Marées, Walters and Barye, Koehler and Marc — but Gay stresses that "in the modern manner" the clear hierarchy of older patronage gave way to something more ambiguous: donor and recipient bound by friendship and correspondence rather than clean obligation, with "the respective roles of donor and recipient... no longer so clear-cut as they had been in past centuries."

The psychology of dependency is Gay's real subject. He reads patron-artist bonds through mixed feelings rather than economics: Constable's swing between relief at having "got the public into his hands" and dread that "the publick is hard, cruel and unrelenting"; Fiedler's rueful aphorism that a relationship like his with Marées is, like all of life, "simultaneously a tragedy and a comedy"; the "romantic notion that a genius must be fondly indulged" straining against real limits until Fiedler's patience broke. Artists, Gay notes, were "often ambivalent about such alliances, glad to be free of them yet eager for the security they promised" — a Freudian ambivalence that runs through the whole chapter.

Patronage and collecting doubled as self-construction. Abraham Willet's Amsterdam mansion, crammed with the Willet coat of arms and a sequence of self-portraits (including one in seventeenth-century militia costume), and Alfred Bruyas's roughly thirty images of himself (one as the suffering Christ), show the "disinterested" sponsor's narcissism breaking through. Courbet's The Meeting, commissioned by Bruyas, restages the patron-artist encounter so that the painter, not the patron, comes off as the superior figure — a reading a hostile cartoonist pushed further by putting Bruyas on his knees before Courbet.

Collectors underwent a recurring "education to modernism." Havemeyer, Camondo, Faure, Perier, the reconverted Koehler, and the Fischers all follow the same arc: safe Old Masters, Barbizon landscapes, or academic salon painting first, then a conversion — sudden or gradual — toward Impressionism, Postimpressionism, and beyond. Gay's fullest case is Sergei Shchukin, whose commissions of Dance and Music from Matisse, and his letters suggesting themes for future canvases, complete the movement from buyer to sponsor: "The customer had become a patron."

Louisine Havemeyer is the more venturesome half of her marriage. She drives the couple's three successive Courbet nude purchases, culminating in La Femme au perroquet, which she defends in her memoir as art that must be kept in America "for the future generations." Her husband holds nominal authority and has to be "begged" and gently maneuvered — Gay's aside on gendered authority inside a bourgeois collecting partnership that was, on taste, functionally egalitarian.

Taste never converged on modernism. Set against the conversion narratives are collectors who never moved: Walters paying record prices for Meissonier and Gérôme deep into the 1880s, his adviser Lucas satisfied with "a bland menu" of minor French academics, Frick and Gardner ranging eclectically without committing to the avant-garde, Mrs. Potter Palmer the rare purist for Impressionism alone. Gay's closing judgment refuses a single verdict: bourgeois collectors "acted as agents of cultural change," and "if, as the avant-garde charged, they all too often followed the fashion, there were times when they made it."

Notable passages

The Constable/von Meck passage (c1) states the artist's-eye ambivalence toward patronage directly, weighing the "caprice of an exigent and interfering backer" against the impersonal "callousness of the market" — neither escape is clean, which is Gay's point about mixed feelings rather than a case for either system.

The Fiedler–Marées breakup (c2) is the chapter's sharpest statement of patronage's psychological cost: an "openhanded, disinterested, and patient" backer undone by a client whose genius came wrapped in paranoia and unappeasable grievance, ending in Fiedler's own diagnosis of the relationship as tragicomic.

The Willet passage (c3) is Gay's clearest case of patron/collector narcissism: a private mansion turned into a shrine to its owner's coats of arms and costumed self-portraits, offered as evidence that "disinterested" bourgeois cultural sponsorship regularly carried "narcissistic dividends."

Louisine Havemeyer's memoir passage on La Femme au perroquet (c4) captures both the couple's risk-taking and its gendered mechanics — her plea to "keep it in America... for the future generations" and her husband's ready consent once persuaded, showing a bourgeois household actively legislating taste rather than passively receiving it.

The Shchukin/Matisse passage (c5) delivers the chapter's thesis in miniature — "the customer had become a patron" — as commissioned canvases and a warm personal letter turn a wealthy purchaser into a genuine Maecenas of living modernist art.

The closing passage (c6) is Gay's own summary judgment on the whole chapter's evidence, refusing to cast bourgeois collectors as either simple philistines or simple heroes of the avant-garde: they "acted as agents of cultural change" who sometimes followed fashion and sometimes made it.

This chapter's positive image of bourgeois businessmen actively bankrolling and even co-authoring modernist art (Koehler funding Marc and Kandinsky's Blue Rider almanac, Shchukin commissioning Matisse) sits in productive tension with carey-intellectuals-and-masses: where Carey reads modernism as the literary intelligentsia's defensive contempt for a philistine mass public, Gay's Maecenases show the same despised bourgeois class as modernism's patrons and midwives, not only its target — a complication of, rather than a match for, Carey's dichotomy.
