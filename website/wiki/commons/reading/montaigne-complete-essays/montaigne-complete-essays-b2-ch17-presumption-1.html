Of Presumption (opening section): self-estimation, style, and the body

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Opening third of Montaigne's 'Of Presumption': he defines presumption as over-valuing oneself and undervaluing others, then reports himself as the opposite case (he undervalues what is his own, distrusts his works, style and language), and turns to the body, arguing soul and body must be kept united and that stature and beauty matter, before noting that he is past forty and slipping away from himself.

This slice is the first part of the essay, not the whole of it. Montaigne begins by defining a second kind of glory, "having too good an opinion of our own worth", compares it to the distorting judgment of lovers, and immediately hedges: a man should not think himself less than he is, and if he is Caesar he may boldly think himself the greatest captain in the world. He then licenses self-portrayal for people who have not been placed in public view (citing Lucilius, who confided his life to his books), and confesses a childhood bearing that seemed proud. From there the essay becomes a catalogue of self-assessments. He claims the opposite fault, an inclination to undervalue what is his own and prize what is foreign; he welcomes philosophy that humbles man; he disowns satisfaction with his poetry and prose, describes his rough, irregular style and provincial French; and then argues, via the question of stature and beauty, that body and soul must be treated as one. The slice ends with his remark, at past forty, that he is already a 'half-being'.

Themes

Presumption as a defect of judgment, not of self-knowledge. Montaigne is careful to say he does not recommend self-contempt: "the judgment ought in all things to maintain its rights". The fault is the flattering distortion, which he likens to the way love lends graces to its object. Presumption has two parts, overvaluing ourselves and undervaluing others; this slice mostly examines the first through his own case. Note the attribution: the Lucilius lines and the poets' tags are quoted authority, while the self-report ("I") is Montaigne's own.

Ceremony versus substance. An early aside says "We are nothing but ceremony": we obey convention about words while ignoring reason about deeds, and ceremony permits neither self-praise nor self-blame. This is a small but genuine instance of custom as invisible machinery governing what can be said, and it is what he is pushing against by speaking of himself. It resonates with habit-and-identity, though here the point is manners and speech rather than belief.

Humility as an epistemic stance. He prefers the ancient opinions that "most contemn and undervalue us", and calls man's good opinion of himself "the nursing mother of all the most false opinions, both public and private". Astronomers who cannot explain how the body they move moves are, he says, not to be trusted about the Nile. He cites Scripture (curiosity as a scourge) in support. This is a sceptical, anti-pedantic move, and it anticipates the Apology; note he presents it as his own preference among antiquity's opinions, not as a settled doctrine.

Self-report as method. The essay is explicitly a self-study: he finds no part of his mind with which he is satisfied, claims to be 'sprinkled, but not dyed' with vanity, and says the approbation of others does not raise his opinion of himself. Some of this is plainly a rhetorical pose of modesty, which he half-admits by remarking that his judgment is 'tender and nice' about himself. This is the territory of self-construction-through-writing and personal-essay-as-form, though he is describing how he judges himself rather than the writing process as a technique.

Writing, style, and language. He says his works disgust him on rereading, that he has an idea of a better form he cannot catch, that great ancients astound him, and that his own style is "an inform and irregular way of speaking, a popular jargon, a proceeding without definition, division, conclusion". He is better at speaking than writing because gesture and voice carry words. He knows his own fault ("Brevis esse laboro, Obscurus fio") and defends the occasional low manner by pointing to Xenophon and Plato. He also says his French carries the barbarism of Perigord and admires blunt Gascon, and that Latin, his mother tongue, has been lost through disuse.

Body and soul as one. The unit argues, against those who "disunite and separate our two principal parts", that the soul should "espouse and be a husband" to the body. He draws on Christian doctrine (the whole man rewarded or punished) and on the Peripatetics, and criticises other sects for losing sight of 'Man' and 'Nature'. Beauty is then treated as the first human distinction, citing a verse about land being divided by looks and strength. He then describes his own below-average height as a handicap for a man in command, with Marius, Aristotle, Plato and the anecdote of Philopoemen as support (Aristotle and Plato are reported opinions, not Montaigne's own).

Ageing and the self that slips away. The closing lines are a short, quiet turn: past forty, he is 'engaged in the avenues of old age', what remains 'will be but a half-being, and no more me', and he steals away from himself every day.

Notable passages

Against the flattering self-image, and for humbling philosophy (c1). Montaigne's report of his own tendency to undervalue what he owns, and to embrace the opinions that 'most contemn and undervalue us', leads to the claim that overestimation of man breeds false opinion and to the Mercury-epicycle and Nile jibe. Hedge: he says he cannot root out the error, only attempt to correct it.

His own estimate (c2). He says he could not think meanly of himself more than he already does, that he counts himself one of the common sort, and that his only value is knowing his own value; vanity, if any, is 'sprinkled, but not dyed'.

Dissatisfaction with his own works (c3). The reread-and-be-ashamed passage, with its ideal 'disturbed image' he cannot reach, and his astonishment at the ancients.

Style and his own manner of speaking (c4). A self-description of his rough, extreme, unpolished manner, with the qualification that handling things superficially and pushing them home are both skills.

Body and soul united (c5). The longest passage of argument in the slice, and the one in which Montaigne moves from self-description to a general claim about human nature, with Christians and Peripatetics as support.

'No more me' (c6). His brief account of health and age, ending with the lines on stealing away from himself.
