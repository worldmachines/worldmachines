Montaigne on the stone, inherited traits, and the distrust of physic (essay XXXVII, first part)

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Opening third of Montaigne's essay on heredity: he describes his piecemeal, uncorrected way of writing, his bout with the stone and how he meets pain and death, then turns the inherited disease and his family's aversion to physic into a sustained distrust of medicine as a human invention set against nature's order.

This opening slice of the essay (it begins at the head of the chapter and breaks off mid-argument, before Montaigne's later turns) starts with a note on how the book is composed, then moves to the kidney stone he has acquired in the years of writing. He reports how he lives with pain, argues that philosophy should not demand a composed countenance in suffering, and then wonders how a drop of seed can carry a father's form, thoughts and disease. His own stone, he supposes, comes from his father, and so does an "hereditary" antipathy to doctors. The rest of the slice is a case against physic: from family longevity, from experience of people who take much physic, from nations that lived without it, and finally from a view of nature's "general order" that ought to be followed rather than fought.

Themes

The book as a record of change, not a revised system. Montaigne says he writes only in idle hours and at home, in interrupted stretches, and that he never corrects "first by any second conceptions", altering at most a word of phrasing. His aim is "to represent the progress of my humours", so that each piece is seen "as it came from the forge". He wishes he had begun sooner to track "my mutations". This is the essay as a technology of self-study (see personal-essay-as-form and self-construction-through-writing); note it is Montaigne's own self-report about method, and it sits next to a small anecdote about a servant-transcriber who steals pieces of the manuscript, which Montaigne treats lightly.

Living with the stone: preparation versus event. Montaigne reports eighteen months of the disease, five or six long fits, and a surprising habituation: "so much are men enslaved to their miserable being" that they will accept any condition provided they may live. He backs this with exempla he borrows (Maecenas's verses, Tamerlane's killing of lepers, the Antisthenes and Diogenes exchange). He distinguishes pains of the mind, to which he feels himself relatively insensible, from corporeal pains, which he feels keenly; yet pain turned out less than he had imagined, which confirms for him that the faculties of the soul, as we use them, "more trouble the repose of life than they are any way useful to it". He hopes the stone will finish his reconciliation with death, while fearing it might push him into wishing to die. He credits his earlier meditation on such accidents for his composure, though he calls himself a "roughly handled" apprentice.

Against ceremonial composure. Montaigne rejects the philosophical precept demanding a stern countenance under pain as "ceremonial": philosophy forms us "for ourselves, not for others; to be, not to seem". Groaning, even roaring, is allowed if the mind keeps its footing; he cites Epicurus (as reported, via a Latin boxer analogy that stands untranslated in the text) for permitting the sage to cry out. This is a judgment of his own, set against the Stoic-flavoured rule he reports and rejects. He also tests himself in the worst pain by starting remote conversation to confirm his reason still works.

Heredity as an everyday miracle. He turns the usual humility about nature's hidden qualities into a barb: confessing ignorance of far-off wonders is a "crafty humility that springs from presumption", and ordinary things, like the seed that carries "the impression not only of the bodily form, but even of the thoughts and inclinations of our fathers", are wonders enough. He gives anecdotes (Lepidus's family, the Theban lance-head mark, Aristotle on a nation with women in common) and his own case: his father felt no sign of the stone until sixty-seven, yet Montaigne, begotten decades earlier, got it, alone among his siblings. He asks how the disposition lay hidden so long and says he would believe whoever can explain it "in as many other miracles as he pleases", provided the explanation is not more fantastic than the thing explained. The question is posed, not answered.

Inherited aversion to medicine, then partly turned against itself. He claims an hereditary "hatred and contempt" of physicians, supported by the long unmedicated lives of his father, grandfather and great-grandfather, and by the family exceptions (the uncle who answered the doctors' threat with "I am then a dead man"; the brother who took medicine and died early). He then hedges against his own instinct: conditions that spring "without reason" are "vicious", a disease to wrestle with, and he has "supported and fortified" the antipathy with arguments. He also qualifies himself with fairness: he concedes that health is the one thing worth any cost, that some natural simples work, and that the physicians should not take advantage of him now that he is ill. He allows the survival of his family's "experience" is a matter of fortune as much as reason.

Nature versus artifice, with the physician as the figure of artifice. The objection is not to remedies the earth produces or to the name of medicine, but to "the inventions of our mind, our knowledge and art", by which "we have abandoned Nature and her rules". He parallels this with law: we call a heap of first-come laws justice, and those who attack its abuse do not blame the virtue itself. Doctors are said to corrupt health to keep their authority; peoples and eras without physic are offered as counterexamples (Romans for six hundred years, Cato, Arcadians, Libyans, local country remedies), all reported from Plutarch, Pliny, Herodotus and local observation, and the evidence is anecdotal. He ends by commending patience before the "general order of things" that "takes care of fleas and moles": follow it, and do not try to bribe it with fears or purges. Compare the late-modern suspicion of legibility-driven systems in Legibility and the contrast between custom-given and artificial order in habit-and-identity, though Montaigne's naturalism is not a theory of institutions and uses "nature" loosely.

Notable passages

How the book is made. The opening paragraph (c1) is Montaigne's clearest statement here of his method: a patchwork written in intervals, left unrevised to show his changes. It matters for michel-de-montaigne as a writer who treats the accumulating record, not the final doctrine, as the work.

"To be, not to seem". In c2 he moves from his own pain to a general claim about philosophy's job: to govern the understanding, not to police gestures. The passage is self-report fused with argument, and his own anecdote of testing himself under pain is evidence he offers for it.

The seed and the stone. In c3 the argument that heredity is a wonder as great as any miracle and his own case of the delayed disease. The hedge matters: he does not claim to explain it, only to refuse a more fantastic explanation.

Mistrusting the inventions of the mind. c4 is the thesis of his critique: nature is honoured, human art that has abandoned nature's bounds is not, and law is brought in as a parallel.

The order that drags the unwilling. c5 closes the slice with a quasi-fatalist piety about nature's order, mixing a theological nod ("in God's name") with a medical naturalism; it should not be read as a general rejection of care, since he has just allowed health to be worth any cost.
