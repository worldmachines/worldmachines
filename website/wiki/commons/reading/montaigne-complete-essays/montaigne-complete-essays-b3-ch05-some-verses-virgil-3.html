Jealousy, Chastity and the Flesh of Words (Upon Some Verses of Virgil, middle slice)

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In the middle of 'Upon Some Verses of Virgil', Montaigne treats jealousy and female chastity as a futile, self-defeating obsession, advises husbands to avoid the 'tormenting and unprofitable knowledge' of cuckoldry, and then turns to praise the vigorous, thought-driven Latin of Virgil and Lucretius against French affectation.

This slice, from the middle of a long essay, runs in two movements. The first and longer is a worldly, ironic discussion of jealousy, the supposed duty of female chastity and the cuckold's predicament. Montaigne opens by calling jealousy the most foolish of the passions, then works through exempla (Cratis's he-goat, Octavius and Pontia Posthumia, Galba and Maecenas, Messalina and Silius, the Scythian women) and a stream of Latin verse. His practical conclusion is that bridling women's desire is both folly and counterproductive, that the search for proof is worse than the disease, and that men should evade the knowledge. The second movement, introduced by Lucretius's lines on Venus and Mars, swerves into literary criticism: ancient Latin poets are praised for language that is 'well thought' rather than merely well said, French 'scribblers' are faulted for novelty-hunting, and the French language is judged abundant but not pliable. The slice begins mid-argument (at the close of a passage on jealousy) and ends mid-argument on language; it does not contain the essay's opening or its close.

Themes

Jealousy as the most foolish and self-defeating passion. Montaigne reports that envy 'had never to do with me' and that he knows jealousy 'by sight, and that's all'. He describes it as a disease with the most things for aliment and the fewest for remedy, one that turns the causes of good-will into causes of hatred and 'rebounds' to an extreme opposite to its cause. The Octavius story is an exemplum of love converted to murderous hatred. Note the framing: he is describing the passion mostly as it afflicts women ('these poor souls, weak and incapable of resistance'), a claim in the voice of a sixteenth-century gentleman that the text states rather than defends.

The impossibility and incoherence of policing chastity. Montaigne argues that the will is 'a very supple and active thing', that dreams defeat it, and that 'there is neither continency nor virtue where there are no opposing desires'. Inconsiderate protestations of coldness are 'a kind of self-accusation'. The ideal men impose (Fatua who never saw a man, Hiero's wife who never noticed his breath) would require women to 'become insensible and invisible to satisfy us'. He then loosens the category by custom and exemplum: complaisant husbands (Galba, Phaulius), the East Indian elephant custom, Phaedo, Solon's law and Herodotus. Chastity's meaning is shown to vary by place and arrangement; Montaigne says the knot lies 'principally' in the will, but the examples show how contingent the judgment is. This sits near habit-and-identity and the broader Montaignian theme of custom as invisible machinery, though here custom is cited piecemeal rather than theorised.

Not-knowing as the prudent policy. 'Curiosity is vicious throughout; but ‘tis pernicious here.' The cuckold's disgrace lies in being known, and 'we say a good wife or a happy marriage, not that they are really so, but because no one says to the contrary'. Marital reputation is thus a social fiction sustained by silence, and Montaigne counsels discretion over exposure, citing the Romans who sent word home before returning. He also offers consolations (honest men are cuckolds too, 'nature runs much in parallel, in compensation') and a half-joking observation that the frequency of the accident has 'passed into custom'. His tone is consoling and sardonic, not systematic; he concedes in places that the whole subject is miserable ('Miserable passion!').

Prohibition inflames desire. Montaigne asks whether the severity of the obligation lays on women 'does not produce two effects contrary to our design', making suitors keener and women readier to yield. Raising the value of the place raises the value of the conquest; Venus has made 'the laws her bawds'. Messalina's story is read (his own interpretation, 'what better interpretation can we make') as an appetite that needed a watchful husband, and he adds a first-person observation that extreme toleration, when it dissolves, 'produces the most severe revenge'. Whether the Messalina reading is plausible history or witty inference is not something the text settles; it is offered as interpretation.

Self-portrait of timidity. Inside the discussion of opportunity in love, Montaigne turns to himself: 'superstitiously afraid of giving offence', a great respect for the woman he loved, bashful to deny and to be denied, delegating requests to a third person 'to blush for me'. He calls the quality 'very ill suiting my universal form' and generalises with 'what are we but sedition and discrepancy?' This is the essay as self-study, a compact instance of the inconsistent, divided self (see michel-de-montaigne and self-construction-through-writing).

Language that is thought, not ornament. Sparked by Lucretius's passage on Venus and Mars, Montaigne praises Latin poets whose speech is 'all epigram', with 'nothing forced, nothing languishing'; when he reads it he says 'not that ‘tis well said, but well thought'. Words 'of flesh and bone' signify more than they say. Fine wits do not invent words but 'enrich their own', whereas contemporary French writers chase new words and lose 'old' ones that were 'more sinewy'. He judges French rich in stuff but poorly cut and insufficiently pliable, and suggests borrowing from hunting and war and transplanting like herbs. This ties to his sense of writing as embodied judgment rather than borrowed dexterity (cf. personal-essay-as-form); it is also a defence of an ancient standard against contemporary taste, which is a position Montaigne takes, not a neutral report.

Notable passages

The jealousy diagnosis (c1) shows Montaigne at his most clinical: jealousy feeds on the husband's virtue, health and reputation, so nothing a good man does can cure it. The chastity passage (c2) is his central argument that desire cannot be legislated away, with the memorable point that boasting of cold virtue is self-accusation. The curiosity passage (c3) is the practical heart of the first movement, equating marital honour with the absence of public talk. The prohibition passage (c4) reverses the moralist's logic and ends in the cynical 'all swine’s flesh, varied by sauces'. The bashfulness passage (c5) is the self-portrait inside the topic. The language passage (c6) shows how the essay moves, by association from Lucretius's erotic verbs to a theory of style.
