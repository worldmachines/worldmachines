Montaigne on educating a child: judgment over stored learning (first third)

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne opens his essay to Diane de Foix by confessing his thin learning and borrowed style, declares that he writes only to portray himself, then argues that a tutor should cultivate a pupil's own judgment through digestion, conversation, travel and bodily hardening rather than memorized authority.

The first part of the essay "Of the Education of Children", addressed to Diane de Foix, Comtesse de Gurson, who is expecting a child. It falls in two movements. First Montaigne gives a long, self-deprecating preface: he has only nibbled at the sciences, reads Plutarch and Seneca without retaining them, feels weak beside the better writers he meets, and disapproves of authors who stitch borrowed passages into their own work, while admitting that he too is a borrower. He says he writes only to show himself and claims no authority. Then, prompted by a visitor who thought the previous chapter should have said more on the subject, he turns to the education of a boy of quality. He calls it the hardest difficulty of human science, doubts that early inclinations can be read, and offers one fancy "contrary to the common method": choose a tutor with a well-made rather than a well-filled head, who draws the pupil out, tests what he has digested, and sends him into the world. The slice ends with advice on restraint in conversation, argument and political loyalty.

Themes

Self-portrait and the disclaimer of authority. Montaigne frames his writing as the "idle reveries" of a half-educated man. He compares his book to a portrait of his "old bald grizzled" self and says he has "no other end in this writing, but only to discover myself", one who may be "another thing to-morrow". These are his own reports and hedges, and they do not claim to be doctrine. This is the essay as a technology of self-knowledge (personal-essay-as-form, self-construction-through-writing, michel-de-montaigne).

Borrowing, citation and the ethics of the commonplace. He condemns scribblers who insert whole pages of the ancients and so make their own prose look "sallow and deformed". He contrasts Chrysippus, who put the whole Medea into a book, with Epicurus, who has no quotations at all. He then admits that he too tries to make his style "go hand in hand" with his thefts. He separates concealed theft from the openly acknowledged cento. This is an early reflection on reading and writing in a world of accumulated texts, which loosely rhymes with the information-overload themes in printing-press-and-information-overload-eisenstein, though Montaigne is concerned with authorial honesty rather than the volume of print.

Education as judgment, not memory. The central claim is Montaigne's own. Pedagogues pour knowledge "into a funnel", and the pupil only repeats it. He wants the pupil to taste, choose and doubt. Learning is digestion, and to "disgorge what we eat in the same condition it was swallowed" shows indigestion. Following another means finding nothing. The bee image gives the aim: borrowed fragments are transformed into honey, "that is to say, his judgment". Montaigne takes Socratic method as his model (socratic-thinking). The pupil should hear diverse opinions and may "remain in doubt" if he cannot choose, which fits Montaigne's sceptical temper. The Aristotelian of Pisa is the foil.

Custom and the disguise of nature. He argues that children's early inclinations are obscure and unreliable. Grown men take on habits, opinions, laws and customs that "alter, or at least disguise" their true disposition. He nonetheless counsels against over-trusting early signs, and he disagrees mildly with Plato's Republic on this point. This touches on habit-and-identity, though here custom is a mask over nature, not yet the full machinery of the later essays on custom. In the same spirit he tells the pupil not to claim a licence against "the authority of custom", a licence he allows only to great souls such as Socrates.

World, body and hardening. The tutor should use ordinary life, conversation and early travel as books. Travel is for observing humours, manners, customs and laws, not for tourist trivia. The pupil should be taken from his mother's lap and toughened so the soul is not oppressed. Montaigne draws on his own tender body, and he warns that stoic examples of fortitude are sometimes only "toughness of skin". He links this to the civil war, in which honest men are threatened with the whip and the halter.

Conversation, argument and loyalty. The pupil should listen and be modest in company, should argue only with worthy opponents, and should yield to truth as soon as he sees it, because arguing is not a trade. Montaigne wants him loyal to his prince but not bound by personal obligations that would bribe his judgment, and he sketches the courtier's corrupted speech.

Notable passages

The self as sole subject. The passage beginning "For these are my own particular opinions and fancies" is a compact statement of Montaigne's authorial stance: he delivers his views "as only what I myself believe, and not for what is to be believed by others", has no end but to discover himself, and expects to change.

The funnel and the trotting horse. The pedagogue pours knowledge in while the pupil repeats it. Montaigne wants the tutor to let the pupil speak, in the manner of Socrates and Arcesilaus, adjusting to the pupil's pace. He adds that this calibration is "one of the hardest things I know".

Digestion. The stomach image turns on altering what one takes in. The pupil should work with a free and natural pace and not a servile one. Montaigne quotes a Latin line here, and he is also reporting the Aristotelian of Pisa as an exemplum.

The bees. Bees "cull their several sweets" and then make honey that is their own. Judgment is the product, and the pupil need not say where he got his materials. This answers the preface's worry about borrowing, though Montaigne's own bee image, drawn from a tradition he does not name here, is turned to his own use.

Knowing by rote. "To know by rote, is no knowledge." Bookish learning is a poor ornament, and, by analogy with dancing and riding, one learns to judge and speak by exercising judgment and speech.

Travel and conversation. Travel and company should whet the wits "by rubbing them against those of others", not produce measurements of monuments or reports of petticoats. Montaigne wants the pupil to learn the customs and laws of other nations, and to go young to nations whose language differs most from his own.
