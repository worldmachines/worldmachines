Reason's Measure of God: Afterlife, Sacrifice, Plural Worlds and the Limits of Speech

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In this slice of the Apology, Montaigne argues that human reason, anthropomorphic religion and ordinary language all fail when they try to measure the divine, running through afterlife absurdities, cruel sacrifice, plural worlds, the relativity of 'nature', and the paradoxes of speech and Pyrrhonist doubt.

This is the eighth slice of the Apology for Raimond Sebond and it begins mid-argument, still pressing the case that man cannot form a worthy idea of the divine. Montaigne first mocks the sensual paradises of Plato and Mahomet and shows, by the arguments of the ancients, that any reward 'after death' founders on the question of what the recipient would be, since change or the separation of body and soul means it is 'no more us'. He then surveys how antiquity made its gods in man's image and fed them human cruelty (sacrifices, self-mutilation), turns to the claim that God's power exceeds our 'municipal law' of nature and that there may be many worlds, widens this into the point that 'nature' only means 'our understanding', lists the ancient doubters, and finally attacks the pretension of language (the liar paradox, Pyrrhonist self-cancelling doubt, 'God cannot...' formulas). The slice ends on the old scoffer's list of what God cannot do and a Latin verse on the irrevocability of the past.

Themes

Attribution and register. Much of the slice is reported voice. The 'we should tell him from human reason' speech is Montaigne ventriloquizing reason against Plato; the Epicurean, Plutarch, Stilpo, Pliny/Herodotus marvels, and the 'old scoffer' (whose list of divine impossibilities Montaigne reports and then says a Christian should keep from his lips) are borrowed. Montaigne's own judgment appears in lines such as 'I cannot believe they speak as they think', 'This way of speaking in a Christian man has ever seemed to me very indiscreet and irreverent', and in his fideist deference to the church's 'beaten' road.

Afterlife and identity. Reward and punishment presuppose a continuing person, but change, transmigration (Caesar in a lion; the phoenix and the silk-worm) and the soul-body separation all dissolve the self; hence Plato's recompense falls on 'something else'. Quoted Lucretian verse carries this (what has ceased to be is no more). Montaigne treats it as an argument human reason can mount against Plato, and balances it with St Paul: the promises must be imagined 'unimaginable, inexplicable, and incomprehensible'.

Anthropomorphic religion and cruelty. Antiquity flattered the divine by giving it our appetites, offerings and 'inhuman vengeance'; the catalogue (Getae, Amestris, Mexican idols, Carthaginians, Lacedemonians, Iphigenia, the Decii, Corybantes, Mahometans) leads to the judgment that religion 'filled' itself 'with several ill effects'. Montaigne's argument is partly moral (punishing the innocent cannot expiate the guilty; the offence lies in the will) and partly theological (nothing of ours can be likened to the divine without staining it).

The relativity of 'nature'. 'Thou seest nothing but the order and revolution of this little cave in which thou art lodged'; what we call natural law is a 'municipal law'. If there are many worlds, we cannot know that our rules bind the others; even on Earth the New World lacks corn and wine, and the travellers' monsters (Pliny, Herodotus, Plutarch, all hedged 'if... to be believed') show how variable humanity may be. The key epistemic move is that to go 'according to nature' is merely to go 'according to our understanding'. This rhymes with the Dawn-Day-Dusk habit of treating one era's machinery as nature itself, though Montaigne's frame is theological and sceptical rather than historical.

The doubters and the failure of language. A string of ancient denials (Anaxagoras on snow, Protagoras, Zeno, Parmenides) shows reason can establish nothing; Montaigne then argues that most of the world's disturbances are 'grammatical', and that even the liar paradox exposes logic. The Pyrrhonists lack a language for their doubt and shelter under the rhubarb comparison, which Montaigne links to his own question 'What do I know?' and its emblem of a balance. See also socratic-thinking for the related theme of knowing one's ignorance, though Montaigne's doubt is more radical and more fideist.

Notable passages

Plato's afterlife and the problem of identity. The first long passage works through why reward 'after death' is incoherent for a creature made of body and soul, and ends by turning on reason itself: she 'does nothing but err throughout, but especially when she meddles with divine things', even with scripture's lamp, and loses herself in 'a labyrinth of a thousand several paths' once off the church's road.

Gods made in our image. The Antiquity passage links anthropomorphism to sacrifice and to the observation that nearly every nation has offered human victims; it is the clearest statement here of religion as projection of human passions onto the divine.

Municipal law and many worlds. The address to man ('Tis a municipal law that thou allegest...') separates the rules made 'for thee' from God's freedom from them, and then moves to the plurality of worlds.

'Nature' as our understanding. The paragraph on miracles and 'occult properties' is Montaigne's own argument that the monstrous is simply what exceeds the observer's knowledge.

Grammatical wars and the Pyrrhonist's language. Includes the 'hoc' controversy, the liar paradox, and the rhubarb image; the editor's footnote on the transubstantiation reference is not part of Montaigne's text, so the reading is only that 'hoc' is a one-syllable dispute that created great quarrels.

Against 'God cannot...'. Montaigne objects to limiting divine power by 'the laws of men's mouths', then quotes the scoffer's consolation that God cannot undo the past, and says this is what a Christian should not let slip.
