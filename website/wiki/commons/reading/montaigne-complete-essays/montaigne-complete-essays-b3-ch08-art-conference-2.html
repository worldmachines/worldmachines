Of the art of conference, second half: fortune, borrowed wit, and judging authors

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In the second half of this essay on conversation, Montaigne argues that rank, success and silence manufacture false reputations for wisdom, that fluent speakers often merely hold borrowed sentences they do not understand, that arguing with fools is futile, and then turns to judging authors (Tacitus) and to the licence of speaking of oneself.

This slice (the latter part of the essay on conference, beginning mid-argument) opens with silence and rank as props to a reputation for wisdom, then argues that offices are distributed by fortune and that events are poor evidence of merit. It moves to the habits of conversation: Montaigne distrusts the grandeur that surrounds powerful speakers, warns that men are "rich in borrowed sufficiency", and says folly cannot be cured by passing admonition. A short passage on witty banter among friends and on asking people how they judge their own work leads to a long digression on reading Tacitus, on what is an author's own versus borrowed (Commines, Tacitus, Seneca, Q. Cicero), and on whether a writer may speak of himself. It closes on historians relating belief rather than endorsing it, and Montaigne's self-exposure "standing and lying, before and behind".

Themes

Fortune, not merit, makes and unmakes the great. Montaigne (in his own voice) says dignities "are of necessity conferred more by fortune than upon the account of merit", since princes can only choose by conjecture (family, wealth, learning, popular voice). He cites the maxim that we should not judge counsels by events, with exempla borrowed from the Carthaginians, the Roman triumph-denials, Seiramnes the Persian, and Thucydides on the stupid governing well. Most worldly affairs, he says, "are performed by themselves", and his own reasoning is moved by breaths of chance. He hedges with questions ("What if the plainest reasons are the best seated?") rather than flat claims, and admits he refers "the stress and main of the business" to heaven.

Reputation is a function of place and display. We judge a promoted man "as we do by counters, according to the prerogative of his place", and are amazed at his fall. Montaigne distinguishes bodily deference from the understanding: "my reason is not obliged to bow and bend; my knees are." Anecdotes (Megabyzus and Apelles, Melanthius, Antisthenes and the asses, the Mexican coronation oath) are borrowed illustrations of how office and silence cloud judgment. He is more apt to suspect capacity that comes with grandeur, because power lets a man steer a discussion by a nod or silence.

Borrowed sufficiency and the art of testing a speaker. Montaigne's own method, which he says he draws great advantage from, is not to accept a good-sounding word at once: a man may say a good thing without seeing its force, and he admits this may hold of himself. He asks fools to specify "why, or how" a judgment holds; universal verdicts are like saluting a crowd rather than persons by name. Vague praise ("That is fine" after a page of Virgil) is the safe refuge of the cunning. He prefers to let the ignorant sink deeper in the mire than rescue them, yet holds that correcting folly takes long apprenticeship (Cyrus's remark), a duty owed to one's own people and not to the first passer-by.

Obstinacy and self-satisfaction. What vexes him in folly is that it is more satisfied with itself than reason can be; prudence leaves us timorous while temerity brings joy and assurance. Heat in argument is "the surest proofs of folly", and the ass is the image of grave assurance. By contrast his own conduct in friendly banter is to endure retaliation, let a point pass, and defer revenge; he hates rough play.

Judging works and the problem of what is one's own. He asks people how far they are satisfied with their own work, since authors misjudge their own books as much as others do and a work may outstrip its maker; he places his own Essays "now high, or low, with great doubt and inconstancy". The Commines/Tacitus/Seneca/Cicero sequence shows the same thought recurring, so one must know what is the author's own before praising him. Montaigne reports himself as little read, and so on his guard against praising borrowed wit.

Tacitus and speaking of oneself. Montaigne reads Tacitus through (a rare event; he says he has not kept to one book an hour together for twenty years), values him as a judge of manners and a school of "ethic and politic discourses", fits his pen to a "troubled and sick state", yet disputes his severity toward Pompey and his reading of Tiberius's letter. He calls it a little mean in Tacitus to apologise for mentioning his own office; a man of sound judgment uses his own example freely, and Montaigne dares "not only speak of myself, but to speak only of myself", while claiming he can regard himself as he would a neighbour or a tree. This is the clearest self-study statement in the slice and bears on self-construction-through-writing and personal-essay-as-form, though here it is argued as a rule of candour about worth, not a programme of self-knowledge.

Historians relay belief; Montaigne is monarch of his matter. Reporting Tacitus on Vespasian's cure and the Latin tags on transcribing more than one believes, Montaigne holds historians should "deliver to us history, more as they receive it than as they believe it"; judging belief belongs to divines and philosophers. He contrasts himself: master of his own matter, accountable to none, yet not always believing himself, letting doubtful sallies go "at a venture". See michel-de-montaigne.

Notable passages

Fortune and events (c1). The core of the argument that office is given by luck and that success is no proof of counsel, ending in the flat conclusion that events "are a very poor testimony of our worth and parts." Note the mix of Montaigne's own judgment with borrowed authority (Thucydides, the Seiramnes anecdote, Latin tags whose translations are not in the text).

Counters and the knees (c2). Place makes us read merit into men; Montaigne separates ritual deference from intellectual deference. The Antisthenes and Mexican coronation exempla extend this to the way peoples deify the king they have chosen, an anecdote reported, not endorsed as fact beyond the text.

Borrowed sufficiency (c3). Montaigne's practical rule for conversation: do not accept a good word until you know how it is lodged in the author, and press for reasons. He turns it on himself: he may not understand all he borrows.

Folly is not cured by admonition (c4). Instruction belongs to long education and to one's own people; Montaigne declines to tutor strangers, and refrains from opposing false talk in common discourse.

Speaking only of oneself (c5). Criticising Tacitus's excuse, Montaigne sets out his own licence: speak of oneself as of a third person, distinguish oneself as from "a neighbour or a tree", and note that we speak of God as much as we like though we know Him less than we love ourselves.

Receiving versus believing, and the postures of the self (c6). The historian's duty to relate common belief, and Montaigne's contrasting self-description as sovereign of his matter who still distrusts himself.
