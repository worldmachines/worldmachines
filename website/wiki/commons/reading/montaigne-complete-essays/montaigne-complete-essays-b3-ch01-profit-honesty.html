Of Profit and Honesty: Utility Does Not Prove an Action Honourable

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne argues that public utility never by itself makes an action honest, drawing a personal line against lying, betrayal and double-dealing in the civil wars while conceding that states need vicious services and that a prince may be forced to break faith as a misfortune, not a virtue.

The opening chapter of Book Three begins with a disclaimer of his own foolish talk, then turns on a claim: Tiberius refused to poison Arminius and chose the honest over the profitable. From there Montaigne works through the relation between public advantage and private conscience. He concedes that governments need vicious offices and that deceit is often useful, but he declines to perform them himself. He reports his own conduct as a mediator between princes during the civil wars (frank, partisan only with moderation, never lying, taking few secrets). He then collects a long run of anecdotes in which traitors are rewarded and then punished by the very people who used them. He closes on a prince forced to break his word, on promises extracted by fear, and on Epaminondas as the model of justice kept inside violence. The final line denies that utility proves an action honourable.

Themes

Necessary vice, delegated. Montaigne holds that human nature and government both contain vice: "Vices there help to make up the seam in our piecing, as poisons are useful for the conservation of health." His conclusion is not to approve the vice but to leave such commissions to "men who are more obedient and more supple", while he takes the easier and less hazardous parts. This is a division of moral labour, offered with some irony and with a hedge about his own weakness.

Sincerity as a way of working, not only a virtue. His report of his mediation says that open speech wins belief, that a man who "treats without any interest of his own" is not suspected, and that he says nothing to one side he could not say to the other "with a little alteration of accent". He credits fortune for much of his success. He also admits the method is "a little dissonant from the ordinary forms" and that "innocence itself could not, in this age of ours, either negotiate without dissimulation, or traffic without lying", so he stays out of public employment. He answers those who call his plainness an art by saying that "The way of truth is one and simple; that of particular profit ... is double, unequal, and casual."

Against neutrality and against double service. He rejects the wavering middle in domestic strife (the Latin verse says it is "no middle way but none"), as treason in one's own country. He also rejects offering full service to both parties as lacking prudence as well as conscience, since the man you betray for knows you will do the same to him. What he recommends is a moderate, tempered affection for one side, to "swim in troubled waters without fishing in them". He also condemns those who mistake private interest for duty and "kindle and begin a war, not because it is just, but because it is war." He keeps a limit on obedience: the laws have given him a master, but "actions must receive commission from the public appointment", while will and desire remain his own.

Two justices. Natural, universal justice is distinguished from "that other justice which is special, national, and constrained to the ends of government". He reports Dandamis's verdict that Socrates, Pythagoras and Diogenes were too subject to the laws. Montaigne states this as something he follows, then says he follows "the common phrase" that calls some natural, necessary actions dishonest. The passage is notable for how much it concedes to the critic of law while he keeps to his own conduct.

The betrayer is the first to be punished. The anecdotes (Jaropelk and the Hungarian gentleman, Antigonus and the Argyraspides, the slave of Sulpicius, Clovis, Mohammed II, the Roman Senate, Amurath I, Witold of Lithuania) are exempla from other writers and from reported events, not Montaigne's own arguments. He uses them to claim that whoever commissions treachery despises the instrument, and that the instrument suffers "a prostitution of conscience". The commissioning party wants to erase the memory of the deed, and to "foist" some show of virtue into it. He also includes things seen in his own time (soldiers who hanged their friends to save themselves), and he says these men are worse off than those hanged.

The prince's necessity, hedged. If a prince must break his faith, Montaigne calls it a misfortune and "a lash of the divine rod", not vice. His answer to what the remedy is: "None". He then adds that if the prince does it without regret his conscience is in a sorry condition, and that a man too tender to do it "could not more excusably or more decently perish". Timoleon is the case of one who did it and wept. The example is left open, with the Senate and the Syracusans deferring judgment. Montaigne says these are "dangerous examples, rare and sickly exceptions to our natural rules".

Promises bind even when extracted by fear. Against "rules in philosophy that are both false and weak", he holds that an oath given to robbers must be paid, and that he himself makes conscience of disowning even a careless word. A promise is excusably broken only if it was for something wicked in itself.

Epaminondas and the limit on what the common good can ask. The figure of Epaminondas, who joined gentleness to fierce action and would not kill outside due form of law, is the one positive model. The argument turns to the present: we need not harden ourselves, since "'tis enough to dip our pens in ink without dipping them in blood." He then offers a pair of civil-war soldiers who killed their brothers, one with shame and one asking a reward, as proof of what "time and example" can do. This touches custom and habit as the machinery by which atrocity becomes ordinary, though Montaigne does not name custom as such here.

Notable passages

The passage on sickly qualities and necessary vices frames the whole chapter. It treats cruelty and ill-natured pleasure as natural, then moves from that to the claim that governments need vicious offices. The mediation passage is Montaigne's self-report as a person of public affairs in the Wars of Religion, and it links his temperament to a method. The Pomponius Flaccus passage sets up the line he will not cross (not assassination or poison, but lying, betrayal and perjury), which he states with the Lacedaemonians as a precedent. The passage on the prince is the most carefully hedged and the one most often quoted in discussions of reason of state. It rests on a conscience that remains disturbed. Resonates with niccolo-machiavelli as a counterposition, since Montaigne grants necessity and then refuses to call it virtue; the text itself never names Machiavelli. His retreat from office and his talk of the first-person self also bear on michel-de-montaigne. The legitimacy of states built on practices their own morals condemn loosely rhymes with modernity-machine, though this is a distant echo and Montaigne's frame is moral, not institutional.
