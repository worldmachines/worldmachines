Authority, presupposition and the quarrel over the soul

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In a middle slice of the Apology for Raimond Sebond, Montaigne argues that received principles and authority (Aristotle above all) are accepted on trust, that philosophy's endless disagreement about the soul's nature, seat and origin exposes the weakness of human reason, and that his own self-portrait turned out, by accident, to match ancient opinions.

This is the tenth of fifteen slices of the Apology for Raimond Sebond, beginning and ending mid-argument. It opens by asking whether those who find Sebond's arguments too weak, and who claim to know everything, have even understood their own being: nobody knows how a spiritual impression moves a solid body. Montaigne then attacks the habit of taking received opinion on trust, the tyranny of Aristotle in the schools, and the way every science rests on presupposed principles. He answers the old rebuttals to the doubter (put your hand in the fire) and turns reason on itself. The long middle is a catalogue of philosophers' conflicting opinions on what the soul is, where it sits and where it comes from, with a run of absurd arguments from Plato, the Epicureans and Zeno. The slice ends by returning to the soul, weighing the Platonic doctrine of reminiscence against experience, and arguing that the soul's powers and rewards belong to this life, with the Epicurean view that it is born, grows and decays with the body.

Themes

Belief by authority, reason as a supple tool. Montaigne (in his own voice) says opinions are received "by authority and upon trust, as if it were religion and law", and that reason is "a supple utensil, pliable, and to be accommodated to any figure". Men debate the branches and not the root; they ask what Galen said, not whether it is true. Aristotle is the "god of scholastic knowledge", and his doctrine, he says, is "peradventure as false as another". He claims no more reason to prefer Aristotle than Plato, Epicurus, Thales or any of the long list he recites.

Every science rests on a presupposition. Each discipline borrows its principles from another and then refuses to dispute with those who deny them. Montaigne's answer is that all human presuppositions carry equal authority unless reason makes a difference, so they must all be weighed, "first the generals and those that tyrannize over us". Real principles come only by revelation; the rest is "dream and vapour". This is the hinge where his scepticism meets fideism, though here it is stated briefly and not developed.

Philosophers made us judges; reason cannot vouch for itself. The stock replies to the doubter (the fire, the ice) are called unworthy of philosophy, since it was the philosophers who taught us to make reason "controller-general" of everything. Montaigne contrasts the cannibals and animals, living by nature without Aristotle. Reason is asked to prove itself by itself, and if it cannot speak truly of its own being it is ill fitted to judge foreign things. True reason, he adds, lodges with the Almighty.

Disagreement about the soul as the test case. The soul is the thing we ought to know best, and the report is a catalogue of contradictory definitions (heat, harmony, number, entelechy, blood) and seats (brain, stomach, heart, blood, between the eyebrows). Reported opinions here are the philosophers' own, with Cicero, Bernard and Heraclitus cited as confessing ignorance; Montaigne comments, and mocks Chrysippus's reasoning from the jaw and Plato's featherless biped.

The edges of knowledge are marshes. Following Plutarch's remark on maps, he says the utmost bounds of inquiry end in ravings, so the beginning and end of knowledge are equally foolish. He then gives comic examples: Epicurean atoms needing hooked tails, Zeno's syllogisms that make the world wise.

Philosophers' irresolution as tacit confession; Montaigne's own case. He suggests the ancients treated knowledge like a toy and tacitly led us to "this resolution of their irresolution", perhaps hiding their real view so as not to frighten children. Philosophy has so many faces that all our dreams are in it. Montaigne applies this to himself: his own manners were natural, yet when he dressed them with reasons and examples he was surprised to find them conformable to ancient discourses, "an unpremeditated and accidental philosopher". This links to self-construction-through-writing and michel-de-montaigne's method, though the point here is hedged and partly self-deprecating.

Soul, memory and the limits of eternity (the end of the slice). Montaigne reports Plato's view that learning is remembrance and rejects it by experience: we remember only what we were taught, and the soul would have to recall a pure prior knowledge it never uses. He argues the soul's account must be rendered in this life, since an eternal sentence on a brief life is a disproportion, and cites the Epicurean/Democritean view, the most received among the ancients, that the soul is born, grows and decays with the body. He reports this, and does not here declare it his own conclusion.

Notable passages

The attack on trust in authority (c1) is the clearest statement that doubt usually stops at the branches. The passage on presuppositions (c2) is Montaigne's most compact argument that any system can be built from granted starting points, and that certainty is a symptom of folly. The passage on reason judging the world (c3) turns the philosophers' own demand for reasons against them, and sets the cannibals and animals beside the schools; compare noble-savage, though Montaigne here makes a point about reason and not a claim of primitive virtue. The 'marshes at the edge of the map' passage (c4) is the slice's image of the limits of inquiry. The Italian-language analogy and self-portrait (c5) joins scepticism to the essay form: it fits personal-essay-as-form, and it echoes the Socratic stance of socratic-thinking in that the Plato quotation has Socrates admit he does not know what man is. The passage on philosophers' tacit confession (c6) is Montaigne's charitable reading of why they disagree so much.
