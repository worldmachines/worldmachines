Reason Overpaid For: Beasts, Peasants and the Failure of Philosophic Consolation

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In a middle slice of the Apology, Montaigne argues that human reason, learning and philosophy buy us no real good over beasts, peasants and the ignorant, that knowledge breeds anticipated suffering and madness, and that only humble obedience and a sensible, non-stupefied simplicity serve us, while Stoic and Epicurean consolations fail by his own test of the sick body.

This is a mid-essay slice of the Apology for Raimond Sebond (part 5 of 15); it opens mid-argument on women, cosmetics and the way familiarity breeds disgust, and ends mid-argument on philosophy's recommended forgetting. Its core move is a ledger of what reason has cost us: beasts keep the "essential, durable, and palpable good" (peace, health, innocence) while we keep imagined goods; learning did nothing for Varro or Aristotle against gout or death; artisans and labourers are wiser and happier than university rectors. Montaigne then tests the philosophers' own claims on the body (Posidonius's boast against pain, Pyrrho's hog, the physician's anticipatory illnesses) and finds that ignorance does what philosophy promises, more purely. He turns to a fideist note (obedience as first law, knowledge as first temptation), then backs off from outright stupor, and closes by showing philosophy's consolations of memory to be self-defeating.

Themes

The beasts' ledger. Montaigne notes that by our own confession we leave animals the real goods and reserve to ourselves the imaginary ones: "reason, knowledge, and honour." He uses the Stoics' own admissions (health outweighs wisdom; Ulysses should refuse the potion that makes a wise man a fool) against them, concluding that our preference for ourselves over other animals rests on "foolish pride and vain opinion." The attribution is mixed: the Stoic and Socratic dicta are reported opinions turned against their holders, while the verdict is Montaigne's.

Reason as a bad bargain. The catalogue of human passions (inconstancy, ambition, war, lying, curiosity) is Montaigne's own; the Latin passage comparing reason to wine given to the sick is a quoted opinion that he adopts hesitantly ("I know not whether it had not been better for mankind..."). Learning, he says, is useful "more by opinion than by nature"; he would rather have resembled a hundred artisans than the rectors.

Humility, obedience and the plague of self-opinion. Here the argument turns theological: obedience is "the first law," sin springs from "selfopinion," and ignorance is recommended by religion as "proper to faith and obedience," with Scripture and Epicurus invoked as reported authority. He warns against leaving duty to each man's own judgment, since the "infinite variety of our reasons and opinions" would forge duties that "enjoin us to eat one another." This is fideism, but with a sceptical engine: it is the unreliability of reason that is doing the work. Compare the Counter-Enlightenment's later suspicion of reason (counter-enlightenment), though Montaigne's is a sceptical-devotional register rather than a political programme.

Philosophers' words versus their effects. Cicero's praise of letters and Lucretius's praise of Epicurus as a god are set against the reality of illness and madness; Seneca's and Chrysippus's claims to match God are mocked as hubris; Posidonius "was as sensible of the pain as my footman." Montaigne's test throughout is conduct under pain, not doctrine.

Ignorance as practical medicine; imagination as disease. Pyrrho's hog, the gladiator and the muleteer, the incisions we make more easily on a child or horse than on ourselves: unreflective people endure better. Learned medicine and prognostication give us illnesses in advance ("the stone in his soul, before he has it in his bladder"). Montaigne generalises from physic to all the sciences, and speaks for himself: his ignorance of his health gives him as much hope as fear.

Brazilians, muleteers and the cost of mental vigour. He attributes the long, disease-free lives reported of Brazilian peoples to tranquil souls and simplicity, "without letters, without law, without king, or any manner of religion"; this is an early gesture in the vein that later became the noble-savage idea, here tied to a health claim and offered as his own preference over a climate explanation. The same agitation of the soul that harms the gentleman's amorous performance produces madness: the "most subtle folly" comes from "the most subtle wisdom." The unnamed Italian poet he saw at Ferrara is his concrete case.

Limits of the praise of ignorance. Montaigne declines to endorse total insensibility: citing Crantor against Epicurus, he insists he would want to know he was sick, and that removing the sense of evil would remove the sense of pleasure and "annihilate man himself." Pain is not always to be avoided nor pleasure always pursued. This is a characteristic hedge: the argument against reason does not become a cult of stupor. Good health is mostly the absence of ill, and even pleasure aims at rest.

Philosophy's consolation by memory fails. The counsel to recall past pleasures against present pain is, he argues, useless for a man in a fever (and the Italian line and its inverse show the thinking of pleasure doubles trouble); memory serves "not what we choose, but what she pleases"; wanting to forget imprints a thing deeper. Philosophy resorts to "policy" and "sleight of hand" where force fails.

Notable passages

The slice's best self-contained arguments are: the ledger of goods we allot to ourselves and to the beasts (c1); the artisans-and-rectors comparison with the objection to private judgment (c2); the obedience/knowledge pairing (c3); the physician's anticipated illness (c4); the Brazil and muleteer passage (c5); madness as the child of vigour (c6); and the hedge on insensibility (c7). The Montaigne of these pages is both fiercely deflationary and unwilling to be thoroughly stoic about his own body, a self-report that the essay form (michel-de-montaigne) keeps in play beside the doctrine.
