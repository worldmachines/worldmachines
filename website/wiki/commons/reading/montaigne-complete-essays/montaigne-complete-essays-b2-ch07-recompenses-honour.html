Of Recompenses of Honour: scarcity as the substance of honour

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne argues that honorific awards (orders of knighthood, crowns, titles) work only while they are rare and kept apart from money, that virtue once made common loses its distinction, and that the Order of St. Michael has been ruined by lavish distribution in the troubled France of his day.

A short essay on honorific rewards. Montaigne starts from a report about Augustus: generous with gifts to deserving soldiers, sparing with the true "recompenses of honour". He praises the old invention of valueless distinctions (crowns, garments, titles, coats of arms, orders of knighthood) as a way to reward merit at no cost to prince or people. He then argues that such honours draw their entire worth from rarity and from being kept separate from money, that the French Order of St. Michael has been cheapened by over-distribution, and that a replacement cannot easily be founded in a "licentious" and "sick" time. He closes with a digression on how French usage makes valour the whole of virtue, compared with the way "a good woman" has come to mean only a chaste one.

Themes

Honour is a currency whose value is its scarcity. Montaigne holds that honour "derives its principal essence from rarity". Rewards that have no other value than that few enjoy them are destroyed by being handed out freely: "'tis but to be liberal of them to bring them down to nothing." His examples are the commonplace good deed (no one commends a father for educating his children, or a great tree in a forest of the same) and Sparta, where no citizen boasted of valour because it was universal.

Keep honour out of the market. Gentlemen, he says, have always prized pure honours above gainful ones. Mixing in riches would "debase and abate" the estimation. Money is a general-purpose payment that buys servants, couriers and even flattery, treachery and pimping, so virtue has little appetite for it. The Order of St. Michael was prized precisely because it had "no communication with any other commodity" (commodity here meaning benefit).

Custom makes virtue common, and common virtue unrewardable. Montaigne says no recompense suits a virtue "once passed into a custom", and doubts whether it can then be called great. Military valour is the example: "Use, education, example, and custom" spread it easily, as the civil wars show. He distinguishes it from the true, perfect, philosophical virtue (a constant force of the soul against all accidents) of which he says valour is only "one little ray". This is his own judgment, offered as a deliberate narrowing of the word virtue to its common acceptation.

Debasement is hard to reverse. He argues it is better to withhold the order from someone due it than to lose the "fruit of so profitable an invention" by lavishness. Those who deserve least now affect to disdain it. A new order cannot be founded in the "tumultuous season", since its rules would need strict curbs the age cannot bear, and the memory of the old order's disgrace must first be forgotten. The institution is thus in a decay that reform cannot start from within; this loosely rhymes with the WMP interest in how institutions lose function (see habit-and-identity for custom's role), though Montaigne offers no cyclical model, only a diagnosis of one order.

Language encodes what a society values. In the closing paragraph he notes that French vaillance derives from valeur and that "a good man" at court means a valiant man, as Latin virtue comes from vis, force. He offers two possible origins (the strong mastering the weak, or warlike nations privileging their familiar virtue) and compares the way a "woman of virtue" has come to mean only a chaste woman, as if men were indifferent to every other fault. He leaves the origin question open; he does not claim to know which explanation is right.

Notable passages

Valueless distinctions (c1). Montaigne calls the invention of vain, in themselves valueless distinctions "a pretty invention", received in most governments. It sets up the essay: honour is symbolic currency that costs nothing yet motivates.

Honour apart from profit (c2). The Order of St. Michael is his case study: it had no greater commodity than that it had no communication with any other commodity, and gentry craved it above any office. Mixing riches in would lower esteem.

Why money is the wrong payment for virtue (c3). Money pays servants and vice alike, so virtue is less eager for it; honour needs rarity.

Common virtue is no virtue (c4). The great tree in a forest of the same, and the Spartan who did not boast of valour. A reward to something customary "is once passed into a custom", and Montaigne wonders whether it can be called great at all.

Valour is one ray of virtue; civil war and unity (c5). Valour is made common by custom, and if all France, Catholic and Huguenot, were united in some common enterprise, its ancient military reputation would flourish again. This is a rare place where the essay touches the Wars of Religion directly, as an observation about how much custom and common purpose, not nature, produce such virtue.

The word 'virtue' and gendered slippage (c6). Etymology as evidence of what a nation prizes, and the parallel with chastity.

Exemplum note: the Augustus anecdote is reported ("They who write the life of Augustus Caesar, observe") and Plutarch is cited only to be set aside; the argument about scarcity is Montaigne's own. Relevant background on his own self-study and form: michel-de-montaigne.
