Writing as himself, then love as a joke of nature: Upon Some Verses of Virgil (middle slice)

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In the middle of 'Upon Some Verses of Virgil', Montaigne defends writing at home and in his own rough idiom as the condition of a book that is exactly himself, confesses his imitative, forgetful mind, then reduces love to bodily appetite and a divine joke on human dignity, condemns the self-invented shames and austerities of men, and argues for delay, reserve and the will's consent in courtship, with comparisons to Italy and France.

This slice opens mid-essay with Montaigne's remarks on how he writes: he avoids the company of books (except Plutarch, whom he plunders), writes at home in a remote country so that the work stays "exactly mine", will correct accidents but not his habitual faults, and admits he is an imitator who picks up whatever he looks at, and whose best thoughts vanish before he can use them. He then turns, "laying books aside", to love and sex, which he reduces to bodily appetite, a mockery by nature or the gods that levels the wise and the fools. He attacks the shame, secrecy and self-torturing austerity that people build around the act and around life itself, and then, praising veiled and delayed Spanish and Italian courtship over French impetuosity, argues that love should serve the will and not just the body, discusses Italian marital constraint against French licence, and advises women (and men) to abstinence or at least discretion. The slice ends on the Alexander and Thalestris anecdote. It is the fourth of five parts and begins and ends mid-argument.

Themes

The book as the self, including its faults. Montaigne says his book's "principal end and perfection is to be exactly mine". He will correct "the faults of inadvertence, not those of custom", because correcting his constant imperfections would be "a kind of treason". He refuses to fight custom with grammar ("they who would fight custom with grammar are triflers") and keeps his Gascon words and risky phrases. The test is fidelity: "Do I not represent myself to the life?" This is a clear statement of the essay as self-portrait and of authenticity as a deliberate editorial rule. It connects with michel-de-montaigne, personal-essay-as-form and self-construction-through-writing, though here the emphasis is on refusing improvement, not on self-construction.

Learning versus natural experience; the anxiety of influence. He says the sciences treat love "after an artificial" way: his page makes love and understands it but not Leo Hebraeus or Ficinus; he would "naturalise art as they artificialise nature". Books "humble and discourage" him, so he keeps them away while writing (the painter of bad cocks, Antigenides), yet he cannot do without Plutarch, from whom he "purloins" legs and wings. This is Montaigne's own self-report, supported by borrowed anecdotes.

The porous, imitative self. He confesses an "apish, imitative quality": early verses showed the last poet he read, he speaks a different language at Paris than at Montaigne, and whatever he looks at he "usurps". The anecdote of Alexander's apes, who ruin themselves by imitating hunters, is borrowed, and he uses it to describe imitation as a danger (vices especially stick). It qualifies the confident self-portrait of the first paragraph: the self he renders is unstable and suggestible, and this is part of why he avoids beaten arguments and picks "every subject" as equally fertile. He also reports that his best thoughts come on horseback and vanish, like dreams he tries and fails to retrieve.

Sex as nature's joke on human pretension. Speaking "more simply and materially", he says love is the thirst of enjoying the desired object, and Venus the pleasure of discharging one's vessels. He reports Socrates' definition and Plato's saying that the gods made man for their sport, and concludes that nature ordered the most agitating and common action "to make us equal", putting "fools and wise men, beasts and us, on a level". Even the most prudent man is "an impudent fellow to pretend to be prudent and contemplative" in that posture. These lines mix his own view ("I believe it to be true") with quoted authorities. He also quotes Alexander that sex and sleep make us know we are mortal.

Shame and self-inflicted misery as cultural machinery. Nature pushes us to the act and then leaves us to call it shameful: "Are we not brutes to call that work brutish which begets us?" He lists the Essenians, Zeno, the Athenians on Delos, people who will not be seen eating, and Turkish ascetics who "purport to grow better by being worse". These are reported examples, not endorsements, and he draws a general moral: "We are only ingenious in using ourselves ill", and humans follow "the laws of thy parish" while ignoring "those of God and the world". This is a version of his custom-as-invisible-law theme (compare habit-and-identity), directed against self-invented rules that offend nature, and it is not a plea for licence in general.

Veiled, delayed love and the consenting will. Montaigne prefers the Spanish and Italian reserve of glances and signs to explicit description (Martial "emasculates" him) and says "Both the action and description should relish of theft". He recommends delay ("without hope and without desire we proceed not worth a pin") and the thrift of favours. He insists that love should serve the will, not a body without affection, and condemns necrophilic examples as the same madness. Hedging runs through this: he writes as an older man with only "the ears" left, adopts what he calls his age's privilege of counsel, and advises abstinence, or failing that modesty and appearances (the Aristippus story).

Custom, constraint and licence across nations. He compares Italy and France through marriage customs: a severe law over Italian women makes them "get on fire" once the fence is broken, while French liberty is also extreme, and he concludes that "no discipline can curb them throughout" and that moderation is needed. He judges that a woman who passes through a school of liberty has more to rest confidence upon than one who passes through strict discipline. National comparisons are brief and impressionistic and do not form a theory.

Notable passages

Writing at home to be "exactly mine" (c2). Montaigne says his remote Gascon setting yields a less polished but more his-own work, and that correcting only accidental errors keeps the portrait true: "all the world knows me in my book, and my book in me."

Books, Plutarch and the painter's cocks (c1). A compact picture of how he uses sources: he avoids books while composing, but plunders Plutarch.

The apish imitator (c3). He admits he catches the manners and style of whoever he studies, and uses Alexander's apes as a cautionary exemplum (borrowed, not his own invention).

Love reduced to appetite, and the gods' sport (c4). The turn to the "simple and material" view of love, with Socrates and Plato cited, ends on the claim that the gods made man for their sport.

Using ourselves ill (c5). His moral conclusion after the catalogue of shameful or ascetic nations and sects: human intelligence is "a dangerous tool" when misapplied, and people obey invented rules and ignore nature's.

Delay and the long galleries of love (c6). A statement of his aesthetic of courtship: prefer the unhurried approach, since pleasure grows with the steps and degrees. Compare the modern language of desire and delay with the Renaissance courtly background, though the text only supports what Montaigne says here.
