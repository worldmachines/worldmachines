Montaigne on his own body: sleep, table, regimen and ageing (Of Experience, middle slice)

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In the middle of 'Of Experience', Montaigne turns his own body into evidence, cataloguing his sleep, diet, drinking, senses and ageing habits, and argues that a loose, pleasure-led regimen, a mind that stays calm, and a natural, gradual death serve health better than rigid medical rules or the habit-forming gradation of precautions.

This slice of 'Of Experience' (it begins and ends mid-essay) is a sustained self-inventory in which Montaigne offers his own body as the test case for what experience teaches. It opens with his sleeping habits and a short praise of the military life, then moves through his health, his dreams, his upbringing at table, his compassion for the weak, his eating, drinking and fasting habits, his failing senses and his restless limbs. Running underneath is a consistent view: follow appetite and the present moment, distrust invariable rules, keep the mind tranquil, and accept decay as natural. Montaigne is reporting himself, not legislating, and he repeatedly hedges ("I do not yet certainly know", "whether useful or no, I have heard disputed"). Related pages: michel-de-montaigne, habit-and-identity, personal-essay-as-form.

Themes

The self as the only dependable specimen. Almost every paragraph is first-person report: he sleeps eight or nine hours, can stand all day but tires before he is warm, rides rather than walks in mud, eats greedily, bites his tongue and fingers, and cannot sit still ("though I was seated, I was never settled"). Authorities (Plato, Epicurus, Augustus, Favorinus, Democritus) appear as foils or comparisons, not as proof; he sets his own practice beside theirs and sometimes says plainly that it differs. This is the essay as a technology of self-knowledge, though here knowledge of the self is mostly physical. See self-construction-through-writing only loosely: the writing here is inventory rather than self-making.

Against invariable rules; for liberty and the present. On fasting and clothing he argues that any fixed form hardens into dependence: "I avoid the invariable in these laws of fasting", because "all you have got is the loss of your liberty of doing otherwise but to your prejudice." His account of the warm cap that becomes a quilted hat, then a hare's skin, then a cap under the hat, is a compact model of how precaution accumulates and traps ("there is no end on't"). He puts it as a rule of his own temperament, not a universal; he also reports that he has changed his own mind on whether to dine (formerly he skipped dinner, now finds that digestion is better while awake). This resonates with habit-and-identity, where habit is the invisible machinery; here Montaigne uses habit as something to be kept loose on purpose, and he says the body's tastes themselves shift (radishes, white wine and claret).

Custom as the default, with a self-aware exception. He says "The most usual and common method of living is the most becoming; all particularity, in my opinion, is to be avoided" and "Public usage gives the law in these things" even while cataloguing his own oddities (salt-free bread contrary to local custom, wine mixed ahead of time by his father's physician's prescription). He does not reconcile the two; the reader should not flatten them into a single doctrine.

Upbringing: plainness and sympathy for the weak. His father had him raised among the poorest in a village and chose godparents of the meanest fortune to attach him to those "who most need our assistance". Montaigne says the design did not fail entirely: he feels a natural inclination towards ordinary people, would condemn a faction more sharply when flourishing than when overthrown, and follows Flaminius (helps those in need) rather than Pyrrhus (truckles to the great). Cheilonis, who switches sides to whichever stands in need of her, is an exemplum he admires, not a rule he states for politics. Note he is carefully reporting a disposition, not a political programme.

Mind and body: a tranquil mind as ballast. He says his mind was pleasant during a quartan ague, that physicians wrongly read his complexion as a secret passion of the mind, and that he fears "a thousand passions and agitations of the mind" more than bodily faintings. He thinks the temperature of his soul has often raised his body from its lapses. He also admits the mind's superiority is not matched: "If my body would govern itself as well, according to my rule, as my mind does, we should move a little more at our ease."

Death arriving by degrees. The central, most quotable theme: decay is already death mixed into life. A tooth fell without pain; parts of him are dead or half dead; he "melts and steals away" from himself. He takes consolation that his death will be "just and natural", cites Solon's threescore and ten against the myth of longer ancient lives, and applies his love of the mean to length of life. He reports Plato's view that death by age is the easiest. The portraits comparison ("how many times is it no longer me") puts a changing self against a stable image, a theme worth tracking against self-portraiture.

Sleep, dreams, appetite, society. He is sceptical but open about dreams ("there is art required to sort and understand them"; he does not know whether Plato is right about divination from dreams, but notes the testimony of Socrates, Xenophon and Aristotle). At table, he prefers the society to the dish ("no sauce so appetising, as that which is extracted from society"), sides with Epicurus on that point, and differs from him on fasting, which he uses to heighten the pleasure of abundance rather than to wean himself from it.

The military life. A short, apparently enthusiastic passage praises soldiering as noble in cause and execution, then turns it: death is more abject in bed than in a fight, and "Vivere, mi Lucili, militare est" (the Latin line is quoted; Montaigne's point is that anyone who has braced himself for common life need not raise his courage to be a soldier). This is Montaigne's own survey of an occupation's pleasures, not a straightforward endorsement of war; compare how elsewhere (outside this slice) he treats the Wars of Religion, which this slice touches only in the condemnation of the flourishing faction.

Notable passages

Death mixes with life. The paragraph beginning "Death mixes and confounds itself throughout with life" argues that decay anticipates its hour, uses the portraits at twenty-five and thirty-five as evidence, and ends with a complaint against forcing nature to run so far that she must hand us over "to the mercy of a foreign and haggard countenance" and to art (medicine). It is the clearest statement here of the natural/artificial contrast.

Dying by degrees. "God is favourable to those whom He makes to die by degrees" brings together the tooth falling out, the melting away of the self, the consolation of a natural death and the appeal to the mean.

The slippery gradation of precautions. The passage from "I avoid the invariable in these laws of fasting" to "something more after that" gives his general case against forming a regimen: habit makes the body demand ever more.

His father's plan. "If I had any sons, I should willingly wish them my fortune" and the account of his nursing among the poor ties his tastes and sympathies to an educational design, and ties both to leaving formation "to fortune, under popular and natural laws".

Spectacles and the unnoticed decline. The paragraph on his eyes and hearing observes that each loss comes so gently that "I shall be stark blind before I shall be sensible of the age and decay of my sight", and that "A man must screw up his soul to a high pitch to make it sensible how it ebbs away." It is a small piece of phenomenology: gradual change is invisible to the one undergoing it.

Mind over body. The paragraph on the quartan ague and the physicians' misdiagnosis shows his claim that the soul's temperament can carry the body, and his refusal to complain of natural decadence (the Latin line asks who wonders at a swollen throat in the Alps), just as he does not regret that he will not last as long as an oak.
