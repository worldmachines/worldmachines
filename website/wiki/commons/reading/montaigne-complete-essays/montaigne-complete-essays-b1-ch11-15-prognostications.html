Prognostications, constancy, ceremony, and the limits of valour

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Five short early essays in which Montaigne mocks divination and almanac-prophecy while admitting his own sudden inklings, redefines constancy as judgment held firm under involuntary fear, shrugs at princely ceremony, and weighs the limits of valour and the proper punishment of surrender and cowardice.

This unit gathers five brief essays from the early Essays. In "Of Prognostications" Montaigne notes that oracles were discredited even before Christianity and that the church has abolished most divination, then ridicules the survivors (astrology, almanacs, obscure prophetic books) as a hunt for lucky hits among countless misses, while conceding that he himself has had Socrates-like inklings that served him well. "Of Constancy" argues that constancy does not mean refusing to dodge or fear danger; he reports the Stoic view that the first shock of fear is involuntary and that what counts is withholding consent. "The Ceremony of the Interview of Princes" treats courtesy as local convention that he follows lightly, refusing to be "enslaved to ceremonies". The last two essays turn to military justice: valour has bounds beyond which it becomes folly, so those who obstinately hold an indefensible fort may justly be punished, and cowardice (as distinct from treachery) is better punished by shame than by death, though he hedges that disgrace may make men desperate.

Themes

Prophecy as confirmation bias, with Montaigne's own exception. Montaigne's own judgment (not a reported opinion) is that almanac-makers "stumble upon a truth amongst an infinite number of lies", that nobody records the failed predictions, and that obscure, ambiguous prophecy lets "posterity" interpret it "according to its own fancy". The Diogenes anecdote (a borrowed exemplum) supplies the survivorship-bias argument: the drowned are not in the votive paintings. Yet he does not flatten this into pure rationalism: he allows that Socrates' daimon may have been an impulse "which obtruded itself upon him without the advice or consent of his judgment", and reports his own weak-in-reason but violent-in-persuasion promptings that served him well. He says he would sooner regulate his affairs by a die, and notes that republics, and Plato's model one, leave much to lot. Prediction is also shown as politically consequential: the Marquis of Saluzzo is said, as it was reported, to have defected from Francis I out of terror of favourable prophecies for Charles V. Interest for the WMP frame: forecasting as a social force that can fulfil itself, and the problem of telling signal from retrospectively remembered hits; this resonates with the project's insistence on separating falsifiable claims from interpretive framing, though Montaigne is sceptical of the whole enterprise where WMP is not.

Constancy is judgment, not insensibility. Montaigne defines constancy as bravely suffering what cannot be avoided, while taking every honest means of avoiding harm; flight (Scythians, Spartans at Plataea, per Plato's Socrates) can be fortitude. Against the soldier's pride in not flinching he reports cannon-shot anecdotes and then states, for his own part, that he starts at a sudden harquebuse. He attributes to the Stoics (a reported view he uses, not necessarily endorses) the distinction between the involuntary first impression and consent, and between the philosopher, in whom fear does not reach the seat of reason, and the ordinary man, whose fear corrupts judgment. He notes the Peripatetic moderates rather than exempts himself from perturbation. This is the body-and-self-knowledge strand: reason and bodily reflex as separable layers.

Custom and civility as light machinery. The ceremony essay is a small study of conventions of precedence that differ by country, city and society (who waits for whom, who goes out to meet whom). Montaigne's stance: know the forms, follow them, but refuse servitude to them, since "it is much better to offend him once than myself every day". Courtesy still matters: it "begets liking" and opens the door to learning from others. A mild instance of habit and convention as invisible structure; compare habit-and-identity, though here the point is chosen, partial conformity rather than critique.

Virtue has limits, and rules of war are a legibility device. Valour past its bounds becomes temerity; wartime custom punishes obstinate defence of an untenable place so that no henroost would resist an army. Montaigne then complicates the rule: what counts as "tenable" is judged relative to the attacker's force and his pride, so a conqueror's self-regard can turn the standard into a pretext for slaughter, and the conclusion is a bleak maxim about falling into the hands of a victorious enemy judge. In the cowardice essay he distinguishes faults of infirmity from those of treachery and malice, mentions the view that only acts against conscience are fairly punishable (and its use by opponents of capital punishment for heretics), surveys ancient and recent penalties (Charondas' shaming in woman's dress; degradation of nobility), and worries that disgrace makes delinquents "desperate". He ends by allowing that extreme, unexampled cowardice may be taken as proof of treachery. Hedged and unresolved rather than a thesis. Faint resonance with Legibility in the sense of rules that make conduct judgeable, though the text is about military law, not state-scale measurement.

Notable passages

Die over dreams (c1). "I, for my part, should sooner regulate my affairs by the chance of a die"; the chance-in-government point and Plato's lot-based marriages follow, as an observation that republics have always handed part of governance to chance.

Almanacs and the drowned (c2). The core argument against prognostication: random hits are remembered, misses are not, and the Diogenes reply about missing portraits of the shipwrecked.

Riddling prophecy and the daimon (c3). Montaigne's eyewitness report of men in public confusion abandoning reason for the stars, his diagnosis that obscurity lets readers find what they want, and then the Socratic concession about his own sudden inclinations.

Stoic fear (c4). The first impression versus consent, philosopher versus ordinary man; the best statement in the unit of constancy as sound judgment surviving a body that trembles.

Valour's bounds (c5). Opening of the obstinate-fort essay: virtue shading into vice at hard-to-discern confines, and the rationale for punishing the untenable defence.

Infirmity versus malice (c6). The cowardice essay's central distinction and its link to conscience and the punishment of heretics.
