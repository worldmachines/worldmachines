Against measuring the possible by one's own capacity

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne argues that dismissing reports as false or impossible merely because they exceed our own comprehension is arrogant folly, since custom rather than knowledge makes familiar things seem ordinary, and he closes by applying the same humility to religious obedience in the civil wars.

Montaigne opens with a metaphor of belief as a seal pressed into a soul, easier to impress the softer the wax, which explains why children, the common people, women and the sick are readily persuaded. He then turns the point against the opposite vice: the presumption of those who "fancy themselves wiser than their neighbours" and condemn as false whatever seems improbable to them. He confesses he was once one of these, pitying believers in ghosts, prophecies and witchcraft, and reports that he changed his mind by reason, not by new experience. He then argues from custom (familiar marvels seem unremarkable, and would seem incredible if newly presented), from the limits of our scale of reference (the man who thinks the first river he sees is the sea), and from testimony (Froissart, Plutarch, Pliny, and especially St. Augustine's miracle reports). The essay ends with a sharp turn to the religious troubles of his day: Catholics who concede articles of belief as indifferent weaken their position, and Montaigne reports his own experience of finding neglected Church rules well grounded.

Themes

Credulity and incredulity are twin errors. Montaigne first grants that softness of mind makes people easy to persuade, then insists that blanket rejection is equally presumptuous. His remedy is suspension: of unlikely things attested by trustworthy people, "we ought at least to leave them in suspense". He invokes the maxim "Ne quid nimis" attributed to Chilo. The hedge matters: he does not say believe everything; he rejects Bouchet's relic miracles as unauthoritative while refusing a general, "offhand" condemnation of all such stories.

Self-report as evidence. The shift is autobiographical. He was once the sceptical pitier of the credulous, and now finds himself the one "to be pitied". Notably he says experience did not alter his opinions; reason taught him that to deny something impossible is "to circumscribe and limit the will of God, and the power of our mother nature, within the bounds of my own capacity". The theological framing (God's will, nature's power) is Montaigne's own, and carries the argument.

Custom, not knowledge, removes strangeness. This is the essay's most portable idea. Our teachers lead us to knowledge "groping in the dark", and it is "custom" that dissolves wonder; things we see daily we neither admire nor ask the causes of. The Lucretian verse on the river, tree and man (each person takes the largest he has seen as the largest that exists) makes the epistemic point that our sample is the limit we mistake for nature's. This rhymes with habit-and-identity and with the question of how familiarity makes infrastructure invisible, though Montaigne's concern is epistemic humility rather than any theory of eras.

Testimony and the weight of authority. Montaigne sets the instance of news travelling faster than messengers (the Comte de Foix in Froissart, Pope Honorius, and Plutarch and Caesar on reports preceding events) against the schoolboy confidence that corrects Pliny. He asks whether anyone alive can match Augustine and the bishops Aurelius and Maximinus in virtue, piety, learning and judgment, and whether we should charge them with ignorance, simplicity or imposture. The argument is a challenge posed to the reader as a rhetorical dilemma, not a claim that the miracles occurred.

Limits of truth set by oneself bind oneself. Once you fix the borders of truth and error by your own understanding and are then forced to believe stranger things, you must abandon your own limits. He adds that yesterday's articles of faith are today's fables, and closes with the aphorism that glory and curiosity are the "scourges of the soul", one pushing us into everything, the other forbidding any matter to be left undecided.

Religious position in the civil wars. Montaigne's political-religious note is that partial concession of doctrine is a strategic and conscientious error: one must submit wholly to ecclesiastical authority or throw it off entirely, not decide "what and how much obedience we owe to it". Here his scepticism yields conservative fideism rather than liberal picking and choosing. Notably, he derives this from the same humility as the earlier argument, and cites a personal test (neglected rules proved well founded after discussion with learned men).

Notable passages

The seal-and-balance image and the claim about who is most led "by the ears" set up the essay's first pole. The pivot to the second pole is the confession that he himself used to pity the credulous. The central methodological passage is the claim that condemnation as "false and impossible" circumscribes God and nature. The custom and river passages supply the epistemology. The Augustine passage tests the reader's willingness to call the best witnesses fools or frauds. The final passage turns the principle onto the Church disputes of the day, ending on the pairing of glory and curiosity as soul's scourges.

The essay may be read alongside michel-de-montaigne's wider sceptical stance, though in this chapter the scepticism cuts against dismissal of the unusual rather than against belief, and it ends in deference to authority.
