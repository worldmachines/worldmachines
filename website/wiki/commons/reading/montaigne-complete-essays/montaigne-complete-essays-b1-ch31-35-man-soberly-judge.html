Divine Judgement, Pleasure and Death, Fortune's Art, a Public Registry, and Clothes as Custom (Essays I.31-35)

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Five short essays in which Montaigne warns against reading God's will into worldly outcomes, reports ancient and Christian precedents for welcoming death over pleasure, catalogues Fortune's uncanny knack for out-doing reason, sketches his father's proposal for a public registry of wants, and argues that custom alone, not nature, makes clothing seem necessary.

Five brief essays. In I.31 Montaigne argues that unknown things are the natural territory of imposture and that men who claim to decode God's motives from events are among the impostors; faith should not be propped on success. I.32 collects the ancients' view that life may be left when it holds more ill than good, then reports Seneca's counsel to Lucilius and the story of St. Hilary and his daughter as cases of pushing contempt of life against worldly goods. I.33 strings together anecdotes in which Fortune seems to act with more judgement than human prudence. I.34 relays his father's idea of a public office where wants and offers are registered, plus his father's household journal. I.35 reasons that humans are naturally fitted for exposure and that custom alone makes clothing seem indispensable.

Themes

Ignorance as the ground of credulity (I.31). Montaigne, citing Plato, says confidence flourishes where the audience cannot check: "nothing is so firmly believed, as what we least know." He ranks alchemists, astrologers, fortune-tellers and physicians together and would, "if I durst," add interpreters of God's designs. This is Montaigne's own judgement, delivered with a hedge, and it is a sceptical move aimed at theological over-reading rather than at faith itself.

Faith should not be audited by events (I.31). For a Christian it is enough "to believe that all things come from God". Montaigne objects to supporting religion by the prosperity of enterprises, and points to the contemporary religious war: those who boasted of Rochelabeille as proof, then explained Moncontour and Jarnac as fatherly scourges, "blow hot and cold." He also notes the Arian deaths as an example anyone could extend arbitrarily (to Heliogabalus, to Irenaeus), so such patterns prove nothing. The close is a fideist posture: be content with the light the sun gives, and whoever reaches for more may lose his sight. The Latin tag (Quis hominum potest scire consilium Dei) is a quoted authority, not his own prose.

Leaving life rather than being held by pleasures (I.32). The opening reports "most of the opinions of the ancients" that it is time to die when life holds more ill than good. The novelty Montaigne says he found in Seneca (and, to his surprise, from Epicurus to Idomeneus) is applying this to honours and riches: leave that life "or life itself", preferably by untying the knot but if necessary breaking it. He then offers St. Hilary, who prayed for the death of his only daughter to save her from worldly marriage, and whose wife then asked to die too. Montaigne's framing is that this "seems to outdo" the pagan cases; he reports it with "Christian moderation" and does not endorse or condemn it explicitly in this unit. The exempla are borrowed; the comparison is his.

Fortune as artist, physician and judge (I.33). Montaigne lists coincidences: the poisoned wine that killed the Pope and sickened Valentinois; the lady's husband captured on his wedding day; Constantine founding and ending Constantinople; walls that fall by miracle in one siege and drop back intact in another (so Fortune also mocks miracles); Jason cured by a wound; Protogenes' sponge; Isabel driven to a safe port; the son who killed Timoleon's would-be assassin. The refrain is "Fortune has more judgement than we" (a verse attributed to Menander in the text). The essay does not argue a thesis in prose; it assembles evidence that outcomes can look rational without intention. Read beside I.31, it shows the same scepticism cutting both ways: events look like justice, yet he refuses to read them as divine verdicts.

A defect in government: no clearing-house for needs (I.34). His father proposed a public place where a clerk would register wants and offers (pearl buyers, travellers, servants, masters, artisans), since "conditions...hunt after one another" and fail through ignorance of each other. Montaigne links this to two learned men who died in poverty because their need was unknown. This is a small design for information infrastructure, and for matching without a market-maker; it rhymes with the project's interest in legibility and coordination, though here the aim is charity and convenience, not control. He also records his father's household journal and regrets neglecting that custom himself.

Custom, not nature, makes clothing necessary (I.35). Montaigne begins by saying that speaking on this "invade[s] some of the bounds of custom". He reasons that other creatures are clothed by nature, so humans are not likely the sole creatures left defective, that exposed faces and hands show the body can bear air, that nations of the same or colder climates go unclothed, and that "'tis custom only which renders that impossible that otherwise is nothing so." A long run of exempla follows (the beggar "all face," Massinissa, Agesilaus, Caesar, Hannibal, Egyptians' harder skulls per Herodotus, Poles' king). He admits his own habit: he cannot endure to go unbuttoned. The late sections turn to extreme cold and its military consequences, and the essay ends with the Mexican king changing apparel four times a day, which sits uneasily with the "natural" argument. This is a good small case of habit-and-identity: the learned body mistakes habit for nature.

Notable passages

Imposture and the unknown. The opening of I.31 states the sceptical principle: strangeness lends credit, and distance from "our ordinary reasons" removes the means to dispute. It is Montaigne's own diagnosis, with Plato as support.

Against reading providence into outcomes. The Rochelabeille passage is a concrete critique of partisan providentialism in the wars of religion: success as proof becomes a trap when luck turns. He wants "the solid and real foundations of truth" instead.

Humble ending of I.31. Montaigne turns to the image of the sun's rays: the person who strains for greater light loses his sight. It is an image of limits on human reason.

St. Hilary and the daughter. A strange, borrowed exemplum of withdrawing from pleasures at any cost. Montaigne emphasises that Hilary acts "at the outset" where Seneca's counsel is a last resort, and that it concerned his only daughter. His wife's later request closes the story with "mutual content." The unit offers no commentary on whether this is admirable.

Fortune as artist. The doubled sequence of Fortune as physician and painter makes the claim concrete: accident does what skill could not.

Father's registry. A plain proposal with a plain rationale: needs and means go unmatched for want of knowing. It is the nearest thing in the unit to an institutional design.

Custom and the body. The beggar's reply and the Duke of Florence's fool are the anecdotes where custom's grip becomes visible; the passage on custom as the sole thing making clothing "impossible" to do without is the argument's core. Compare michel-de-montaigne more broadly on custom as invisible machinery.
