Gods in Our Image, Science as Poetry: the Apology's Attack on Human Presumption (middle slice)

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In the middle of the Apology, Montaigne argues that human beings make God, gods and the heavens in their own image, that philosophy and astronomy are plausible poetry rather than knowledge of what is, and that we do not even know our own bodies or what lies at our feet.

This slice of the Apology for Raimond Sebond (the ninth of fifteen) begins mid-argument, with the claim that we "make the Divinity to pass through our sieve", and runs through three linked sweeps. First, a catalogue of how humans fashion gods to their own measure: philosophers who bind God to destiny, a Stoic-style syllogism that makes the gods our companions, pagan deification, divine seductions and gods with departments, and the generalisation that every creature (Xenophanes' goose) ranks the universe by its own kind. Second, a turn to "human and natural things": the heavens are described with the coachmaker's axles and wheels, and philosophy is recast as "sophisticated poetry" that presents what is plausible, not what is. Third, the same charge is brought against anatomy and psychology, and the slice closes on Thales and the Milesian girl: we are as ignorant of what is under our feet as of the stars. The slice ends there; it does not itself reach the essay's conclusion.

Themes

Anthropomorphism as the engine of religion and theology. Montaigne holds that arrogance makes us weigh God on our scales: because events matter to us we conclude they matter to him; because our labours burden us, Strato exempts the gods from office. The theme is stated sharply as "‘Tis far from honouring him who made us, to honour him that we have made." He reports (and mocks) a chain of reasoning in which the world's having produced us proves it has soul and reason, and "we are companions" of God; he attributes the whole pattern to men who "imagine themselves in his stead". This is mostly reported opinion held up for ridicule, with Montaigne's own verdict in the framing sentences. He then states the general mechanism: everything "assimilates the qualities of all other things to its own proper qualities", so we cannot imagine past our own form. The Christian counter-position is carried by a quoted St. Paul and by Montaigne's assertion that God's condition "is above the order of nature" and that "our ways lie too low"; this is the fideist half of the double move (a hedge: he attacks reason's reach, not belief as such).

Gods as human institutions. The long run of exempla (Augustus with more temples than Jupiter, Agesilaus refusing the Thracians' offer of deification, the sexton dicing with Hercules, Paulina and Serapis, Plato's divine parentage, Caunians chasing foreign gods with swords, the six and thirty thousand specialised gods) are borrowed from the ancient sources and serve one point: religion divides the divine like a municipal bureaucracy. Resonates loosely with WMP's interest in institutions as the infrastructure of belief, though Montaigne's target is human presumption, not any machine's lifecycle. He also reports without endorsing Scævola's and Varro's view that people must be ignorant of many truths and believe many falsehoods.

Philosophy and science as plausible fiction. The heavens are fitted with gold axles and silver spokes, as if there had been "coachmakers, carpenters, and painters" up there. Montaigne's own claim follows: nature should "lay open her bosom to us", and then "what abuse, what mistakes should we discover in our poor science!" Philosophy is "sophisticated poetry"; science, like cosmetics or legal fiction, hands us as "current pay" things it admits were invented, such as epicycles, eccentrics and concentrics. Plato is quoted as admitting only "most likely to be true". This is a sharp early statement that models are made, not found, which rhymes with WMP's own modelling modesty (the Oracle as an editable, non-final model), though Montaigne draws a sceptical, not a constructive, conclusion. Archimedes' red-hot iron sun and Anaxagoras' burning stone show the best authorities contradicting one another and common observation.

The human body and soul as another invented machine. Anatomists have as much "retrogradation, trepidation, accession, recession" in the little human body as in the heavens, dividing the soul into parts and places; "they possess it not". The contrast with painters makes the point: we forgive vagueness about remote islands but demand exactness for what is familiar, and on that standard the sciences of man fail. A faint link to the legibility theme: the body is rendered readable by a scheme of offices and vocations, and Montaigne judges the scheme "botched with a thousand false and fantastic patches". Do not read this as a modern critique of legibility; the target is the learned and their inventions.

Turn the gaze to what is at your feet. Thales stumbling over the Milesian girl's obstacle, Democritus' line, and Socrates in Plato close the slice: "every philosopher is ignorant of what his neighbour does; aye, and of what he does himself". Montaigne also endorses Socrates' view, "and mine too", that "the best judging of heaven is not to judge of it at all", and says he will depart "more ignorant of all other things than my own ignorance". This anticipates the self-knowledge turn of the Essays (see michel-de-montaigne and socratic-thinking), but the slice itself says knowing ourselves is as hard as knowing the stars, not that it is an easy refuge.

Notable passages

The making of gods. The sentence "‘Tis far from honouring him who made us, to honour him that we have made" and the quip "he cannot make a worm, and yet he will be making gods by dozens" are the slice's sharpest compressions of the anthropomorphism thesis, with Agesilaus' retort to the Thracians as its exemplum.

Xenophanes and the goose. The goose who believes the whole universe exists for it, followed by "So flattering and wheedling a bawd is nature to herself", is the humorous centre of the argument: species-centred perspective is built into every knower. It is borrowed from Xenophanes via Montaigne's sources, but he adopts it.

Scævola and Varro on useful falsehood. A rare place where the Apology states the political use of false religion; Montaigne reports it as what these men "make us" as an excuse, and does not himself endorse it.

Coachmakers in the heavens. The image of artisans fitting wheelwork behind the sky, and the wish that nature would show her workings, anchor the sceptical claim about cosmology.

Philosophy as sophisticated poetry. The cosmetics simile (ivory teeth, cotton padding, paint) and the list of epicycles, eccentrics and concentrics give the slice's most quotable account of theory as useful invention, closing with Plato's honest "most likely to be true".

Thales and the Milesian girl. A short anecdote converted by Socrates into the claim that each philosopher is ignorant of his neighbour, of himself, and "whether beasts or men". It ends the slice.
