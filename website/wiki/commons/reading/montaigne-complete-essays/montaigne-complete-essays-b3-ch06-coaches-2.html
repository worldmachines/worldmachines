Of Coaches (second half): the discovery of another world and the shame of its conquest

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In the second half of "Of Coaches", Montaigne moves from the limits of human knowledge to the New World, arguing that the Americans were the Spaniards' equals in understanding and industry and their superiors in virtue, and that the conquest was won by technological surprise and treachery and conducted with a cruelty that betrays both justice and the religion invoked for it.

This slice opens by finishing a digression on extravagant excesses of earlier ages ("such excesses" and "vanities") and turns it into an argument about the narrowness of human knowledge: we see neither far forward nor far backward, we make a great fuss over inventions (artillery, printing) that the Chinese had a thousand years ago, and we mistake our own decrepitude for the decline of the world. From there Montaigne pivots to "another world" lately discovered, which he calls an infant, and spends the rest of the slice on the conquest of Mexico and Peru: what the Americans were, how they were beaten, what the Spaniards did, two set-piece anecdotes of captive kings, a speculative account of why the gold yielded so little, the Mexican doctrine of five suns, the great Peruvian highway, and a return, finally, to the "coaches" of the title, since the Peruvian king was carried on a litter of gold rather than driven. The slice begins mid-essay and ends with the capture of the last Peruvian king; it does not contain the essay's opening (on coaches and the ills of great expense) and I describe only what is here.

Themes

The weakness of our knowledge as a ground for rules. Montaigne says that our knowledge is "a wretched foundation whereon to ground our rules, and that represents to us a very false image of things". He applies this to historical pessimism: we conclude the world is in decline from our own weakness, just as the poet (the poet is unnamed in this slice; his lines are quoted in Latin) concluded its youth from the vigour of his own age. Both inferences are called vain. The argument is sceptical in the manner of the Apology: a local vantage mistaken for the shape of the whole. It is also a rejection of a single story of decline or progress, and in this sense sits near history-machine thinking (no one sees the machine whole) though Montaigne is not proposing a structural theory.

The New World as a second, younger world, and a mirror. Montaigne is tentative about whether it is "the last of its brothers", and fears that "we have greatly precipitated its declension and ruin by our contagion". His claim of parity is explicit: their answers and negotiations show they "were nothing behind us in pertinency and clearness of natural understanding"; the gold gardens and cities of Cusco and Mexico show they were not inferior in industry; and in devotion, law-keeping, goodness, liberality, loyalty and plain dealing they exceeded the Europeans, which is exactly what ruined them. Courage and constancy in the face of pain and death he would match against the most famous ancient examples. This is the same relativist move as in his essay on cannibals (he refers back to it as "my CANNIBALS"), here turned from curiosity to indictment. Compare the later idealisation of the savage in noble-savage; Montaigne's version is more specific and more self-accusing, and he also calls their reply to the Spaniards "the babble of these children", so the condescension is not absent.

Conquest explained by disparity, not merit. Remove the horses, steel, cannon and the surprise of strangers arriving under colour of friendship, and "you take away all the occasion of so many victories". Montaigne explicitly separates technological and tactical advantage from worth, and says an equal-terms contest would have been hard.

What might have been. He asks why the conquest did not fall to Alexander or the ancient Greeks and Romans, who would have "gently levelled" what was savage and mixed in Greek and Roman virtues with native ones. The hypothetical is Montaigne's own, and it is a counterfactual with its own idealisation of antiquity, since he assumes the ancients would have been gentler; the text states this as a wish, not a demonstration. Instead, the Europeans taught treachery, luxury, avarice and cruelty "by the pattern and example of our manners", and he closes the paragraph with the phrase "Mechanic victories!", the trade in pearl and pepper as the motive for turning half the world upside down.

Religion and law as pretexts, and a quiet critique of the Requirement. The Spaniards' formula (a distant king, the Pope's donation of the Indies, tribute, belief in one God, threats) and the natives' reply, which points out that a man who gives away what is not his must love dissension, and that they will not change a religion they have long and happily lived in, are given at length. Montaigne's own gloss is ironic: "A fair example of the babble of these children." His own position on the conquerors' piety is stated directly: faith spreads by gaining men, not territory, and the massacres could not have been for justice or religious zeal. He also notes that many captains were put to death by order of the kings of Castile, and says that God "meritoriously permitted" the plunder to be swallowed by the sea or by the conquerors' civil wars.

Two captive kings. The last king of Peru is ransomed at an enormous weight of gold, then falsely accused, baptised so as to be strangled instead of burned, and mourned in counterfeit. The king of Mexico, tortured with a nobleman, rebukes his fellow sufferer, "And what dost thou think I suffer? am I in a bath? am I more at ease than thou?" Both are told as exempla of constancy that shame the tormentors. Montaigne says that the Spaniards themselves narrate such atrocities, "they not only own it, but boast of it"; the source for these accounts is Europeans' own reports, not native testimony, and he marks one detail (the Mexican cosmology) as coming from "my author".

Gold, coin and circulation. Montaigne explains the disappointing revenue from the Indies by saying that, with no coin, the Americans' gold was hoarded as ornament, whereas "our gold is always in motion and traffic". This is a small, concrete observation about money as circulation, hedged as an explanation of the disappointing yield, and he adds the barb that European kings could hoard too.

The Mexican five ages. The Mexicans, "in some sort more civilised", believed, as Europeans do, that the world was near its end, and read the Spaniards' desolation as the sign. Their five suns (flood, falling heavens with giants, fire, wind with men turned to baboons, and the present fifth) are summarised, and Montaigne's comment is a dry sceptical aside: "What impressions will not the weakness of human belief admit?" He then notes that a fourth change coincides with an astrologers' conjunction some eight hundred years before. This is a cyclical-age scheme that rhymes with the Dawn/Day/Dusk periodisation of world-machines only loosely (those are infrastructural lifecycles, these are mythic destructions), and Montaigne treats it as a curiosity of belief, not as evidence.

Peruvian infrastructure and the return to coaches. Having engaged in the discourse on account of "pomp and magnificence", he says no work of Greece, Rome or Egypt compares with the highway from Quito to Cusco, three hundred leagues, built without scaffolding or beasts of burden. The title theme returns when he notes that the Peruvians had no carriages and carried themselves on shoulders, and the last king was borne on golden staves; the sedan-bearers kept replacing the fallen until a horseman seized him.

Notable passages

The unit's relativist and anti-decline argument. In the first paragraph Montaigne makes one of his favourite sceptical points, that our reasoning about the age of the world derives from our own condition, and puts the printing press and artillery in a Chinese perspective: "We make a mighty business of the invention of artillery and printing, which other men at the other end of the world, in China, had a thousand years ago." Compare the Eurocentric priority claims in eisenstein-printing-revolution.

An infant world and its ruin. The paragraph beginning "Our world has lately discovered another" is the hinge: the New World as young, as equal in understanding, and as damaged by European contagion. The nuance is that the equality is asserted of understanding and industry, the superiority of the Americans in the moral virtues, and the temporary advantage of Europe in nothing but arms and deceit.

The lament for a conquest that might have been gentle, and the verdict on it. The "Why did not so noble a conquest fall under Alexander" paragraph is both counterfactual and accusation, ending on the cost in cities, nations and millions, all for pearl and pepper.

The Spaniards' address and the reply. A rare reported speech from the conquered side, rendered in Montaigne's text with dry humour; the reply identifies the Pope's gift as a gift of what was not his own.

The two kings. Paired narratives of constancy under torture and treacherous judgement, where Montaigne's own indignation shows in phrases like "the virtuous sentence of those very men who had by this treachery conspired his ruin".

Five suns. A compact doxography of another people's cosmology, framed with scepticism toward all human belief including, by implication, the Europeans' own.
