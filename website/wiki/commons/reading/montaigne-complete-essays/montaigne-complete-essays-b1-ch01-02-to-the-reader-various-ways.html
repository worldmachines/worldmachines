Preface, Of Various Ways to the Same End, and Of Sorrow

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne's preface declares the book a private self-portrait, then two opening essays test how conquerors respond to submission versus courage (concluding that man is too unstable a subject for uniform judgment) and why extreme sorrow, unlike moderate grief, strikes the soul dumb.

Three openings of the Essays. The address "The Author to the Reader" presents the book as a domestic, private memorial for kinsfolk and friends, with no aim at the reader's service or the author's glory, and names its subject: Montaigne himself. Chapter I ("That Men by Various Ways Arrive at the Same End") opens with a claim that both submission and defiant courage can disarm a victor's anger, backs it with a run of anecdotes (Edward Prince of Wales at Limoges, Scanderbeg, Conrad III and the duchess-carriers, Pelopidas and Epaminondas, Dionysius and Phyton), and then turns the argument against itself with Pompey, Sylla, and above all Alexander at Gaza and Thebes, concluding that man is a "vain, fickle, and unstable subject". Chapter II ("Of Sorrow") begins with Montaigne's self-report that he is almost free of sorrow and thinks it a vice, and then spends most of its length on a counter-observation: overwhelming grief, like overwhelming love or joy, silences and stuns rather than eloquently laments.

Themes

The book as self-portrait, not performance. In the preface Montaigne disclaims fame and "borrowed beauties" and says he wishes to be viewed "in mine own genuine, simple, and ordinary manner, without study and artifice". The stated purpose is that friends, "having lost me", may recover "some traits of my conditions and humours". He adds a hedge: he paints himself so far as "public reverence hath permitted me", and would have painted himself "quite naked" had he lived among nations under "nature's primitive laws". This is the founding statement of the essay as self-study; it resonates with michel-de-montaigne, self-construction-through-writing and personal-essay-as-form, though here the motive is commemorative and modest, and the self-construction claim is only implicit. The nature/artifice contrast (and the sideways glance at primitive peoples, compare noble-savage) is a single clause, not an argument.

A thesis offered and then undermined. Chapter I's title and first paragraph assert that opposite means (supplication, bravery) reach the same end of mercy. The examples from Edward, Scanderbeg, Conrad, and the Theban jury support it. Montaigne then reports that he himself would yield "to compassion" sooner than "to esteem", while noting that the Stoics count pity a vice. He reasons that yielding to tears may be weakness, whereas yielding to valour shows a strong soul, yet allows that admiration may move lesser minds too. Next Dionysius (who halts the torture because of his soldiers' near-mutiny but still has Phyton drowned), Sylla, and Alexander turn the examples round: the same courage that earned mercy elsewhere earns torture at Gaza and massacre at Thebes. Montaigne's conclusion is a hedged generalization, "Man (in good earnest) is a marvellous vain, fickle, and unstable subject, and on whom it is very hard to form any certain and uniform judgment". He offers Alexander three possible motives (courage too familiar to admire, pride of valour, ungovernable fury) without choosing among them. Scanderbeg's example is also qualified: it "might suffer another interpretation". The essay is thus a small demonstration of the method of weighing exempla against each other; the attributions are all to ancient and recent historical reports, the verdicts are Montaigne's.

Sorrow as vice, and the limits of that stance. Montaigne opens with strong self-report: "No man living is more free from this passion than I"; he neither likes sorrow in himself nor admires it in others, though the world "clothes" it with wisdom, virtue, and conscience. He calls it "always hurtful, always idle and vain" and notes the Stoics forbid it to their sages (the Stoic position is reported, but he evidently shares much of it). He closes the essay with the same self-report, a nature "stubborn" in apprehension that he "by reasoning" hardens daily. Between these bookends he does not argue against sorrow but collects cases where passion is too great to display.

Extreme passion is mute. The Psammenitus story (Herodotean exemplum as told by Montaigne) is the pivot: the king stays unmoved at his daughter's and son's fate and weeps only at a friend's capture; Montaigne first suggests the last blow merely overflowed a full cup, as with the prince at Trent, then lets Psammenitus himself supply the answer that the first two griefs exceeded expression. The painter's veiled Agamemnon, Niobe turned to stone (with the Latin tag "Diriguisse malis", left untranslated in this text), the man-at-arms' father at Buda who dies silent, the lover struck dumb in Catullus' verse, and the line "Curae leves loquuntur, ingentes stupent" all converge: slight cares speak, great ones stupefy. Montaigne then extends the principle to the body of the lover (sudden impotence) and to excess joy (Leo X, the Roman mother, Diodorus dying of shame), which he calls a testimony of the "imbecility of human nature". The weeping that follows the stupor is described as the soul venting and gaining "some room to work itself out". This is body-and-soul phenomenology, not Stoic doctrine, and is in some tension with his opening scorn for sorrow: the Stoic's ideal is silence by reason, but the silence Montaigne describes is by overload.

Notable passages

Preface. The whole address is short and is the best statement of the book's purpose; see chunk c1. Note the double dating at its foot (12th June and 1st March 1580), left as printed.

Man is unstable (end of the Sylla and Pompey paragraph). Pompey pardons Mamertines on account of one citizen, Zeno; Sylla refuses Perugia the same grace for the same virtue in its host. See c2.

Alexander and Betis. The most vivid instance and the one that most clearly reverses the opening thesis; Montaigne's three speculative explanations follow, then the contrasting Theban massacre (about six thousand killed, thirty thousand enslaved). See c3.

Opening of Of Sorrow. The self-report and the Stoic prohibition are distinct voices in a single short paragraph. See c4.

Psammenitus and the prince at Trent. The core demonstration that grief beyond expression can be silent. See c5.

The painter and Niobe. The generalization about the soul's ordinary functions being deprived by excess of grief. See c6.

The mercy passage ("I have a marvellous propensity to mercy and mildness") is a brief self-report not curated separately; it ties the preface's self-portrait to the first essay's examples.
