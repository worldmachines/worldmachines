Judging Death, Self-Hindering Minds, and the Spur of Difficulty

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Three short essays: Montaigne doubts that deaths can be read as proof of constancy, notes that a mind perfectly balanced between two desires could not choose, and argues that difficulty and prohibition sharpen desire while facility and fortification invite contempt and attack, ending with his own unguarded house.

Three brief essays. In the first Montaigne warns that we cannot judge the constancy of another's death, because the dying rarely believe they are dying and because a death met with composure may owe more to fortune or to the speed of the blow than to resolve; he then tours ancient suicides (largely borrowed anecdotes) to separate deaths that are studied and slow from deaths that merely run at the blow. The second, a single paragraph, mocks the notion of a mind evenly poised between two equal desires and reports the Stoic answer, then ends on a Pliny sentence about human uncertainty and pride. The third argues that desire is heightened by obstruction and dulled by easy possession, running from love, veils and divorce to the policing of vice and, finally, to Montaigne's own open, unfortified house during the civil wars.

Themes

Self-importance distorts the view of death. Montaigne (in his own voice) says we value ourselves too highly: it seems that "the universality of things were in some measure to suffer by our dissolution". Like people at sea who think the shore moves, the dying assume the world is as dependent on them as they are on it. He cites Caesar's address to his pilot and the public fancy of the sun mourning Caesar as examples of the same illusion. Hope also whispers that others have been sicker and lived, so few believe their last hour has come.

Constancy in death is hard to read from outside. Montaigne holds that to judge resolution in a man who does not know he is in danger "is not reason", and that many speak lightly to win a reputation they hope to enjoy afterward. Of those he has seen die, he says fortune, not design, disposed their countenances. He distinguishes planned from sudden or lingering deaths, doubts that Heliogabalus's luxurious preparations would have survived the test, and gathers botched or repented suicides (Domitius, Plautius Silvanus, Albucilla, Fimbria) against the steadier Ostorius and Adrian's marked-up chest. These are exempla taken from ancient writers, and the interpretation is his.

The studied death. Against the blind rush at death (those who hasten to execution do not trouble about being dead but about dying) he sets Socrates's thirty days, Atticus, Cleanthes and Tullius Marcellinus, who go by slow abstention and taste death at leisure. Montaigne says it is "far beyond not fearing death to taste and relish it". The Stoic adviser's speech to Marcellinus (reported via Seneca) is an opinion Montaigne relays without endorsing outright; he narrates the outcome approvingly as a death, "not to escape from death, but to essay it". He closes the chapter on Cato, whose second, more furious stroke he finds the truer picture of his death than the statues show; he says he would depict it differently.

Reason undermines its own freedom. In ch. XIV Montaigne ridicules the idea of a mind balanced between two equal appetites, which would starve between the bottle and the ham. The Stoics call the tie-breaking movement irregular and fortuitous; he suggests instead that things always differ a little. He then links this to paradoxical geometry and ends on Pliny's line that nothing is certain and that man is both miserable and proud. This is a hedged, sceptical aside, not a settled thesis.

Difficulty makes desire. In ch. XV he opens with the principle that every reason has its contrary, and turns a saying on preparing for loss around: we hug goods the more tightly the less secure they are. Rarity and difficulty whet appetite, satiety dulls it. Evidence ranges from Lycurgus's decree, the veils and farthingales of women, the horse that wanted only strange mares, to pilgrims preferring distant shrines. Montaigne extends the principle to Church troubles (stirring pious souls, though he says he does not know whether the net balance is positive), to marriage (the Roman liberty of divorce kept wives, while the tied knot slackens will), and to punishment (he says he does not know that executions whet vice, but knows that no civil government was ever reformed by them). The extension of the argument to women as scheming objects of male appetite is a period view that Montaigne states in his own voice.

An open house as policy. Montaigne reports, of his own estate, that facility of entry has perhaps preserved it from the violence of the civil wars: "defence allures attempt, and defiance provokes an enemy." He denies soldiers the glory of a dangerous exploit, notes that where religion is the pretext even relations become unreliable, and observes that garrisoned houses were probably lost by being guarded. He frames his stance as chosen retirement, withdrawing one corner from the public tempest as he does another corner in his soul, and hedges it with a trust in Heaven ("I will neither fear nor save myself by halves") and thirty years of evidence. He is self-reporting, not prescribing a universal rule: a gentleman who cannot really defend himself would, he says, play the fool to make a show of it.

Notable passages

The opening of ch. XIII (c1) is Montaigne's own diagnosis of why dying people and observers misjudge: self-regard and hope. The second passage (c2) sets out the evidential problem for reading anyone's death, and ties it to reputation. The Stoic's speech to Marcellinus (c3) is the sharpest statement in the unit of the case for chosen death, put in a reported voice, and notable for "we incessantly wheel in the same circle" as an argument from satiety. Ch. XIV (c4) is a miniature of sceptical argument about choice and certainty. The argument-heart of ch. XV is the opening principle (c5) and the Roman divorce paragraph (c6), after which comes the long autobiographical close on his gate (c7), the most concrete record in the unit of Montaigne in the Wars of Religion and a possible echo of his wider view that the self is its own most reliable fortress; compare michel-de-montaigne and self-construction-through-writing in a loose way, since here the point is about conduct under threat rather than about writing. The reasoning also loosely rhymes with the way habit-and-identity describes unexamined defaults, but Montaigne here treats appetite, not custom, as the machinery.
