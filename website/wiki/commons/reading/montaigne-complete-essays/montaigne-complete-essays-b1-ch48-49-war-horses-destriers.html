Of War Horses, and Of Ancient Customs: Montaigne on mounts, weapons and the tyranny of fashion

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Two short essays: a digressive catalogue of horses, riding and weapons in war (with Montaigne's preference for fighting on foot and with weapons one commands), and a catalogue of ancient Roman customs introduced by a critique of how fashion rewrites our judgment every few years.

Chapter XLVIII opens with a mock-apology ("I here have become a grammarian") on the Latin origin of the word 'destrier', then wanders through an anecdotal inventory of cavalry practice: trick-riding Romans and Numidians, trained biting horses, the horses of Caesar and Alexander, the social meaning of riding versus walking, disarming conquered peoples, riders and mules, horses as food and drink in extremity, and the Indians who took Spanish horses for gods. Along the way Montaigne states his own view that a man who fights on foot, with weapons he controls, stakes only his own courage, and he expresses distaste for the pistol. The chapter ends with feats of horsemanship he has personally seen. Chapter XLIX, much shorter, begins from a genuine argument (people take present fashion as the only rule and swing their judgment with every tailor's whim) and then lists Roman habits of bathing, dining, greeting, toilet and precedence, some like ours and some not, "to the end that, bearing in mind this continual variation of human things, we may have our judgment more clearly and firmly settled."

Themes

Digression as method, with a personal anchor. The horse chapter has no thesis; it accretes exempla, mostly from Xenophon, Livy, Caesar and Herodotus, and Montaigne flags the drift himself ("Let us go further into this matter since here we are"). The personal note is slight but real: he is most at ease on horseback, and he reports what he has seen (Monsieur de Carnavalet, the stunt-riders at Constantinople, the prince of Sulmona). This is the self-as-subject habit showing through an antiquarian subject; compare personal-essay-as-form and michel-de-montaigne.

Staking your honour on something you do not control. Montaigne's one sustained argument in XLVIII is his own: a mounted man hazards his valour on the horse's fear or fury, so "your honour must answer for it." He infers that the old foot battles were "more firm and furious," and generalises to weapons: choose those "of the shortest sort, and such of which we are able to give the best account." The sword is trusted over the pistol because the pistol needs "a concurrence of several circumstances" (powder, stone, wheel). He hopes firearms will be laid aside, while granting that the ancients had devices (phalarica, slings, engines) that supplied "the effects of our powder and shot." This is a small, early reflection on a technology that distributes agency away from the fighting body; it rhymes loosely with WMP's interest in infrastructure that changes who does the work, though Montaigne's frame is personal honour, not system structure.

Horse and rank as social code. Several exempla are really about custom: riding as the Parthian mark of the free man, the Grand Signior forbidding Christians and Jews a horse, conquered peoples being stripped of arms and horses, the mule as disgrace for a gentleman in one country and a dignity in Abyssinia, and the Indian ranking of elephant, four-horse coach, camel and single horse. Montaigne lets these contradictions stand without comment, which is itself the relativist point of the next chapter. The New World exemplum, in which the conquered offer gold and a speech of peace to the horses and read neighing as a language of truce, is a short, undeclared piece of irony about misreading the invader's machinery; compare the fuller treatment elsewhere in the Essays (not in this unit).

Fashion makes judgment fickle (Montaigne's own judgment). Chapter XLIX argues that people are blinded by present usage, "as every month to alter their opinion, if custom so require," and that the same person condemns in turn the high busk, the low busk and the new fashion. He is willing to forgive the parochial standard (it is "a common vice, not of the vulgar only, but almost of all men") but not the instability of the judgment. The stated purpose of his Roman list is to steady judgment by seeing "this continual variation of human things." This is a clear early statement of the custom-as-invisible-machinery theme; see habit-and-identity.

Ancients as strong in both vice and virtue. After listing Roman luxuries, Montaigne judges (his own view, hedged by "in truth") that moderns copy ancient vices, but lack the capacity to equal them, because "souls, by how much the weaker they are, by so much have they less power to do either very well or very ill." This is the nostalgic pole of the ancients-versus-moderns comparison; the same chapter's relativism cuts against it, and Montaigne does not reconcile them.

Notable passages

The curated passages are: his comfort on horseback (c1); the argument against fighting mounted (c2); the preference for the sword and the doubt about the pistol (c3); the Indians' reading of Spanish horses (c4); the opening of XLIX on fashion and judgment (c5); and the comparison of ancient and modern vice and virtue (c6). The remaining catalogue items in XLIX (baths, dining on couches, kissing, precedence in names, white mourning) are Montaigne reporting ancient practice, largely from Latin poets and historians, and carry no argument beyond illustrating variety. Note that the Latin verse in these chapters is untranslated in this text and is not relied on here.
