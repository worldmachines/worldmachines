Lending the Self, Keeping the Will (Of Managing the Will, first part)

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne argues for lending oneself to public duties while giving oneself only to oneself, using his mayoralty of Bordeaux, his ageing and attachment to custom, and his refusal of partisan blindness in the civil wars to defend a will held in reserve against passion.

This is the first half of Book 3, ch. 10. Montaigne opens with a self-report: few things move or possess him, he works by study and argument to enlarge this natural insensibility, and he holds that "a man should lend himself to others, and only give himself to himself." He then turns to his election as mayor of Bordeaux, where he announced his own deficiencies and his unwillingness to be consumed by the city's affairs as his father had been. From there he argues that the common moral rule, which sends us out of ourselves for the public good, is a salutary exaggeration, and that duty to others is best done with judgment rather than passion. The slice then moves through the natural measure of need (Socrates, Cleanthes), his own age and custom (the Pope's calendar reform), the theatre of offices and masks, and his even-handed judgment in the present civil broils, ending on how partisans let hope and belief be led by their leaders. The slice stops mid-essay, with the argument about fraud in a just cause.

Themes

The will as a reserve, not a ban on action. Montaigne is not recommending idleness. He would not have men "refuse, in the employments they take upon them, their attention, pains, eloquence, sweat, and blood if need be," but the engagement is "only borrowed, and accidentally; his mind being always in repose and in health." The same deed can be done with different degrees of will, and the passionate bystander may suffer more than the soldier. Violent desire hinders execution; he who uses "only his judgment and address" proceeds more cheerfully and fails without affliction. Anger, avarice, haste and eagerness at chess are all offered as cases where impetuosity defeats itself (the Seneca line is quoted in Latin).

Against self-hiring. Men "let themselves out to hire" and cannot stand still; occupation passes for dignity. Montaigne's counter-claim is that we spend time and life prodigally while guarding money, and should "glide over the world" rather than rush through it. This is his own temperament as self-report ("I am of a quite contrary humour"), and he does not present it as a universal law.

Moral precepts as useful distortion. He reports that the sages and the rules of the world drive us outward "for the benefit of public society," and judges that they "preach things as they serve, not as they are." His images are the archer aiming above the butt and the crooked stick bent the other way. He then proposes, as a conjecture ("I believe"), that the more secret doctrine of the Temple of Pallas concerns a sound friendship with oneself, from which the duties to the public follow: "He who does not in some sort live for others, does not live much for himself." Both sides are held: duty to others is real, but the "principal charge" is one's own conduct.

Nature, need and custom. Montaigne reports the philosophers' distinction between natural desires, whose end can be seen, and those of fancy, which "fly before us." Socrates, Metrodorus, Epicurus, Metrocles and Cleanthes are exempla of small need. He then lets habit enlarge nature: "Custom is a second nature, and no less powerful." At his age he cannot change, belongs to the years before the Pope's ten-day reform of the calendar, and says he is finishing this man, not rebuilding another. This resonates with habit-and-identity: custom as what makes a way of life feel like substance. Here, though, it is personal and elegiac, a defence of his own limits, not an analysis of custom as social machinery.

Roles and masks. Most business is farce; we must play a part but not make "real essence of a mask." The Mayor of Bordeaux and Montaigne "have ever been two by very manifest separation." Office holders who swell into their offices are his target. The judgment of an emperor should stand above his empire. This is a clear case of the self-as-subject theme in michel-de-montaigne and self-construction-through-writing: a reserved inner self is what makes public office survivable, though the essay says little here about how that self is constructed.

Judgment against faction. In the civil broils he claims to see good in adversaries and fault in his own side, and to keep "equanimity and pure indifference" apart from the knot of the controversy. He diagnoses hatred beyond the dispute as private grievance wearing public clothes (the ulcer and the remaining fever). He criticises inferring a man's party from his admiration of a Guise or the King of Navarre, and condemning a book for praising a heretic poet. Partisans would have their side's judgment "subserve not truth, but the project of our desires." He adds a self-hedge: he leans the other way for fear of being suborned by his own desire, and says he is "a little tenderly distrustful of things that I wish."

Notable passages

Against letting oneself out to hire. The passage on men whose faculties belong to those who have enslaved them, who "cannot stand still," and whose life is bustle, ends in the advice to glide over the world lightly (c1).

Mayor of Bordeaux's self-presentation. Montaigne describes how he told the city exactly what to expect from him, and that he did not want public affairs to impress him as they had his father, whom he remembers as tormented by them (c2).

The calibrated aim of moral precepts, and the judgment-led actor. The man who acts with judgment and not violent intention "always marches with the bridle in his hand" (c3).

Custom, age, and the calendar. The most personal passage: what is wanting to his custom is wanting to him, and late acquisitions come too late (c4).

Parts and masks. The mayor and Montaigne as two persons, and the judgment of an emperor above his empire (c5).

Fair judgment in the civil broils. His refusal to adore his own side or to infer a man's faction from his tastes (c6).
