Learning to die: pleasure, familiarity and readiness (first half of Book 1, ch. 19)

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Opening from Cicero's claim that philosophy is preparation for death, Montaigne argues that pleasure (rightly understood as virtue's own) is the end of all sects, that fear of death poisons every pleasure, and that the remedy is not evasion but constant familiarity with death, which he reports practising in his own life.

This is the first part of a short essay. Montaigne opens with Cicero's saying that studying philosophy is preparing to die, then recasts the matter: all sects aim at pleasure, virtue is the finest pleasure, and the contempt of death is among virtue's greatest gifts because without it every other pleasure goes dead. He mocks the common remedy of not thinking about death, piles up exempla of sudden and absurd deaths (including his own brother's), and prescribes the opposite course: make death familiar, picture it at every stumble, and so disarm its novelty. He then turns to self-report: he has long entertained imaginations of death, keeps himself always ready, and claims to be prepared to depart without regret. The slice ends mid-essay, on his own readiness; it does not contain the later argument.

Themes

Pleasure as the end, virtue as its best form. Montaigne reports the Ciceronian line and then argues in his own voice that "the thing at which we all aim, even in virtue is pleasure". He says the disputes among philosophical sects on this are "merely verbal", and that the pursuit of virtue is itself pleasant. This is his own judgment, though it is deliberately provocative ("It amuses me to rattle in ears this word").

Fear of death spoils all enjoyment. Death is inevitable, so if it frightens us it is "a perpetual torment". The condemned man carried past fine houses and feasts cannot taste them. The vulgar remedy, not thinking of it, he calls brutish; people will not even make a will until the physician gives them up.

Mortality is nearer than we assume. He reasons against the comfortable expectation of a long life, citing his own age (thirty-nine, hoping for as many more years), the high number of the renowned who died before thirty-five, and a long run of accidental deaths borrowed from classical and recent report (Aeschylus, the tortoise, the comb, the grape-stone). The one exemplum from his own family, his brother Captain St. Martin killed by a tennis ball, is a first-hand report; most of the rest are inherited anecdotes.

Familiarity as technique. The prescription is to "disarm him of his novelty and strangeness" by converse and constant imagination, with the Egyptian skeleton at the feast as exemplum. This is habituation as a method: the sting of the thought wears off through "often turning and returning" it. It rhymes with the project's interest in custom and habit as machinery that makes things feel natural (habit-and-identity), though here habit is used deliberately to undo a fear rather than to hide a convention.

Death-premeditation as liberty. "The premeditation of death is the premeditation of liberty; he who has learned to die has unlearned to serve." Knowing how to die frees one from subjection and constraint. Note he hedges that nature must help art here.

Self as witness. Montaigne offers his temperament ("not melancholic, but meditative"), his anecdote of the memorandum written a league from home, and his claim of readiness. This is the essay as self-study (michel-de-montaigne); the claim of perfect readiness is self-report of a stated disposition, not proof.

Notable passages

Pleasure and virtue (c1). The argument that all aim at pleasure, that virtue's pursuit is itself pleasant, and the reversal of the claim that difficulty makes virtue austere.

The condemned man and the common remedy (c2). The image of the criminal who cannot taste his feast, the "bridle the ass by the tail" jibe at not thinking of death, and the fear of the very word.

Against the long-life assumption (c3). The direct address ("Fool that thou art!") and the argument from experience over physicians' tales, plus the age-at-death reckoning.

Disarming death and liberty (c4). The turn from fear to familiarity, the Egyptian skeleton, and the line tying death-premeditation to freedom.

Temperament and habituation (c5). Montaigne's own practice of imagining death, and how repetition dulls the sting.

Booted and spurred (c6). The tablets anecdote and the declaration of readiness, with the rider that his leave of everything but himself is soon taken.
