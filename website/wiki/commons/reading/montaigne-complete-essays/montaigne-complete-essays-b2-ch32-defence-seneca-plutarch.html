Defence of Seneca and Plutarch: credibility, constancy and the measure of the possible

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne defends Seneca against a Huguenot pamphlet's use of Dion's slanders, and Plutarch against Jean Bodin's charges of credulity and partiality, arguing along the way that we must not measure what is possible by what we ourselves could do.

Montaigne opens by saying that Seneca and Plutarch, from whom his book is "wholly compiled" of borrowings, have earned his defence. He answers two attacks. First, a pamphlet by one of "the so-called reformed religion" compares Charles IX's government with Nero's and the Cardinal of Lorraine with Seneca. Montaigne thinks the comparison flatters the Cardinal, and he rejects the pamphlet's source, the historian Dion, as inconsistent and unreliable, preferring Tacitus and the Roman historians. Second, Jean Bodin (in his Method of history) accuses Plutarch of ignorance and of writing things "incredible, and absolutely fabulous", citing the Spartan boy and the fox-cub, Pyrrhus's sword-stroke, the fining of Agesilaus, and Plutarch's unequal Greek and Roman parallels. Montaigne rebuts each. The long middle of the slice turns the Spartan example into a catalogue of endurance under torture, from Egyptians, a Spanish peasant and Epicharis to Gascon peasants and women in the civil wars, and then into a general rule about judging others' capacities.

Themes

Debt and loyalty to authorities. Montaigne frames the defence as repayment: the two authors lent "assistance ... to my age and to my book". He does not claim them as infallible; he concedes that Plutarch tells a story variously in different Lives, and that taking things on trust from other hands is unavoidable for what we have not seen.

Weighing testimony. Montaigne judges the witnesses, not just the claims. Dion is discounted for self-contradiction and, in his words, for weak judgment in Roman affairs; Seneca's own writings and the Roman historians outweigh him. Note the partisan edge (he thinks Romans are better witnesses than "Greeks and foreigners"). He also pre-empts a reading that his admiration for the Cardinal is hostile: he praises the Cardinal's wit and zeal while denying he equals Seneca.

Limits of the possible are not the limits of my imagination. The key argument: "We are not to judge what is possible and what is not, according to what is credible and incredible to our apprehension". Everyone treats himself as the "sovereign stamp of human nature". Montaigne sets against this a humbler self-assessment: he sees great souls as "infinitely beyond" him, cannot match them, but keeps them in sight and judges "what so elevates them" because he finds "some seeds" of it in himself. This is self-knowledge used as a calibrating instrument, with the hedge that he lacks the wing but his judgment goes along.

Endurance, custom and obstinacy. The Spartan story is made unremarkable by custom (for the Spartans, being caught in theft was the supreme disgrace). Montaigne piles up cases (Egyptians, a Spanish peasant, Epicharis, Gascon peasants he has met himself in the civil wars) and notes that men burn "for opinions taken upon trust from others, and by them not at all understood". He draws a pointedly deflating moral: "obstinacy is the sister of constancy, at least in vigour and stability". The same physical courage serves virtue, faction, or sheer temper; this resonates with habit-and-identity and custom-as-machinery, though here the point is about credibility, not a theory of custom.

Plutarch's parallels as method. Against Bodin's charge of favouring the Greeks, Montaigne argues that Plutarch weighs "manners, natures, and parts" rather than "fortunes" and renown, that great actions are not always the most renowned, and that Plutarch never claims equality: he compares pieces and circumstances one by one. Montaigne adds that it is "folly to judge, at one view, of things that have so many aspects", a characteristic distrust of global verdicts.

Notable passages

The opening and the Cardinal of Lorraine comparison (c1) show Montaigne's self-description as a borrower and his reluctance to flatter a contemporary at Seneca's expense. The Dion passage (c2) is his rule for discounting an inconsistent witness. The Bodin passage (c3) contains his criticism of Bodin's chosen example (the faculties of the soul are harder to bound than the limbs) and his insistence that he does not find the Spartan story even "rare and strange". The civil-war passage (c4) is Montaigne's own report of peasants he has known and seen, set beside the ancient exempla. The general rule about possibility (c5) is the essay's most portable idea, close in spirit to the sceptical humility of the Apology without being the same argument (michel-de-montaigne). The defence of the parallels (c6) shows how he reads Plutarch as a moral comparer, not a ranker of nations.

Attribution note: the charges against Seneca come from the unnamed pamphleteer via Dion; the charges against Plutarch are Bodin's, quoted by Montaigne; the Spartan, Egyptian, Spanish and Epicharis stories are exempla from Plutarch, Cicero, Marcellinus and others; the Gascon peasants and the stripped man in the ditch are Montaigne's own testimony.
