Of the passions of body and soul: Caesar's ambition and Spurina's scars

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne compares bodily appetite (curable by satiety, cold, or mutilation) with soul-passions like ambition (which grow by fruition), uses Julius Caesar as the case where ambition overmasters lust and ruins a magnificent nature, then judges Spurina's self-disfigurement admirable but imprudent, and argues that moderate living in the world is harder than ascetic withdrawal.

Montaigne opens by noting that philosophy gives reason sovereignty over the appetites, and that love is held the most violent of them; he then points out that desires of the body are, unlike those of the soul, subject to satiety and to material remedies. A run of exempla follows: amputation, snow and vinegar, hair shirts (including a prince who could not bear one till night), and Xenocrates, who burned the members that rebelled when Lais was slipped into his bed. Julius Caesar is the long central case: an inveterate lover whose ambition always overrode love, a man of real clemency, sobriety and courage whose one vice spoiled the rest. After shorter parallels (Mohammed, Ladislaus of Naples), Montaigne arrives at Spurina, the Tuscan youth who mutilated his own beauty so as not to inflame others, and he gives a hedged verdict: more admiration than approval. The unit closes by arguing that withdrawal from common duties is easier than keeping upright in ordinary life.

Themes

Two kinds of appetite. Bodily desires can be remedied materially and are subject to satiety; passions that reside wholly in the soul, "as ambition, avarice, and the rest", leave reason only its own resources and "grow sharper and increase by fruition". This asymmetry is the structural argument, and Caesar is brought in as its demonstration: the "peculiar" soul-passion of ambition governs and finally beats the amorous one. Montaigne extends the point to Mohammed and, hedging, grants counter-examples such as Mark Antony, though he "make[s] no doubt" ambition would win in an equal contest. This is his own judgment, offered with exempla borrowed from Suetonius, Plutarch and Oppius.

Divided assessment of Caesar. Montaigne does not flatten Caesar: he lists his learning, eloquence, sobriety, loyalty to friends, justice, and above all clemency to enemies, then says ambition "was the rudder of all his actions" and made a liberal man a public thief. He also allows that clemency may partly have been calculation (to "cajole his enemies"), yet reads it as proof of "marvellous confidence and grandeur of courage". He is troubled by admiring so great a man and yet calls his name "abominable to all good men", saying he can "excuse victory that it could not disengage itself from him". Both sides are kept in view; the verdict on the vice is firm.

Self-mutilation as misdirected virtue. Spurina's act is the rare case of someone who condemns his own beauty because it inflames others, "as if a man were responsible to himself for the faults of others". Montaigne gives his "free opinion": the design was good but defective in prudence; the scars may provoke hatred, envy or calumny, since vice can extract occasion from any form. Better to make the gift an example of "regularity and virtue". Note that it is Montaigne reporting an example and judging it, not endorsing it; his rule is that excess is enemy to his rules.

Moderation is harder than abstinence. The closing paragraph reverses the ascetic's prestige: those who retreat from civil duties are discreet but their constraint earns no "reward of difficulty"; it is easier to abstain from the sex than to behave rightly in the society of a wife; "the well living of Scipio has a thousand fashions, that of Diogenes but one". This fits Montaigne's recurring preference for ordinary, embodied, social virtue over heroic austerity (compare michel-de-montaigne), though here he says so briefly and the surrounding Caesar section runs on a different, more historical logic.

Habit, body and mind as machinery (a loose resonance). The unit treats desire as something that works through body and clothes and customs (hair shirts that do not make wearers chaste), which touches the theme of habit-and-identity only loosely; the text itself does not discuss custom. Treat it as a faint rhyme, not a claim.

Notable passages

Body remedies fail to reach the soul. The hair-shirt anecdote and Montaigne's remark that emotions "are often seen under rude and slovenly clothes" undercut mechanical asceticism; the prince's own claim is reported, and Montaigne doubts he ever tried "the most violent".

Xenocrates and the soul-passions. Xenocrates burns his rebellious members, a rigor Montaigne contrasts with passions that reason can only fight with its own means and that do not satiate.

Ladislaus and the poisoned handkerchief. A counter-example in which a king makes pleasure the end of ambition; he and the physician's daughter die together by poison. Reported, with a plain "very remarkable" from Montaigne.

Caesar: the nature spoiled by one vice. The summary of how ambition corrupts clemency, bounty and sober greatness into tyranny, "a name without form and without body", with the claim that it built glory upon the ruin of the republic.

Spurina. The centrepiece, with its Latin verse (untranslated in this text), the self-scarring, and the measured verdict.

Moderation over withdrawal. The closing argument, in Montaigne's own voice, that use according to reason is harder than abstinence.
