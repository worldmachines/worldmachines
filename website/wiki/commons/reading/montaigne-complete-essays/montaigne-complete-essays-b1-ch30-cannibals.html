Of Cannibals: barbarism as the custom of other people

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne uses a second-hand report of Brazilian Tupinamba-style natives, gathered from a servant and sailors, to argue that 'barbarism' is whatever is not the custom of one's own country, that these people are barbarous only by the rule of reason and that Europeans exceed them in cruelty and injustice.

Montaigne opens with Pyrrhus and other Greeks who judged the Roman army by its order and found nothing barbarous in it, a lesson that we must judge by reason and not by common report. He then turns to the New World. He has long had in his house a man who lived ten or twelve years in Villegaignon's 'Antarctic France', and he first clears the ground: the Atlantis story (via Plato's Solon) and the Carthaginian island (via the pseudo-Aristotelian book of marvels) are each rejected as accounts of the newly found land, and he digresses on how rivers and seas reshape the earth (the Dordogne, the sands at Medoc). He then justifies his witness (a plain man, and so more likely to tell the truth than the 'better-bred', who gloss), and describes the natives: their houses, drink, dancing, priests, prophets, wars, treatment of prisoners and ritual eating of the dead, polygamy, songs, and a visit of three of them to Rouen. The essay's turn is that he calls them barbarous only 'in respect to the rules of reason', not in respect to Europeans, who exceed them in barbarity.

Themes

Barbarism is relative to custom. Montaigne says nothing in this nation is barbarous and savage 'excepting, that every one gives the title of barbarism to everything that is not in use in his own country'. We have 'no other level of truth and reason than the example and idea of the opinions and customs of the place wherein we live'. This is his own judgment, not a reported opinion, and it is the essay's thesis; it sits close to the logic of habit-and-identity, where custom is the invisible machinery that makes local norms feel like reason itself. Note he does not flatten the claim into pure relativism: he still allows that the natives are barbarous by the rules of reason.

Nature against artifice. The 'wild' fruits are those nature produces by her own course; the ones we call cultivated are the ones whose natures we have 'changed by our artifice'. Art, he says, has almost smothered nature, and cannot even match a bird's nest or a spider's web. These people have 'received but very little form and fashion from art and human invention' and are near their 'original simplicity'. He then catalogues, in a long negation, what they lack (traffic, letters, magistrates, riches and poverty, contracts, property, clothing, agriculture, metal, wine) and says it surpasses the poets' golden age and Plato's Republic. This is the textual root of the later figure of the noble-savage, though Montaigne's version is hedged by his reliance on a witness's report, by his admission of their warfare and ritual eating, and by his refusal to idealise away their faults.

Testimony and its discipline. Montaigne trusts a simple informant over cosmographers and 'better-bred' men who 'gloss upon it' and 'cannot forbear a little to alter the story'. He insists that each man should 'write what he knows, and as much as he knows, but no more', and condemns those with one narrow experience who then write 'the whole body of physics'. He also admits the limits of his own access: his interpreter was poor and the conversation with the captain yielded little. A reflexive point on how knowledge of other worlds is mediated, and on the self as a limited observer (michel-de-montaigne).

Cruelty at home outweighs cruelty abroad. He grants that the natives roast and eat a dead prisoner, as a representation of extreme revenge rather than for food, and says he is not sorry that we should note the horror of it, 'but that, seeing so clearly into their faults, we should be so blind to our own'. He holds there is more barbarity in torturing a living body, or in having it worried by dogs and swine 'under colour of piety and religion', than in eating a dead one. He adds antiquarian support: the Stoic view of corpses, the besieged Gauls at Alexia, and the physicians' use of human flesh in medicine. The Wars of Religion are in the background but are only alluded to ('lately seen').

Honour, war and the true victory. Their wars rest on 'the sole jealousy of valour', not conquest, because their land is fertile and they covet only what nature requires. Prisoners are well treated, then threatened, to extort a word of submission; the point is that the true victory is the confession of the enemy's spirit. Montaigne extends this to a digression on valour located in the heart and not in horse or arms, with Leonidas and the Spartan captain Iscolas as exempla: there are defeats more triumphant than victories. These Greek examples are borrowed and bent to his point.

Their reasoning, and ours. The prisoner's song (that his captors will eat their own ancestors' flesh) and the love-song to the adder are offered as proof that this is not a stupid people following custom without judgment. Of the song he says there is 'an invention that nothing relishes of the barbarian'; of the love-song, that it is 'perfectly Anacreontic'. Marriage practice (wives who procure more wives for their husbands) is defended by Biblical and Roman parallels. At Rouen, three natives found two things strange: grown armed men obeying a child, and the 'halves' of men begging at the doors of those 'full and crammed with all manner of commodities' without taking them 'by the throats'. This is the essay's outside view of European hierarchy and inequality. Montaigne closes lightly, with the captain whose only privilege is to lead in war and to have paths cut for him, 'for they wear no breeches', a joke that deflates without retracting.

The foreboding of ruin. He also says the three visitors do not foresee that 'the effect of this commerce will be their ruin, as I presuppose it is in a very fair way'. This is a hedged forecast, not a reported fact: a plain-spoken prediction of what contact will cost them.

Notable passages

The definition of barbarism (c1). The passage on custom and the wild fruits carries the essay's central argument in a compact form and sets up nature against artifice.

What they lack (c2). The long negation addressed to Plato is the source of the essay's utopian reputation, and it is worth reading with its hedge: it reports 'as my witnesses inform me', and the same text goes on to describe war and ritual cannibalism.

Who is the barbarian (c3). The turn from their cruelty to ours is the moral centre. The surrounding hedge ('we may then call these people barbarous, in respect to the rules of reason') keeps the claim two-sided.

The witness (c4). The rules for trustworthy testimony, with the self-limiting maxim to write only what one knows.

The prisoner's song (c5). A paradigm of Montaigne arguing from a cultural product (a poem) that these people reason.

The view from Rouen (c6). The visitors' two questions about obedience and inequality are the sharpest inversion of the European gaze; consider the rhyme with a world-machine view in which the legibility of hierarchy is taken for natural, though Montaigne himself does not draw the connection, and the passage is reported speech filtered through a bad interpreter and his own memory (he forgot the third thing).

One caution for later use: all the ethnography is second-hand (a servant and sailors), and Montaigne reports that he tasted their bread-like compound and holds artefacts at his house, but the essay never claims first-hand observation of the customs themselves.
