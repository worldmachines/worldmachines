Of Vanity (final slice): precept and practice, public virtue, digression, Rome, and looking inward

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In the closing slice of Of Vanity, Montaigne argues that philosophers' precepts and their lives part company, that private virtue is unfit for public affairs in a corrupt age, defends his digressive style and his love of ancient Rome, and ends on the Delphic command to look into oneself.

This is the last fifth of "Of Vanity" and it wanders by design. It opens with the gap between the rules men preach and the lives they lead (judges, philosophers, a reforming wit), and Montaigne uses it to say that the writer who speaks of himself must keep his pen and his feet together. He then explains why he withdrew from public employment: private virtue, "scholastic and novice", is unfit for a world of "wavings, corners, and elbows", and the factions of France all defend their cause with dissimulation. A long self-defence of his disjointed method follows, then an affectionate account of his bond with the dead and with ancient Rome, a short reckoning of his debts to Fortune (no children, a Roman citizenship that is an "airy favour"), and finally the Delphic god's reproach that man alone is turned outward and spills himself.

Themes

Precept and practice diverge, and Montaigne is half-forgiving about it. He opens by listing hypocrisies: the judge who writes a sentence and a love-letter on the same sheet, the philosophical lecture that never "touches or stings your conscience". He reports the explanation that ancient teachers speak now as legislators for the crowd and now for themselves (Solon), and the licence claimed by Antisthenes and Diogenes for the strong, as with physicians who eat melons while prescribing syrups. The reported opinions are the ancients', not his. His own judgment is that the rules are out of proportion to what anyone can perform: "The laws which condemn us not to be able, condemn us for not being able." He does not conclude that rules should be dropped; he concludes that human wisdom "is so great an enemy to consistency".

Writing about oneself removes the licence. Those who only speak of things may split action from reasoning, but not those who speak of themselves: "I must march my pen as I do my feet." This ties the essay as self-portrait to a standard of consistency that he applies to himself, not others. (Compare self-construction-through-writing and personal-essay-as-form, though Montaigne frames it as an obligation of honesty more than as construction.)

Private virtue does not scale to public affairs. Montaigne reports his own failed attempt to carry his "rough, new, unpolished" rules of living into public service, and gives the image of the herb that conforms to a foreign soil sooner than it reforms it. He denies that competence at self-government implies competence at government (Socrates unable to count his tribe's votes; Saturninus: "you have lost a good captain, to make of him a bad general"). He also says he feels ambition at times and resists it. This is a self-report of temperament ("liberty and laziness"), not a general rule against public service. Hedged: he also says that he would have declared himself between Caesar and Pompey.

Civil-war France: virtue is relative to the age. He claims that every party in France defends itself with disguise, that the most just party is the least affected member of a "worm-eaten body", and that "civil innocence is measured according to times and places", with the Spartan Agesilaus's courtesy to a former enemy as an example that would seem unremarkable to Spartans and absurd to "monkeyish capets". His practical stance is conservative and withdrawn: obey the magistrates one has, serve while the image of the old laws shines, and withdraw if they split. This is a custom-relativist reading of virtue that sits near habit-and-identity, though here applied to politics rather than belief.

Digression as method, and contempt for obscurity. Montaigne admits that the "medley" is off his theme, and defends leaps and skips by Plato, Plutarch and the poets: it is "the indiligent reader who loses my subject, and not I". He wants his matter to show its own joints without connectives, but also says he "mortally hates" affected obscurity (Aristotle is rebuked for boasting of it), and explains that he lengthened his chapters because short breaks "dissolve the attention before it was raised". He sets himself against "trouble-feast reason" and says he will bring "vanity itself in repute".

Rome and the dead. He was raised among the dead and knew Rome before he knew his own house; he serves the dead because they cannot help themselves, and a benefit to them has no "retrogradation and reflection". Finding himself of no use to his age, he throws himself on the ancient one (free Rome, not its birth or old age). He pleads a pleasure in places that moves more than texts, and then in the living Rome as the one universal Christian city. He says this is a vain pleasure but not one worth blaming.

Fortune, children, honours. He thanks Fortune for never outraging him beyond his strength, and warns that many perish in port. He says that childlessness is not a defect, that children are hardly to be desired in so corrupt a time, and that he has kept his house in as good a state as he inherited. He values only real goods, so the Roman burgess-ship pleases him as a vain favour, and he admits to being "full of inanity and foppery".

Looking outward is nature's anaesthetic; the Delphic reproach. The slice ends with an observation about habit: we look outward because looking at ourselves "is a painful motion", and nature "has wisely thrust the action of seeing outward". He then gives a speech in the voice of the Delphic god, urging man to look into himself and calling him "the investigator without knowledge, the magistrate without jurisdiction, and, after all, the fool of the farce". This is Montaigne's ventriloquised god, not a doctrine he states in his own name, though the self-study project is plainly in sympathy with it.

Notable passages

The passage on writing as one lives (c1) is the cleanest statement here of the essayist's standard of consistency. The passage on public virtue (c2) and the one on the divided France (c3) show Montaigne's retreat from politics in the Wars of Religion, with attention to what he does and does not claim. The digression passage (c4) and the one on obscurity (c5) are his poetics of the essay as a form. The Rome passage (c6) is the emotional centre, and the Delphic speech (c7) is the closing frame for the title, since vanity here means emptiness of the outward-turned human being. Echoes of michel-de-montaigne and of the studied-interior self in studiolo-and-self-cultivation are loose; the text itself does not use that vocabulary.
