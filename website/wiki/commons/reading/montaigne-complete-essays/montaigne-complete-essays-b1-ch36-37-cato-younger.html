Admiring Cato, and the Many-Faced Soul

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne defends the admiration of heroic virtue (Cato) against debunking contemporaries, judging others by their own model rather than his, then argues that the soul is a succession of shifting passions, so laughing and weeping at the same event, or mixed feeling toward one person, is no proof of pretence.

Two short essays. In "Of Cato the Younger" Montaigne begins from tolerance, a refusal to measure other people by himself, and uses it to defend the admiration of exemplary virtue against fashionable debunkers who hunt for base motives behind great deeds. He sets out the criterion that real virtue must be done by itself and for itself alone, tells the Spartan story of Aristodemus, then turns to poetry (the contest of five Latin poets praising Cato, and the nature of poetic rapture). In "That We Laugh and Cry for the Same Thing" he cites rulers who wept at the death of enemies they had destroyed, and argues that such mixed feeling is not hypocrisy: the soul holds several motions at once, one dominant, and passes between them too fast to notice, so that a single continuous character or a single sincere emotion is an illusion of our own making.

Themes

Judging by the other's model, not one's own. Montaigne reports himself as unusually willing to credit lives unlike his, "more easily admit of difference than uniformity amongst us." He is not continent but approves the Feuillans and Capuchins, loving them "the more for being other than I am." His own weakness does not reduce the esteem due to others' force. This is self-report, a claim about his temperament, and it sets up the later defence of Cato. It is a mild cultural-relativist posture applied to individuals; compare the self-study of michel-de-montaigne more generally.

Crawling but looking up: judgment over conduct. He admits he "crawl[s] upon the slime of the earth" yet keeps his judgment "regular and just" and his will right "where my legs fail me." He hedges: it is "something," not much. The gap between what he approves and what he does is stated openly, not resolved.

A decayed age and the purity test for virtue. He claims the present age has lost even the imagination of virtue, which has become "college jargon," an ornament. Actions that look virtuous are driven by "profit, glory, fear, custom," and so are not virtue in the doer, since "virtue owns nothing to be hers, but what is done by herself and for herself alone." This is a rigorist standard, stated in Montaigne's own voice here, and notably severe. Custom appears as one of the foreign causes that counterfeit virtue, an echo of the custom-as-hidden-machinery theme (see habit-and-identity), though here it is a moral rather than anthropological point.

The Aristodemus exemplum (Plutarch-derived anecdote). At Plataea the Spartans, "great judges of virtue," denied the prize to the bravest man, Aristodemus, because he fought to redeem his reputation after Thermopylae. This is an anecdote offered as illustration of the purity test, not Montaigne's argument itself.

Against debunking. Montaigne attacks contemporary wits who ascribe "vile interpretation[s]" to the great deeds of antiquity, and says with a hint of dry humour that he could contrive a hundred plausible hidden motives for any action. He would rather lend noble figures "a shoulder to raise them higher": it is "the duty of good men to portray virtue as beautiful as they can." He diagnoses the debunkers as malicious, as confining belief to their own capacity, or (his preferred guess) as lacking sight strong enough for virtue's splendour. Cato is his instance: he cites Plutarch's anger at those who said Cato died from fear of Caesar, and says Cato would rather have acted nobly and earned ignominy than glory. Note the tension with the earlier purity test: he asks for charity toward motives while holding that motive decides virtue.

Poetry as rapture beyond rules. Deferring to the richness of Cato, he lines up five Latin poets praising him (the quoted verses are Latin, untranslated in this text), claiming a child can sense a rising scale of power to the last. Then: there are far more poets than judges of poetry; supreme poesy "is above all rules and reason" and acts like a flash of lightning that ravishes judgment; inspiration passes from poet to actor to spectators like the loadstone that magnetises needles. He names Ovid, Lucan and Virgil as the three colours poetry has had for him.

The soul as plural and quick. In the second essay the central claim is that apparent contradictions in feeling are normal. Using the analogy of bodily humours, one passion rules the soul but "those of less authority may upon occasion reassume their place and make a little sally in turn." Children laugh and cry at the same thing; travellers leaving home always feel something troubling; brides weep at the bedchamber. Montaigne applies it to himself: he curses his servant in earnest and then "turn[s] the leaf," he is cold and then fond to his wife and neither is counterfeit, and he mutters "Confound the fool!" at himself without taking that as his definition. The passions come as fast as the sun's rays, "darts out her passions" imperceptibly, so "if we would make one continued thing of all this succession of passions, we deceive ourselves." This is an early statement of the self as discontinuous, in the same vein as the essay as self-observation (self-construction-through-writing), and it cuts against the legibility of a fixed character; though here it is offered as psychological observation, not as political or systemic argument.

Exempla for mixed feeling. Antigonus weeping over Pyrrhus's head, Rene of Lorraine mourning Charles of Burgundy, Count Montfort grieving over Charles de Blois, Caesar turning from Pompey's head, Xerxes in joy and then tears at his host seen by Artabanus, Timoleon lamenting his brother. These are historical reports from the sources he is drawing on; he concedes that "the greatest part of our actions are no other than visor and disguise" and the heir's tears may be a mask, so he is not denying hypocrisy, only denying that mixed feeling proves it. Even Nero, sending his mother to be drowned, felt some horror and pity. Timoleon's case carries the point: he mourns his brother, not the tyrant, and "one part of his duty is performed; let us give him leave to perform the other."

Notable passages

The opening of the first essay (c1) states Montaigne's tolerance in his own voice and sets the dual stance of approving what he cannot do. The Aristodemus passage (c2) is the clearest worked example of the purity test for virtue. The anti-debunking passage (c3) is where Montaigne argues for charitable interpretation of exemplary figures and names Cato as the pattern of human virtue and constancy. In the second essay the humours analogy (c4) is the explanatory core, the servant and wife passage (c5) is the personal application, and the closing passage on revenge (c6) generalises: "everything has many faces and several aspects."
