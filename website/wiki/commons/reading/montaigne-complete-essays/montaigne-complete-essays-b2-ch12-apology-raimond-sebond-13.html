Opinion, Custom and the Missing Natural Law (Apology for Raimond Sebond, slice 13 of 15)

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In a late slice of the Apology, Montaigne piles up cosmological, religious and legal diversity (the world's eternity, the uncanny parallels of New World and European belief, climate-shaped minds, the philosophers' warring definitions of the sovereign good, the contradictions of 'natural law') to argue that human opinion, reason and prayer lack any stable authority, while deferring to God and revelation as the only firm foundation.

This slice is the middle-to-late stretch of the Apology's assault on human reason, and it begins mid-argument. It opens on a catalogue of ancient opinions about the world's age and fate (Plato, the Egyptian priests, Heraclitus, Epicurus), then turns to the resemblances Montaigne finds between customs and beliefs of the newly discovered Americas and those of Europe. From there it argues that beliefs, like crops, rise and fall with climate and season; that we cannot even know what to wish for (Midas, Socrates' prayer, the Lacedemonians); that philosophers have produced hundreds of rival sects on the supreme good; and, at length, that justice cannot be derived from reason, custom or the supposed "natural laws", since every candidate is denied by some nation. It closes on a run of exempla (Lycurgus on theft, Aristippus and Diogenes, Solon, Socrates) showing that reason finds grounds for any practice, and on a verse about each people hating its neighbours' gods.

Themes

The vast concurrence of customs, and a providential gloss. Montaigne reports (largely from the Spanish accounts of the New World, which he does not name here beyond "they found nations there") a long list of things the Indians shared with Europe: circumcision, crosses, fasting, a flood story, a belief in a day of judgment, writing in cypher, burial of wives with husbands, the name-change on promotion. He is struck that such concurrence "by no means seem[s] to proceed from our natural meditation", and exclaims that "The human mind is a great worker of miracles!". Notably he then explains the Christian-looking parallels as "vain shadows of our religion", testimony to its "dignity and divinity", a pious reading, while the very next sentences list contrary diversities (a purgatory of cold rather than fire; subjects dressing meanly before the king). The passage is evidence of the hedge: the same data is read as both convergence and diversity. It rhymes with the Epicurean remark that other worlds replicate ours, which Montaigne says would be more confident had Epicurus seen the New World.

Belief as a product of place and season. If opinions "have their revolution, their season, their birth and death, like cabbage plants", then they lack "magisterial and permanent authority". Citing Vegetius and the Egyptian priests' remark to Solon on Athenian versus Theban air, he argues that climate shapes not only bodies but "the very faculties of the soul", and that peoples are inclined to liberty or servitude, superstition or unbelief, by where they live. The consequence he draws is sceptical: if even human nature has been "many ages wide" of the truth in some matters, what assurance have we that this age is not mistaken?

We cannot know what to desire. Even in wishing, the mind "cannot so much as covet what is proper for it". Midas, Cleobis and Bito, and the Lacedemonian prayer illustrate that what we ask for may harm us, so the right posture is to leave the choice to God ("Thy will be done"), who loves man "more than himself". Here the scepticism resolves into explicit fideism, in Montaigne's own voice, with Latin verse carrying the point. He also gives a short personal aside about his youthful longing for the order of St. Michael, which fortune granted by cheapening it.

The sovereign good as an unsettled field. Varro's count of 288 sects is cited; Montaigne sketches the rival answers (virtue, pleasure, nature, knowledge, freedom from pain, suspension of assent). He distinguishes Pyrrhonian ataraxy, which they do not assert positively, from Arcesilaus, who by affirming constancy of judgment as the good "quitted Pyrrhonism". He then wishes that Justus Lipsius or another would compile a register of the ancient philosophers' opinions together with how they lived up to them, a notable early statement of the wish for a comparative, biographical history of philosophy.

Law is accidental; "natural law" fails the universality test. Obeying one's country's laws, the philosophers' counsel, makes duty "accidental"; truth "ought to have a like and universal visage". Montaigne cites the English changing religion several times in his lifetime, a capital offence turned lawful at home, and the thought that victory or defeat could make loyalists traitors. The Delphic god's pronouncement that every one's true worship is that found in use where he is turns religion into "a thing of their own contrivance, useful as a bond to their society"; Montaigne immediately thanks God for having seated Christian belief "upon the eternal foundation of his holy word", a piece of fideist insulation that sits beside the corrosive argument without dissolving it. The philosophers' appeal to natural laws is mocked: of the three or four they name, none is not "contradicted and disowned" by some nation; universal consent, the only likely criterion, is absent. He lists extremes (incest, infanticide, parricide, community of wives) as sanctioned somewhere. Then he allows it "credible that there are natural laws for us, as we see them in other creatures", but lost in us because reason "shuffle[s] and confound[s] the face of things". Protagoras, Aristo and Thrasymachus are reported as holding that justice is only authority or the convenience of the superior; Montaigne reports them without clearly adopting them.

Reason finds a ground for every practice. Funerary cannibalism of one's father as piety, Lycurgus's defence of theft, Aristippus against Diogenes, Solon's tears, the Indians' public coupling, the Scythians' sacrifice of strangers: each shows that reason "finds appearances for divers effects; 'tis a pot with two ears that a man may take by the right or left". These are exempla borrowed from classical sources, and the cannibal passage anticipates the argument of "Of Cannibals" without leaning on it here.

Resonances

The argument that custom does the work we attribute to nature resonates with habit-and-identity and with the relativism the project reads in michel-de-montaigne. The claim that laws and religions are "a bond to their society" and that the "natural" is manufactured by reason's inconstancy touches the world-machine idea of infrastructure that feels natural from inside, though Montaigne's frame is individual and sceptical, not an era-scale cycle, and he explicitly exempts revealed Christianity from the relativizing. The cosmological catalogue of recurring ages (the sun changing course, the world burned and renewed) has a loose echo of cyclical history, as reported opinion only. The New World parallels belong with the contact-era material in mann-1493, although Montaigne's interest is in belief, not ecology or trade.

Notable passages

The curated chunks are: the New World concurrence of customs and its pious gloss (c1); the argument from climate and season that opinions have no permanent authority (c2); the prayer and Midas passage on not knowing what to wish (c3); the claim that the laws change by border and fortune, and the Delphic god on worship (c4); the failure of natural laws to meet the universality test (c5); and the lost natural laws and the example of parricidal piety (c6).
