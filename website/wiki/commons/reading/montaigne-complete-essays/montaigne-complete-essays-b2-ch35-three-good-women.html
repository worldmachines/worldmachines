Of Three Good Women: Marriage Tested by Death, and Seneca's Reason to Live

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne, after complaining that wives show their love only once their husbands are dead, offers three Roman exempla (Pliny's neighbour's wife, Arria, Pompeia Paulina) of wives who died with or for their husbands, then closes on Seneca's own letter arguing that one may rightly stay alive for the sake of someone who loves one.

The essay opens with Montaigne's own sour observation that good wives are rare, that the real test of a marriage is the whole length of the companionship, and that widows in his age save their displays of love for after the death, when they are cheap. He then, "not at all dissenting from our customs", offers three women whose goodness was of another and "so austere" a kind: the wife of Pliny's neighbour, who counsels and shares her husband's suicide from an incurable disease; Arria, who stabs herself first to show Paetus how; and Pompeia Paulina, who opens her veins beside Seneca but is bound up by Nero's order and survives, pale for life. A short aside praises true stories out of books over invented ones. The essay ends with a turn on Paulina's husband: Seneca, quoted from a letter to Lucilius, says he stays alive out of consideration for her, so that his own forbearance counts as much as her willingness to die.

Themes

Montaigne's own view: love is proved in life, not in mourning. This is first-person judgment and the only part of the essay that is straightforwardly his. He distrusts ceremonial grief ("consider her deportment, her complexion, the plumpness of her cheeks under all those formal veils; 'tis there she talks plain French"), reads widowly display as aimed at the next husband, and quotes a Latin tag (that those mourn most loudly who grieve least). The widow-prince anecdote makes the same point comically. He is hedged only by the opening concession that husbands, too, have enough to do.

The exempla are borrowed, and they run against his own customs. The three stories are reported from ancient authors, not Montaigne's experience; he calls them "my three very true stories". He presents them as examples "of another kind than are now in use", admirable but unlikely to be imitated. Note that he does not argue that suicide is right in general here; he reports and praises specific conduct and, in Arria's case, ranks her action above the poet's epigram, since she was "promotress and adviser" and her last concern was to free Paetus from the fear of dying with her.

Class and virtue. Montaigne remarks that the first wife was "of mean condition", where rare virtue is no great novelty, while the other two were noble and rich, "where examples of virtue are rarely lodged". This is an aside in his own voice, passing and unelaborated.

Stoic death as performed teaching. In the Seneca scene (an exemplum from the Roman historians, not Montaigne's own reasoning) the philosopher turns his death into the proof of his studies: now is the time to show "not by argument and discourse, but effect" what his philosophy was worth. He leaves his friends "the image of my life and manners". Montaigne lets the scene run with its details of slow blood, poison, the hot bath, and Nero's afterthought of saving Paulina.

Honest affections can override the Stoic's right to die. The final section is Montaigne's own turn of argument, through Seneca's words: Seneca stayed alive for Paulina, and Montaigne imagines Seneca counted this as equal to dying for her. The quoted letter says the rule of living is "not so long as they please, but as long as they ought", that returning to life for another is greatness of courage, and that one may lend oneself to friends. Montaigne attributes this to Seneca's "stoical humour" and endorses its excellence in closing ("as excellent as they everywhere are"). The essay therefore reverses its own direction: after three deaths for husbands, it ends on the opposite duty, to live on for a spouse.

History as a stock of stories. Montaigne remarks that one could make a whole body of true tales out of books, adding only "the solder of another metal", as Ovid did with his fables. A small note on borrowing and compilation as a mode of composition; compare how the Essays themselves are stitched from quotation. See personal-essay-as-form for the larger form, though this passage speaks of narrative anthology rather than the self-portrait.

Notable passages

The complaint about widows (c1) states Montaigne's test of marriage: constant gentleness, loyalty and agreeableness over time, not post-mortem grief. It is fully his voice and sets up the contrast with the three exempla.

Pliny's neighbour's wife (c2) is the plainest case of a wife sharing the husband's death: she examines the disease, concludes it is incurable, advises suicide, and ties herself to him by the waist for the leap into the sea.

Arria and Paetus (c3) carries the famous "Paete, non dolet" scene. Montaigne's comment judges the deed nobler than the epigram told it, and reads Paetus' own following suicide as shame at needing the example.

Seneca and Paulina (c4) is the longest scene: Seneca's last speech to friends, his plea to his wife, her counter-offer, and his answer that the constancy is the same but "the beauty and glory of thy part are much greater".

The stories as stories (c5) is Montaigne's comment on true tales and on assembling them with only a little solder, an interesting remark on compilation and authorship.

Seneca's reason to live (c6) is the essay's turn. Via Seneca's letter to Lucilius, it argues that love can oblige one to remain alive, with a quoted ethic ("we must hold the soul fast in our teeth") that qualifies the Stoic's freedom to die. It resonates loosely with socratic-thinking-style testing of a doctrine against a lived case, though here the testing is Montaigne's arrangement of sources and not a dialogue; the connection is only partial.
