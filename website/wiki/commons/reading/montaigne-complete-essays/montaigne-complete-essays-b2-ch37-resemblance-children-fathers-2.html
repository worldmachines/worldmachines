Against the Physicians, and a Word for the Baths

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In the middle slice of this essay Montaigne mounts a long, anecdote-laden attack on the medical profession (its self-serving credit-taking, mystification, endless doctrinal reversals and contradictory advice on the stone) and then, from his own experience, offers a qualified endorsement of spa baths as pleasant and harmless rather than miraculous.

This is the middle slice (of three) of the essay on the resemblance of children to their fathers, and it begins and ends mid-argument. The whole slice is Montaigne's sustained indictment of physic, stitched from anecdotes (a Lacedaemonian, the Emperor Adrian, Diogenes, Nicocles, Plato, Aesop, the Egyptian law) and from his own sickbed experience as a sufferer from the stone. He argues that doctors claim every cure and disown every death, that they cultivate mystery to secure belief, that their science is a chain of mutual refutations from Hippocrates to Paracelsus, that the task they set themselves is practically impossible, and that their advice on the stone is self-contradictory. The slice closes on a milder note: Montaigne's own use of baths, which he praises as wholesome and pleasant while denying them any miraculous power. michel-de-montaigne argues here as a patient, not as a physician, and says so.

Themes

Unfalsifiable expertise. Physicians, Montaigne says, appropriate every happy outcome ("the accidents that have cured me, and a thousand others, who do not employ physicians, physicians usurp to themselves") and blame every failure on the patient's posture, a rattling coach, an open window, or a bad fancy. Even a worsening illness is turned into proof that it would have been worse without them. The Aesop tale of the sick man, to whom every symptom is "good" until he dies "with being too well", dramatises an art whose interpretive frame absorbs any evidence. Nicocles's quip (the sun lights their successes and the earth covers their failures) and Plato's remark that physicians alone may lie at pleasure are reported authorities he endorses with approval.

Mystification and belief. He says that had he been of their counsel he would have made the discipline "more sacred and mysterious". Montaigne reports (as the doctors' own rule) that the patient's confidence matters more than the doctor's learning, that the drugs are chosen for their strangeness (tortoise foot, lizard urine, rats' excrement for stone sufferers), and that their error was only to let their quarrels become public. The irony is aimed at the profession's failure to keep up its mystery, not at mystery as such; he notes the sensible old rule that a single physician should attend a patient. The point about foreign drugs (what do the nations that supply guaiacum make of our cabbage and parsley?) is that value follows strangeness and cost, a point about the sociology of belief.

History as refutation. The catalogue of etiological theories (humours, blood, atoms, spirits, and so on) and the succession from Hippocrates through Chrysippus, Erasistratus, the Empirics, Thessalus, Crinas, and Charinus down to Paracelsus is a historical argument: each regime of practice abolishes its predecessor wholesale, so the present one has no better claim. This is the scepticism of the Apology applied to a single practical art; Montaigne does not claim to know the true cause of disease himself, only that nobody agrees. That there is nothing at stake in misjudging the height of the sun, but everything when "our whole being is concerned", gives the argument its practical bite.

Impossible demands and the cost of error. The physician must know the patient's temperament, thoughts, place, season, the planets, the drug's weight, origin and age, and mix it in perfect symmetry; one spring out of order suffices to destroy us. He adds the incredulity of compound drugs, whose ingredients are supposed to sort themselves to separate organs, and the tailor-and-cook analogy for the Egyptian division of medicine by organ. Personal grief colours it: his friend was killed by physicians afraid of stopping a dysentery.

Experience as evidence, with hedges. He cites cases he knows (a Paris gentleman cut for a stone that was not there; a bishop friend, whom Montaigne himself helped persuade to be cut, found at death to have only a kidney complaint), and that he "never found three of one opinion". He concludes surgery is more certain than physic because it sees and feels what it does. He also says the stone regimen offers a contrary for every counsel (urinate often/not often, bathe/do not, women/not), and he "could not" be given a proposition for which he could not raise an equal contrary. The conclusion is not that one should reject all care, but that those who follow "their own appetite and the advice of nature" should not be reviled.

The baths: a calibrated exception. In the last paragraph Montaigne reports having travelled to nearly all the famous baths of Christendom and found the reports of miracles false, yet judged bathing wholesome, bathing's disuse a loss, and the waters pleasant, simple, harmless, appetite-giving. They cannot raise men from desperate disease, and the cheerfulness and company are "the best and surest part" of the effect. This is a deliberate asymmetry: the same sceptical test (does it demonstrably work, can it do harm?) that condemns the physician's drugs lets the baths pass, because they claim little and risk little.

Notable passages

The Aesop tale of the physician for whom every symptom is "good" is the most compact picture of unfalsifiable authority in the slice. The paragraph beginning "As to what remains, had I been of their counsel" is Montaigne's sharpest treatment of mystification and the sociology of trust in experts. The catalogue of rival disease theories and the chronological chain of reversals carry the historical case, while the paragraphs on his own experience with stone (the Paris gentleman, the bishop, the paired contradictory regimens) give the empirical case. The closing bath passage shows his positive, tempered stance toward remedies that are natural and unpretentious. Everything here is Montaigne's own judgment or tales he approves; the Greek and Roman medical catalogue and the anecdotes are borrowed exempla, and the Latin verse quotations stand untranslated in this text.
