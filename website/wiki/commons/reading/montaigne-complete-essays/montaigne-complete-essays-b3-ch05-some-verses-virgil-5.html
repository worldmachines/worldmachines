Montaigne on women, ageing desire, and the unity of body and soul (closing slice of "Upon Some Verses of Virgil")

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In the closing stretch of "Upon Some Verses of Virgil", Montaigne defends women's inconstancy, his own frank speech about sex, and an ageing man's wish for love as a tonic, argues that body and soul cannot be separated in pleasure, and ends by claiming the sexes are cast in the same mould.

This is the last of five slices of the long essay and begins mid-argument. Montaigne first admits that men judge women's inconstancy unjustly, since desire by nature has no fixed end, and then turns on himself: an old man's feeble, ridiculous lust is a shame he would spare any woman he respects. He then defends his own unvarnished way of writing about sex, describes how he conducted his affairs (candid, sparing of promises, protective of the women's honour), and recommends love, taken in moderation, as medicine for age. A long middle passage argues that nothing in us is purely corporeal or purely spiritual, so philosophy's austerity toward bodily pleasure is too strict. He closes by renouncing love with the young, preferring its season in early youth, mocking women who will not honour the mind of an ageing body, and declaring that men and women are "cast in the same mould". Much of the unit is shot through with untranslated Latin verse (mostly Latin love-poetry), which he uses as ornament and as witness.

Themes

Inconstancy is natural, and symmetrical. Montaigne opens by calling men "unjust judges" of women and says he confesses the truth "when it makes against me". Desire, like avarice and ambition, has no end and "ever goes beyond its possession"; so inconstancy is "in some sort more pardonable in them than in us". The Joanna of Naples anecdote and Plato's law of viewing the betrothed naked are borrowed exempla, offered half-comically in support. The tone is mock-advocacy rather than a settled thesis.

Ageing and the ridiculous body. He speaks as an old man ("for the little that I am able to do now") who hates the spectacle of a feeble desire strutting "a true flame of flax", and says nature should be content with having made age "miserable, without rendering it ridiculous too". He says he would trouble no woman he must reverence. This is self-report, delivered with candid humour about his own member and "enormous injury" done by nature.

The self-portrait owed to the public; against verbal scrupulosity. "I universally owe my entire picture to the public." He grounds his wisdom in "liberty, in truth, in essence" and treats civility and ceremony as "daughters indeed, but illegitimate". He argues that where laws of decency are most elaborate, the "primitive laws of common reason" are less observed, and that whoever writes only the "reverend and canonical" leaves out half of life, divided "betwixt folly and prudence". He claims it is not judgment but "nature" that chose this scandalous speech, and that he only "excuses" it. This echoes the essay's theme of self-construction-through-writing and the project of personal-essay-as-form; it also resonates with habit-and-identity in treating decorum as custom that displaces natural duties, though Montaigne's "nature" here is itself a rhetorical appeal he does not defend further.

His own conduct in love. Responding to men who presume husbandly authority over women who favour them, he reports in the first person that he never pretended more affection than he had, was sparing of promises, took the hazards of assignations on himself, and never broke with a woman to hatred or contempt. He admits to rashness and anger, and says that if he began again he would do the same, "how fruitless soever it might be". This is self-assessment with hedges, and its self-flattery should be read with that in mind.

Love as moderate tonic, against philosophical austerity. Love is "a sprightly, lively, and gay agitation" for which, "as an experienced physician", he would prescribe moderation to rouse an ageing man. Panetius, Agesilaus, Anacreon and Socrates (the shoulder-sting anecdote) are reported exempla: Socrates "was a man, and would neither be, nor seem, any other thing". Philosophy's precept that the soul should merely follow the body in love he calls "somewhat over strict", fit only for a body in its best plight.

No pure body, no pure soul. The central philosophical move: "there is nothing in us, during this earthly prison, that is purely either corporeal or spiritual". He contrasts the saints' penance, in which the soul leads the body into pain, with the injustice of making the soul a servant in pleasure. The soul should "hatch and cherish" bodily pleasures. This is Montaigne's own argument, set against Stoic and ascetic positions he reports.

Renunciation and the exchange of pleasure. Ageing lovers should not insinuate themselves into the gaiety of the young; a man who receives pleasure and confers none is mean; he would rather die than live on charity. He prefers contemplating young beauties to acting "second in a pitiful and imperfect conjunction", dislikes artificial beauty (Arcesilaus's reply to Hemon), and places love's proper season in early adolescence, with Bion and Homer as cited witnesses. Marguerite of Navarre's remark on thirty-year-old beauty is reported as a woman's view.

Sexes in the same mould. He charges women with excusing a man's lack of understanding for beauty but never a decayed body for its mind, then concludes that "males and females are cast in the same mould" and that, "education and usage excepted, the difference is not great", citing Plato's Republic-style inclusion of women and Antisthenes. It is hedged and borrowed rather than a developed theory, and it sits beside the earlier male-centred, mock-chivalrous framing.

Notable passages

Owing his picture to the public (c1). Montaigne's manifesto for frankness: the wisdom of his instruction lies in liberty, truth, essence, not in provincial rules, and a scrupulous verbal superstition costs us the necessary and just.

Folly and prudence (c2). Writing only the reverend and canonical leaves out half of life; he excuses himself only for his "humours" and says nature, not judgment, chose his scandalous way of speaking.

His conduct in love (c3). The "free contract" and the report of how he behaved: a rare detailed self-report of practice, with an unflinching admission of his own faults.

Love as prescription (c4). Moderate love as hygiene for ageing, with Panetius and the physician conceit.

Body and soul (c5). The unity argument and critique of ascetic hierarchy; compares the soul's role in pain and pleasure.

What love would do for him (c6). Love as the one passion left to keep an ageing man "in breath", set against avarice, ambition and lawsuits, then the sober recognition that it is a "commodity hard to recover".

Same mould (c7). The essay's close on the near-equality of the sexes, with its qualifying "education and usage excepted".
