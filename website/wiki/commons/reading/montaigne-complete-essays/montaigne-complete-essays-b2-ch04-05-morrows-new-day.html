Deferring the Letter, and the Witness Within: Montaigne on Prudence, Conscience and the Rack

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne praises Amyot's Plutarch and uses a Plutarchan anecdote to weigh civility against prudence in delaying unopened letters, then in 'Of Conscience' argues that conscience is a self-informing witness that torments the guilty and steadies the innocent, and on that ground condemns judicial torture as an uncertain and inhuman test of truth.

Two short essays. Chapter IV ("To-Morrow's A New Day") opens with praise of Jacques Amyot's French Plutarch, which Montaigne credits, despite admitting he understands no Greek, with raising unlettered people "out of the dirt". It then turns to a Plutarch anecdote: Rusticus, handed a packet from the emperor mid-declamation, waited until the end to open it. Montaigne grants the civility but doubts the prudence, pairs curiosity with its opposite vice, negligence (his own inclination), and offers cases where delay proved fatal (Boutieres, Caesar, Archias of Thebes and his proverb "Business to-morrow"). He ends by refusing a firm rule: fortune keeps her own right. Chapter V ("Of Conscience") begins with a civil-war encounter in which a stranger's terror reveals an uneasy conscience, generalises to conscience as an inner accuser (with Bessus, Apollodorus, Epicurus and Hesiod as witnesses), contrasts the steady confidence of a good conscience (Scipio's three exempla, and Montaigne's own report), and pivots to judicial torture, which he judges an unreliable and cruel instrument, closing on a grim story of a general who has a soldier's belly cut open to test a woman's accusation.

Themes

Prudence is not reducible to rule. In chapter IV Montaigne separates courtesy (not interrupting a declaimer, which he allows a wise man) from self-interested delay (not breaking one's dinner or sleep, which he calls inexcusable especially in a public minister). His closing line, that it is hard "to give so exact a rule upon moral reasons, that fortune will not therein maintain her own right", is characteristic hedging: he builds a distinction and then admits the cases overflow it. The Plutarch anecdotes are reported exempla, and he tests Plutarch's praise of Rusticus rather than accepting it.

Self-report about temperament. Montaigne says he leans toward negligence rather than curiosity, never opens letters meant for others, and is vexed with himself if his eyes stray over a great man's correspondence: "Never was man less inquisitive or less prying into other men's affairs than I." This is the essayist using his own disposition as the measure (see self-construction-through-writing), though the claim is a self-description, not a general norm. He also admits ignorance of Greek, a candid limit on his praise of Amyot.

Conscience as an internal tribunal. Montaigne's own judgment is that conscience "makes us betray, accuse, and fight against ourselves", supplying witnesses against ourselves when none exist. The supporting voices are borrowed: Bessus and the sparrows, Apollodorus's dream, Epicurus on the hiding-hole, Hesiod correcting Plato that punishment is born with the sin, and the Latin verse on guilty sleep-talking (left untranslated in the text). His own contribution is partly experiential: he can say he has gone through hazards with a steadier pace from knowing the innocence of his intentions. Scipio's episodes are exempla of confidence; Montaigne notes Livy's gloss that Scipio's lofty spirit and fortune made him unable to stoop to defending his innocence, which complicates the lesson, since proud assurance and innocence look alike.

Civil war and illegibility. The opening anecdote shows how a faction war erases outward markers: the enemy is "not distinguished from yourself by any apparent mark either of language or habit", nourished under "the same law, air, and manners". Identity cannot be read off the surface, and Montaigne himself fears being asked his name; he reports losing men, horses and a beloved page to such mistakes. The fugitive gentleman reads his own conscience onto the world, imagining his heart visible through visor and cassock. A mild resonance with Legibility: here the problem is the failure of external legibility between neighbours, and conscience works as an unwanted interior legibility, though the text makes no such systemic argument.

Against the rack. The longest sustained argument is Montaigne's own: torture "seems to be rather a trial of patience than of truth", since the strong conceal the truth and the weak say what is false ("why should pain sooner make me confess what really is, than force me to say what is not?"). He traces the practice to a belief in the force of conscience (it is supposed to help the guilty confess and fortify the innocent) and rejects that inference as uncertain, citing Philotas as one falsely confessing. He notes that nations deemed less barbarous by the Greeks and Romans, who call them so, find it horrible to tear apart a man of whom they are still in doubt, and that torture can be worse than execution. He calls it "very inhumanly, notwithstanding, and to very little purpose, in my opinion", while granting that some defend it as the least evil human weakness could invent. The inverted barbarian remark echoes Montaigne's cultural-relativist register elsewhere, but here it is a single aside.

Notable passages

Amyot and Plutarch (c1). Montaigne's praise for Amyot's translation as an act of cultural uplift ("we dare now speak and write; the ladies are able to read to schoolmasters; 'tis our breviary") is a small document of vernacular learning and reading, tied to personal-essay-as-form only loosely: Plutarch is the main source the essays draw on, and Montaigne declares his dependence on a translation.

Curiosity, negligence, and his own habit (c2). A compact vice-pairing and self-report, with Montaigne placing himself on the negligent side.

The proverb of deferral (c3). The three cases of ignored warnings (Boutieres, Caesar, Archias) and the Greek proverb "Business to-morrow" give the chapter its title and its exempla; the lesson drawn is hedged.

The gentleman who feared his own conscience (c4). The civil-war scene and Montaigne's own thesis statement on conscience as self-betrayal.

Against torture (c5, c6). The reasoning against the rack and the closing story, which Montaigne presents as one he cannot source ("I know not where I had this story") and which he says "exactly matches the conscience of our justice". Whether "An instructive sentence" is grim irony or approval is left ambiguous in the text; the story itself shows a verdict reached by mutilating the accused, and its placement after the critique reads as an illustration of judicial cruelty, though the text does not spell this out.
