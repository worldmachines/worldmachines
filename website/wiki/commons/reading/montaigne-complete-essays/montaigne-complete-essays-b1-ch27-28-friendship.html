Of Friendship, and the Heading of La Boétie's Sonnets

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne frames his essays as grotesques around a borrowed centrepiece, then argues that perfect friendship (his with La Boétie) is a fused, unique, indivisible union that outranks kinship, love, marriage and Greek pederasty, ending with a disclaimer about La Boétie's 'Voluntary Servitude' and a bare heading for the sonnets that follow.

The unit holds all of Book 1 chapter 27 and only the title and dedication line of chapter 28. Montaigne opens with a painter's trick: a finished picture in the middle of the wall, surrounded by grotesques. His own writings are the grotesques, so he will borrow a centrepiece from Estienne de la Boetie, the discourse 'Voluntary Servitude'. He then writes the centrepiece himself in a different register, an account of the most perfect friendship, with La Boétie as the only instance he claims. He ranks it against kinship, erotic love, marriage and the Greek love of youths, and finds all of them lacking. Its marks are fusion of wills, no accounting of benefits, indivisibility, and an unexplained fatedness. He closes with grief for La Boétie, then (in quotation marks) a short note explaining why he is not printing the 'Voluntary Servitude' text. Chapter 28 appears only as a heading with a dedication to Madame de Grammont.

Themes

The essay as grotesque. Montaigne describes what he scribbles as 'grotesques and monstrous bodies, made of various parts, without any certain figure'. This is a self-deprecating statement of form (see personal-essay-as-form, self-construction-through-writing). The painter's borrowed centrepiece is a hedge: he says he lacks the power to offer 'a rich piece, finely polished'. The 'Voluntary Servitude' text was to be the polished middle, and the unit ends by explaining it is withheld.

Friendship defined by what it excludes. Montaigne argues that relationships mixed with pleasure, profit or public interest are 'so much the less friendships'. Children and parents lack the equality and frankness friendship needs, and the obligation to reprove a father is improper. Brothers are bound by estates and rivalry, and these ties are imposed by law and nature, so 'there is less of our own choice'. The Greek and Stoic views he reports (Aristippus on children, Chilo on loving as if one would later hate) are reported to be tested. He endorses Chilo's maxim only for ordinary friendships.

Love, marriage, the Academy: his verdicts. Erotic love is 'more active, more eager, and more sharp' but fickle, a fever that destroys itself in fruition. Friendship grows with enjoyment because it is 'spiritual'. Marriage is free only at entry. Montaigne also states that women's 'ordinary talent' is insufficient for friendship, and that it is 'without example' that the sex has arrived at it. This is his own claim and is an obvious limit of his argument. He reconstructs the Academy's account of the love of youths, where the beauty of the soul was supposed to replace bodily beauty. He accepts it only as 'a love which ended in friendship', and rejects the practice as against 'our manners'.

Fusion without reason. The central claim is that the two souls mix 'with so universal a mixture, that there is no more sign of the seam'. When asked why he loved La Boétie, he can only say 'because it was he, because it was I.' He ascribes the union to a 'fated power' and 'some secret appointment of heaven', and says they were drawn together by reputation before they met. He hedges ('I think') but does not retreat. This is the clearest case in the unit of explanation reaching its limit.

No debts, no rivals. Because the friends' wills are one, benefit, obligation and thanks lose meaning: 'one soul in two bodies' (credited to Aristotle). The Eudamidas anecdote (borrowed from the tradition, not Montaigne's own) shows a will that bequeaths the friends a chance to serve him. The Blosius–Laelius exchange shows how perfect trust in a friend's will can look like sedition from outside. Montaigne defends Blosius on the condition that both friends are 'guided by virtue' and reason. Friendship is also indivisible, since 'Nothing is extreme, that has its like', so a friendship of three or more is not this kind.

Ordinary relations are different. He judges servants and physicians by one competence each ('if he be a good cook'), and prefers wit at table. He says only that this is his method and not advice for others. He doubts any reader without experience can judge, since ancient writings on friendship seem to him 'flat and poor' beside the experience.

Grief as the proof. The last movement is personal testimony. Four years with La Boétie make the rest of his life 'smoke, an obscure and tedious night'. He is 'no more than half of myself' and feels he 'defrauds' La Boétie by outliving him. Latin elegy (Catullus-like laments, left untranslated here) frames this.

Voluntary Servitude disclaimed. In the closing quoted note Montaigne reports that the discourse was misused by those 'aiming at disturbing and changing the condition of our government', and that they mixed it with their own writing. He therefore does not print it. He insists it was a boyhood exercise on a hackneyed theme and that La Boétie was a loyal citizen who hated the commotions of the time. This is Montaigne's own protective framing, not a neutral description, and it recasts the 'liberty against tyrants' text he praised at the start. Chapter 28's heading then stands alone.

Notable passages

The grotesques (c1). The painter simile sets Montaigne's own account of his form: a patchwork of parts without 'any certain figure'. It is the essay's frame and links to the later self-portrait claims in michel-de-montaigne.

Because it was he, because it was I (c2). The central statement of the friendship, including its inexplicability, the fated meeting and the mutual 'losing' of wills. It is also the passage most often cited from the book.

Blosius and the friend's will (c3). Montaigne tests friendship against civic duty. He does not claim friendship overrides virtue, but says that a perfect friend's will can be trusted as one's own, so what looks like a pledge to evil is no such pledge.

Indivisibility (c4). Montaigne argues that perfect friendship cannot be shared, and that it dissolves ordinary obligations such as secrecy because the friend is 'not another, but myself'.

Languishing life (c5). The testimony of loss, in which the friend's death turns later pleasures to affliction.

The boy of sixteen (c6). The disclaimer about 'Voluntary Servitude' shows Montaigne managing a politically dangerous text in the civil wars. He defends La Boétie's obedience to the laws he was born under, and in the same breath concedes the tyranny essay was in circulation and being misused.
