Of Physiognomy (first part): Socrates, borrowed learning, civil war, and falling back on oneself

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In the first third of "Of Physiognomy", Montaigne praises Socrates' plain, natural wisdom against borrowed learning, doubts that books and Stoic exertion arm us against death better than peasants' native constancy, then turns to the civil war's ruin of discipline and his own exposed position as a moderate, concluding that he must fall back on himself.

This slice opens Book 3, chapter 12 and runs from the chapter's opening through Montaigne's report of his own losses in the civil troubles. It begins with Socrates as the model of a wisdom that is plain, common and natural, and uses him to attack the appetite for learning and the habit of borrowing what is "another's". It then compares Seneca and Plutarch and sets the constancy of poor peasants above the school-taught constancy of philosophers. A sharp turn follows: Montaigne describes the French civil war as a self-poisoning disease with no discipline, argues (with Favonius and Plato) that a state must not be cured with so mortal a drug as rebellion, and recounts how his own moderation left him suspected by both sides. He ends by deciding that the safest course is to rely on himself, and that the losses were "profitable inconveniences". The slice ends at "Potentissimus est, qui se habet in potestate"; the rest of the chapter is not covered here.

Themes

Borrowed authority versus natural resources. The chapter opens by saying "Almost all the opinions we have are taken on authority and trust", and adds that this is no bad thing given how weak our own judgment is. Socrates, whose discourses we admire only out of "reverence to public sanction", is held up because he drew on carters, joiners, cobblers and masons and "nothing in all this borrowed from arts and sciences". Montaigne's claim is that "We are all of us richer than we think we are; but we are taught to borrow and to beg." This is Montaigne's own judgment, offered as self-report as much as doctrine.

The vanity and danger of learning. Knowledge is a good with "a great deal of vanity and weakness" and it is a risky purchase because it can only be stored in the soul, so we return from the market "either already infected or amended". Montaigne approves, with some irony, of those who vow ignorance as well as poverty and chastity. He reports that books "have not so much served me for instruction as exercise", that Cicero's Tusculan Questions enriched his tongue but not his courage, and he admits that his own book contains many borrowed subtleties. He hedges: he will not "sift them no further".

Seneca versus Plutarch, and the peasant. Seneca's sweat and panting against death suggest, to Montaigne, an impetuous nature hard pressed by its enemy; Plutarch's more disdainful way he finds more manly and persuasive, one that "wins" the judgment where Seneca "ravishes" it. This is Montaigne's own critical preference, stated as opinion ("in my opinion", "I am apt to believe"). The gardener who buried his father or son that morning and the poor who rename their diseases "a cough" or "a stitch" stand as lived evidence that nature supplies constancy without Aristotle or Cato. The example is Montaigne's observation of his own neighbours.

Civil war as autoimmune disease. The war is "monstrous": it "ruins itself with the rest", comes to quell sedition and is itself full of it, and "Our physic makes us sick!" Its licence spreads like air; armies hold together only by foreign mercenaries; generals must obey their soldiers. "Long toleration begets habit; habit, consent and imitation" is the mechanism by which good natures are corrupted. Montaigne contrasts French indiscipline with Roman and Turkish military discipline (the orchard, the egg, Selim and the gardens of Damascus), which are reported exempla, not his firsthand knowledge.

Against reform by rebellion; legitimate wickedness. Citing Favonius and Plato (reported authorities, with Montaigne claiming he was "a Platonist in this point before I knew there had ever been such a man"), he holds that even tyranny does not justify a remedy that costs citizens' blood. He distrusts those who think they advance salvation by "the most express causes that we have of most assured damnation", and says nothing is worse than when wickedness "assumes, with the magistrates' permission, the cloak of virtue". Divine and religious titles bait ambition, avarice, cruelty and revenge.

The moderate in the middle. Montaigne reports being robbed on all sides, a Guelph to the Ghibelline and a Ghibelline to the Guelph. He notes his own habit of not justifying or explaining himself, which feeds suspicion, and admits that the offence troubles him more than the loss.

Self-reliance as consolation. Having looked round for a friend to trust his old age to, he finds himself "bare", and concludes that it is safest to "trust to myself in my necessity". Men "throw themselves upon foreign assistance to spare their own"; "no one is arrived at himself". Calamity is a mallet that forces the hoops down on a vessel ready to fall apart, and an exercise for worse to come. The slice closes on the maxim that true liberty is power over oneself. This links the chapter's early theme (own resources over borrowed ones) to the political one. See self-construction-through-writing and michel-de-montaigne; Montaigne's turn to the self here is practical and forced by circumstance rather than programmatic.

Notable passages

Socrates as the plain model. The opening argues that we cannot see the beauty of simplicity because we value only what is "pointed and puffed out and inflated by art", and that Socrates' wisdom of carters and cobblers is what really serves "the use of life". It is the unit's starting thesis and sets up all later contrasts.

Richer than we think. A short, compact statement of the borrow-and-beg diagnosis, linking appetite for wealth and power to appetite for knowledge. It echoes later arguments for the self as resource, and loosely rhymes with habit-and-identity only in the sense that education forms us toward dependence; Montaigne does not use that frame here.

Books gave exercise, not instruction. Montaigne's most candid self-report on reading: his courage in death is as nature framed it, and the Tusculans enriched only his tongue. Relevant to WMP interest in what stored learning actually does.

Peasants and the names of diseases. The garden labourer and the gentle diminutives for illness give a social-historical glimpse of vernacular endurance set against school philosophy, with Montaigne's framing as admirer from outside.

A monstrous war. The self-devouring war and the physic-that-sickens image. This is a first-hand account of the French Wars of Religion as institutional dissolution (discipline gone, soldiers commanding generals), loosely rhyming with a Gramsci-gap or decay-sequence reading of a collapsing order, though Montaigne offers moral and medical diagnosis and no cyclical theory.

Not curing the state with rebellion. The Favonius and Plato argument for patience and his warning against the cloak of virtue. Montaigne qualifies his Platonism with a pointed remark about not being instructed by a heathen on reliance on God.

Falling back on oneself. The search for a friend and the discovery that only self-reliance is certain, which anticipates the chapter's later themes and his persona as moderate in an age of factions.
