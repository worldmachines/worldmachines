Custom, Conformity and the Danger of Innovating on Laws

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In the second half of 'Of Custom', Montaigne shows that received laws and usages rest on weak foundations yet argues that a wise man should inwardly judge freely while outwardly conforming, because innovation endangers an interlocked state, as the French civil wars showed, though he concedes that necessity can occasionally justify bending the laws.

This slice is the closing part of the essay on custom (it opens mid-argument, after a remark on how people apply good sayings to others and not to themselves). Montaigne first shows how custom makes people content with whatever they were bred to, citing Darius's Greeks and Indians, and how "use veils from us the true aspect of things". He then reports his own failed attempt to ground a customary practice in first principles, and runs through French legal, judicial and sartorial usages that look absurd once the mask is off. From there he turns to the practical rule: keep the soul free, conform in externals, and do not alter a received law, since government is an interlocked structure and he has watched novelty produce civil war. He ends by admitting that extreme necessity can require the laws to bend, and gives Spartan and Roman exempla of doing so by subterfuge.

Themes

Custom as the veil over things. Custom reconciles each person to their station (Highlanders do not pant after Touraine) and makes the foreign monstrous: people raised to liberty find other governments unnatural, and those raised under monarchy, freed from one master, "presently run" to make another. The Darius anecdote is a Herodotean exemplum that Montaigne uses symmetrically: the Greeks recoil from eating the dead, the Indians from burning them. His own gloss is that familiarity hides the true aspect of things. This is a strong precursor of the idea that habit is the invisible machinery behind what feels natural (compare habit-and-identity).

Unmasking the foundations, then declining to act on the result. Montaigne reports (as his own experience) that when he tried to justify a received usage by its origin rather than by law and example, "I found the foundation so weak" that he nearly lost conviction himself. Plato's cure for unnatural loves (public opinion, poets' horror stories) is cited as an exemplum of how even philosophers lean on custom, while Chrysippus, who refuses that crutch, lands in wild opinions. Stripping the "hoary head and rivelled face of ancient usage" overthrows judgment yet leaves it "restored to a much more sure estate". He then lists the oddities of his own legal order: laws unpublished in the people's language, bought and sold judicial office, a fourth estate of lawyers, and the contradiction between the laws of honour and the laws of justice. He notes with local pride that a Gascon gentleman first resisted Charlemagne's imposition of Latin and imperial law.

Inner freedom, outer conformity. Singular fashions are "marks of folly and vain affectation"; the wise man withdraws his soul from the crowd to judge freely, but lends his actions, labours, fortune and life to common opinion, as Socrates did in refusing to escape by disobeying the magistrate. This is a stance of self-possession that anticipates his wider project of self-study (self-construction-through-writing), though here it is applied to public life: thought is private, conduct is public.

Government as an interlocked structure; against innovation. Montaigne doubts that any manifest benefit from altering a law outweighs the danger, because the state is built like a structure in which moving one brick is felt by the whole body. The Thurian halter, the Spartan law-giver and the Ephor who cut Phrynis's strings are exempla of hostility to novelty (the Ephor condemns the string simply because it is new, which Montaigne reports without endorsing every detail). He then speaks for himself: he has "a great aversion from a novelty" because he was an eyewitness of its evils in the civil wars. First movers are first overwhelmed, imitators are more vicious than inventors, and new vices take plausible names (he cites Thucydides). It is presumption and "strange self-love" to overthrow public peace for one's own opinions, trading certain vices for merely disputable errors. Resonates with a Burkean or conservative temperament and with Montaigne's lived Wars of Religion context; it is a counter-reformation of the self rather than a rejection of reason, since he locates the problem in private reason's "private jurisdiction".

Obedience, religion and the limits of private reason. The Christian injunction to obey the magistrate is offered as a mark of the religion's utility, with the Passion itself submitted to ordinary justice. The follower of received forms pleads simplicity and example; the reformer "usurps the authority of judging" and must discern both the defect of what he abolishes and the virtue of what he introduces. Divine miracles are not precedents for human deviation. Montaigne says this consideration kept even his unruly youth in check, and that few in the present quarrel really understand either side; their medicine only stirs the humours without purging.

The exception: necessity. Montaigne closes by qualifying the rule. Urgent necessity sometimes requires the laws to "yield and give way", since legitimate procedure is "cold, heavy, and constrained" against unbridled violence; Octavius and Cato are blamed for letting the country suffer rather than bend the law. He then lists subterfuges: suspending laws for a day, shifting a calendar date, the Spartans making Aratus nominal admiral so that Lysander could still command, and Pericles' advice to turn a law-tablet rather than remove it. He ends with Plutarch's praise of Philopoemen, who knew how to overrule the laws when public necessity so required. So the essay does not end in plain conservatism; Montaigne holds both the primacy of received law and its occasional suspension, without resolving the tension.

Notable passages

Use veils the true aspect of things. The Darius anecdote and the Highlanders/Scythians line show custom as a mechanism of contentment and horror (c1).

The mask taken off. The claim that stripping custom overturns and then re-establishes judgment, with the critique of French law that follows, is the essay's relativist core (c2).

Wise man within, conformist without. The explicit statement of the public/private split, grounded in Socrates and "the rule of rules, the general law of laws" (c3).

The brick and the body. The image of the state as a structure where moving one brick is felt by the whole is the essay's key argument against change; compare the infrastructural view of a world machine in world-machines, though Montaigne's frame is a single monarchy rather than an era (c4).

Self-love and civil war. His most personal and polemical passage, tying innovation to the civil wars and to presumption (c5).

Making the laws do what they can. The closing concession that necessity can warrant yielding the laws, which keeps the essay from being a simple defence of the status quo (c6).
