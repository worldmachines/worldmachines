Voluntary Death: The Stoic Case, Its Limits, and the Custom of Cea

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne surveys the ancient and modern case for voluntary death, pairing Stoic arguments that the keys of life are in our own hands with counter-arguments from duty, constancy and hope, and closes on the Marseilles and Cea customs where suicide is publicly reasoned, licensed and witnessed.

The essay opens with a brief disclaimer: to philosophise is to doubt, and Montaigne, who writes "at random and play[s] the fool", leaves final determination to "the authority of the divine will". He then assembles a long dossier on self-killing. First comes the Spartan and Stoic case (death as the liberty that makes life bearable; "one door into life, but a hundred thousand ways out"; the suicide offends no law, as one does not offend the law against thieves by embezzling one's own money). Then he turns, signalling that "this does not pass without admitting a dispute", to the opposing case: we are God's garrison and our country's debtors, virtue is shown in enduring rather than fleeing, life is "our being, 'tis all we have", and flight from death often runs into it. He then asks what occasions justify a "reasonable exit", piling up exempla (Cleomenes, Josephus against Brutus and Cassius, Pliny and Seneca on diseases, Razis, Ninachetuen, Vibius Virrius, Astapa, Abydos) and ends with institutional customs: Marseilles' public hemlock, and the old woman of Cea who dies with Sextus Pompeius as witness.

Themes

Death as the keys of life (Stoic position, reported with sympathy). Sayings of Damidas, Agis and the Spartan boy, and the Stoic claim that the wise man lives "as long as he ought, not so long as he can", frame suicide as the guarantee of freedom: "Living is slavery if the liberty of dying be wanting." Montaigne voices this in his own register too ("The most voluntary death is the finest. Life depends upon the pleasure of others; death upon our own"), and offers the medical analogy that ordinary cures already trade life for relief. Attribution is blended: the Stoics, Hegesias and Diogenes are cited, but several sentences read as Montaigne's own rhetoric, and he later qualifies it.

The counter-case: duty, constancy, nature. Montaigne reports, as an opinion held by "many", that we cannot quit the "garrison of the world" without God's licence, and that country and the afterlife both claim us. He then himself leans to constancy: "more constancy in suffering the chain we are tied to than in breaking it", Regulus above Cato; suicide from fear is "cowardice, not virtue". A distinct argument from nature follows: hating and despising oneself is "a disease particular to man", and the wish to be an angel (or to buy the absence of evils by dying) is self-defeating, since no one remains to enjoy the benefit. Plato's Laws are cited for ignominious burial of the cowardly self-killer.

Judging the occasion; hope and precipitation. Admitting that "there is to be some limit", Montaigne tests cases. Cleomenes refuses early suicide while "an inch of hope" remains; Josephus survives, Brutus and Cassius die "before the due time"; d'Anguien nearly forfeits a victory. His own testimony is small and concrete: "I have seen a hundred hares escape out of the very teeth of the greyhounds." Seneca's retort (fortune has no power over one who knows how to die) is set against the old adage that all is to be hoped for while one lives; he does not declare a winner. Pain and fear of a worse death "seem to me the most excusable incitements".

Catalogue of motives (exempla, mostly from classical and historical sources). Honour (Ninachetuen, Boges), conjugal fidelity (Sextilia, Paxaea, the wife of Fulvius), patriotism (Nerva, Vibius Virrius, Jubellius Taurea), chastity (Pelagia, Sophronia), avoiding ignominy or a worse death (Scribonia, the Sicilian of Gozzo, the prisoner tricked into fasting). Montaigne's few personal glosses are telling: sexual violence is judged the worst violation of conscience on the ground that it mixes "a little consent", yet he prefers the Toulouse woman's wry thanks to God and Marot's "doux nenny" to the Parisian author's urging of despair.

Collective suicide and social contagion. Astapa, Abydos and the Indian city beseiged by Alexander show self-destruction as a crowd phenomenon. Montaigne observes that such resolutions "seem the more fierce and cruel in proportion as the effect is more universal, and yet are really less so than when singly executed", because "the ardour of society [ravishes] particular judgments". Philip's grant of three days to the Abydeans is bleakly ironic. Tiberius's law (the self-killed keep their estates; the condemned forfeit them) shows state interest bending the incentives of death.

Hope of a greater good and the plasticity of the word "despair". St. Paul, Cleombrotus after Plato's Phaedo, Jacques du Chastel, and the idol-worshippers crushed under the chariot show that a voluntary end can issue from "a calm and temperate desire proceeding from a mature and deliberate judgment" rather than despair. The bishop's sword-in-hand charge is ranked above the self-immolation of idol devotees for magnanimity; the contrast is Montaigne's own, and ethnographic details about the "new discovered world" are reported secondhand.

Custom as institution (the essay's title). Marseilles kept hemlock at public charge for those who, before the six hundred of its senate, gave reasons; the same law "was also in use in other places". At Cea, a woman over ninety, content and prosperous, explains herself to her citizens, invites Pompeius to witness, dies after an orderly distribution of goods and a running commentary on the poison's progress. A Hyperborean people closes the essay with a ritual leap from a rock. This anchors the essay in custom: a practice that sits as a crime in one regime is a regulated, even honoured, rite in another. Montaigne adds no overt verdict on the custom itself; his closing remark concerns pain and fear of worse death as excusable motives. This connects with habit-and-identity and with Montaigne's wider custom-relativism, though here the relativism is shown by exempla rather than argued.

Notable passages

The opening disclaimer (c1) is a characteristic Montaigne hedge, deferring to divine authority while presenting himself as a doubter rather than a chairman of the debate; it governs how the rest should be read, as an inquiry and not a ruling. The Stoic centerpiece (c2) is the strongest statement of the pro-suicide case and mixes quoted doctrine with Montaigne's own phrasing. The turn to constancy (c3) and the argument from nature against self-contempt (c4) are the counterweight; note that c4's "it is against nature for us to contemn and make little account of ourselves" is a rare appeal to nature in an essay otherwise dominated by exempla. The collective-suicide passage (c5) is the unit's most original social observation about the contagion of resolve. The St. Paul and Cleombrotus passage (c6) complicates the label "despair", while the Cea narrative (c7) gives the title its meaning and presents a suicide as dignified, communal and legally attended. For world-machines readers, the unit shows custom and law as machinery that sets the terms on which the most private act is permitted, punished or ritualised, in the spirit of habit-and-identity; it is a different register from Legibility concerns, though the Marseilles senate's requirement of reasons given to an authority is a small instance of an institution making a death readable.
