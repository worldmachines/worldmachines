Of Virtue, and Of a Monstrous Child

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne argues that heroic virtue is usually a fit or impulse rather than a settled habit, tests that claim against Pyrrho, suicidal widows, fatalist soldiers and assassins, then in a short second essay treats a conjoined child as a prompt to say that nothing contrary to custom is contrary to nature.

Two essays. "Of Virtue" (II.29) opens with a distinction between the "flights and emotions of the soul" and "a resolute and constant habit": Montaigne doubts that the elevated qualities of the ancient heroes can "so thoroughly tinct and imbue the soul" as to become ordinary and natural. He then runs a chain of exempla about extreme constancy: Pyrrho's failures to live his doctrine, a husbandman and a young gentleman who mutilate themselves in rage, a woman of Bergerac who drowns herself after a night's resolve, Indian widows and gymnosophists who die by fire as custom or profession, believers in fate who go naked to war, and the assassins of the Prince of Orange. The essay does not state one thesis; it keeps asking when a resolute act reflects a settled disposition, when it reflects passion, custom or belief. "Of a Monstrous Child" (II.30) is a short report of a conjoined child Montaigne saw exhibited for money and a herdsman in Medoc, followed by a brief argument that "monsters" are only unfamiliar to us.

Themes

Impulse versus habit (Montaigne's own judgment). He reports "by experience" that souls roused by others' discourses or examples are launched "much beyond their ordinary stretch", but this is "a kind of passion" that ravishes them from themselves; afterwards they slacken until "upon every trivial occasion, the losing of a bird, or the breaking of a glass" we are moved like common people. He allows that a very imperfect man may do anything "order, moderation, and constancy excepted", and cites unnamed "Sages" that a man is best judged in his everyday habit. This is a self-report as much as a claim about heroes ("we, who are but abortive births of men"). It connects with habit-and-identity, though Montaigne's concern here is whether virtue can become habit at all, not how habit forms identity.

The sceptic who cannot live his scepticism. Pyrrho, who "erected so pleasant a knowledge upon ignorance", is reported (as a tradition, "'tis said") to have lived in total indifference, kept from harm by his friends, and to have borne cautery without wincing. Montaigne remarks that bringing the soul to such imaginations is something, joining effects to them more, and doing so habitually "almost incredible". The two Pyrrho anecdotes (the scolding of his sister, the dog) show him conceding that "it is very hard totally to put off man". The conclusion is the anecdote's own, not Montaigne's explicit verdict, but it fits the essay's theme.

Extreme acts, ambiguous causes. The mutilations and the drowning are contemporary local cases Montaigne reports as heard or nearby; he notes that if the second had been done in cold reflection and for religion, as with the priests of Cybele, we would call it high. The woman's resolution being "a whole night forming" is what he finds remarkable. The Indian widows and gymnosophists (reported from "a certain author of our times" and tradition) are a contrast: there it is custom, competition for honour and "express profession of their order", and what makes the wonder is "this constant premeditation of the whole life". Custom is thus presented as the machinery that can make what looks like heroic virtue ordinary. This is the nearest approach to cultural relativism in the essay, and Montaigne does not judge the practice.

Fate, faith and courage. Montaigne reports the schoolmen's reply to the fatum objection (God sees rather than foresees; events cause knowledge, not knowledge events), then observes that commanders use fatalism to encourage soldiers and that it is easily said but not easily believed. The Bedouins of Joinville, the two Florentine friars, and the Turkish youth taught by a hare are exempla of faith producing action. Montaigne's own comment is that the story of the hare shows "how flexible our reason is to all sorts of images", and he says of a learned man who changed his faith on a whimsical ground that he finds the miracle "stronger, taken the contrary way". He is sceptical of reason as a guide to belief, and his remark on his own age's faith is sharp: it is "very light".

Resolution and motive in assassins. On the killers of the Prince of Orange and the attacker near Orleans, he reasons about the practical conduct of each (the poignard, the pistol, the escape across the river) to judge whether they went to certain death, and concludes the motives of such persuasion "may be diverse, for our fancy does what it will, both with itself and us". The Assassins close the essay as a group for whom killing an unbeliever is the nearest way to paradise. Montaigne gives no explicit moral conclusion, so the essay ends on the question it began with.

Monsters and nature (Of a Monstrous Child). After describing the child in clinical detail, he offers a political reading (the double body as a favourable sign of the king keeping the parts of the state under one law), but immediately withholds it: "in things already past there needs no divination", as Epimenides "always prophesied backward". He then states that what we call monsters are not so to God, that whatever falls out contrary to custom "we say is contrary to nature, but nothing, whatever it be, is contrary to her". This is a direct statement that the category of the unnatural is a product of custom and novelty, and it complements the habit theme of the first essay. It resonates with michel-de-montaigne's wider scepticism about what counts as natural, though here the framing is theological (God's wisdom) where elsewhere it is empirical.

Notable passages

The opening distinction (c1) frames the whole chapter, and carries its self-deprecating tone. The Pyrrho passage (c2) is the sharpest test case of living a doctrine. The widows and gymnosophists passage (c3) is the longest cultural description; its attribution is to a contemporary traveller, not to Montaigne's observation. The fatum passage (c4) is a rare place where he reports a theological solution in detail. The monstrous-child description (c5) and the closing argument (c6) belong to the second essay and should not be read as part of the argument about virtue.
