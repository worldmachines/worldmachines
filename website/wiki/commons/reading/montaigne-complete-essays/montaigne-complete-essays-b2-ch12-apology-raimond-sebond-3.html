Beasts as reasoners: the animal catalogue in the Apology (slice 3 of 15)

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In a middle slice of the Apology, Montaigne piles up anecdotes from Plutarch, Aristotle, Arrian and others to argue that beasts show reasoning, teaching, friendship, justice, thrift and even religion, so that man's claimed superiority rests on custom and ignorance of animals' own qualities.

This slice sits mid-argument in the Apology for Raimond Sebond and contains no new thesis statement; it is an extended inventory of animal behaviour offered as evidence against human superiority. It opens with cooperative hunting and the cunning of the cuttle-fish, moves through animal medicine, the dog at the three-way crossing (reasoned by Chrysippus), trained and teaching animals (blind men's dogs, the play-acting dog, Susa's oxen, nightingale lessons, dancing elephants, the pie), and the elephant's war service. It then turns to Montaigne's own interpretive claims: strangeness breeds contempt, the creatures' unknown faculties, the closeness of animals to us in generation, justice, friendship, desire, avarice and thrift, and ends on war as a human defect beasts do not covet. Nearly all the facts are reported from authorities (Plutarch, Aristotle, Arrian, Juba, Cleanthes, Lucretius, Oppian); Montaigne's own voice appears in the framing and in a few first-person sightings.

Themes

Animal knowledge is reason, or at least no less than ours. Montaigne rejects the dodge that animals act only on nature's instinct: to say so is "not to take from them the dignity of knowledge and reason, but with greater force to attribute it to them than to us". The reported Chrysippus (a scornful judge of animals elsewhere, Montaigne notes) is made to concede that the dog at the crossroads reasons by elimination. The standard of proof is observation, mostly of ordinary household animals.

Teaching and learning as the test. Montaigne argues that teaching needs more understanding than being taught, and cites animals that teach (nightingales instructing young, per Aristotle) and learn (parrots, dancing elephants, the pie that retreats into silence to prepare a new imitation). The blind man's dog, which he says he has himself watched, is his favored case of judgment beyond training. The elephants are claimed to differ from men less than man differs from man; this is Montaigne's own stated maxim ("what I usually maintain").

Contempt for the strange, and the limit of our standing to judge. Montaigne generalises from foreigners who were taken for brutes because their language and manners were unfamiliar: "All that seems strange to us, and that we do not understand, we condemn." Animals have qualities in common with us, on which we may reason by comparison, and qualities particular to themselves, about which "what know we what to make of them!". Here the animal argument doubles as a cultural-relativist one (compare the Of Cannibals and Of Custom concerns), and the sceptical move is that absence of visible signs (religion in other beasts) does not license a negative verdict. The elephants' apparent worship and the ants' apparent parley (Cleanthes) are offered as hints of mutual communication from which we are excluded "through our own defect".

Superior faculties we lack. The remora that halts galleys, the hedgehog's wind-forecasting, the polypus's active colour change, the cramp-fish, birds of passage and the mother dog's choice of the best whelp are cited as effects "much greater than ours" that imply some excellent faculty in them unknown to us.

Our nature is theirs; our additions are artifice. Generation, nourishment and dying are alike, so whatever man adds above the beast cannot come from reason's meditation. Using the Epicurean sorting of desires (natural and necessary; natural and not necessary; neither), he says almost all human desires are superfluous and artificial, and that false opinion floods the natural ones like strangers crowding out a city's inhabitants and imposing new laws and customs. This sits near the Montaignian theme of habit as invisible machinery (see habit-and-identity), though here the agent is opinion rather than custom.

Moral qualities in beasts. They show a sort of justice (rewarding benefactors, equal sharing with their young), friendship more lively and constant than ours (the dogs of Lysimachus and Pyrrhus leaping onto the pyre), sympathy beyond reason, avarice (Thales's mule, which he reads as a picture of human avarice) and thrift (ants). Montaigne does not flatten this into praise: animals also have jealousy, malice and cross-species or incestuous couplings, which he reports without moralizing.

War as human weakness. In the closing paragraph he asks whether war, "the greatest and most magnificent of human actions", is a prerogative or a testimony of our weakness; beasts do not covet the science of destroying their own kind, and the Latin verse says lions and boars do not kill their own. He asks the question rather than settling it, though his phrasing leans to the second reading.

Notable passages

The dog at the three ways (c1). Chrysippus is forced to grant that the dog performs a disjunctive syllogism, and Montaigne ends by asking whether the dog does not know this of itself as well as from Trapezuntius, a jab at learned logic. The argument is an exemplum borrowed from the ancients, not Montaigne's experiment.

The guide dog (c2). Montaigne's own observation: dogs forsake a plain path to keep a blind master from the ditch. He asks how a man could teach a dog to put its master's safety above its own convenience, and whether this can be grasped "without ratiocination".

Strangeness and judgment (c3). The pivot of the slice: Montaigne says he has insisted on remote examples only because we prize the unusual, claims that ordinary animals show the same wonders, and applies the logic to men from distant countries taken for savages. Resonates with the relativism of noble-savage discourse, though Montaigne's point here is epistemic humility rather than idealization of the foreign.

Elephants' religion and the ants' ransom (c4). An exemplum chain: elephant ritual in the morning sun, Cleanthes' ants negotiating a ransom for their dead. Montaigne's hedge is that we cannot infer that others lack religion merely because we do not see it.

Natural and artificial desires (c5). An Epicurean taxonomy used to indict human appetite as mostly artificial, with the metaphor of strangers displacing the natural inhabitants of a city. The city metaphor is Montaigne's own and politically charged, though he applies it to the soul and appetite, not to history.

War (c6). The closing question, which turns the animal comparison back on human pride in its most celebrated activity.

Self-reference: in a parenthesis Montaigne admits he is "sadly confounding all order" and does not propose arrangement here any more than elsewhere in his book, which bears on personal-essay-as-form.
