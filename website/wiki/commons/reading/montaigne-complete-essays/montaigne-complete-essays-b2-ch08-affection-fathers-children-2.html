Fathers, heirs, and the children of the mind

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In the second half of 'Of the Affection of Fathers to Their Children', Montaigne moves from the deceived old householder and Montluc's regret at his stern fatherhood, through inheritance, custom and the weak roots of 'natural' affection, to the claim that books are a man's truer offspring.

This is the closing part of the essay on paternal affection; it begins mid-argument, finishing a portrait of an aged master of a household who is managed and deceived by those around him. Montaigne then turns to Marshal de Montluc's grief at never having shown his dead son affection, and uses it to say that he himself opens up to his family. He next discusses inheritance (mothers who control estates, wills, the law of succession, the exclusion of women), argues that 'natural' parental affection is weak and shaped by custom, and ends by arguing that works of the mind are a man's more authentic children, with exempla of authors who died with their books and a closing nod to Pygmalion.

Themes

Old age as managed deception. The opening lines describe a household in which letters are screened and the master sees only 'an image prepared and designed beforehand'. Montaigne generalises sourly about wives, sons and servants (Cato's 'So many servants, so many enemies') and says it is 'well for old age that it is always accompanied by want of observation'. His own defence is not suspicion but 'diversion and resolution', and turning every report about others back on himself. The remarks about women are Montaigne's own, of his time, and are prejudiced; they are stated as his opinion, not as exempla.

Reserve versus openness (Montluc). Montluc's lament, which Montaigne reports and endorses as 'rational', is that paternal gravity was a 'vain disguise' that deprived father and son of each other. Montaigne draws the lesson from his own bereavement (the friend is implicitly La Boetie, though the unit does not name him): consolation lies in 'no reserve or secret' having been kept. He claims to open himself to his family and cites Caesar's report of Gaulish custom, where sons did not appear before fathers until they bore arms, as a contrasting cultural arrangement. Compare the self-disclosure project in self-construction-through-writing.

Custom and law over private will. Montaigne holds that goods are 'not properly ours' and that one should leave division to 'the custom of the country': the laws 'have considered the matter better than we know how to do'. He mocks testators who play with their wills 'as with apples or rods' and 'masculine substitutions' that propose 'a ridiculous eternity to our names'. He half-jokes that his own dullness at books might have disinherited him. The Platonic legislator's speech, reported as an ornament, goes further: you are not your own, nor are your goods; they belong to family and above all to the public. This is Plato's voice, though Montaigne presents it approvingly. It is a law-as-infrastructure view of property, with only a loose rhyme to habit-and-identity: Montaigne trusts inherited rule over individual judgement, not because it is rational but because it is stable.

'Natural' affection is weak and made by habit. Montaigne asserts that the affection 'to which we give so great authority has but very weak roots'. His evidence: wet-nurses who come to love fostered children more than their own, and a goat-suckled child he saw that refused any other goat and died. He concludes that beasts alter natural affection as easily as humans, and doubts Herodotus's tale of Libyan children identifying their fathers by instinct. The point is that attachment is formed by custom and proximity, a cousin of the claims in habit-and-identity.

Books as truer children. The essay's pivot: what we engender 'by the soul' comes from nobler parts, 'we are both father and mother in this generation', and these children 'more vividly represent us'. Exempla (Labienus, Cremutius Cordus, Lucan, Epicurus, Augustine, Epaminondas, Phidias, Pygmalion) are borrowed from classical sources; Montaigne's own application is that he would rather have begotten a book 'through society with the Muses' than a child 'by lying with my wife', and that 'If I am wiser than my book, it is richer than I.' He hedges by hedging with 'I know not whether' and 'I make a great question'. See personal-essay-as-form.

Notable passages

Montluc's regret. The Marshal's own words, reported by Montaigne: 'That poor boy never saw in me other than a stern and disdainful countenance'. Montaigne endorses the complaint and extends it to himself.

Consolation without reserve. Montaigne's turn to his own friend's death; a note of self-report where the essay is warmest, with the characteristic self-question 'am I the better for being sensible of this; or am I the worse?'.

The legislator's reply. A reported Platonic dialogue in which dying citizens ask for freedom to reward their carers, and the legislator replies that neither they nor their goods are their own. Montaigne uses it as an ornament, not an argument he explicitly adjudicates, though it fits his preference for custom.

The goat-nursed child. An anecdote Montaigne says he saw himself, used to show that nature's bond is formed by habit and is easily redirected.

Books and bodily children. The core claim: mind-offspring are more fully ours. The book-burning episodes (Labienus entombed himself alive; Cassius Severus said he carried the contents in memory; Cremutius Cordus starved himself; Lucan died reciting his verses) are classical anecdotes. Montaigne's own comment is that punishing writings with death mixes cruelty with 'things that nature has exempted from all sense and suffering'.

Epicurus and Augustine. Rhetorical questions: would a man with a choice between a deformed child and a foolish book not choose the child's misfortune over the book's? Hedged with 'peradventure' for Augustine, and extended to Montaigne's own book, given 'absolutely and irrevocably'.
