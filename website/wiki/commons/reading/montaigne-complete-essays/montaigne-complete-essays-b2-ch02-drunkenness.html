Of Drunkenness: Degrees of Vice and the Limits of Stoic Constancy

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne starts from the claim that vices differ in degree, ranks drunkenness as a coarse but comparatively harmless one, reports ancient tolerance of it and his own moderate habits, then turns on the Stoic ideal of the unshakeable sage, arguing that even the wisest soul is frail and that heroic constancy is a kind of madness or rapture beyond ordinary judgment.

The essay opens by insisting that vices, though all vices, are not equal: confounding their "order and measure" flatters murderers and tyrants, so a science of distinguishing "betwixt vice and vice" is needed. Drunkenness is then ranked as "gross and brutish", wholly corporeal, yet Montaigne piles up counter-examples (drunk Germans who keep their ranks, Roman confidants who kept secrets drunk) and reports ancient tolerance of it, including Stoics, Socrates, Cato, Cyrus and Plato's Laws. He pivots to his own taste: he is more averse by constitution than by argument, judges the vice less harmful to society than others, and enjoys wine only as an accompaniment to eating. A digression on his father's chastity and physical vigour interrupts the topic ("But let us return to our bottles"). The second half abandons wine for the question whether a wise man's soul can be overcome by it, and answers by attacking Stoic and Epicurean pretensions to invulnerability: the sage is a frail man, nature keeps "light marks of her authority", and the extravagant defiance of torture or pain reads as frenzy, a courage "broken loose from its place", akin to poetic fury and prophecy.

Themes

Vices have gradations. Montaigne argues against the Stoic doctrine (which he hedges: "peradventure the Stoics understand them so") that all vices are equal. Collapsing the ranking is morally dangerous because it lets great offenders console themselves. The point is his own judgment, not a reported opinion, and it sets up drunkenness as a test case of ranking.

Drunkenness: condemned in principle, softened by evidence. He calls it "an unmanly and stupid vice" yet "less malicious and hurtful" than the rest, since most vices "more directly jostle public society". Exempla (Josephus, Augustus and Piso, the Germans) show drunkenness does not always destroy discretion, while the Attalus/Pausanias story and the widow near Bordeaux, relayed from a lady, show its worst depths. Note the mix: the Pausanias story is from histories he has read, the Bordeaux anecdote is hearsay. He also defers to "the authority of ancient opinions" and admits his taste and constitution oppose the vice more than his reasoning does.

Self-report as evidence. He says he cannot understand extending drinking "beyond thirst" and an "appetite artificial and against nature"; his last draught is his largest and he seldom tastes the first glass well. Even his ironic praise of long drinking ("we are to take greater liberty and stick closer to our work") sits beside a cutting remark about a great lord whose sobriety after five quarts was "to the detriment of our affairs". The tone is deliberately mock-advocatory; a reader should not take every sentence as endorsement.

Custom shifts with time. Montaigne notes that drinking has been curtailed since his father's house and speculates it is because his generation is "more addicted to Venus"; the father digression, with its portrait of chastity, punctilio and physical feats, is a reminiscence of how a prior age's habits seemed.

The sage is only a man. The pivotal move is from "whether the soul of a wise man can be overcome by the strength of wine" to a general claim: "The most regular and most perfect soul in the world has but too much to do to keep itself upright." Lucretius goes mad from a love philtre; an apoplexy stuns Socrates like a porter. Nature keeps involuntary signs (pallor, trembling, tears) "to teach man his mortality and our weakness". Moderating inclinations is enough; suppressing them "is not in him to do".

Heroic constancy as madness. Montaigne cites Metrodorus, Anaxarchus, martyrs, the child in Josephus, Antisthenes, Sextius and Epicurus defying pain, and says that "there is some phrenzy, some fury, how holy soever" in these. He frames them as a courage that leaves its natural seat, like soldiers or poets later astonished at themselves, and cites Plato and Aristotle on madness and prophecy. Wisdom, by contrast, is "a regular government of the soul" with measure and proportion. This is Montaigne's judgment, with Plato and Aristotle brought in as support; the martyrs and the child are reported, not endorsed or mocked, and he explicitly leaves that sect to itself while admitting a holy fury may be at work.

The essay resonates with WMP interests in self-study and the essay as a method (see michel-de-montaigne and self-construction-through-writing), though the connection is loose here: the unit does not theorize the self, it simply uses his own body and habits as evidence against abstract ideals. Its anti-Stoic view of human limits also rhymes, half-fittingly, with scepticism about legibility and measurement, but that is our reading, not Montaigne's.

Notable passages

The ranking of drunkenness (c1). "This one is totally corporeal and earthly": the soul takes part in other vices, this one overthrows understanding and renders the body stupid. A statement of ordinary moral prejudice that the rest of the essay complicates.

Ancient tolerance (c2). Stoics who advise a sometime drunkenness "to refresh the soul", Cato reproached as a hard drinker, Cyrus boasting of drinking more than his brother, Persian councils warmed with wine. Montaigne reports these and then admits deference to antiquity.

His own taste and the moral accounting (c3). The key self-report: constitution against discourse, the vice "costs a man's conscience less than the others", and an anti-connoisseur remark that the Germans pour down rather than taste.

Plato's licence (c4). Plato's Laws allowing drink after forty under a discreet chief, calling drunkenness "a true and certain trial of every one's nature", with bans on drink for soldiers, magistrates, by day, and on the night of begetting. Reported opinion, used as counterweight to the vice's bad name.

The sage's weakness (c5). "To what vanity does the good opinion we have of ourselves push us?" down to "what is there more frail, more miserable, or more nothing?": the hinge of the essay.

Fury beyond the ordinary (c6). The wild sallies of the Stoics and Epicurus are pushed on by "a courage that has broken loose from its place", linked with poets, prophecy and Aristotle's mixture of madness, closing with wisdom defined as measured self-government.
