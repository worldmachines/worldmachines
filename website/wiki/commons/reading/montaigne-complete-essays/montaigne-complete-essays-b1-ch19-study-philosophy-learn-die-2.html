Learning to die: nature's consolation and the visor of ceremony

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In the second half of 'That to Study Philosophy Is to Learn to Die', Montaigne urges keeping death constantly in mind yet living on in action, argues from his own experience and from nature's ventriloquized speech that death is no evil and life's length is immaterial, and concludes that the terror of dying comes from the funeral ceremonial rather than from death.

This slice (the later part of the essay; it opens mid-sentence on a builder's broken-off works and ends with the closing paragraph on dying in war versus at home) moves in four steps. Montaigne first says a man should keep acting and planning, and let death find him "planting my cabbages", while still making death a daily topic of talk and reading. He then answers the objection that the real thing outruns rehearsal, using his own experience of illness and the gradual, unnoticed decline of age. Next he gives a long prosopopoeia in which Nature herself argues to us that death is part of the world's order, that dying is spread through the whole of life, that a day or a century is equally "all there", and that nothing about death can be experienced as a loss. Finally he asks why death looks worse in a bedroom than on a battlefield or among peasants, and answers that the fear belongs to the apparatus of deathbed ceremony, a mask to be removed. The essay is heavily built from classical quotation (Lucretius, Horace and others, mostly left untranslated in this text), and the exempla (Lycurgus, Egyptian feasts, Caesar, Socrates, Aristotle's Hypanis flies, Chiron, Thales) are borrowed authority, not Montaigne's own observation.

Themes

Death as a standing practice, not a project. Montaigne reports his own habit: "I have a particular fancy for that subject", he collects the manner of men's deaths, and says that whoever "should teach men to die would at the same time teach them to live." He pairs this with a counter-emphasis that is his own: we are "born to action", and the point of premeditation is indifference, so that death catches him mid-task and "still less of my gardens not being finished." The man who dies lamenting an unfinished chronicle is his negative example. Reported custom (Lycurgus placing graves by churches, the Egyptian death's-head at feasts) is offered as a technology for habituating people; see habit-and-identity for the wider Montaignean theme of habit as the machinery that makes things bearable or unbearable.

Self-report against the 'best fencer' objection. He concedes the objection that dying may exceed imagination, but replies from his own case, hedged with "I hope" and "which makes me hope": in health he finds the resolution to die harder than in a fever, and he has found maladies "in much greater horror" at a distance than when afflicted. Distance magnifies; Nature loosens attachment as illness engages. He adds that old age is a slow, insensible death of youth, 'harder' than the final dissolution, and cites Caesar's quip to the old soldier ("Thou fanciest, then, that thou art yet alive").

The soul's sovereignty (Stoic strand). If the soul can assure herself against death she is "mistress of necessity" and has "the true and sovereign liberty here on earth". He then says that even religion rests on the contempt of life. This is a reported, largely Stoic-Epicurean position that Montaigne endorses here but which he elsewhere tests; note that the claim that religion's human foundation is contempt of life is his, and stated flatly.

Nature's speech: death inside life. The longest section is spoken by Nature, not by Montaigne, assembling Lucretian arguments: death is part of the order of the universe; "the day of your birth is one day's advance towards the grave"; "Life in itself is neither good nor evil"; the year has "played his part, and knows no other art but to begin again"; a short life and a long one are equal "for there is no long, nor short, to things that are no more"; mortals pass on the torch like runners. The cosmology is cyclical and indifferent, a view of time as repetition. It rhymes loosely with the long-cycle frame of world-machines, though it is a consolation for individuals, not a theory of eras. Nature also makes the point that immortality would be worse (Chiron) and that Thales' indifference of living and dying was her own teaching.

Ceremony and the visor. The closing paragraph is Montaigne's own observation and the sharpest sociological claim of the slice: the dread of death comes from "those terrible ceremonies and preparations wherewithal we set it out", from the sickroom's weeping, tapers and physicians. Like children frightened of a familiar face in a visor, we must remove the mask, and underneath is "the very same death" a servant died without apprehension. Class contrast (peasants more assured than people of quality) is offered as evidence. Fear here is learned, institutional, a product of custom.

Notable passages

The passage on planting cabbages (c1) establishes his anti-solemn stance: rehearse death, but do not let it halt ordinary work. The passage collecting deaths (c2) is a rare moment of self-description of his writing practice, including the wish for a 'register' of deaths. The reply to the fencer objection (c3) is his experiential argument and carries his hedges. The religion and Socrates passage (c4) gives the symmetry argument (no more reason to grieve for the future than the past) and the quip "And nature them". Nature's speech (c5, c6) is the philosophical centre and is not Montaigne speaking in his own voice, though he closes it with "These are the good lessons our mother Nature teaches." The final ceremony passage (c7) turns from philosophy to a critique of the social staging of death. Compare the essay-as-self-study function discussed under michel-de-montaigne.
