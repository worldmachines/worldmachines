Against the angels: enjoying one's being (closing pages of Of Experience)

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In the closing pages of "Of Experience" Montaigne argues against philosophies that despise the body and seek to rise above the human condition, defending temperate, attentive enjoyment of ordinary life, nature's pleasures and one's own being as the real masterpiece of wisdom, with Socrates as his model.

This slice is the final stretch of the whole Essays (the last fifth of "Of Experience", Book 3). It begins with the pleasures of the table and company and widens into a sustained defence of bodily and mental pleasure taken together, directed against philosophies that despise the body or aspire to a superhuman state. Montaigne parades exempla (Socrates, Caesar, Alexander, Cato, Scipio, Epaminondas, Brutus) to show that great souls are those who can lay serious business aside and take up ordinary life with ease. He then turns to himself: he has a private vocabulary for time, he deliberately savours contentment (even waking himself from sleep to taste it), and he accepts nature's gift, including appetite, as a duty owed to the giver. The slice ends with the warning against "transcendental humours" and a prayer for a gay and sociable old age.

Themes

Pleasure is received, neither pursued nor shunned. Montaigne rejects "inhuman wisdom" that despises culture of the body, but also mocks Xerxes seeking new delights. He admits (self-report) that he receives pleasures "a little too warmly". He is also sceptical of pleasure's weight: the mind makes everything sour or sweet according to its "insatiable, vagabond, and versatile essence", and "we are all wind throughout". The defence of pleasure is therefore not naive.

Body and soul are a marriage, not a divorce. Aristippus (body only) and Zeno (soul only) are both faulted; Pythagorean contemplation versus Socratic conduct is resolved in Socrates, whom Montaigne makes his master. He quotes the Platonic and Socratic view that mental pleasure goes first but not alone, and that temperance is "the moderatrix, not the adversary of pleasure". His own formulation: the mind should "take place there and sit, but not lie down" at table. These are Montaigne's reported authorities used to support his own stance; the Stoic and Academic formulas ("to live according to nature", "to consent to nature") are reported as hard to define because we have "confounded" nature "with artificial traces".

Greatness of soul lies in the ordinary. Against the glorification of public deeds, he claims that to regulate conduct and to take repose is a greater work than composing books or taking empires. The exempla (Scipio gathering cockle shells, Socrates dancing, drinking, playing with children, bearing poverty and a shrewish wife, Brutus reading Polybius) are borrowed from classical anecdote; Montaigne's use is to show that relaxation "wonderfully honour[s]" a strong soul and that moderate things suffice.

Attending to time and life. Montaigne distinguishes "passing away time" (what he does with ill hours) from tasting good hours "over again". Because life is short he would lengthen it in "weight" rather than duration. He criticises those who live for hope and shadows, whose "fruit and end of their pursuit is to pursue", and cites Alexander's saying about labour as an example of this restlessness. This is the self-study motif (see self-construction-through-writing) turned from writing to living: the essayist's attentiveness becomes a practice of enjoying consciousness itself.

Anti-transcendence. The closing movement attacks those who would "put themselves out of themselves, and escape from being men": "instead of transforming themselves into angels, they transform themselves into beasts". He flags that he exempts devout souls devoted to divine things (a "privileged study"), yet says he has observed "supercelestial opinions and subterranean manners" to agree. Alexander's claim to divinity is deflated by Philotas's quip and the Athenian inscription to Pompey. Ends with the humility: seated on the highest throne "we are but seated upon our breech".

Theological framing. Montaigne repeatedly frames enjoyment as gratitude to God: refusing or disfiguring the gift is "ungrateful and wicked". This is a pious, not libertine, justification, and he places it beside the classical authorities without ranking one above the other.

Notable passages

The stance on pleasure (c1) and the Socratic "When I dance, I dance" passage (c2) state the method. The reversal of the hierarchy of deeds (c3) argues that living well is the greatest work, and the passage on time (c4) is the most intimate self-report, including the habit of savouring contentment. The defence of the "most human" philosophies and Socrates as master (c5) is the doctrinal centre, and the closing passage (c6) is the rejection of transcendence and endorsement of the "common and human model".

For World Machines the resonance is modest and indirect. Montaigne's refusal of angelic or exalted aims and his preference for the "common and human model" sit in the line of michel-de-montaigne's essay as a technology of self-knowledge (personal-essay-as-form) and rhyme loosely with the scale-versus-lived-experience tension behind Legibility, though Montaigne offers no institutional argument here; the connection is an echo, not a claim of the text. His appeal to "nature" as a guide is also explicitly hedged: nature has been confounded with artifice and is hard to define.
