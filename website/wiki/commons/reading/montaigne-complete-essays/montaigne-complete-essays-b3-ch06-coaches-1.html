Of Coaches (opening half): causes, fear, and the economics of royal liberality

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The opening of 'Of Coaches' wanders from the plurality of causes and Montaigne's own seasickness through fear, Socratic composure and his fragile temperament to war-chariots, royal extravagance, and an argument that a king's liberality is a weak virtue next to justice and that Roman amphitheatre spectacle is magnificence misspent.

This first slice of the essay opens with a remark on how authors pile up plausible causes, then lets a question about seasickness pull Montaigne into self-report on fear, Socrates' retreat, and his own fragile, unrecoverable temperament. A confessed intolerance of coaches and boats leads to a digression on war-chariots, then to the eccentric conveyances of Roman emperors, and from there into a sustained political argument: monarchs who seek honour through lavish expense misunderstand their station, liberality is a poor and even dangerous royal virtue, and justice is the true one. The slice ends mid-catalogue, in the middle of a description of Roman amphitheatre spectacles. Despite the title, nothing in this slice concerns the New World; that turn, if it comes, lies outside it.

Themes

Plural causes and ingenious explanation. Montaigne opens by saying that great authors, when they write of causes, use both those they think true and those they do not, "provided they have in them some beauty and invention"; since we cannot be sure of the supreme cause, we "crowd a great many together". The Latin tag he quotes says one cause is not enough, but several, of which one may be right. The joke explanation of blessing sneezes (attributed by him to Aristotle, as hearsay: "they say") demonstrates the method. This is a sceptical stance toward explanation itself, offered with a smile.

Experience against borrowed theory. Plutarch, whom he praises as the author who "has best mixed art with nature, and judgment with knowledge", explains seasickness as fear. Montaigne, "very subject to it", knows this cause does not concern him "not by argument, but by necessary experience". The self is here a test-case against authority, and he reports that he has never lost his judgment in danger, adding that "a man must have courage to fear" (self-construction-through-writing is a loose rhyme: the self as the ground on which borrowed claims are checked, though Montaigne does not frame it that way here).

Fear, composure and foresight. The Socrates anecdote comes from Alcibiades (a reported exemplum, not Montaigne's observation): Socrates retreats last, unhurried and watchful, and pursuers decline to attack such men. Montaigne draws his own moral, that "nothing so much throws us into dangers as an inconsiderate eagerness of getting ourselves clear of them", and objects to the idiom that says a man fears death when he merely foresees it.

Fragile self. Montaigne says he could not survive being conquered by a vehement passion: "I have no after-game to play". He inverts Epicurus (a reported opinion he rejects): whoever has once been a very fool will never be very wise. He presents his dullness of apprehension as nature's compensation, hedged with "or, if you will, dull". He also admits his motion-sickness is oddly specific (interrupted motion bothers him, steady carriage does not) and says he cannot "otherwise express it"; he has declined the physicians' belt remedy, preferring to wrestle with his own defects.

Royal display as pusillanimity. After the chariot digression (Hungarian war-wagons used against the Turks, reported as history within memory, and a heavy gentleman's chariot), Montaigne lists Roman and Merovingian extravagances (Antony's lions, Heliogabalus, Firmus' ostriches) as exempla. His own judgment: it is "a kind of pusillanimity in monarchs" to seek honour by excessive expense among their own subjects. He sides with Isocrates and Demosthenes against Theophrastus, and cites Aristotle against spectacle; he would have the money spent on ports, walls, hospitals, colleges, roads, and he laments the unfinished Pont-Neuf.

Liberality is not a royal virtue. A king "has nothing properly his own; he owes himself to others", and authority exists for the people, as a physician exists for the patient. Liberality in a sovereign is measured against his means and so counts for nothing; immoderate bounty breeds ingratitude, since "covetousness has nothing so properly and so much its own as ingratitude". Montaigne prefers a covetous prince to a prodigal one. Royal virtue consists chiefly in justice. The Cyrus story (a reported exemplum) offers the counter-model: gifts bestowed on subjects become a treasury of friendship, and Cyrus calls his friends "more faithful treasurers" than mercenaries.

Spectacle as public machinery. The emperors' excuse was that their authority depended on the people's goodwill; Montaigne distinguishes private citizens funding games from masters imitating them. The long, admiring-yet-ironic catalogue of amphitheatres (marble tiers, a stage that flooded for sea battles, golden nets) is ambivalent: he calls it "a fine thing" and then lets the sheer excess speak. This faintly resembles WMP interest in infrastructure of legitimacy (modernity-machine concerns are not really at stake; the fit is only that spectacle is treated as a mechanism of rule).

Notable passages

The essay's opening on causes and the sneeze (c1) sets the tone of ingenious, unserious explanation. Montaigne's self-report on fear and judgment (c2) and on his unrepairable soul (c3) are among the most personal in the slice. His judgment on kings who spend to be honoured (c4), his argument that liberality is a weak royal virtue (c5), and the Cyrus anecdote (c6) carry the political argument. Quotations from Latin poets in the slice stand untranslated in the source and are not relied on here.
