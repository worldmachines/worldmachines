Montaigne on Appetite, Physicians and the Stone (Of Experience, middle slice)

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In the middle of 'Of Experience', Montaigne defends living by one's own appetites and letting illnesses run their natural course against the shifting, self-contradicting authority of physicians, then reasons himself into a cheerful, practical acceptance of his kidney stone as a mixed blessing, a teacher of death and a foil to pleasure.

This slice is the third of five parts of 'Of Experience' and begins and ends mid-argument, entirely inside Montaigne's discussion of sickness and medicine. It opens with the claim that the sick are safest keeping the way of life they were bred to, and that physicians' prescriptions of a contrary regimen do harm. Montaigne reports that he himself obeys his appetites in sickness and health, mocks the contradictory authority of doctors, and counsels patience with illness as a natural process with its own span. He then stages a long speech by his own 'mind' persuading his 'imagination' that the stone is a bearable, even honourable, disease; reports the habits of self-record that make his pains familiar; praises the stone's clean, intermittent character against other diseases; and closes by saying he judges only by present sensation and distrusts medical prognosis. The slice does not contain the essay's opening or its conclusion; it describes only these passages.

Themes

Custom as the safe regimen, and the harm of change. Montaigne argues that 'change, be it what it will, distempers and puts one out', and gives examples of Perigordins, mountain people, Bretons and Basques whom physicians would put on a contrary diet. Habit here works as embodied infrastructure that cannot be swapped out without damage; compare habit-and-identity, though Montaigne's claim is dietary and bodily rather than about identity.

Appetite over prescription, with pleasure counted as profit. He reports that he gives 'great rein' to his desires, hates remedies worse than the disease, and holds that 'pleasure is one of the chiefest kinds of profit.' This is self-report, not a general rule he prescribes to others; he adds that his tastes have followed his stomach's changes on their own. He also recalls his youthful sexual licence in Latin tags (untranslated in this text).

The diversity of medicine undermines its authority. The 'art of physic' changes 'according to climates and moons'; one physician condemns what another prescribed; a physician who died of the stone is said by colleagues to have been harmed by his own abstinence. The point is sceptical: opinions on regimen 'embrace all sorts and forms', so Montaigne feels free to follow his own sense.

Letting nature take her course; impatience as self-ruin. Diseases, like animals, have lives and limits, and he who tries 'imperiously to cut them short by force' lengthens them. He cites Crantor, the Mexican greeting to newborns ('endure, suffer, and say nothing'), and Plato's report of Aesculapius, in the manner of exempla, to support accepting the 'laws of our condition'. Life, like music, is a mixture of contrary tones, and one cannot keep only the pleasant ones. Montaigne's own hedge: he concedes 'such an one died of it; and so shall you', i.e. example proves little.

Imagination managed rather than argued with. Montaigne says he consults doctors little because they alarm the patient with prognostics; instead he 'assists, flatters and deceives' his imagination. The long internal speech casts the stone as an old man's common dues, a noble disease, a late and paternal chastisement, a school of fortitude, and a gentle memento mori ('thou shakest hands with it once a month'). He labels these 'arguments, weak and strong' and admits the consolation is a rocking-to-sleep, with new stratagems ready tomorrow. Do not read this as a doctrine of resignation; it is a self-administered therapy offered with irony about its own persuasiveness ('could it persuade as it preaches').

Self-record as experience. 'For want of natural memory, I make one of paper': he notes each symptom, and rereads the notes like Sibylline leaves to find precedent for consolation. Custom also grounds his hope that nature will not change course. This is the book's theme of experience as accumulated personal data; compare self-construction-through-writing, noting that here the writing is a clinical log rather than literary self-portrait.

Pain and pleasure as linked; the stone's advantages. Health is sweeter after a near and contiguous sickness; nature gave pain 'for the honour and service of pleasure and indolence'. Montaigne cites the Stoics on vice and virtue (an opinion he treats as less defensible than his own), and Socrates after his fetters came off. He then lists the stone's merits: it is clean, leaves no train of other diseases, lets him ride ten hours, spares the understanding, and has no uncertain causes needing doctors' interpretation.

Fear as self-inflicted suffering; scepticism about prognosis. 'He who fears he shall suffer, already suffers what he fears.' He judges only by 'actual sensation, not by reasoning', and notes that those who trust many counsels are pressed by imagination without any bodily pain. Nature's ways are 'inscrutable', and signs of the future are few except old age. The slice ends with his playing at reporting symptoms to physicians when healthy, to test their verdicts.

Notable passages

The passages on impatience (c1) and the mixed texture of life (c2) gives the slice's central stance: grant 'free passage to diseases', let nature 'take her own way', and mingle goods and evils, since being 'cannot subsist without this mixture'. The consolation speech to the imagination (c3) is Montaigne's reasoning on his own stone, and c4 carries its sharpest turn: 'thou diest because thou art living'. The paper memory (c5) is a compact account of experience as a notebook of precedents. The passage on fear (c6) states that the fear of pain compounds pain and that medical prognosis is vain.

For michel-de-montaigne more broadly, the slice shows the Essays' method of testing received authority (physicians, Stoics, Plato) against self-observation, without Montaigne claiming universal validity for his own case.
