Montaigne on his own limits: idleness, candour, and a bad memory (Of Presumption, middle part)

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In the middle of 'Of Presumption', Montaigne catalogues his bodily clumsiness and idle, free-willed temperament, declines ambition and fortune-chasing, praises plain truth-telling over princely dissimulation, and confesses his ruinous memory and his aversion to compelled action.

This slice is the middle of the essay 'Of Presumption'; it begins mid-self-portrait and ends mid-discussion of memory, so it neither opens nor concludes the essay. Montaigne first lists bodily skills he lacks (music, dancing, swimming, fencing, even legible handwriting) and says his body suits his soul: firm, but idle and only willing to labour at its own inclination. From this he derives a philosophy of small expectations: he takes the 'lowest walk' as the safest, dislikes deliberation and suspense, and turns down ambition as 'daughter to presumption'. He then argues that his age is so depraved that plain honesty would make a prince great, and condemns feigning and dissimulation at length, citing Aristotle, Apollonius, Aristippus and Soliman's refusal to condone a breach of faith. He admits the pride and incivility that may hide in his own frankness. The slice closes with a long confession of a nearly absent memory, which links to his habit of compelled actions failing, and to how he reads, borrows and forgets.

Themes

Self-study through deficiency. The self-portrait is made largely of what Montaigne cannot do. He presents it flatly and without apology: 'my bodily qualities are very well suited to those of my soul'. The method is one of self-knowledge by inventory, and it fits the project of the essay as a form (see michel-de-montaigne, personal-essay-as-form). Whether the self-deprecation is also a kind of pose is left open; he himself concedes that his candour may carry 'some mixture of pride and obstinacy'.

Freedom, idleness, and a refusal to be governed. He claims a soul 'free and entirely its own', never subjected to a master, and so 'of no use to any one but myself'. He works only at his own pace and by his own desire. This is a self-report about temperament, offered with some irony about its uselessness to others, not a general rule.

Low expectations as a way to meet fortune. 'Not being able to govern events, I govern myself.' He prefers the beaten track and misfortunes that are plainly bad to the torment of hope and fear ('The fear of the fall more fevers me than the fall itself'). He declines ambition, citing his friends' advice and Chancellor Olivier's saying that the French are like monkeys climbing a tree. He draws a distinction in who may rightly gamble: a younger brother with nothing to lose may be excused, but not the heir carrying a family's honour. The Latin verse here is quoted from other poets and he uses it to support his own stance. A note of fatalism runs through this: he is deliberately limiting what he cares about rather than trying to master circumstances.

A depraved age and the political value of honesty. Montaigne says his good qualities would have counted as faults in his day, and that being born in a corrupt time makes you 'reputed virtuous good cheap'. He nonetheless reasons that a prince who became known for humanity, truth, loyalty and temperance would win the goodwill of the people. This is a hedged claim: 'I am much deceived if he do not'.

Against dissimulation. He 'mortally' hates 'this new virtue of feigning and dissimulation', calling it cowardly and servile. The argument runs in layers: truth-telling is the fundamental part of virtue and should be loved for itself; a liar is never believed even when truthful; those who boast that they cannot rule without dissembling warn everyone that they lie; and a prince who breaks faith for one gain loses all future negotiation. Aristotle, Apollonius and Aristippus are cited as authorities, and the Soliman anecdote is an exemplum borrowed from the historical record. He still allows that 'A man must not always tell all, for that were folly', so the rule is no duty of total disclosure but that what is said be what is thought. The theme sits at a distance from, and in tension with, the Machiavellian position implied by the princes he criticises (see niccolo-machiavelli); he does not name Machiavelli in this slice.

Memory, constraint, and judgment. Montaigne says he has no memory at all, cannot take a spoken commission without a note-book, and forgets names, hiding places and even his own writings. He ties this to a wider refusal of command: whatever he does naturally he cannot do on strict injunction. The examples are the drinking at table, the condemned archer who refuses to shoot for his life, and the man whose counted steps go wrong. He argues that the will, pressed, defeats itself. He then turns the defect into a method of reading: 'I turn over books; I do not study them.' What he keeps is what his judgment has used, while authors and places are lost. This casts his borrowed quotations as absorbed material and concedes that he is 'very often quoted to myself'.

Notable passages

The passage on being unfit for the service of others (c1) states his freedom and idleness most directly. The passage on the 'lowest walk' and fear of falling (c2) gives the core of his attitude to fortune and risk. The remark that ill luck is good for something and that a depraved age makes virtue cheap (c3) joins his self-assessment to a political claim about princes. The long run against dissimulation (c4) is the clearest ethical statement in the slice. The archer and drinking examples (c5) set out his psychology of will and constraint. The closing passage on turning over books without studying them (c6) shows how his bad memory shapes his practice of reading, quotation and judgment.

The broader point of this slice for readers is how an account of one's own weaknesses is used as evidence for a stance on ambition, honesty and learning, with Montaigne leaving room for his own possible pride.
