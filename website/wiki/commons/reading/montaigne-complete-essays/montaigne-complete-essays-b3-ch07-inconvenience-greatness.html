Greatness as Solitude: Montaigne on the Inconvenience of High Station

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne argues that high station is a burden, since power removes honest contest, judgment and pleasure and breeds flattery, while professing his own preference for a moderate condition and hedging that refusing greatness can itself be ambition.

Montaigne opens by turning the usual envy of greatness around: since we cannot reach it, we may console ourselves by naming its defects. He then reports his own preference for a moderate station (the third at Paris rather than the first), professes distaste for both ruling and being ruled, and sympathises with the burden of kingship. The central argument is that sovereign power corrupts the conditions of real pleasure, contest and honest judgment: princes are never truly opposed, never truly praised, and are imitated even in their vices. The essay closes on anecdotes in which wise men yield to power rather than risk it. Montaigne hedges throughout, and part of the opening is a confession of his own limits rather than a universal claim.

Themes

Refusing greatness is easy, and may itself be ambition. Montaigne says that enduring misfortune is hard but being content with a moderate fortune and avoiding greatness is "a very easy matter" for him. He then complicates his own claim: the glory of refusal may hide "worse ambition than even in the desire itself", since ambition does best by "obscure and unfrequented ways". Greatness also has a built-in advantage, that one can descend from it without falling. His own preference is self-report, not doctrine: "I love myself too well", and he measures good fortune "not by the height, but by the facility". He sets the obscure, quiet Balbus against the glorious Regulus and says he could attain the first by use and approach the second only with veneration (he notes Cicero would speak better on the comparison).

Dominion, active or passive, is disliked. He says he disrelishes all dominion and cites Otanes, who gave up his claim to the Persian throne on condition that he and his might live free of both command and subjection save to the ancient laws. This is Montaigne endorsing an exemplum ("did as I should willingly have done"), not an argument for politics in general. He excuses kings' mistakes more than most, given the "intolerable weight of their function", and says that being on record may spur even a mediocre king to virtue. He also notes that superiority and inferiority are bound to envy and contest, that he believes neither party about the other's rights, and that two Scottish authors recently argued the king is worse off than a carter and above God respectively.

Power destroys contest, and so pleasure. The "incommodity" he sets out to remark is that sovereigns are denied the pleasure of trial against equals. Everyone lets the prince win, as Brisson missed his blow against Alexander. Carneades' remark (reported) is that a horse is the only honest opponent of a king's son. Montaigne draws on his own childhood, when playmates held back against him. Ease of overcoming everything is "to slide, not to go; 'tis to sleep, and not to live": omnipotence leaves a man begging for opposition "as an alms". Homer's wounded gods illustrate the same point, that virtue and honour are built on exposure to hazard.

Flattery and imitation corrupt judgment and manners. Princes' good qualities cannot be perceived without comparison, and their ears are deafened by uniform approval. Royalty swallows the other qualities, and its glare hides the man. The senate's prize of eloquence to Tiberius, which he refused as unfree, shows one who understood this. Courtiers imitate defects (Alexander's tilted head, Dionysius's purblindness, feigned deafness) and also vices, which Montaigne lists as spreading by fashion, an example more dangerous than Mithridates' flatterers who had their limbs cauterised, because these let the soul be cauterised.

Closing: prudence yields to power. Favorinus yields to Adrian ("would you not have him wiser than I, who commands thirty legions?"), Pollio will not contest with Augustus, and Dionysius's punishments of Philoxenus and Plato show the risk. Montaigne endorses these ("And they were right"), so the essay ends by confirming the thesis that no honest exchange is possible with power.

Wiki-link: the essay's self-report of a chosen moderate station touches michel-de-montaigne; the invisible machinery by which courtiers copy a ruler's defects loosely rhymes with habit-and-identity, though Montaigne here treats it as flattery, not custom.

Notable passages

Greatness as something to rail against (c1): the opening reversal, with its caution that refusing greatness may be a subtler ambition.

"I love myself too well" (c2): the preference for the third at Paris over the first, measured by facility not height.

The weight of kingship (c3): Montaigne's sympathy with kings, and the Scottish authors who place the king below a carter and above God.

No contest for princes (c4): the childhood anecdote, Brisson and Alexander, and Carneades on the horse.

Omnipotence as sleep (c5): the philosophical core, that without hazard and opposition there is no pleasure or virtue.

Prudent deference (c6): the closing anecdotes, which conclude that to contest with the powerful is not prudent.
