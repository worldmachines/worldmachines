Eloquence, Fame and the Letter: Montaigne on Cicero, Princes and His Own Style

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne faults Cicero and the younger Pliny for vanity about their eloquence and published letters, argues that fine speaking is no chief talent for a prince or statesman, then defends his own matter over style and describes his blunt, hasty, unceremonious letter-writing as a function of friendship and sincerity.

This short chapter continues a comparison (begun in the preceding essay, which is not in this unit) between Cicero and the younger Pliny. Montaigne reads their begging of historians for a place in the record and their publication of private letters as evidence of an immoderately ambitious nature, then generalises: skill in speech, music or other accomplishments is no honour to a ruler or statesman unless it stands behind the qualities proper to his office. He turns to his own book, complaining of readers who praise its language rather than its matter and explaining how his stories and quotations work. He then contrasts Cicero's letters with those of the two philosophers (unnamed in this unit: "these two other philosophers"), tells a story about Cicero freeing a slave for postponing a speech, and closes with a long self-portrait of his own letters: hasty, unrevised, blunt, hostile to ceremonial formula and flattery.

Themes

Eloquence is not a great person's business. Montaigne's own judgment, built on a string of exempla (Xenophon and Caesar, Scipio and Laelius, Demosthenes on Philip, Philip scolding Alexander for singing well, Iphicrates, Antisthenes on Ismenias), is that deeds, not fine speaking, are what recommend a man of rank. Praising a king for being a good painter or runner at the ring is "a kind of mockery and offence". The Plutarch-sourced line is that excellence in less necessary qualities testifies against a man, that he has misspent his study. Note that these anecdotes are reported authorities, and Montaigne uses them to support his own view.

Cicero and Pliny as vain. Montaigne presents the solicitation of historians and the publication of unsent letters (excused as not wanting to lose "their labours and lucubrations") as "meanness of spirit" in two consuls. This is his reading, offered as evidence of character; the Eros anecdote is a further, explicitly characterising story. He qualifies the attack once: unless Cicero's eloquence is allowed to be so perfect as to "form a complete body of itself".

Matter over language, and the essay as seed-bed. Montaigne says he would rather no one praised the language of his essays, since this "depress[es] the sense". He claims, with a hedge ("I am much deceived if..."), that few writers have put more worth noting on paper. He only "musters up the heads", and his stories and quotations are not only example, authority or ornament but carry "the seed of a more rich and a bolder matter" for himself and "others who shall be of my humour". This is a rare explicit statement of his method of allusive citation and compression, and it bears on personal-essay-as-form.

Letters as friendship, not publicity. Two unnamed philosophers also promise eternity to letters, but accommodate a friend's vanity for a good end, and fill the letters with substance that makes the reader wiser rather than more eloquent. Montaigne confesses he would have liked to publish his whimsies as letters, but lacked a real, settled correspondent, "as I once had" and could never address "vain names", being "a sworn enemy to all manner of falsification".

Sincerity against ceremony. He hates flattery, and finds the formulae of courtesy ("life, soul, devotion, adoration, vassal, slave") so debased by indiscriminate use that real devotion has nothing left to say it with. This is a small piece of social diagnosis: custom has inflated and emptied the language of honour, so that a new custom "would discharge us of that trouble". Montaigne claims to honour most those to whom he shows least ceremony, and prefers to be read in the heart. The same stance governs his composition: letters are written post-haste, never transcribed, with blots and dashes, and "those that cost me the most pains, are the worst". The first word begets the second; the connection to self-disclosure as unrevised process rhymes with self-construction-through-writing, though here the topic is letters, not the essays.

Notable passages

c1: Two consuls and their lucubrations. Montaigne's charge of ambition against Cicero and Pliny, and the argument that Xenophon and Caesar wrote because their acts transcended their eloquence. He also says he would take it ill to be dispossessed of the belief that Scipio and Laelius wrote Terence's comedies, citing Terence's own confession.

c2: Qualities misbecoming a condition. The central general claim about what to praise in a ruler, with the Demosthenes, Plutarch and Iphicrates examples. The Iphicrates reply ("I know how to command all these") sums up the ideal: a king's skill is government, not performance.

c3: On his own language and method. The paragraph in which Montaigne asks not to be praised for style and explains the unexplored seeds in his stories. It is the clearest self-description of the essay as compressed, generative matter.

c4: Away with that eloquence. The sharpest condemnation of self-enchanting rhetoric, with its single exception for Cicero, followed by the Eros story as an exposé of the orator's temperament.

c5: Letters, friend and falsification. His wish to have written letters rather than essays, had he had a worthy addressee; and his ill-fitting style for ceremony and public business.

c6: Post-haste, unrevised. His compositional practice: no transcription, tolerated blots, the principle that labour spoils. A short, concrete account of spontaneity as authenticity, offered with the caveat that the practice is his own and "very remote from the present practice" of his age.
