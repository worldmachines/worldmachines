Fortune, Clemency and the Folly of Suspicion

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne pairs two stories of clemency toward would-be assassins (a French prince at Rouen, Augustus and Cinna) to argue that human prudence cannot master events, that Fortune governs medicine, art and war as well as counsel, and that where outcomes are uncertain one should choose the honourable course and meet danger with open confidence rather than suspicion.

The chapter opens with two matched anecdotes. A French prince at the siege of Rouen, warned by the queen-mother of a plot, confronts the conspirator and sends him away unpunished, setting the charity of his own religion against the killing counsel of the other. Augustus, advised by Livia to try clemency where severity has failed, pardons Cinna, wins him as a friend, and is never plotted against again. Montaigne notes that the French prince was nevertheless later caught in a similar treason, and concludes that human prudence is vain and Fortune mistress of events. From this he widens the claim to physic, poetry, painting, war and deliberation, then returns to the political question: what should a man in danger of conspiracy do? His answer, hedged throughout, is to take the honourable course, refuse corrosive suspicion, and show confident trust, with a run of further exempla (Dion, Alexander, Scipio, Caesar, Dionysius, the Duke of Athens, the hunted Roman) and two episodes he reports from his own experience or knowledge.

Themes

Fortune over prudence. The Rouen prince and Augustus are offered as parallel acts of mercy with different outcomes, and Montaigne declines to credit the clemency for Augustus's safety or to blame it for the prince's later fate: "neither does any one know if he had proceeded otherwise, whether by that means he had avoided the end his destiny had appointed for him". Both stories are exempla he received (from Amiot, and from the Roman tradition); the moral is Montaigne's own.

Every art leans on chance. He turns the praise of lucky physicians around: other arts need Fortune's hand too. His self-report on medicine is personal and combative: he hates and fears physic, lets nature work, and fears that he would "assist her adversary". Poetic raptures, a painter's happy accident, and a reader finding in a book "a richer sense" than the author intended all show Fortune at work beyond the maker's knowledge. This anticipates his later treatment of the essay as something readers complete, though he does not draw that connection here.

Wisdom distrusts itself. In counsel and war, "the more piercing, quick, and apprehensive" wisdom is, "the weaker it finds itself". Citing Sylla, he says commanders use debate "for custom's sake" and leave the best of the enterprise to Fortune, sometimes dressing rash resolutions as omens for their soldiers. This is a quiet scepticism about deliberation, short of the full Pyrrhonism of other essays.

When uncertain, choose honesty. Because the shortest road cannot be known, he recommends the course of greatest "honesty and justice"; a man who fails while doing the noble thing is not to be blamed for his intention and keeps the glory.

Suspicion is a worse tyranny than the plot. Anticipating conspiracy by punishment has profited few ("witness so many Roman emperors"); guards cannot protect a prince because "whosoever despises his own life, is always master of that of another man". Dion refused to investigate, and Alexander drank the potion while handing the physician the warning letter. Montaigne judges that act the finest image of Alexander's mind. Advisers who preach distrust "preach to them ruin and dishonour"; he knows one man ruined by such advice and another advanced by the opposite.

Trust must be performed without fear. Scipio crossing to Africa, the king who put himself in his enemies' hands, and Caesar facing mutinous legions illustrate confident exposure, but Montaigne adds a hedge: the gesture works only if the face is clear of fear. His own cases are a governor who went out to a mob, whom Montaigne faults not for going but for shifting from a commanding to an entreating manner (and again to terror when his nose bled), and a muster where his advice to ride out cheerfully and call for full volleys produced mutual confidence. The crowd he calls a "many-headed monster" more susceptible to reverence and fear than to humanity.

Appearances of intelligence; living with the worst. Dionysius pays a stranger who admits his secret is only to claim a secret; Montaigne draws the lesson that princes do well to publish what they learn of plots, while the Duke of Athens erred by killing his informer. He ends with the hunted Roman who surrendered to end his fear, judging that to prepare for the worst is better than "continual feverish fear of an accident for which there was no cure", and to take consolation that the feared thing may never come.

Notable passages

The Rouen story (c1) is told on the authority of Jacques Amiot and shows the prince pre-empting the plotter by revealing his knowledge, then reframing the conflict as one of religions' charity. It is a Wars-of-Religion exemplum, and Montaigne's praise is the prince's, not a doctrinal claim of his own.

The Livia and Augustus sequence (c2) uses the physicians' maxim (try the contrary when the usual recipes fail), which sits oddly beside Montaigne's own contempt for physic; the chapter does not remark on this. Augustus's two-hour speech to Cinna is the exemplary core, and the closing sentence generalises it to the mastery of Fortune.

The physic passage (c3) is a clear first-person statement of Montaigne's trust in nature over medicine, a theme that later grows in the essays on experience and illness (not in this unit).

The art-and-Fortune passage (c4) generalises beyond politics to authorship and reading: creative achievement exceeds the maker's intention, and the reader may find more than the author put there. Compare the self-making of personal-essay-as-form, though this passage concerns luck rather than self-knowledge.

The "Wherefore" paragraph (c5) is the pivot where the chapter draws its practical rule from uncertainty: choose honour over calculation. It is a limited ethic of acting well under ignorance of outcomes, not a rejection of prudence in general.

The muster episode (c6) is Montaigne's own advice from a council in which he was in the minority, and is the best evidence of how he thought public office under suspicion should be handled.

For WMP, the chapter's themes of statecraft against a background of civil war, and of the limits of calculation in the face of events, sit near michel-de-montaigne's wider scepticism; any link to niccolo-machiavelli is by contrast only: where the prudential tradition counsels guards, suspicion and pre-emptive punishment, Montaigne recommends open trust, though he never names Machiavelli here.
