Of Repentance: the self as passage, and what repentance can honestly mean

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Montaigne uses the question of repentance to define his project of painting the passing self, arguing that real repentance belongs only to deliberate, rooted vice, that he rarely repents because his actions match his nature and the occasion, that private virtue outweighs public show, and that old age's imposed reformations are not virtue.

The essay opens as a statement of method: "Others form man; I only report him." Montaigne paints a changing, particular, ordinary self ("I do not paint its being, I paint its passage") and claims the whole human condition lives in any private man. From this he turns to the titular question. He says he very rarely repents, defining the conscience he claims as that of a man, not an angel or a horse, and qualifies it at once: he speaks "inquiring and doubting" and "I do not teach; I only relate." He then argues that vice leaves repentance in the soul like an ulcer, that a good conscience is its own reward and should not rest on others' opinion, that true repentance concerns settled, willed vice rather than sudden passion, that regret over what exceeds one's nature or one's foresight is not repentance, and that the reformations of old age, produced by decay rather than judgment, are no virtue. The essay ends with Montaigne's candid report that age worsens the mind and that he is resisting it as best he can.

Themes

The self as moving subject. Everything is in motion, even constancy ("a slower and more languishing motion"), so the book records the self hour by hour. Montaigne admits he may contradict himself but "never contradict the truth" (a saying he attributes to Demades). He insists the ordinary private life is as good a subject as any, that he presents himself by his "universal being" rather than as grammarian, poet or lawyer, and that in this book "who touches the one, touches the other": work and workman cannot be separated. This is a strong statement of the essay as a technology of self-knowledge; see personal-essay-as-form and self-construction-through-writing. He hedges his own authority throughout: he claims fidelity, not art, and says he dares to speak truth only "as much as I dare."

Conscience as inner tribunal, against public opinion. Vice, he says, is condemned by sound judgment and begets inward repentance; virtue brings a "natural rejoicing" that is "the only reward that we can never fail of." Grounding virtue in others' approval is unsafe in a corrupt age (he quotes a Latin line, in the text untranslated, to the effect that what were vices are now customs). He explicitly counts as vices even those things that mere opinion, authorised by law and custom, has made so, which touches custom as the invisible standard of morals; compare habit-and-identity. Friends' reproaches and praises are often mismeasured; "You yourself only know if you are cowardly and cruel" while others see "your art" and not your nature.

Private life outweighs public performance. An "exact life" is one that is regular "in private," where there is no study or artifice. He cites exempla (Bias, Julius Drusus offering to pay double to make his house visible, Agesilaus lodging in temples) and the proverb that no man is a prophet in his own country, applying it to his reception in Gascony as a printed author. Public renown is a pageant put off with the robe. Virtue is "not in flying high, but in walking orderly"; Socrates in his mean employment is preferred to Alexander in his great theatre. Heroes are imagined as monsters (Tamerlane's eyebrows, Erasmus speaking only in adages) because we cannot couple their common faculties with the astonishing ones.

What counts as real repentance. Montaigne distinguishes sudden-passion sins, which may be recanted, from vices "rooted in a strong and vigorous will," for which a sudden repentance is "very hard for me to imagine." He tells of the Armagnac peasant-thief who half-repays his victims: that man hates theft less than poverty, and repents only in proportion as he recompenses. He criticises Stoic precepts that demand correction while forbidding disturbance of the soul's repose, and says devotion is easy to counterfeit. Repentance, if real, must "sting me all over"; a mere wish to be other than one is (to be an angel or Cato) is not repentance, because it touches things not in our power. A reported opinion that he rejects: the Pythagorean idea that men take a new soul to approach oracles, which he allows only as something extrinsic and lent.

Judging the past by its occasion; fortune over fault. He does not judge former decisions by outcomes: "I do not consider what it is now, but what it was then." Errors came "not for want of good understanding, but for want of good luck"; there are hidden "mute conditions" in men and matters that prudence cannot foresee. Phocion's reply (content that things went well, not repenting his advice) is an exemplum Montaigne approves. He also reports his own indifference to advice, giving and receiving little, and his wish to "settle and wholly contain myself within myself." Past events he treats through the Stoic chain of causes, the "great revolution of the world." Here he explicitly borrows a Stoic frame while earlier criticising Stoic practice.

Against the repentance of old age. He "abominates" the incidental repentance age brings: being made chaste by catarrhs and the stone is "neither chastity nor temperance." His reason is the same as in youth, only weaker; "if there be convalescence, 'tis an enchanted one." He wants to be presented uniformly ("I am the same within that I am without"), would live his life again as lived, and rejects men estimating him "by what I have ceased to be." Yet he is not a cheerleader for age: he sees it breeding envy, injustice, malice and sourness, and speculates (as his own surmise) that Socrates may have colluded in his condemnation to avoid decline. He confesses that age "gets foot by foot upon me."

Notable passages

The self as passage (c1). The opening paragraph sets the terms: the world and even the pyramids are in motion, so the self can only be caught as it passes, and the soul "is always learning and making trial" rather than resolving. It is the clearest statement in the unit of the essay-as-trial against settled doctrine.

The disclaimer (c2). A short hedge that reframes the whole essay: his conscience is a human one, he speaks "inquiring and doubting," and defers to common beliefs. "I do not teach; I only relate." Carry this hedge when quoting his strong claims about not repenting.

Socrates and Alexander (c3). The ordinary, unobserved conduct of life is rated above conquest. Montaigne prefers the answer "Carry on human life conformably with its natural condition" to "Subdue the world." It is a counter to the glory-driven public record and a view of virtue that is non-heroic.

The definition of repentance (c4). He may wish to be other than he is, but this is not repentance, which "does not properly touch things that are not in our power." Repentance must be universal as a tincture, not a patch.

Fortune and prudence (c5). Counsel is bound to its time; he blames fortune and not his work. This anticipates a modern distinction between decision quality and outcome.

Old age and enchanted convalescence (c6). A frank, physical argument: reform won by decay is no virtue, and he would rather owe good conduct to judgment than to disease.
