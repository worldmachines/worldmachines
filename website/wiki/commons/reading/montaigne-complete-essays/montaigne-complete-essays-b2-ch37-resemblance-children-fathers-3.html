Against Physic: Lahontan, the Goat, and the Letter to Madame de Duras

Commons · Essays of Michel de Montaigne · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In the closing third of his essay on physic, Montaigne piles up anecdotes and arguments against medicine (the Lahontan villagers ruined by a doctor, the he-goat's stones, the impossibility of trustworthy empirical proof), prints a letter to Madame de Duras disclaiming authorship as a trade in favour of living well, and ends by defending his stubborn aversion to drugs while welcoming the diversity of opinion.

This is the last slice (3/3) of an essay on Montaigne's own resistance to medicine, and it begins mid-argument, on the varied practice of the baths in Germany and Italy. Montaigne cites this variety as evidence that even the part of physic "least depending upon art" is shot through with confusion and uncertainty. He then tells two stories: a mountain community corrupted by a notary and a physician, and his own experiment with he-goat's blood. He distinguishes the physicians (whom he honours as men) from their art (which he attacks), and diagnoses the credulity of patients as fear of death and pain. A long epistemological argument follows: medical "experience" cannot be established by chance trial, since the search space is vast and the record-keepers few. A letter to Madame de Duras then interrupts the argument, and the slice closes with the Pericles anecdote, a self-justification, and a short praise of human diversity.

Themes

Custom and the fragility of an unmedicalised way of life. The Lahontan story is Montaigne's account ("as they say") of a people governed by "the reverence to custom", who needed no judge, advocate or beggar. The village was undone by two outsiders, a notary and a physician. The physician taught the names of diseases and replaced garlic with "strange mixtures", and so "made a trade not only of their health, but of their lives". The villagers themselves report that they never felt the evening air to be harmful until physic named it so. The tale is told by Montaigne, who reports it from the inhabitants and offers it as a cautionary exemplum. It resonates with habit-and-identity: a custom-held form of life is sustained by not being made explicit. It also rhymes with the project's Legibility theme, since naming organs and fevers makes bodies readable to a profession. The fit is partial, because Montaigne is not building a theory of administrative legibility; he is telling a story of corruption by learning.

Distrust of physic joined to personal tolerance of physicians. Montaigne says outright, "I honour physicians" as persons, and "‘tis their art I inveigh against". He sends for them when sick "only to have their company", and lets them order only what is indifferent to his palate and custom. He observes that physicians who refuse physic themselves "would accommodate their practice to their rules, if they did not themselves know how false these are". This is his inference and his own argument, not a report from authority.

Credulity comes from fear. "‘Tis the fear of death and of pain, impatience of disease" make belief "pliable"; most men "acquiesce and permit" rather than believe. The exempla (the Babylonians' public square, Homer and Plato on Egyptians as physicians, the hundred-ingredient pill that left the gravel unmoved) show a general habit of folk and expert remedies. Montaigne's wry practical conclusion is that if he must take physic he would prefer the old women's remedies, since "if they do no good, they will do no harm".

The epistemology of experience. The sharpest argument here is sceptical. Most medicinal virtue is an occult "quintessence" known only by use and custom. Montaigne accepts a few cases with a plausible guide (Galen's viper-wine, animal example) but finds incredible the story that remedies were found "by fortune". A man cannot know where to begin among plants, animals and metals, and cannot rule out that the cure was the disease's natural term, something else eaten, or a grandmother's prayers. He also objects that only "three witnesses, three doctors" record experiments and then "lord it over all mankind". It is the Pyrrhonist side of Montaigne in miniature, aimed at induction and testimony rather than at reason in general.

Self-portrait and the refusal of authorship as vocation. The inserted letter to Madame de Duras is the unit's most self-referential passage. He says the book is to present him "such as I naturally am", and will outlast him only a few years or days. He declares himself "less a writer of books than anything else", with studies meant "to teach me to do, and not to write", and he describes his whole business as to "frame my life". The letter ranks conduct over written product, and it shows the book as a faded likeness of a livelier self ("towards the bottom of the barrel"). It resonates with self-construction-through-writing and personal-essay-as-form, though Montaigne here denies the book is the real work, which pulls against the idea of self-construction through writing. He also says he wants praise while alive, not posthumous renown, and cites Tiberius' opposite humour as ridiculous. Pliny and Celsus are cited as precedent: they "cut its throat" while he only pinches.

Constancy hedged; diversity affirmed. Citing Pericles' amulets as a sign of sickness, he says he may one day grasp "six drachms of opium" and warns that this would signal a mind in fever, not a changed judgment. He disclaims responsibility for his "future constancy". He closes by saying his aversion is inherited from his ancestors, and that he does not hate contrary opinions, since variety is nature's way and "their most universal quality is diversity".

Notable passages

Lahontan (c1). The notary and the physician spoil a custom-governed village; the villagers' own testimony is that they found themselves "oppressed with a legion of unaccustomed diseases" and their lives cut "shorter by the half". The narrative is a reported tale, not verified by Montaigne.

The goat (c2). Montaigne bought the received remedy of he-goat's blood for the stone, but found stones in the goat's paunch, which he takes to prove the blood is no cure, because "nothing is engendered in a body but by the conspiracy and communication of all the parts". He adds a sidelong note on family women storing up "little trumperies" and "triumphing" over their successes.

Against experiment-by-chance (c3). The cascade of "the many nations in the French; the many ages in age" ends with the three-witness objection to medical authority.

The letter to Madame de Duras (c4). The famous insistence that he is "less a writer of books than anything else" and "Those whom I see make good books in ill breeches, should first have mended their breeches".

Pericles and the opium (c5). The self-aware anecdote shows his limits: he will not pledge himself against future fear.

Diversity of opinion (c6). Montaigne's explanation of his tolerance, that variety is the most general way of nature, "more in souls than bodies".
