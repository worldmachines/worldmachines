England's Global Trade Triumph and the Slow Grinding of the Industrial Revolution

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
England's eighteenth-century commercial revolution redirected her trade from nearby Europe toward a distant periphery (India, the Americas, the Caribbean), was underwritten by a rapidly built network of coastal shipping, canals, roads and early railways, and combined with a centuries-long, undirected erosion of the ancien regime economy to produce an industrial revolution that England encountered rather than pursued.

This unit closes Braudel's account of England's eighteenth- and early-nineteenth-century ascent in three movements: first, the 'commercial revolution' that pivoted English trade away from Europe toward a distant periphery (India, the Americas, Africa, the Caribbean), argued through the historiographical dispute over whether foreign or domestic trade drove the industrial revolution; second, the buildout of inland transport (coastal shipping, river improvement, canal fever, turnpikes, early iron rails) as an active precondition of that growth rather than its passive by-product; and third, a closing methodological statement that the industrial revolution was not a pursued goal but a slow, undirected outcome of forces already in motion since the Restoration.

Themes

Victory abroad meant retreat from Europe and advance into the periphery. England's trade with nearby Europe was, on Braudel's account, actually declining relative to her overseas trade after 1760: high domestic prices and labour costs let France and the Dutch undercut her in the Mediterranean, the Levant, Italy and Cadiz, while she held only Portugal and Russia among European markets. The compensating gains came through force (India in 1757, Canada in 1762, the African coasts) and through circumstance (the newly independent United States kept buying English goods; the Napoleonic Wars pushed France and Holland out of world trade entirely). Braudel states the resulting relationship in explicitly core-periphery terms: the periphery becomes 'a reserve to be drawn on by the world-economy she dominated' — Braudel's own économie-monde vocabulary, with Wallerstein's hierarchy of zones as the implicit interlocutor.

The domestic-versus-foreign-trade dispute is a false binary Braudel refuses to resolve by elimination. Contemporary observers (Simond, and an unnamed critic of the 'Continental system') insisted the 'vulgar notion' that England's wealth came from foreign trade was false, pointing to her internal division of labour and machinery. Braudel grants that customs records make domestic trade look two or three times larger than foreign trade, but stresses what those records omit: capital movements, the slave trade, freight, and remittances from planters and nabobs. He treats internal and external growth as 'multipliers' that 'pooled their strength,' and endorses Amalendu Guha's proposal to compare surpluses rather than totals — India's roughly £2 million a year in extracted surplus set against England's £6-19 million in domestic investment between 1750 and 1820.

Inland transport is treated as a cause, not a consequence, of commercial expansion. Coastal 'colliers' (three-quarters of the British navy, over 100,000 sailors by 1800), improved and lock-equipped rivers, the canal boom beginning with the Sankey canal (1755) and the Duke of Bridgewater's canal, turnpikes from 1654, and the earliest wooden-then-iron rail lines (culminating in Stephenson's Rocket, 1814) together form what Braudel calls a proliferation of transport that 'preceded the demands of trade, and then helped it to expand.' The Bridgewater vignette — a duke running a capital-intensive canal scheme on paper credit while sometimes lacking a coin for the postillion — is offered as proof that well-judged infrastructure speculation, not blind 'canal fever,' built the network.

The mills of history grind exceedingly slow: an anti-event, undirected historiography. The unit's final section is Braudel's clearest methodological statement in this stretch of the book: the industrial revolution 'was already there in embryo... by the Restoration (1660), yet nothing emerged very quickly.' What changed across a century and a half was cumulative and largely invisible in the short term — guilds losing their grip, landed property structures eroding, mercantilist protections being enacted and later abandoned — until the accumulated change became visible as a named 'revolution.' Braudel closes by explicitly denying that this was a pursued objective: England's economy 'was not moving towards any goal, rather it encountered one.' This resonates strongly with history-machine (Lohse) — a paradigm assembling silently and distributedly from independent components until it lurches into visibility, against 'great men' historiography — though Braudel's picture is more continuously gradualist than a single 'lurch': the guild decline, the Great Fire's aftermath, and the renewed Navigation Act are concurrent slow erosions rather than discrete components snapping into a paradigm all at once. The same closing claim also half-resonates with Configurancy (Rao) — a national economy that 'encountered' its own transformation rather than teleologically pursuing it mirrors configurancy's non-teleological co-emergence, though configurancy is pitched at the more general register of agents and worlds co-emerging, not one country's undirected economic trajectory.

Notable passages

The opening statement of the chapter's thesis — 'the English commercial revolution in the eighteenth century' with export-industry output rising from 100 to 550 against 100 to 150 for the home market — sets up the reciprocal-revolutions argument that structures everything else in the unit (c1).

The 'periphery... reserve' passage is Braudel's most explicit statement of core-periphery logic applied to England's shift away from Europe (c2), and it sits directly behind his handling of the Guha surplus comparison, where the concrete India investment figures (£2 million a year extracted, against £6-19 million in domestic investment) let him adjudicate the domestic-versus-foreign-trade debate without simply picking a side (c3).

The Bridgewater canal anecdote — an entrepreneur who 'often did not have so much as a crown to pay the postillion' despite running a paper-credit-financed canal scheme rivaling the Bank of France in circulation — is Braudel's chosen illustration that transport infrastructure investment was judicious speculation, not folly (c4).

The two closing passages carry the unit's historiographical argument: the claim that the industrial revolution needed 'a good deal of destruction, adaptation and restructuring' before it could move (c5), and the final line refusing any teleological reading — England's economy 'encountered' its transformation rather than pursuing it (c6). Together these are the passages that make this unit's argument reachable for downstream citation; the rest of the material (customs-record limitations, the OCR-garbled map captions on population and transport networks) is supporting texture rather than load-bearing claim.
