Success in Asia, Failure in America: The V.O.C.'s Monopoly and the W.I.C.'s Ruin

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
The V.O.C. defended a profitable but violently administered Asian spice monopoly whose accounts mysteriously deteriorated after 1696 even as an oligopoly of top merchants captured its real gains, while the West India Company failed to replicate that success in Brazil because it never gained territorial control of production.

This unit tracks the Dutch East India Company (V.O.C.) through the costly violence that sustained its Asian spice monopoly, into the still-unexplained financial "rot" that set in around 1696 and left even Braudel doubting his own thesis about merchant profit, and then turns to contrast that Asian success with the Dutch West India Company's failure to hold Brazil — closing on Braudel's own verdict that little Holland could not swallow Asia, Brazil, and Africa at once.

Themes

A commodity market that behaved like a two-pan scale. The V.O.C.'s "perennial problem" was calibrating what it extracted in Asia to what Europe would actually buy, on a double Batavia–Amsterdam timetable. Braudel's image is exact: Asia and Europe are "the two unequally laden trays on a scale," so that European demand for pepper and spices, driven up by the European invasion of Asia itself, fed back into Asian purchase prices even as growing shipments pushed European selling prices down. This is Braudel's world-economy logic in miniature — two zones bound into a single system, each move on one side registering on the other.

Monopoly was enforced by force, and force was expensive. The spice monopoly's "authoritarian price control" and "supervision of the quantities marketed" required a standing apparatus of coercion: garrisons in Ceylon eating up nearly all the cinnamon profits against a Kandyan king "who has never been tamed," a Banda monopoly imposed by war, deportation, and slave replacement of the native population, and a "perpetual battle with native shipping and trade" in Java that only reached a provisional resolution with the conquest of Macassar (1669) and Bantam (1682). Success and cost rose together; this was not a market transaction but an administered, garrisoned extraction regime.

Scale grew as the underlying profit mysteriously rotted. Braudel piles up figures to establish that the V.O.C. was vast — 6.5 million florins of capital in 1602 (ten times the English company's), over 100–160 ships, some 8,000 sailors, a payroll ballooning toward 150,000 by 1788, a million men shipped east across two centuries — yet the accounts (imprisoned in a structural split between the Amsterdam "factory Nederland" books and the Batavia overseas books that could never be reconciled into one balance sheet) show only modest profits even in the thriving seventeenth century, a decline setting in around 1696, and profits reaching zero by roughly 1724, after which the Company survived only by borrowing to pay its own dividends. Braudel is candid that this surprises and half-undermines his own longstanding claim that long-distance trade was capitalism's "super-trade."

Capitalism as capture above the market, not profit within it. The chapter's sharpest analytical move is to locate the Company's real "capitalist" logic not in its corporate profit-and-loss but in a standing syndicate of top Amsterdam merchants and bewindhebbers (administrators drawn from the same patrician families as the governing Heeren XVII) who controlled the purchase contracts, blocked competing bids, and warehoused goods until forced to sell on their own terms. Braudel's verdict — "the V.O.C. was a machine which stopped where the profits of trade monopolies began" — is his three-storey-house thesis in one sentence: capitalism is the distinct top layer sitting on, and skimming, the market economy beneath it, not a synonym for that market.

The eighteenth-century collapse had several converging causes. English direct cash purchasing in China (1698) and the Bengal–China tea-for-silver/opium circuit outflanked the V.O.C.'s older indirect junk-trade habits; internal warfare devastated the Coromandel coast; and — more damningly — endemic fraud among Company agents (who, unlike their English counterparts, were barred from legal private trade) opened a gulf between the "imperturbable burghers" of Amsterdam and an increasingly autonomous, luxury-soaked colonial society in Batavia, described as reproducing "the strange, heady and morbid atmosphere of Goa."

America demanded a different kind of machine than Asia did. Braudel frames the West India Company's failure in Brazil (Recife and Olinda seized, Maurice of Nassau's able seven-year governorship, final loss of the Nordeste by 1654, peace in 1661, reduction to Curaçao and Surinam) as evidence that Holland's Asian model — small, garrisoned trading posts extracting from populations that "could be ill-treated with impunity" — did not transfer to a continent where the Dutch faced organized European rivals (Portugal, backed by England, and a Spanish America "more solid than appearances suggested") and needed actual territorial control of production, not just of trade routes.

Notable passages

The opening paragraph stages the Asia–Europe interdependence through Pyrard de Laval's 1610 observation that what cost the Portuguese one sou now cost the Dutch four or five, and the vanished 1599 Banda prices for cloves and nutmeg — concrete evidence of the "scale" tipping as Dutch buying power itself inflated Asian prices.

The Ceylon–Banda passage is the clearest statement that monopoly was paramilitary administration: a garrison "consumed almost 'all the gains made by the sale of cinnamon,'" a peasant revolt over wages, and a Banda population rebuilt with roughly two thousand slaves imported from Bengal and Arakan after war and deportation gutted the original inhabitants. The unresolved king of Kandy, and the Java wars that ran on for decades after this passage, read as local sovereignty simply refusing to be absorbed into the Company's order — a rough, pre-modern cousin of what World Machines calls capture-resistance, though it is a matter of standing armies and royal courts holding ground rather than the Liveness-era autonomy the term was coined for.

The Cornelis Bicker/bewindhebber passage is where Braudel names his real subject: not the Company as such but "the great capitalist merchants" who "were in the pockets of" the purchasing syndicates from the very start, so that even their apparent shift from indiscriminate seventeenth-century dealing to eighteenth-century specialization "does not alter the terms of the problem." The line that follows — the V.O.C. as a machine that stopped exactly where trade-monopoly profits began — is the unit's thesis sentence.

Braudel's aside on the "modesty of the profits" is a rare moment of authorial self-interrogation ("Can I have been wrong?") that keeps the note honest: the numbers he assembles do not obviously support the very capitalism-as-super-trade argument that structures his whole three-volume project, and he says so before offering the oligopoly-capture explanation as a resolution. Read alongside the earlier scale figures, this also has a mild resonance with World Machines' decay-sequence: the Company kept growing in visible apparatus — more ships, more staff, more silver dispatched to Asia — even after the underlying profit function had already gone to zero around 1724, form persisting past function, though this is a single firm's ledger rather than the civilizational infrastructure the decay-sequence describes.

The Brazil section opens by testing and rejecting a tidy essentialist explanation (Holland as a "parasite plant" unsuited to build a New World from scratch) against the counter-evidence of Dutch numbers sent to Asia and Portugal's own success in Brazil — Braudel prefers structural to essentialist explanation. It closes on the diagnosis that the Dutch "mistake had been to try to erect a commercial superstructure without gaining control of production, without colonizing the territory in the modern sense of the word" — a claim that resonates with World Machines' legibility-as-machine-core (durable extraction required the cadastral, administrative grip that legibility describes, which the Dutch had in neither Asia's full sense nor Brazil's at all), though Braudel's frame here is commercial-institutional rather than Scott's high-modernist state apparatus.
