England's Trading Supremacy: Becoming an Island, and the Pound Sterling

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
England's rise to trading supremacy over the enlarged European world-economy is traced to two intertwined developments: its unplanned self-isolation into an autonomous 'island' polity between 1453 and 1558, and the near-unbroken three-century stability of the pound sterling from Elizabeth I's 1560-1 reform onward.

Braudel opens by dating England's supremacy within the enlarged European economy (glimpsed by the 1713 Treaty of Utrecht, visible by 1763, achieved by the 1783 Treaty of Versailles) and then works backward to its two foundations: England's accidental turn into an autonomous, insular 'national market' after being expelled from continental entanglement (1453-1558), and the pound sterling's almost uninterrupted stability from 1560-1 onward, which he treats as the single strangest fact in English economic history and refuses to explain by any one cause.

Themes

Insularity was a by-product of defeat, not a strategy. Braudel is emphatic that England 'without realizing it at the time, became... an island' only after the Hundred Years' War ended and Calais was finally lost (1453-1558); before that turning point she had been 'bodily linked with France, the Netherlands and the rest of Europe,' acting as one province within a single Anglo-French battlefield. Being 'sent packing' from the continent, not any design, redirected English energy inward: land improvement, forest and marsh reclamation, and attention to Scotland, Ireland and an 'unabsorbed' Wales. Braudel's own gloss is that a reduced, well-defined territory was 'much more amenable to the rapid formation of a national market' - insularity as the precondition for legibility and coherence at the national scale, not its opposite.

The Channel as boundary-infrastructure, felt only belatedly as an advantage. 'The Channel, the Straits of Dover and the North Sea had become a barrier, a floating bulwark protecting the island,' and Braudel stacks two witnesses across nearly fifty years - an anonymous Frenchman in 1740 calling England 'ready made for commerce,' and Arthur Young in 1787 relishing how the Channel 'conveniently separates England from the rest of the world' - to show a natural boundary being retroactively recognized as constitutive of a distinct economic unit, something 'not... perceived as such' for a long time.

Reformation and Discoveries compounded the separation. The 1529-33 break with Rome ran parallel to the continental break, monastic land sales gave the economy 'a boost,' and after the Great Discoveries England's Atlantic-facing position turned a former continental 'excrescence' into 'the point of departure for the new worlds.' Braudel pairs this with a genuine undercurrent of English anxiety and hostility toward Europe (Sully's 1603 remark that the English hate the French 'with a hatred so strong... that one is tempted to number it among the natural dispositions of this people'), reading a possible 'inferiority complex' behind England's aggression rather than simple confidence.

A serial, reactive institution-building against successive continental rivals. England's response to being drawn back into world trade through its cloth exports was to expel or dispossess each dominant foreign interest in turn: Italian merchant bankers driven out, Hanseatic privileges stripped (1556) and the Stalhof taken (1598), the (Royal) Exchange founded against Antwerp (1566-8), the Stock Companies launched against Spain and Portugal, the Navigation Act (1651) aimed at Holland, and an aggressive eighteenth-century colonial policy aimed at France. Braudel's closing line on the pound makes this serial antagonism explicit: 'its clear identification of the enemy: today Antwerp, tomorrow Amsterdam, the next day Paris' - a rehearsal, at the century scale of city rivalries, of the same succession-of-dominant-centres logic (Venice-Antwerp-Genoa-Amsterdam-London) that runs through the volume, and which loosely rhymes with world-machines' pattern of one infrastructure supplanting another at friction points - though here the churn is measured in decades and named enemies, not the millennium-scale, largely anonymous succession the world-machines framework describes.

The pound as legibility infrastructure: a fixed unit of account underwriting credit. Braudel states directly that without the pound's fixed value 'there would have been no easy credit, no security for those lending money to the sovereign, no confidence in any contract... no rise to greatness, no financial superiority.' He contrasts this with continental workarounds that needed extra machinery to manufacture the same trust - the fairs of Lyon and Besançon inventing fictional stable moneys of account (the ecu au soleil, the ecu de marc), the Banco di Rialto and Bank of Amsterdam charging an agio on 'bank money' as a guarantee of security - where the Bank of England, founded on an already-stable pound, 'needed no such guarantee.' A single durable standard of measure doing the work that elsewhere required compounding institutional scaffolding is a clean instance of what the World Machines framework calls legibility-as-machine-core: standardized units enabling scale and confident long-distance contracting, though the resonance is with a single national currency rather than the cadastres, timetables and censuses legibility is usually illustrated with.

Braudel explicitly refuses 'great man' history for the pound's survival. Asking whether Thomas Mun single-handedly saved sterling's stability in 1621, Braudel answers his own question: 'can one man, however brilliant and far-sighted, have been responsible for a monetary process affecting the entire English economy and even developments in Europe?' He instead credits a 'strange alliance' - the 1630 Anglo-Spanish silver-transport deal - and, more broadly, insists there is 'not one single explanation but a series of explanations,' pragmatic expedients accumulating into a coherent long-run pattern without any guiding theory. This is a direct, textbook instance of the history-machine pattern: an outcome that looks in hindsight like a designed policy but that Braudel insists assembled itself from independent, uncoordinated components (a silver deal, American bullion, a Dutch king's credit needs, John Locke's property-rights arguments) rather than from any single actor's foresight.

The 1694-6 crisis as a case study in whose interests prevailed. Facing a collapse of confidence (bad harvests, war-driven specie exports, the guinea rocketing from 22 to 30 shillings), Lowndes wanted a 20% devaluation while Locke fought for the pound as 'an invariable fundamental unit,' defending property-owners and contract-holders. Braudel's own explanation for Locke's victory is structural rather than philosophical: the new Dutch-born king's government had committed to unpopular long-term borrowing, Amsterdam creditors were already invested in English public stocks, and 'an absolutely unassailable credit standing was necessary' - so the state paid the full £7 million recoinage cost itself rather than risk that credibility.

Drift to a de facto gold standard as accumulated, unplanned consequence. Braudel treats England's slide toward gold - the guinea's persistent overvaluation relative to the Dutch silver ratio, repeated downward adjustments (1698, 1717 under Newton) that never quite closed the gap - as an extension of 'Gresham's so-called law': an over-priced currency (gold) becomes 'bad' money that drives out the 'good' (silver), reversing the usual formulation's polarity. He links the shift to England's Methuen Treaty (1703) access to Brazilian gold and, later, to a Portuguese trade deficit that pushed England toward paper money altogether - the 1797 Bank Restriction Act (meant to last six weeks, holding for twenty-four years) being the 'final miracle,' underwritten not by bullion but by 'the huge output of the British Isles.'

Notable passages

The chapter's title claim rests on the founding passage that England became an island 'without realizing it,' an accidental autonomy following the end of continental entanglement (1453-1558). The Channel-as-'floating bulwark' passage, stacking a 1740 French observer against Arthur Young in 1787, shows that advantage being recognized only belatedly. The catalogue of expulsions and reprisals - Italian bankers, the Hanseatic Stalhof, the Royal Exchange founded 'against Antwerp' - culminates in the mischievous 1749 line that 'the English consider their own Pretentions as Rights, and the Rights of their Neighbours as Usurpations.' On the pound itself, Braudel states baldly that fixed currency was the precondition for credit, security and 'rise to greatness' - the chapter's clearest legibility argument. His refusal to credit Thomas Mun alone for sterling's 1621 survival, reaching instead for a 'strange alliance' with Spain, is the chapter's explicit anti-great-man methodological moment. And the closing passage reframes the whole three-century story not as foresight but as 'the repeated result of the aggressive tension' of an island identifying a new enemy in each generation - Antwerp, then Amsterdam, then Paris - with the pound's stability as 'a weapon in this battle.'
