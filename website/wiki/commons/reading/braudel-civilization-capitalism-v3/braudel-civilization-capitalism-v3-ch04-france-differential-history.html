Differential France: shifting dividing lines and the interior colonized by its own margins

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel traces the succession of proposed dividing lines across France (the Paris meridian, the Nantes–Lyon parallel, the contested Rouen/Saint-Malo–Geneva line) and argues that France resolves instead into three persistent zones — rich, outward-facing Atlantic ports and eastern border towns flanking a poor landlocked interior that the border zones actively colonize, an inversion of the usual sense of "periphery."

Surveying a century and a half of French historiography on how to divide France into "two" or more zones — Spooner's specie-based Paris meridian, the later Nantes–Lyon parallel, the contested Rouen/Saint-Malo–Geneva line, Rémond's tripartite demographic map — Braudel argues that no single boundary is permanent; instead France's economic geography settles into a persistent, if variously drawn, three-way structure: rich outward-facing border zones (the Atlantic ports of "France I" and the chain of eastern towns of "France III") flanking a poor, landlocked interior ("France II"), with the wealthy margins actively colonizing and subordinating that interior rather than the reverse.

Themes

A succession of dividing lines, not one fixed line. Spooner's sixteenth-century east/west meridian (continental copper-money provinces versus Atlantic pieces-of-eight provinces) gives way to a seventeenth–eighteenth-century Nantes–Lyon north/south line (industrious north versus depressed south, per Goubert and Delumeau), which gives way in turn to the Rouen–Geneva or Saint-Malo–Geneva line, and eventually to a modern west/east axis (the underdeveloped "French desert" versus a Germany-facing east). Braudel's point is explicitly methodological: there is no single and indelible dividing line running through the country, only "a single line swivelling like the needle on a dial" — development and underdevelopment are not fixed geographically, and any given line's shift has exogenous as well as endogenous causes (contact with the "prevailing supremacy of the north," echoing earlier attractions to Italy and to the Atlantic).

Testing a boundary against the numbers. Braudel stages a live quantitative dispute over the Rouen/Saint-Malo–Geneva line: Michel Morineau's 1750 trade-balance figures reverse its usual reading, showing the south in surplus (two-thirds of exports) and the north in deficit; Braudel counters that raw export volume conflates wealth with the "economic revenge" of poor regions turning to industry, and prefers J.C. Toutain's 1785 per-capita output ranking, which puts Paris far ahead (280% of the national average) and Brittany last — restoring, if unevenly, the north's edge. The unit models Braudel's habitual move of taking a plausible historical narrative and pressure-testing it against whatever series exist, while flagging how thin and short-spanned (barely a century, 1697–1780/1805) the available ancien-régime statistics are for settling the question.

Margins are rich, the interior is poor — and Braudel refuses to call this "periphery." The unit's sharpest theoretical move: the French margins, with very few exceptions, have always been comparatively rich, while the interior has been comparatively poor — the opposite of what a casual reader might expect. Braudel deliberately withholds the word periphery here, since in his own terminology (shared, he notes, with "many writers" — Wallerstein chief among them) periphery means the backward regions removed from the privileged centres of a world-economy. France's border zones are the opposite: rich, connected, outward-facing. The distinction only holds at a given spatial scale — France's coastal and eastern margins are locally central even as France as a whole is semi-peripheral to Amsterdam or London.

Three Frances. Braudel names a tripartite scheme: France I, the string of Atlantic "terminal" ports (Dunkirk to Marseille); France II, the vast interior; France III, an "uninterrupted chain" of eastern towns from the Mediterranean and the Alps to the North Sea (Grenoble, Lyon, Strasbourg, Lille and others), less homogeneous than the ports but united by comparative freedom from the French customs system (Alsace, Lorraine, Franche-Comté were "effectively foreign" territories). Two extended case studies carry this: Lille, a "turntable of trade" whose specie returns routed through Holland or England rather than to Paris; and Saint-Malo, a "barren rock" whose merchants nonetheless ran the Cadiz silver trade and treated even nearby Pontorson, nine leagues off, as barely reachable — border towns oriented to the ocean and to Europe, indifferent to their own hinterland.

The interior colonized by its own periphery. The unit's culminating claim inverts the expected causal arrow: it is not that the poor interior failed to develop on its own, but that wealthy border towns actively organized forays inland, dominating what Braudel calls a "neutral," unresisting central space. Bordeaux annexed the Périgord's trade; more strikingly, Toulouse — landlocked despite the Garonne and the Canal du Midi — was "satellized" by the triple pull of Lyon (silk), Bordeaux and Marseille, its merchants reduced to acting as commission agents for outside wholesalers even in the grain trade. This is Braudel's own core–periphery dynamic replaying at a sub-national scale, and it frames his closing question — the unresolved dialogue between "trading France" and "territorial France" — as one about whether trading France's advantages could ever have been enough to master a territorial mass that, unlike Amsterdam or London, France as a whole never occupied a first-rank position to mobilize.

Reaching for legibility, and falling short of it. A long methodological digression surveys the ancien régime's own attempts to measure its internal differentiation: the 1664 "general surveys" of the généralités, the Burgundy intendants' survey (1697–1703), Orry's 1745 survey (shelved so completely it was rediscovered by accident in 1952) sorting provinces into five "faculties" from "well off" to "indigent," and André Rémond's unpublished eighteenth-century atlas of cereal yields, grain prices, fiscal pressure and population density. This resonates with legibility-as-machine-core — a state groping toward measuring and ordering its own territory for governance and taxation — though it is legibility straining against its means: the surveys are "primarily descriptive," span too short a period to separate lasting structure from passing circumstance, and repeatedly go unpublished or unfinished. Braudel's verdict, that the internal realities and relationships of France during the ancien régime have yet to be discovered, is itself evidence that this proto-legibility apparatus was centuries away from a later state's mature, totalizing legibility.

Notable passages

  • "A single line swivelling like the needle on a dial" (c1) states the unit's governing method: dividing lines are historical artifacts of shifting criteria, not fixed geographic facts, and their movements have causes outside France as well as inside it.
  • Margins vs. interior, and the refusal of "periphery" (c2) is the unit's key conceptual pivot — Braudel reserves "periphery" for his world-economy vocabulary and uses D'Argenson's 1747 journal entry to certify, from inside the period, that the interior was the poor zone and the coast the rich one.
  • Morineau's reversed trade balance (c3) shows the Rouen/Saint-Malo–Geneva line being tested, and partly overturned, by 1750 export figures — Braudel modeling skepticism toward both the received line and the data that seems to refute it.
  • Defining France I / II / III (c4) lays out the tripartite spatial scheme — Atlantic ports, interior, eastern border chain — that structures the rest of the argument and the Lille and Saint-Malo case studies that follow it.
  • Toulouse "satellized" (c5) is the unit's clearest statement of border-zone towns colonizing the interior, inverting the naive assumption that landlocked backwardness is simply endogenous.
