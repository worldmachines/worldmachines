Weights and Measures: The Search for National Economic Magnitudes

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel defends 'orders of magnitude' retrospective national accounting — patrimony, national income, per capita income — tests coefficients like Bairoch's wage-multiplier and correlational ratios (debt:GNP, budget:GNP, patrimony:income) against historical cases from Venice to France to England, and concludes that the secular rise of GNP and the rise of the state's share of national income are among the few visible continuities in European history.

This section (headed 'Weights and measures' in the original, though its subject is economic rather than physical metrology) is Braudel's methodological apologia for retrospective national accounting: he surveys two centuries of 'calculators' who tried to size national economies before modern statistics existed, defines the three variables historians can hope to estimate (patrimony, national income, per capita income), tests proposed coefficients and correlations (Bairoch's wage multiplier, debt:GNP, budget:GNP, patrimony:income ratios) against concrete cases — Venice, Orleans, England, France, Austria, Russia, colonial America — and closes by naming two things this exercise makes visible across centuries: GNP's near-uninterrupted rise, and the state's steadily growing appropriation of national income.

Themes

Orders of magnitude, not precision. Braudel is explicit that he is not proposing a 'revolutionary new form of economic history' but only 'orders of magnitude' and 'plausible' coefficients that 'cannot claim to be accurate.' He accepts margins of error of 10–20% for modern aggregates and nearer 30% for historical ones, and treats this imprecision as a feature of the genre rather than a reason to abstain — a stance he attributes partly to Paul Bairoch and to Ernest Wageman's dictum (cited via Das Ziffer als Detective) that figures should be made to corroborate one another even when no complete series exists.

A genealogy of 'calculators.' The unit opens with a lineage of pre-statistical estimators — William Petty, Gregory King, Boisguilbert, Isaac de Pinto, Vauban, Turgot, Charles Dutot — each attacking the same problem (comparing the wealth of nations) from fragmentary data, long before Lavoisier's 1791 calculations or twentieth-century national accounting (which Braudel dates to 1924). Braudel treats their scattered, independent efforts as the raw material from which a genuine discipline — national accounting as, per Jean Marczewski, 'a science in its own right' — eventually crystallized.

Three variables, three kinds of measurement. Braudel defines patrimony (the stock of accumulated wealth, 'least in use today, which is a pity'), national income (the flow of wealth, viewable from production, income, or expenditure), and per capita income (GNP divided by population, 'the key statistic' for growth) as the irreducible toolkit, while stressing that no 'audit of national wealth' has ever existed and that even the basic G.N.P./N.N.P./N.I. equations conceal real definitional 'scholastic debates.'

Bairoch's wage-multiplier and its limits. The unit's centerpiece empirical claim is Bairoch's finding that multiplying an unskilled labourer's daily wage by roughly 196–200 yields per capita national income for nineteenth-century Europe, and that the same coefficient extends, with declining reliability, back into the sixteenth–eighteenth centuries. Braudel tests it case by case — Venice in 1534 (too low a result), Orleans in 1525 (too high), Vauban's France in 1707 (a good fit) — treating the mismatches themselves as historically informative rather than as failures of the method.

Growth, development, and progress are not synonyms. Braudel distinguishes GNP-focused 'development' from per-capita-focused 'growth,' and revives 'progress' (neutral vs. non-neutral) to classify cases where per capita income rises without structural transformation — his running example is eighteenth-century Portugal, whose Brazilian gold gave it (and its king) income rivaling or exceeding France's without any accompanying structural innovation, a pattern he explicitly likens to Kuwait's oil wealth in his own present.

Correlations as a substitute for missing data. Where direct figures fail, Braudel builds cross-checking ratios: national debt should not exceed roughly twice GNP without risk (tested against England, France in 1561 and 1789, and Maria Theresa's Austria); budget:GNP runs 1:10–1:20 in pre-industrial economies (used to bound Venice's and Russia's GNP from their fiscal revenue); patrimony:national income runs roughly 3:1 to 7:1 (Keynes, Kuznets, Gregory King, applied by Alice Hanson Jones to estimate 1774 American colonial incomes); and bullion in circulation runs about 1:10 against patrimony.

Two visible continuities. Having assembled these approximations, Braudel names what he takes to be genuinely durable patterns beneath the noise: GNP's 'regular rise... come hell or high water' (even world wars are, in the long run, 'only interruptions'), and the rise of the state, 'devouring everything,' whose share of national income has grown over the long term from Rome's collapse through medieval reconstruction, the Black Death's collapse, and the state's slow rebuilding from the mid-fifteenth century onward into the modern fiscal state.

Notable passages

The methodological disclaimer ("But I would not wish the reader to misinterpret my intentions... to guess at the potential of retrospective accounting") is the unit's thesis statement: everything that follows is offered as plausible order-of-magnitude reasoning, not verified data, and should be read that way.

The definition of the "three variables" (patrimony, national income, per capita income) and the admission that 'no such thing yet exists as an audit of national wealth' set up the entire chapter's vocabulary and its central limitation — historians are stuck choosing single approximate figures with no way to verify them independently.

The Bairoch wage-multiplier passage is the unit's one attempt at a portable quantitative law, and Braudel immediately stress-tests it against Venice, Orleans, and Vauban's France, modeling the historian's proper skepticism toward an elegant coefficient rather than either accepting or rejecting it outright.

The Portugal/Kuwait passage is the sharpest conceptual move in the unit: it uses a resource-windfall case three centuries apart to show that per capita income and structural development can come apart entirely — income rising while nothing about the productive structure changes.

The national-debt-to-GNP passage ('the debt could reach twice G.N.P. without risk... There was never any danger of going through the ceiling') gives Braudel a load-bearing rule of thumb he then applies across four separate polities (England, France 1561 and 1789, Austria 1748) to judge fiscal health without needing precise figures for any of them.

The closing passage on the rise of the state — collapse with Rome, reconstitution in the eleventh–thirteenth-century 'industrial revolution,' collapse again with the Black Death, then an 'uninterrupted movement' from the mid-fifteenth century into the modern fiscal state — is Braudel's own centuries-spanning cyclical narrative of institutional collapse and reconstruction. It resonates with world-machines's own claim that deep institutional infrastructures rise, dominate, and decay over multi-century arcs, though Braudel's cycle is driven by demographic catastrophe and fiscal capacity rather than a legibility/divergence/liveness sequence, and his 'awe-inspiring recession' of the fourteenth century reads more as a civilizational collapse-and-rebuild than a gramsci-gap between two still-functioning machines — the resemblance is in the shape of the arc, not the mechanism.

More directly, the whole quantifying project of this unit — patrimony, GNP, per capita income, debt ratios, fiscal-pressure percentages — is itself an exercise in legibility-as-machine-core: rendering past economies countable, comparable, and auditable using the same impulse (cadastres, double-entry bookkeeping, national accounts) that the modernity-machine scaled into state capacity. And the unit's opening genealogy of independent 'calculators' (Petty, King, Boisguilbert, Vauban, Bairoch) whose scattered efforts only later cohered into national accounting as 'a science in its own right' is a plausible instance of a history-machine: a paradigm assembling silently from distributed, uncoordinated components before it became visible as a discipline.
