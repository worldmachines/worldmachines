The Far East's World-Economies and Europe's Self-Inflicted Conquest

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel maps the Far East as three (soon four, with Europe's arrival) interlocking world-economies pivoting on India, then shows that Europe's tiny numbers of merchants and soldiers conquered Asian trade and eventually India itself only by borrowing native crews, brokers and sepoy armies, backed by a one-way flow of American silver and by naval gunpower that substituted for territorial control until the era of Dupleix and Clive.

Braudel opens by describing the pre-fifteenth-century Far East as three gigantic, coexisting world-economies — Islam, India, and China — pivoting on India's central position and periodically fusing, via a pendulum of alternating advantage, into something close to a single super-world-economy; he then argues that from the fifteenth century a fourth world-economy, conjured up by the Portuguese, Dutch, English and French, forced its way into this system despite chronically minuscule numbers of settlers, soldiers and merchants. The chapter's puzzle is how so few Europeans achieved so much: the answer, developed under the heading "India's self-inflicted conquest," is that Europeans never acted alone — native crews, local brokers and money-changers, and above all sepoy armies did most of the actual work of commerce and conquest. Braudel then turns to bullion (Asia would sell only for gold and silver, making American treasure Europe's real ticket of admission), to naval power (command of the sea substituted for control of land, until the later territorial turn under Dupleix, Bussy and Clive), to the organization of the India companies as proto-multinational trading-post networks straining against the slowness of communication with Europe, and closes with a methodological aside explaining why, of all the Far Eastern world-economies, he has chosen India as his exemplary case.

Themes

A near-singular Far Eastern world-economy. Braudel's spatial model treats Islam, India and China as three world-economies that, thanks to the monsoon and trade winds, can plausibly be read after the fifteenth century as one intermittent, pendulum-driven whole, with India's merchants (Gujarat, Malabar, Coromandel) holding the central position against Arab, Persian and Chinese rivals. This is Braudel's own version of a world-machines-like claim — a deep, centuries-spanning infrastructure (trade winds, monsoon timing, a hierarchy of ports) organizing life across a vast space with shifting dominant centres (Bantam, Atjeh, Malacca, later Batavia and Manila) — though the fit is partial: Braudel's world-economies are geographically exclusive and economic rather than three machines in permanent civilizational friction over the same ground, and his pendulum is bilateral (East vs. West of India) rather than triangular.

Europe as an outnumbered fourth world-economy. Braudel insists the disproportion between European means and European results was structural, not incidental: 10,000 Portuguese settlers across the whole sixteenth-century empire, 1,200 English in Calcutta, 114 Europeans holding French Mahe in 1777, and — startlingly — only 31,000 English in 1805 sufficient "to rule the entire country" of India. Contemporaries (an American of French origin in 1812, Victor Jacquemont in the 1830s) called English rule in India "artificial, abnormal and exceptional," and Braudel insists artifice here meant intelligence, not fraud.

"Self-inflicted conquest." The chapter's sharpest thesis: European military and commercial success in Asia was substantially performed by Asians. Ships were crewed by Malays, Lascars and Filipino half-breeds; company armies were overwhelmingly native troops, with Dutch Batavia fielding nine or ten thousand Malay auxiliaries and 2,000 Chinese soldiers against 1,000–1,200 Europeans; and it was the recruitment of sepoys — "using Indian troops to conquer India" — that Braudel treats as the decisive, almost paradoxical innovation, whoever first thought of it (Martin, Dupleix, or the English "in imitation"). Commercially, the pattern repeats: Armenians, Banyans, Parsees, Gujaratis and Jews from Mocha besieged every European factor with services no European could do without, to the point that English factories routinely borrowed from Indian bankers (surviving the 1720 South Sea Bubble liquidity crisis on Indian credit) and British rule in Bombay left Parsee and Gujarati merchants undisturbed for decades because the trade could not run without them.

Bullion as the price of admission. Since the Far East showed little appetite for European goods, trade balance required Europe to pay in gold and especially silver — American silver, reaching Asia via the Levant/Persian Gulf, the Cape route, and the Manila galleon. Braudel explicitly reframes this bullion drain, usually read as European weakness, as the reverse: a "stranglehold" of silver over the Far Eastern economies, though he qualifies the claim immediately — European merchants were themselves hostage to the unpredictable arrival of the Cadiz silver fleet, and the 1680–1720 silver scarcity deteriorated England's and Holland's terms of trade in Asia.

Command of the sea, not the land. Before the later territorial conquests of Dupleix, Bussy and Clive, European strategy — articulated in maxims from Almeida, Albuquerque, a Dutch commander at Hirado, a wary Chinese resident of Macao, and Thomas Roe — was deliberately maritime: control the ships and the commerce follows, since holding forts on land without naval superiority is worthless. This traded on India's historically pacific maritime customs (the sole exception being the thirteenth-century Chola navy), letting a handful of European warships blockade ports, levy safe-conduct ransoms, and threaten trade — as in Sir Josiah Child's 1688 war against Aurangzeb — without ever needing superiority of numbers on land.

The India companies as proto-multinationals. Braudel describes trading posts, factories and settlements (ranked in that descending order of importance) as a form of purely commercial colonization: Europeans settled at existing trade nodes, used networks already in place, and left production and haulage to local communities. The eight-months-each-way Atlantic/Cape route (versus a record 72-day overland dash via Alexandria in 1780) forced London and Amsterdam to delegate real authority to local boards, making the companies "a state within or outside the state," wrestling with shareholders, bondholders and fixed capital across an information lag of six months to a year.

Closing method note. Braudel admits the history of Asia's "subterranean" economic life is hard to recover — filtered through company archives and siloed Orientalist specialisms — but credits a rising generation of Far Eastern historians with opening the field, and defends choosing India (not the easiest case, but the pivotal, central one) as his exemplar, while flagging his one disagreement with the Indian historiographical convention of an India "Middle Ages" running until the Raj.

Notable passages

The opening definition of the Far East's three world-economies and their pendulum dynamic ("Three world-economies, one intermittent whole") sets the spatial frame the rest of the chapter argues within. The numbers passage ("The disproportion of numbers") is the empirical anchor for Braudel's central puzzle. The sepoy line — "using Indian troops to conquer India" — and the merchant-collaboration passage ending "indestructible as couch-grass" together carry the "self-inflicted conquest" thesis in its military and commercial forms respectively. The bullion passage reframes silver flows as leverage rather than weakness, with Braudel's own authorial "I regard" making the interpretive move explicit. Finally, the Almeida/Albuquerque maxims on sea power give the chapter's strategic logic in the historical actors' own words, before Braudel notes the later turn to outright territorial conquest.
