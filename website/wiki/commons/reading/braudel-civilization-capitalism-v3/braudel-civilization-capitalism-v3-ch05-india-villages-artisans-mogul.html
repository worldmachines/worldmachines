India's Villages, Artisan Industry, and the Fragile Machinery of Mogul Rule

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
India's village economy — never self-sufficient but bound to the state by money and taxation from village headman to Mogul court — fed a technologically distinctive artisan and textile sector and a de facto national market, while the Mogul Empire itself functioned as what Braudel calls an astonishing machine, rigid yet fragile, that decayed from internal corrosion and an unresolved Hindu-Muslim rift rather than collapsing under foreign conquest.

Braudel opens by demolishing the picture of India as timeless, self-sufficient villages, arguing instead that from well before the Delhi sultanate a money economy penetrated the village base and was harnessed by a tax apparatus running from village headman and khud-kashta elders up through zamindars and jagir-holding nobles to the Mogul court; he then follows the same logic outward into a large, technologically distinctive artisan and textile sector, into what he tentatively calls India's "national market," and finally into the Mogul Empire's institutional character — its religious accommodation under Akbar, its Persian-derived elite culture, its army-centred state — and its long, contested eighteenth-century decline, which he attributes chiefly to internal corrosion (a condottieri-like military aristocracy, an unresolved Hindu-Muslim rift) rather than to foreign conquest or straightforward economic collapse.

Themes

The village was a fiction of self-sufficiency. Braudel insists on the plural "villages," not "the village": rather than an intangible, unchanging unit, the normal Indian village was "open to the outside world, subject to various authorities and to the markets which watched it closely, emptied it of its surpluses and forced upon it the convenience and the dangers of a money economy" (c1). He traces the money economy back centuries before the Mogul conquest of 1526 — even before the Delhi sultanate's fourteenth-century administrative machine — citing L. C. Jain's claim that India had bankers six centuries before Christ (c1).

Taxation as a drive-belt running from peasant to summit. The chapter's real subject, as Braudel frames it, is "the vitality drawn up from the base to animate and nourish the great political and social corpus" (c1) — a vertical, extractive circulation he compares explicitly to contemporary Russia. Absentee jagir-holding nobles, hereditary zamindar tax-collectors, and village khud-kashta elders (a privileged, tax-favored minority collectively liable for the village's dues) form the transmission chain that converts harvests into the cash the state and aristocracy both prefer; money itself, he writes, was "the indispensable mechanism which made the whole … great machine function, from its peasant base to the summit of society and the business world" (c2). Yet peasant productivity, he argues (via Irfan Habib), was likely higher per capita around 1700 than 1900, thanks to underused arable land, a favorable climate, two annual harvests, and lucrative cash crops like indigo — a system that nonetheless left "visible poverty among the rural masses" as a predictable structural feature.

Whether caste blocked an Indian industrial revolution. Braudel stages the historiographical debate over India's "ancestral industry" on the eve of British conquest: the Weberian view holds that caste froze artisans into fixed trades and stifled technological initiative, but Braudel sides with Irfan Habib's rebuttal — unskilled labor pools fed new skilled trades as needed (diamond miners returning to peasant work when mines closed; a caste of Maharashtra tailors partly converting to indigo-dyeing) (c3), and workers migrated toward demand much as in Europe. The more serious obstacle, in his account, was not caste but a cost calculus: wooden, rudimentary tools coexisted with extraordinary manual specialization ("a job that one man would do in Holland here passes through four men's hands before it is finished," per the Dutchman Pelsaert) (c4), because cheap, abundant labor made expensive European-style iron machinery uneconomical — not, per Habib, technically necessary. The same population that used wooden gears for irrigation also produced prized crucible steel, competitive artillery by Aurangzeb's reign, and — "the wonder of wonders" — Surat- and Bengal-built teak ships that could outlast European vessels three- or fourfold.

A national market held together by money, not by a single production network. Braudel hedges the term but uses it anyway: India, "for all its size, had a degree of coherence in which the money economy was an important or rather essential element," sustaining caravans (kafilas), river transport, and complementary regional exchange in food and textiles across enormous distances (c5). Unlike Florentine or European textile production, India's cotton and silk industries were organized in separate, uncombined compartments — spinning, weaving, bleaching, printing — bound together not by vertically integrated workshops (karkhanas were the exception, serving nobles and the emperor) but by merchant middlemen who advanced cash to weavers against future delivery. Surat, Bengal ("another but bigger Egypt"), and the Gujarat "industrial bloc" anchored this system, whose cotton output Braudel judges the world's foremost until the English industrial revolution.

The Mogul Empire as an "astonishing machine, rigid yet fragile." Akbar's achievement was getting Hindu and Muslim communities to cohabit; the Moguls' second was overlaying a Persian-derived elite civilization (language, arts, administration) onto an Islamic institutional model administered through provincial sarkars and district parganas. But the empire's core was its army — some 200,000 horsemen and 40,000 gunners under omerah/mansabdar nobles whom Braudel likens to Renaissance condottieri, prolonging wars for pay rather than risking decisive, career-jeopardizing victories. Holding this together required, in Braudel's own words, "an energetic and effective ruler" and a country willing "to refrain from disturbing the social, political, economic and religious order which governed it" (c6) — conditions that could not hold indefinitely. This resonates, with real limits, with world-machines: an infrastructure fusing institutions, extraction, and ideology that needs continuous maintenance and dies of internal fatigue as much as external shock — though Braudel's "machine" is one dynasty's fiscal-military apparatus over roughly two centuries, not a civilizational, millennium-scale order, and he offers no Dawn/Day/Dusk periodization of his own.

Decline as internal decay, contested in its dating and its economic meaning. Braudel surveys competing dates for the "beginning" of Mogul decline (1707, Aurangzeb's death; 1739, the Persian sack of Delhi; 1757, Plassey; 1761, Panipat) and sides with those who see the empire dying "from within" rather than being conquered from outside. His mechanism is corrosion: jagirs turning into hereditary property against the rules, princes and omerahs trading on the side, an over-recruited and pleasure-softened army, and — beneath the surface — an unresolved Hindu-Muslim rift surfacing in the Mahrattas' revolt under Shivaji and in Sikh resistance. He resists reducing this to Aurangzeb's personal post-1680 policies, and he resists the easy equation of political decline with economic collapse, drawing a parallel to the French Wars of Religion (fought amid economic health) and suggesting the late eighteenth century may have ridden a wider Canton-to-Red-Sea commercial revival even as Mogul authority crumbled. The omerahs' twice-daily ritual visits to reassure themselves the emperor still lived — continuing even as the substance of imperial authority hollowed out — loosely echoes the decay-sequence's move from functional to performative and allegorical, though Braudel is describing one fragile regime's court theater over decades, not a centuries-long infrastructural bleed.

Notable passages

  • c1 — "villages... thousands and thousands of them." The opening reframing (plural, not singular) that licenses everything else: a village economy already interlaced with money and outside authority, its surplus "vitality" flowing upward to sustain the whole political order — and, per Jain, a money economy with roots six centuries before Christ, well before the Delhi sultanate's administrative machine of the fourteenth century.
  • c2 — "the jagir was granted him for a fairly short term." The short-tenure, cash-preferring jagir system and the "great machine" line make explicit that coin, not kind, was the cornerstone of the system — the mechanism converting dispersed peasant surplus into a fungible resource for court and aristocracy alike.
  • c3 — Habib's rebuttal of caste rigidity. The clearest statement of the historiographical stakes: caste did not freeze the workforce, since unskilled reserves and long-run economic pressure could and did reassign whole caste sections to new trades.
  • c4 — Pelsaert on Indian craftsmanship. The tools/skill paradox in one line — rudimentary wooden implements paired with extreme division of labor — that Braudel uses to relocate the real constraint on Indian industrialization from culture (caste) to relative factor prices (cheap labor vs. costly iron machinery).
  • c5 — "a national market." Braudel's own hedged coinage for Indian internal coherence, tied immediately to Surat, Bengal, and Gujarat as its organizing poles — the passage that most directly engages the world-economy/national-market distinction central to this volume.
  • c6 — "an astonishing machine, rigid yet fragile." The single line that best captures Braudel's diagnosis of Mogul power: an apparatus requiring constant energetic maintenance, whose failure mode is internal fatigue rather than sudden external defeat.
