By Way of Conclusion: Past and Present

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel closes Civilization and Capitalism by testing his historical model against the twentieth century, arguing that capitalism is a long-term structure of choice-making elites in symbiosis with the state, sharply distinct from the market economy and the informal lower economy beneath it.

Braudel closes The Perspective of the World by refusing the "classic conclusion" that merely rehearses a book's arguments; instead he "launches" the historical model of capitalism built across three volumes onto the waters of the twentieth century, treating the present as a live test of whether that model is still "seaworthy," and arriving at what he calls the book's essential message: capitalism, understood as a long-term structure of choice-making elites bound in symbiosis with the state, must be sharply distinguished from the market economy it sits atop.

Themes

History as a test against the present, not a rehearsal of the past. Braudel explicitly declines to summarize the preceding volumes ("the classic conclusion... is inappropriate in a work of history, which can never claim to be complete"). Instead he treats the present day as the proving ground for the conceptual apparatus he built for the pre-industrial period, asking whether it is "not the secret aim and underlying motive of history to seek to explain the present," and framing history, now in dialogue with the social sciences, as "a measure of the present as well as of the past." He is careful to disclaim strong predictive or explanatory closure ("I make no claim to explain the present in the light of history"), a historiographical humility that sits at an angle to — even while it anticipates the ambition of — the kind of forecasting world-machines pursues: where WMP wants dated, falsifiable claims, Braudel wants only to see whether his categories still fit.

Capitalism as a long-term structure, not a sequence of stages. Against any story of mercantile capitalism progressing in phases toward industrial and then finance capitalism, Braudel insists the "whole panoply of forms of capitalism — commercial, industrial, banking" was already deployed in thirteenth-century Florence, in seventeenth-century Amsterdam, and in London before the eighteenth century. Merchants never specialized; capital moved fluidly between trade, banking, speculation, and production long before the industrial revolution, and continued to do so after it (the Wendel family as steelmasters, bankers, and arms suppliers all at once). This is his longue durée argument in miniature: capitalism persists through "structuration, destructuration and restructuration" rather than decisive ruptures, "essentially true to itself" even across the industrial revolution.

Capitalism's defining privilege is the freedom to choose. Rather than a sector or a technology, capitalism for Braudel is a social position: the "ability to choose," resting on scale, credit, communications networks, and the informal "unwritten rules and personal contacts" of a "powerful minority." It keeps its own preserves — top-level real estate, stock-exchange speculation, large-scale industry, flag-of-convenience shipping — while leaving most of the market economy (small business, agriculture, modest enterprises) to operate under its own steam, "just as in the past capitalism did not control the whole of the market economy." Its vitality lies precisely in the ability to "change horses in mid-stream," summed up in the Amsterdam merchant Henry Hope's remark about trade, which Braudel extends to capitalism itself: "It is often ill but it never dies."

Symbiosis, not opposition, with the state. Braudel rejects both the "all-powerful state" narrative and its mirror image (capital "crushing the freedom of the individual"): state and capital "coexist very comfortably," with the state absorbing the costliest, least remunerative burdens — infrastructure, education, welfare, social security — while capital captures subsidies, contracts, and access to foreign markets. This collusion "dates back to the beginning of the modern period," regular enough that state crises have historically doubled as capitalism's crises: Braudel cites the faltering of the state of Castile and of the French monarchy in the 1550s. Capitalism, in other words, is inseparable from the territorial state whose fortunes it shares — a domestic instance of the city-centred-economy-versus-national-market-and-territorial-state tension running through the volume.

Culture as prop, occasionally as challenge. Culture "in the end contribute[s] the best of itself to propping up the existing order," supplying capitalism's ideological defenses (inequality as natural, the self-made man, the "hidden hand" of the market). Braudel treats 1968 as the exception that tests the rule: a genuine cultural revolution that "broke habits and taboos" and left capitalism, under attack "from new groups opposed to power in every form," in a less secure position — yet one absorbed by "a long-suffering society whose patience gives it prodigious powers of resistance." The shape of this — an old legitimacy visibly cracking, no new order decisively installed, the crisis eventually metabolized rather than resolved — rhymes with gramsci-gap, though on a decade-long cultural timescale rather than the centuries-long civilizational transition the term usually names.

Crises concentrate rather than kill capitalism. Answering Boris Porchnev's charge that "bourgeois" historians describe capitalism's rise but never its end, Braudel argues collapse would require both a violent external shock and a credible alternative, neither of which he sees in the 1970s crisis (worse than 1929, but not the "final crisis of capitalism"). Crises instead drive centralization and concentration — the automobile industry's consolidation into a handful of makes is his example — leaving survivors stronger. This self-renewing, non-terminal resilience is a partial but instructive counter-rhyme to world-machines's Dawn-Day-Dusk arc: where a world machine is expected to actually decline and cede ground to a successor, Braudel's capitalism explicitly resists any fixed decay curve, reorganizing its "means of domination" indefinitely rather than dying on schedule.

Capitalism versus the market economy — the book's essential message. Braudel restates, and extends into the present, the three-storey model of the whole volume: monopoly capitalism at the top, a genuinely competitive "market economy" of small and medium firms beneath it (Prato's textile workshops against the ruins of small-business New York), and, below both, an informal "lower floor" — fraud, barter, moonlighting, housework — that some economists put at 30 to 40% of activity in industrialized countries. The relation between the top two tiers is a "dialectic," not simple domination: big firms need small ones for sub-contracting, flexibility, and invention (Lenin's remark that capitalism "begins in the village market-place" is read by Braudel as an unwitting tribute to the creative powers of the market's lower storey). The unit closes on the political stakes of the distinction — whether a market economy's freedoms can be preserved without surrendering to either monopoly capital or a monopoly state, citing the Prague Spring's failed synthesis of "socialism at the top, freedom at the base" — and on a closing image that is pure world-economy logic: a country "at the centre of a world-economy can hardly be expected to give up its privileges at international level," binding this domestic three-tier model back to the book's dominant-centre/dominated-zone hierarchy.

Notable passages

  • The opening refusal of the "classic conclusion" and the decision to "throw open the doors and windows" and test the model against the present sets the whole unit's method.
  • The four-part roadmap paragraph — ending "capitalism as distinct from the market economy, which is for me the essential message of this long quest" — is the unit's table of contents in miniature.
  • The claim that capitalism's forms were already "deployed" from Florence to Amsterdam to London rejects any staged-progression account of capitalist development.
  • "The chief privilege of capitalism... remains the ability to choose" is Braudel's most compact definition of capitalism as a social position rather than a sector.
  • The Castile/French-monarchy passage ties capitalism's fate to the state's across centuries, exemplifying the book's state-and-capital symbiosis argument.
  • The closing paragraph on the "country at the centre of a world-economy" restates the volume's core-and-periphery logic as the reason the political problem of market economy versus monopoly resists easy resolution.
