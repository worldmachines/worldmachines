Russia as a World-Economy Apart: State, Serfdom, and the Market

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel argues that Muscovite Russia formed its own self-contained world-economy, centered on Moscow and oriented for most of its history as much toward Turkey, Central Asia, and China as toward Europe, held together by an omnipotent Tsarist state that engineered serfdom from above even as a vigorous peasant market economy grew up within and against it.

Braudel turns from western Europe to Muscovite Russia to argue that it constituted a world-economy in its own right rather than a mere periphery of Europe's: a state-built, serfdom-based, market-threaded system centered on Moscow, oriented for most of its history as much toward Turkey, Central Asia, and China as toward the Baltic, and shaped above all by the sheer immensity of its own distances.

Themes

A world-economy apart, with its own core and periphery. Braudel endorses Wallerstein's placement of Muscovy "outside 'European Europe'" before Peter the Great, grouping it with the Ottoman lands as zones the western world-economy could pressure but not absorb. But the unit's real claim goes further: Russia is a world-economy in itself, with Moscow as its dominant center, the Volga axis as its "capitalist backbone" (explicitly likened to the Venice-Bruges axis of fourteenth-century Europe), a Dunkirk-to-Marseille analogy mapping Archangel, St Petersburg, Nizhniy Novgorod and Astrakhan onto France, and Siberia and the Black Sea steppe as peripheries. Braudel is applying his own three-rule model of the world-economy (dominant center, hierarchy of zones, slowly shifting boundaries) reflexively to a non-European case.

East, not West, was Russia's main trading partner. Through the sixteenth century, trade with Turkey, Central Asia, Iran and China outweighed and ran at a healthier balance than trade with Europe, which took only raw materials in exchange for currency and manufactures. Braudel reframes Russia's eastward orientation not as backwardness but as what let it avoid Poland's fate, where European demand hollowed out both towns and state authority.

The state, not the nobility, built serfdom. Unlike the Polish/Hungarian/Bohemian "second serfdom," engineered by nobles standing between peasant and market, Russian serfdom was constructed top-down by the Tsar to secure a loyal service nobility (the pomechtchiki), through a sequence of legal closures: the 1497 code's St George's Day escape valve, Ivan IV's 1580 "temporary" suspension of peasant movement, and the 1649 code's abolition of any statute of limitations on returning fugitives. The same state ran direct monopolies (salt, spirits, furs, the triennial Peking caravan) through the gosti, a small bonded merchant class "answerable for all these duties with their lives and personal property."

Serfdom did not preclude a market economy - it selected for a particular kind of one. Because most Russian serfs owed obrok (cash and kind dues) rather than barshchina (labor dues, capped even at its worst at three days a week), they needed market access to raise cash, which financed both landlords' direct farming and the state's tax revenue. This produces the section's central paradox: alongside the serf's legal degradation to "a thing" - saleable, deportable, conscriptable - ran vigorous serf entrepreneurship: kustari craft production, whole manufactory towns like Ivanovo, and wealthy serf merchants like Gratchev, whose fortunes could dwarf their own masters' without buying them clean freedom.

Towns stayed villages; fairs, not cities, carried long-distance trade. Russia's national market matured early, but its urban form did not - contemporaries called it "one big village," its shops far more numerous but far tinier than Amsterdam's, arranged in bazaar-like radj rows. Fairs, not the roughly 273 towns of 1720, did the work western cities' merchant infrastructure did elsewhere; the absence of a modern credit system left usury (up to 13 percent a month) standing in for capital markets.

Distance is the structural key. The unit closes by naming distance itself as what gave the Russian world-economy its shape, its protection against invasion, and its internal division of labor between zones - the clearest statement of Braudel's zonal-hierarchy model applied to this case.

Notable passages

The opening paragraph stakes out Russia's position "outside 'European Europe'" via Wallerstein, paired with the Ottoman lands as another zone of near-autonomy - the framing the rest of the unit complicates by showing Russia is not simply outside a world-economy but constitutes one. The "great rock in the middle of the sea" passage is the unit's sharpest image of state omnipotence, explicitly noting that Russian towns, unlike western ones, conferred no freedom on their inhabitants. The 1580/1649 passage traces serfdom's legal closure to its endpoint - no interval, however long, protected a fugitive from forced return. The Gratchev passage is the unit's best concrete case of serf capitalism straining against its own legal cage. The Moscow/France analogy is Braudel's most explicit statement of Russia's internal core-periphery structure. The closing distance passage names the single variable - sheer geographic scale - that Braudel treats as doing the most explanatory work in the whole section.

World Machines resonances

Braudel's insistence that Muscovy is a world-economy "in itself," coexisting alongside rather than absorbed into the European and Ottoman zones, rhymes with WMP's claim that machines always coexist in friction - though the rhyme is partial: Braudel's plurality is geographically parallel and roughly contemporaneous, not a Dawn/Day/Dusk succession within one lineage. The Tsarist law-code sequence (1497 -> 1580 -> 1649) and the state's direct commodity monopolies read as an early, agrarian-fiscal legibility-as-machine-core project - the state substituting itself for intermediate, noble legibilizing structures - though it fixes bodies to land by decree rather than by Scott-style mapping and measurement technology. Peasant flight to the frontier, black-market fur and vodka trade, and above all Gratchev concealing his fortune "under the names of merchants acting for him" read as a conatus straining against a capture regime that WMP's capture-resistance names, though the resistance here is economic concealment inside the system (buying exit at an extortionate price) rather than a Liveness-style escape beyond its reach. Most strikingly, the closing claim that distance itself was "protecting [Russia] against foreign invasion" is a strong partial fit for thicket-terroir - a non-portable local condition doing real defensive work - though here it is an accident of geography, not a curated, portal-bordered space defending a chosen interior culture.
