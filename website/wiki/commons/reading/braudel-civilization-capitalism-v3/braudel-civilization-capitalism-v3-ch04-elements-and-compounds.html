The National Market as a Hierarchy of Scales

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel builds the national market from a nested hierarchy of economic scales—isolate, market town, pays, province—and argues its emergence was driven not by abolishing internal customs barriers or achieving price convergence, but by the slow political and economic construction of a Territorialwirtschaft able to rival the leaner, primary-sector-free Stadtwirtschaft of city-states like Venice, Genoa, and Amsterdam.

This unit runs from the opening of Chapter 4 — Braudel's inquiry into the "classical notion of the national market" — through his account of the nested scales that compose it (isolate, market town, pays, province), his statistical case against the standard story that abolishing internal customs barriers produced national markets, his rejection of price-convergence as a test for "national market" status, and his central structural distinction, borrowed from Karl Bücher, between the city-centred economy (Stadtwirtschaft) and the territorial economy (Territorialwirtschaft) — the frame he uses to explain why city-states achieved commercial dominance early while territorial nation-states took far longer to build genuine national markets, and then eclipsed them.

Themes

National markets were built, not born. Braudel insists the coherence of a national market — faster circulation, rising production, expanding demand — looks in the abstract like a natural overspill of the market economy, but regional economies in practice stayed regional; welding them into a national market "had nothing spontaneous about it," requiring both political ambition and the capitalist pressure generated by foreign and long-distance trade. He proposes national markets were most likely to form at or near the centre of a world-economy, correlated with the differential geography of the international division of labour.

Nested scales of economic life. Below the national market sits a hierarchy of units: the isolate (the minimum self-reproducing rural settlement of four or five hundred people), the market town and its dependent villages, the pays (a culturally coherent but administratively fuzzy region — Braudel estimates roughly ten pays to a province, ten cantons to a pays), and finally the province, which he calls "a national economy, indeed a world-economy, in miniature": the same uneven, core-and-periphery structure repeats at every scale.

Provinces as proto-national-economies. Provincial capitals (Dijon for Burgundy, Bordeaux for Aquitaine; divided pairs like Rouen/Caen for Normandy or Toulouse/Montpellier for Languedoc) play at their scale the organizing role Amsterdam or London play at the world scale. Unresolved rivalry between two provincial centres is, for Braudel, a symptom of poor regional integration — "a fir tree with a divided trunk is unlikely to thrive."

Internal customs barriers were a red herring. Against the traditional explanation that removing tolls created national markets, Braudel marshals hard numbers — Carrière's calculation that Rhône tolls took barely 0.35% of traffic value — to argue that toll abolition (early in England, only in the 1770s–90s on the continent) did little to cause economic unification. Economic growth itself, not administrative tidying, was "the truly decisive factor."

Against a priori tests of "national market." Price convergence fails as a criterion: French grain prices split into three zones (north-east, Mediterranean, Atlantic) that never harmonized, yet France plainly had a national market. Braudel therefore adopts Bücher's structural distinction — Stadtwirtschaft vs. Territorialwirtschaft — not a matter of area but of whether a government can "shape and manoeuvre" the whole economy. Mercantilism, on this reading, is the state's first deliberate attempt to do exactly that: an administrative project to render a territory's economy visible and steerable as a single unit, which is worth naming alongside legibility-as-machine-core — Braudel is describing, in embryo, the same impulse Scott identifies in cadastres and national accounting, though transplanted into the pre-industrial fiscal state rather than run as a centuries-long civilizational infrastructure project.

City-states versus territorial states. City-states (Venice, Genoa, Amsterdam) externalized the "primary sector" almost entirely, importing food and raw materials and specializing in trade and luxury production; territorial states first had to discipline agriculture, build a tax-collecting bureaucracy, and raise peasant purchasing power before a national market could exist at all. This is why Amsterdam led early and why England, once its national market matured, "turned out in the end to be an infinitely superior rival to little Holland." The Venice→Amsterdam→England succession Braudel gestures at here half-echoes world-machines's idea of a dominant infrastructure holding the field before being succeeded — though Braudel's succession is a shift in which economic structure captures capitalist dominance, not the replacement of an era's whole institutional-technological substrate.

Notable passages

National markets and the world-economy's centre (c1) states the chapter's opening geographic claim — that national markets form near a world-economy's centre — which frames everything that follows.

The isolate (c2) is the base unit Braudel builds his entire scale hierarchy upward from: the smallest self-sufficient rural settlement, "understood and read like a book."

The province as a world-economy in miniature (c3) is the unit's clearest statement of scale-invariance: the same core/periphery structure that organizes the world-economy reappears, unchanged in kind, at provincial scale.

Carrière's toll statistics (c4) supply the quantitative evidence Braudel uses to overturn the standard internal-customs explanation for the national market's emergence.

Territorialwirtschaft, Stadtwirtschaft, and mercantilism (c5) is Braudel's own structural definition of the national/territorial economy, and his claim that mercantilism was its first deliberate construction.

The city-state's outsourced primary sector (c6) explains why city-states achieved commercial dominance before territorial states could, by identifying exactly what city-states externalized and territorial states could not.
