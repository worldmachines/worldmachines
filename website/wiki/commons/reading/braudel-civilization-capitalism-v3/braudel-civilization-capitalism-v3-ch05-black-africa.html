Black Africa: Collaborator as Well as Victim?

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel traces Black Africa's incorporation into Islamic and European trade circuits — from the spent periphery of the Indian Ocean coast through the structural reasons Europe never conquered the interior to the triangular Atlantic slave trade and its 1815 abolition — arguing Africa was collaborator as well as victim in a traffic it partly priced and profited from.

Braudel situates continental Black Africa within the world-economy framework built up over the volume: excluding the Islamic Maghreb and, largely, the already-spent Indian-Ocean coast, he asks how the sub-Saharan interior and the Atlantic coast were drawn into European and Islamic commercial circuits, and how far African societies were mere prey versus active, price-setting participants in the slave trade that resulted — tracing the pattern from fifteenth-century coastal contact through the fully developed eighteenth-century triangular trade to its nominal abolition in 1815.

Themes

East Africa is dismissed as an exhausted periphery, not a frontier. Before turning to the Atlantic, Braudel disposes of the Indian Ocean coast in a page: it "belonged to the Indian world-economy for which it had been at once an important route and a peripheral zone" well before 1498, its gold trade (Sofala, Monomotapa) already spent by the eighteenth century, its Portuguese remnants "more barbarians than civilized." This is Braudel's own économie-monde vocabulary doing real work — Africa here is not a self-contained unit but a périphérie of someone else's world-economy, valuable only as a supply route until it stops being even that.

The Atlantic coast resisted conquest for structural, not incidental, reasons. Braudel explicitly benchmarks Africa against the Aztec and Inca empires, where Europeans found "terrain as well prepared" — centralized, "well-ordered societies which were comparatively easy to exploit" once decapitated. Africa offered no such target: coastal polities were weak or fragmented, while the solid states (Congo, Monomotapa) sat inland, shielded by continent-scale distance, dense settlement, iron metallurgy, and disease. Europe's response was structural too — not territorial conquest but a network of coastal forts and shipborne trade with total "freedom of manoeuvre" at sea, mirrored by Islam's camel caravans probing the Sahara's edge from the other direction. Both, Braudel notes pointedly, were "aggressive slave-trading civilizations."

The chapter's spine is the "collaborator as well as victim" thesis. Braudel stacks direct testimony — the king of the Congo denouncing his own nobles' "thieves," Garcia de Resende on Africans who "sell each other," Cavazzi on Congolese who would "sell their own parents" for a coral necklace — against the historical fact that slavery was already "endemic in Africa, part of the structure of everyday life" before Europeans arrived, with its own internal recruitment, uses, and probably its own logic of surplus disposal ("getting rid of a surplus of hungry mouths"). He leans on Curtin's point that the slave trade was a sub-system of West African society as much as of the Atlantic economy, and reads the comparative scale of African slave exports against tiny European emigration streams (Seville, New England) as proof of "a fairly vigorous economy," not passive victimhood alone.

Trade legibility and price agency: Africans were not simply cheated. The Senegambia terms-of-trade study (Curtin) shows African exporters' purchasing power rising roughly tenfold, computed by converting the iron bar — Senegambia's money of account — into a "fictional currency" exchange rate against the pound sterling, exactly like any two convertible currencies. Braudel's point is double-edged: European dismissal of African currencies as "primitive" was self-serving, since those currencies (cloth, salt, livestock, iron bars) functioned perfectly well as stores and standards of value, and once prices are actually reconstructed, Africa comes out extracting increasing benefit from an intensifying trade, not simply losing ground to it.

The triangular trade is described as an integrated commercial machine, not a moral abstraction. Braudel walks through the three-cornered voyage (Europe to Africa to the Americas and back), the standard cargo manifest (textiles, copper, iron bars, guns, spirits — down to the named ship Le Prince de Conty out of Nantes in 1704), and the evolving cast of intermediaries: Portuguese lançados and their mixed-race descendants, then English and French traders pushing upriver themselves, then, as companies withdrew, "the blacks had become the subsidiary masters of trade." Volume figures (900,000 sixteenth-century, 3.75 million seventeenth, 7–8 million eighteenth, 4 million nineteenth-century slaves despite 1815 abolition) frame the trade's scale against "the modest means employed and the primitive nature of the transport of the time."

Abolition (1815) did not end the trade; it relocated it and inverted the ethical charge. After the English-driven Congress of Vienna ban, Brazil and above all Cuba became new "reception stations," with the Portuguese and Spanish undercutting collapsed English prices — prompting an indignant English traveller to accuse them of profiting from a trade "voluntarily renounced" by Britain, while contemporary Portuguese protested that abolition itself was an "insidious proposal." Braudel closes with a genuinely ambivalent authorial aside, refusing to let the demographic-resilience argument (population "continued to increase in spite of the slave trade") read as exculpation, while also listing what Europe brought to Africa (maize, manioc, tobacco, Christianity) and gesturing at the existence of "black America" as a fact not to be "lightly dismissed."

Notable passages

The périphérie framing opens the unit: East Africa's status as an appendage of the Indian world-economy, already spent by the time Atlantic Africa becomes the real subject, sets up the geographic exclusion (Maghreb, Indian Ocean coast) that lets Braudel narrow to "the heartland of Black Africa."

The Aztec/Inca comparison and the account of the protected interior (Congo, Monomotapa shielded by distance, density, iron, disease) together explain why Europe's African presence stayed coastal and commercial rather than territorial — a structural argument, not an accident of curiosity or incuriosity.

The three African-voice quotations (the king of the Congo, Garcia de Resende, Cavazzi) are the evidentiary core of the "collaborator" half of the thesis — testimony, not Braudel's inference, that African elites and middlemen actively supplied the trade.

The Curtin terms-of-trade passage is the chapter's most technical move: turning the iron bar's exchange rate into calculable price indices lets Braudel argue Senegambia's real position improved under trade pressure, undercutting a purely extractive reading of the relationship. This imposition of price-calculability onto a currency Europeans publicly dismissed as "primitive" is a strong instance of legibility-as-machine-core — commercial rather than state legibility, deployed here to demonstrate African economic agency rather than to erase it, which is the opposite valence legibility usually carries in the WMP frame.

The interior's passive defense-in-depth (landmass, settlement density, metallurgy, disease, rather than any constructed refusal) loosely rhymes with The Thicket's portal-bordered interior space defended by terroir and opacity — though this is geography and epidemiology doing the defending, not a curated or intentional refusal structure, so the resonance is partial at best.

The closing parenthetical — Braudel refusing to let demographic vitality or the Columbian exchange soften the "blame or responsibility attaching to Europe," while still naming the exchange — is the chapter's most exposed authorial moment, worth keeping intact rather than paraphrasing away.
