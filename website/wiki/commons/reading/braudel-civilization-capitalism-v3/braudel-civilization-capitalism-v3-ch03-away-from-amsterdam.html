A change of perspective: away from Amsterdam

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel leaves Amsterdam as control tower to trace, through four case studies (the Baltic, France, England, the East Indies), how a dominant world-economy exploits subordinate economies via credit and trade networks rather than direct rule, then generalizes the pattern into a hierarchy of core, semi-periphery, and a 'true' periphery held only by force.

Braudel pivots from Amsterdam as the seventeenth-century world-economy's control tower to the lower-level economies it dominates, working through four case studies — the Baltic countries (Sweden, Finland, Gdansk), France, England, and the Dutch East Indies — to show how a dominant economy exploits subordinate ones without directly administering them, then closes by generalizing the four cases into a single structural pattern of core, semi-periphery, and periphery.

Themes

  • Exploitation by attraction, not administration. Braudel frames the whole unit around a single mechanism: a dominant economy can "exploit subordinate economies, while not soiling its own hands with the less profitable activities... or even, most of the time, directly supervising the lesser links in the chain of trade." The four case studies are chosen to show the range of solutions this produces, from loose credit relationships to outright colonial force.

  • Sweden: political precocity, economic captivity. Sweden's political unit formed early (eleventh century, centred on Uppsala), but its economy stayed dependent first on Lübeck and the Hansa, then on Amsterdam — Dutch families (the de Geers, the Trips, and others) settled in Sweden, took Swedish nobility, and penetrated even the mining and timber sectors. Sweden's post-1721 pivot westward (founding Göteborg, chartering a Swedish Indies Company) looked like emancipation but still ran, in the end, through Amsterdam's trade circuits — with the English now supplying capital and crews on top.

  • Finland and Gdansk: two peripheries, two peasantries. The Finnish tar trade chain (peasant, tax-hungry state, cash-holding merchant, bailiff as arbiter) routed orders and credit from Amsterdam through Stockholm down to Viborg, yet Finnish and Swedish peasants stayed comparatively free, protected by political representation (a peasant Fourth Estate in the Riksdag). Gdansk's grain monopoly over Poland shows the opposite: its Polish hinterland was "reduced once more to serfdom." Braudel draws the general (and hedged) inference that grain exported at scale tends toward "refeudalization," while mining or forest activity "might predispose to a degree of liberty."

  • France: subjugated despite superior resources. Louis XIV and Colbert's mercantilist policy and a run of treaties (Nijmwegen, Ryswick, Utrecht) all failed to dislodge Dutch middlemen from French trade, because — in Braudel's account — France had no independent merchant class or enough ships of her own; French merchants were, for decades, "front men for the Dutch firms." The Dutch met French retaliation by simply diversifying suppliers (Portuguese and Spanish wine, milder Iberian salt) and by copying French luxury goods domestically.

  • England: the exception that also bent, then reversed. Cromwell's Navigation Act (1651) and its 1660 confirmation begin England's pushback, but quick-witted Dutch traders found loopholes (the 1667 Treaty of Breda's "regarded as Dutch" carve-out), and Dutch trading houses ran branches in London. England remained, in Braudel's phrase, "a link in the Dutch entrepot trade" until around 1700–1730. Only after 1730, as the Dutch commercial system itself weakened and Dutch capital flowed into English state loans and companies, did the balance flip — culminating in England's sudden 1782–3 turn against Holland: "the English card was a joker — a winning card which suddenly lost the game."

  • The East Indies: voyage, factory, territory — and overreach. Following W. H. Moreland, Braudel gives a three-stage model of Dutch penetration of Asia (trading voyage, then factory concession, then territorial occupation), illustrated by the difficult, nearly disastrous Van Caerden voyage of 1599–1601. Only monopolizing the Moluccan spice trade gave the Dutch real leverage in an already dense Asian trade world; but their insistence on controlling everything ended up "killing the goose that laid the golden eggs."

  • Generalizing into core / semi-periphery / periphery. Braudel's closing move reads all four cases as one structure: the European "ring of second-rank powers" stayed attached to the Dutch centre through ordinary attraction, capital, and credit, needing no force; the "true" periphery — Ceylon, Java, Spanish Latin America, English India — could only be held by force and colonialism, a pattern he traces back to Genoa in Caffa and Venice in Cyprus. He even wonders whether China failed to become a magnetic world-economy of its own because it lacked "a semi-periphery sufficiently strong to provide energy for the core" — Braudel's clearest statement here of the world-economy's tiered structure (his own core/semi-periphery/periphery framework, with Wallerstein as interlocutor throughout the volume).

Notable passages

The opening thesis statement (c1) sets the mechanism the rest of the unit tests case by case. Sweden's arc (c2) shows apparent diversification (Göteborg, the Indies Company) collapsing back into the same Amsterdam-centred dependency — Braudel's own gloss, "the moral of the story is that it is hard to shake off the heavy hand of an international economy," reads almost as a rule of the world-economy form itself. The Gdansk/Poland passage (c3) is where Braudel pairs an economic observation (grain export correlating with re-serfdom) with an explicit structural contrast to free Swedish/Finnish peasants, tying local social order to a zone's position in the world-economy. France's failure (c4) is presented as structural rather than a matter of will — no merchant class, no ships, no independent credit machinery. England's reversal (c5) is the unit's dramatic hinge: a client of Amsterdam's network becomes its usurper almost overnight, which resonates loosely with world-machines's picture of dominant infrastructures being suddenly supplanted — though Braudel's succession here is a single linear hand-off between one hegemon and the next, not the three-machines-always-in-friction model WMP proposes; still, the abruptness of the 1782–3 reversal after decades of embedded Dutch presence in London is the kind of hinge-moment a Dawn/Dusk framing would want to explain. Finally, the East Indies overreach (c6) is Braudel's warning note inside an otherwise triumphalist Dutch story — dominance sustained by monopoly can still poison its own source.
