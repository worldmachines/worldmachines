India's Decline, the Far East's Super-World-Economy, and Whether Any Conclusion Is Possible

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel weighs internal causes (low wages, caste-embedded merchant capitalism) against external causes (deliberate British deindustrialization) for India's nineteenth-century decline, traces the Far East's own super-world-economy through its succession of dominant entrepôts — Malacca, then Batavia, then Calcutta — and closes the chapter by testing Paul Bairoch's GNP-per-capita estimates, which show near-parity between Europe and the rest of the world before 1800 and a widening gap only after the machine revolution.

This unit closes Braudel's survey of the non-European world. It opens by litigating why India fell behind in the nineteenth century — weighing an internal explanation (structurally low wages, a caste-embedded merchant capitalism subordinate to despotic extraction) against an external one (British policy, culminating in deliberate deindustrialization) — then widens the frame to the Far East's own "super-world-economy," centred from about 1400 on the Malacca Strait, and traces its succession of dominant entrepôts: Malacca under Islam and then Portugal, Batavia under the Dutch V.O.C., Calcutta under the English East India Company. It ends with Braudel stepping back to admit the chapter's incompleteness (China, Japan, Safavid Persia all shortchanged) and testing Paul Bairoch's GNP-per-capita estimates, which suggest Europe and the rest of the world were near economic parity as late as 1800 and that the divergence is a post-1800 artifact of the machine revolution.

Themes

Indian capitalism was real but embedded, not sovereign. Braudel rejects the idea that "the peculiar form of capitalism in India" explains its decline. A genuine business hierarchy — merchants, bankers, brokers, shipowners — existed and functioned at the Mogul Empire's "crucial bottlenecks." Lacking the landed dynasties that anchored Western capital, India instead used caste solidarity to stabilize mercantile and banking accumulation across generations (the Jagat Sheths, the Banyan and Parsee business families). But this capitalism operated under a despotic canopy: wealthy merchants "lived in perpetual fear of torture and dispossession," and caste solidarity functioned as the infrastructure that let mercantile striving persist despite that exposure — a pattern that resonates with Conatus (accumulation as a striving that persists under pressure) and with capture-resistance, though the capture here is arbitrary despotic extraction rather than the statistical/bureaucratic averaging those WMP concepts usually name.

Two rival explanations for decline, one dismissed. Internally, Braudel proposes low wages as a structural feature rather than a moral failing: they were the necessary counterpart of a centuries-old inflow of precious metals dating to the Roman Empire, which in turn explains why Indian textiles undercut European ones on price. Externally, and more decisively for Braudel, India was made "an instrument" for British access to the wider Asiatic super-world-economy, its internal balances "distorted and strained," until it was "'deindustrialized', reduced to the role of a major producer of raw materials." He explicitly refuses to blame Indian capitalism itself: eighteenth-century India had a productive agriculture, efficient industry (steel superior to England's until 1810), and a functioning market economy — it simply did not dominate the trading zone it depended on.

A Far Eastern super-world-economy, structured but undefended. Independent of Europe's own world-economy, the Far East had long organized itself around India's earlier (Hindu, then Islamic) cultural and commercial export and China's more reticent, tribute-oriented presence. Braudel calls this zone "structured enough to be penetrated with relative ease, but not sufficiently structured to defend itself" — an invitation to invasion the Europeans exploited but did not originate, since "they were only following in the footsteps of other invaders."

Succession of dominant centres, regional scale. Malacca (from 1403/1409) rose on pure geography — a strait chokepoint sitting at the intersection of the monsoon and trade-wind systems, with a band of equatorial calm that lingered over it — combined with Chinese protection and the Islamic conversion of coastal Java. Its fall to Albuquerque in 1511 did not end the region's hierarchy; Batavia (founded 1619) inherited its role for over a century, only to lose primacy to Calcutta by around 1760. This is the same "succession of dominant centres" logic Braudel applies to Europe's own world-economy (Venice, Antwerp, Amsterdam, London) playing out on a Far Eastern stage — a pattern that faintly echoes world-machines's claim that dominance passes between infrastructural regimes rather than being permanent, though these are century-scale commercial entrepôts, not millennium-scale civilizational machines, so the rhyme is one of structure, not of timescale.

Legible order lost to illegible proliferation. Batavia was built as an engineered, legible town — canals, grid-plan streets, walls, twenty-two towers, everything "neat, clean and orderly." Calcutta, by contrast, grew as an unplanned, chaotic boomtown that a horrified French visitor blamed on "the famous British freedom, as if freedom were incompatible with order and symmetry." Yet it was disorderly Calcutta, backed by Plassey's political conquest, that eclipsed orderly Batavia. This is a vivid, almost literal instance of the legibility-as-machine-core versus proliferation tension: the planned, surveillable settlement did not out-compete the illegible one, because political and military conquest (and access to capital) mattered more than urban legibility — a partial mismatch worth flagging, since Scott's legibility argument is about state control capacity, while here legibility loses to raw commercial-military force.

The machine revolution as domination, not just technology. Braudel's closing argument, anchored in Paul Bairoch's GNP-per-capita estimates (in 1960 dollars), is that Europe and the "Third World" were roughly at parity around 1800 (~$213 vs. ~$200), and that the gap opens only afterward. He is explicit that mechanization "was not merely an instrument of development in itself. It was a weapon of domination and destruction of foreign competition." This reads as a concrete instance of modernity-machine's legibility-driven scaling of control moving into its dominant ("Day") phase through a specific technology — industrial mechanization — that lets one machine out-compete and then structurally subordinate rival economies, though Braudel's frame is a two-bloc domination story (Europe vs. non-Europe) rather than the three-machines-in-friction picture WMP uses.

Notable passages

The Jagat Sheths and Banyan/Parsee material (c1) shows Braudel's rebuttal to "India lacked capitalism": caste, not landed dynasty, was the accumulation mechanism. The low-wages passage (c2) gives his structural account of India's wage floor as tied to the ancient bullion inflow. The deindustrialization passage (c3) states the external half of his two-part explanation. The Malacca geography passage (c4) is Braudel at his most geographically deterministic, cataloguing the exact confluence of strait, calm-latitude, and monsoon that made the site inevitable. The Calcutta passage (c5) is the chapter's most quotable irony: legible Batavia lost to illegible Calcutta. The closing passage (c6) states the Bairoch-backed thesis that the "ancient parity" between Europe and the rest of the world collapsed only after 1800 under a "multisecular distortion" set off by the machine revolution.
