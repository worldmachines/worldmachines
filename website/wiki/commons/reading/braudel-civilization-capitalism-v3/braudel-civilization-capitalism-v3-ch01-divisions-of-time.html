The World-Economy and Divisions of Time

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel classifies the layered time-scales of economic conjuncture, from seasonal shocks through named cycles (Kitchin, Juglar, Labrousse, Kuznets, Kondratieff) up to the barely-visible secular trend, then uses four secular peaks (1350, 1650, 1817, 1973-4) to narrate the world-economy's shifting dominant centres from Venice to Amsterdam to England while asking what these long cycles cost, or spared, ordinary people.

Opening the analytical turn of Volume III, Braudel argues that the "historical monsters" he calls world-economies can only be located in time once the layered rhythms of economic conjuncture are classified — from fleeting seasonal shocks up through named cycles (Kitchin, Juglar, Labrousse, Kuznets, Kondratieff) to the almost invisible secular trend — and then uses four secular turning-points (1350, 1650, 1817, 1973-4) to narrate the succession of dominant centres from Venice through Amsterdam to England, before asking what these long cycles cost, or spared, the mass of ordinary people.

Themes

A taxonomy of economic time. Braudel inventories the cycles historians and economists had named by the 1970s — the Kitchin (three or four years), the Juglar or intra-decade cycle (six to eight years, "that bane of the ancien regime"), the Labrousse or intercycle (ten or twelve years or more, a half-Juglar plus a stalled whole Juglar), the Kuznets or hypercycle (a double Juglar, about twenty years), and the Kondratieff (a half-century or more) — all "synchronic," coexisting and overlapping to intensify or diminish the general trend. Above all of these sits the secular trend, "the longest cycle of all," which he says has been "very little studied" and to which the chapter devotes its second half.

Price coherence as proof of a world-economy. Braudel treats the fact that grain and other prices rose and fell in near-unison across huge distances — Muscovy, Ottoman lands, Spanish America, even Macao and India with a twenty-year lag — as "the most convincing evidence of the coherence of a world-economy penetrated by monetary exchange." He floats Antwerp (against Chaunu's Seville-Vera Cruz) as the likely source of these "conjunctural vibrations," while conceding "perhaps the reality is too complicated to admit of any single centre."

The world-economy as a manufacturing "vibrating surface." Extending a physics metaphor (violin strings, sounding-boards, marching soldiers who must break step on a bridge), Braudel proposes that the world-economy does not merely transmit price shocks passively but "manufactures" the conjuncture itself — a structure in its own right, comparable to an arterial system distributing blood through a living organism.

Four secular peaks narrate the succession of dominant centres. Taking 1350, 1650, 1817 and 1973-4 as "vantage points," Braudel reads off the shift of gravity from a Mediterranean system centred on Venice (1350, amid the Black Death and the closing of the Mongol silk route), to Amsterdam's triumph as the Atlantic system also falters (1650), to England's ascendancy after Napoleonic-era shocks and the crisis of Mexican silver (1817), to an open question of whether 1973-4 marks a comparable civilizational downturn or merely a conjunctural crisis.

Destructuration: one system dies while another is born amid hesitation. Braudel's compact definition of what a secular crisis actually is — a coherent world system completing its decline while another "is being born amid much hesitation and delay" — reads as a structural cousin of WMP's gramsci-gap: an interregnum in which the old order has lost momentum before the new one has fully arrived. The resonance is partial: Braudel's register is economic-structural (price regimes, centres of gravity) rather than the ideological-legitimacy crisis Gramsci and WMP emphasize, but the shape of the interval — old dying, new not yet born, "hesitation and delay" in between — is the same.

Upward and downward trends distribute costs unevenly. In upward secular phases "every wound heals, every breakdown can be repaired" (Hamilton) and multipolarity becomes possible, as when Venice, Antwerp and Genoa all shared "the glory" in the sixteenth century; in downward phases wealth concentrates at a single centre and states turn "touchy and aggressive." But Braudel inverts the expected moral: real wages fell furthest precisely when elite indicators looked healthiest, and rose again — a "golden age" for ordinary people — in the black years after the Black Death, because population pressure eased. He flags the industrial revolution as the one clear break in this pattern, the point where sustained upward growth finally raised living standards instead of squeezing them.

A plea to hold short and long term together. Braudel surveys historians who dismissed the long term (Labrousse: "I have abandoned any attempt at an explanation of long-term movements") against those drawn to it (Kula) or wary of losing lived, event-level history (Morineau, Vilar), and rejects Keynes's "in the long run we shall all be dead" as banal, insisting "we all live in both the long-term and the short-term." The layered, simultaneously-operating cycles of this unit's taxonomy rhyme loosely with world-machines' claim that structures of different tempo and scale always coexist in friction — though Braudel's layers are nested economic rhythms feeding one composite trend, not distinct civilizational machines contesting the same ground. The secular trend's own description — "barely visible in everyday life, but plodding inexorably on," discernible only "measured century by century" — also echoes the history-machine pattern of a paradigm assembling silently until it lurches into visibility, though here the "lurch" is a dated price turning-point rather than Lohse's sudden recognition event.

Notable passages

Braudel's cycle inventory (Kitchin through Kondratieff) supplies vocabulary the rest of the chapter and much of the volume relies on. The price-unison passage is his empirical case that a European world-economy already existed by the sixteenth century. The "vibrating surface" passage is his clearest statement that the world-economy is generative, not merely conductive, of the conjuncture. The four-peaks passage sets up the chapter's narrative method. The destructuration passage is the chapter's most compact definition of what a secular crisis is. The wages-versus-prices passage carries its most surprising empirical claim — that secular decline sometimes helped the many even as it damaged elites — anchored in named research (Hamilton, Phelps Brown and Hopkins) rather than assertion alone.
