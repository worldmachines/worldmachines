The Americas as Europe's self-portrait: frontier, forced labour, and a periphery kept on a leash

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Opening Chapter 5's turn to the non-European world, Braudel casts the Americas as Europe's own self-revealing 'achievement' — a vast, underpopulated continent whose land-labour imbalance produced a succession of Indian, white-indentured, and African slave-labour regimes, all kept working for the metropole through the colonial pact and centralized control even as regional markets and pockets of 'involuntary independence' emerged at its margins.

This unit opens Chapter 5 ("For and Against Europe: the Rest of the World"), where Braudel turns from the Anglo-French core rivalry to survey the non-European world — explicitly flagging that his own account will be told from a Eurocentric perspective even as he asks how much of Europe's rise depended on the rest of the globe — and then moves into the chapter's first case, the Americas: the continent is treated less as Europe's passive "periphery" than as Europe's own self-revealing act, its underpopulated vastness is shown generating both a structural demand for bonded labour and unpoliceable routes of escape from it, "regional or national markets" are traced emerging inside administrative boundaries drawn over empty land, Eric Williams and Marx are invoked to argue that Atlantic slavery was constitutive of European capitalism rather than incidental to it, and the unit closes by insisting that despite America "recreating" Europe's historical stages, the colonial pact and metropolitan centralization kept the continent working for Europe rather than for itself.

Themes

A confessedly Eurocentric survey, held up to its own limits. The chapter opens by naming what "the rest of the world" will mean for its purposes — the Americas, "Black Africa," Muscovy/Russia, Islam, the Far East — and immediately concedes that judging these regions' "success" only makes sense from "a misleadingly privileged position," dramatized by the image of Europe "cut loose to float among the seas and land-masses of Asia" where it "would vanish from sight." Braudel proceeds anyway, framing the chapter's real stakes as whether Europe's industrial revolution was possible "without this constant assistance" drawn from the rest of the world.

America as Europe's own "doing," not merely her possession. Rather than rest on Wallerstein's flat inclusion of America in the sixteenth-century European world-economy, Braudel reaches for Meyerson's idea that "being and doing" are one to argue America was Europe's "doing" — the deed by which Europe most truly revealed her own nature. This reframes conquest as constitutive of European identity rather than incidental to it, though he hedges by noting Lütge and Bechtel's view, which he partly accepts, that the discovery's immediate effect (especially for German history) has been overstated.

Open space as both curse and escape hatch. Vastness cuts two ways: because land was plentiful and cheap while labour was scarce, Braudel treats serfdom and slavery as arising "as if a necessity" imposed by scale itself — yet the same vastness gave the enslaved and the indigenous unlimited places to hide, from lone runaways heading for the mountains and impenetrable forests to the durable maroon republic of Palmares, covering "a region perhaps the size of Portugal" in the Bahian hinterland. This pairing of an extractive regime with the un-mappable terrain that lets its captives slip its grip rhymes with capture-resistance and with The Thicket's picture of a habitat defended by terroir and opacity — though Palmares resists a colonial slave economy rather than the legibility-driven modern state the WMP concept was built to describe, and it is a fugitive settlement under permanent military threat rather than a stable niche.

Markets assembling inside administrative fictions. "Regional or even quasi-national markets" form within Spanish America — around Quito, Charcas, New Galicia's Guadalajara, eighteenth-century Chile — inside viceroyalty and audiencia boundaries originally drawn over half-empty spaces that only later filled with roads, mule-trains, and settlement. Administrative legibility precedes, and outruns, the human geography it claims to govern.

Slavery as capitalism's foundation, not its accident. Braudel invokes Eric Williams's thesis that the essence of mercantilism was slavery, pairing it with Marx's claim that the wage-workers of Europe needed "slavery pure and simple" for their pedestal. He locates responsibility not in individual middlemen — planters, merchants, slave-traders — but in the "almost mechanical logic of a world-economy," and does not flinch from the word genocide for what happened to American Indians and enslaved Africans alike, while noting white settlers did not escape entirely unscathed either. This is Braudel's own core/periphery vocabulary doing real argumentative work, not decoration.

A succession of bonded-labour regimes, driven by economics rather than race. Indian forced labour under the encomienda/repartimiento/mita system collapsed demographically (Central Mexico reduced, he estimates, from some 25 million to one million) before white indentured servitude (engagés) filled the gap, which in turn gave way to African slavery as sugar's fixed-capital demands grew. Braudel insists the sequence had nothing to do with the labourer's colour, and pointedly dismisses Adam Smith's claim that white men could not do manual labour in the tropics, citing German settlers in Jamaica, Italian navvies on the Panama Canal, and white cane-cutters in northern Australia as counter-evidence.

Recreated Europe, but on a short leash. Even as America is made to travel through the long stages of Europe's history — frontier clearing standing in for medieval forest-clearance, pioneer towns for the medieval urban boom — this was never autonomous replication: the "colonial pact" (the exclusif) and deliberate metropolitan centralization kept colonies firmly attached to the mother country. Only societies outside the main trade routes and lacking a single exportable "dominant product" — remote Andean pastoral zones, the Venezuelan llanos, São Paulo's hinterland — achieved a kind of involuntary independence: a periphery-within-the-periphery that reproduces, at smaller scale, the core/periphery hierarchy central to Braudel's world-economy model.

Notable passages

The opening Eurocentrism passage states the chapter's animating, self-undermining question directly, before the analysis even begins. The Meyerson passage makes Braudel's boldest claim about what America means for Europe's own self-understanding. The open-spaces/Palmares passage supplies the frontier's double character as both a slavery-generating scarcity and an escape valve for its victims. The Eric Williams/Marx passage is Braudel's clearest statement of slavery's structural role in European capitalism, not an aberration from it. The genocide/"mechanical logic of a world-economy" passage is his most explicit moral verdict, phrased in his own core-periphery vocabulary. The Adam-Smith-refuting passage closes the labour argument by dismantling climate determinism with concrete counter-examples spanning three centuries and three continents.
