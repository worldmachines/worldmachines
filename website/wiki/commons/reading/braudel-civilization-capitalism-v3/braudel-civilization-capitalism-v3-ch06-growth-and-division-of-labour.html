Beyond the Industrial Revolution: Growth, the Division of Labour, and the Rise of the Industrialist

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Growth long precedes and outlasts the industrial revolution, and its structural counterpart — an intensifying division of labour — reshapes Britain's productive system: dissolving the putting-out economy into the factory, splitting the all-purpose merchant-banker into a new specialist 'industrialist,' sorting the economy into the Fischer-Clark sectors, and reversing the country's economic geography as the industrial north overtakes the agrarian south.

Braudel steps back from the industrial revolution's own vocabulary — industrialism, industrialization, modernization, growth — to argue that "growth" is the broadest and oldest of these categories, running through the whole of history rather than confined to Britain's cotton-and-steam decades; he then traces two of its consequences for eighteenth- and nineteenth-century England: an intensifying division of labour that dissolves the putting-out system into the factory and splits the all-purpose merchant-banker into a specialist "industrialist," and a reshuffling of the national economy into sectors and regions that overturns the country's inherited north-south hierarchy.

Themes

Growth precedes and outlasts the industrial revolution. Braudel surveys the balanced-growth school (Nurske, Young, Hartwell — growth spread evenly across sectors, driven by domestic demand) against the unbalanced-growth school (Innis, Hirschman, Schumpeter, Rostow — growth begins in one leading sector and radiates outward, driven by external markets), and rather than choosing sides proposes recasting the debate as continuous versus discontinuous growth, aligning it with Kuznets's traditional/modern distinction. Traditional growth "hiccupped along in a succession of stop-go movements," with population and output rising together but per capita income falling — "a rise in the national product was always achieved on the backs of those who did the work: this was the law of the ancien regime." Braudel dates Britain's actual shift to continuous growth to 1815, perhaps 1850, or even 1870 — well after the conventional start of the industrial revolution.

Growth potential (longue durée) versus how growth happens (conjoncture). Braudel adopts Kuznets's split between "what makes economic growth possible" and "the way it actually happens" and maps it directly onto his own two timescales: growth potential is a slow, structural transformation of the relations between land, labour, capital, the market, the state and social institutions — a matter of the longue durée, traceable to the thirteenth, seventeenth, or eighteenth century depending on where one looks — while the conjoncture is the immediate, comparatively recent trigger (a technical discovery, an opportunity, "even pure chance") that decides which sector growth erupts through. His example is pointed: had India not been the world's leading cotton producer, Britain's industrial revolution would likely still have happened, just not through cotton. This is Braudel's own historiographical apparatus, developed across the earlier volumes, applied directly to explaining industrialization — and it underwrites his claim that "the industrial revolution was not the source of modern growth" (North and Thomas): the revolution is conjunctural fireworks riding on a structural fuse lit centuries earlier.

The division of labour is growth's shadow, not a minor byproduct. Against Durkheim's claim that the division of labour is "merely a derivative phenomenon... which takes place on the surface of social life... but skin deep," Braudel treats it as growth's most reliable index — and its most disruptive agent, since it "does not merely increase the division of labour, it reshuffles the cards, eliminating old functions and proposing new ones." He extends the idea past manufacturing to the growth of the tertiary/services sector as itself a form of the division of labour.

The putting-out system dissolves slowly, then the factory arrives all at once. The rural, family-based putting-out system — combining "town and country, craft and farming, industrial and family labour" — persisted well into the cotton boom precisely because it gave merchants elastic capacity against fluctuating demand. Mechanized spinning ran decades ahead of handloom weaving, so weaving absorbed ever more (increasingly female and child) labour under brutal conditions before the steam-driven power-loom of the 1820s abruptly ended the trade, throwing "thousands of weavers... on to the streets." Braudel reads the resulting unrest — Luddism, radicalism, trade unionism, Utopian socialism — as the direct social cost of a division of labour that "reshaped both economy and society."

A new social type, the industrialist, breaks off from the all-purpose businessman. Where the same man had traditionally been "merchant, banker, insurer, shipowner, industrialist" at once, the late eighteenth century produces a distinct specialist-entrepreneur who masters production and the market directly, bypassing merchant middlemen (the Fieldens' own American cotton agents; London porter brewers running their own barley factors; Thomas Cubitt replacing sub-contractors with a permanent workforce). Braudel accepts that this is "the beginning of industrial capitalism" but pointedly refuses the stronger claim, made by many historians, that this is when "'true' capitalism begins" — an extension of his three-storey distinction (material life / market economy / capitalism) from the earlier volumes: specialization of function is not the same as capitalism being born.

Classifying the economy: the Fischer-Clark sector scheme. Braudel invokes the primary/secondary/tertiary scheme (Fischer 1930, Colin Clark 1940) and Hartwell's argument that historians underrate the tertiary sector's role — a "services revolution" paralleling the agricultural revolution — while flagging the scheme's fuzziness at the edges (agriculture/industry overlap; whether the huge population of domestic servants belongs in a "superior productivity" tertiary sector at all).

The division of labour rewrites Britain's map. The oldest economic geography of England, split by a Gloucester-to-Boston line into a rich, historically privileged south-east and a poor, high-rainfall north-west periphery (population ratio 1:4, wealth ratio 5:14), is completely inverted by industrialization: the coalfield north (Birmingham, Manchester, Leeds, Sheffield) becomes the new dynamic core while the old core stagnates. Braudel treats this as a rare case of periphery overtaking core — comparable, he suggests, to Scotland's parallel rise, and to modern low-income industrializers like South Korea, Hong Kong and Singapore, where cheap labour itself becomes a competitive advantage.

Resonance with World Machines. Braudel's longue durée/conjoncture split — slow-built structural "growth potential" underneath, fast-triggered conjunctural events on top — rhymes with world-machines's own two-speed structure, where a machine's centuries-long Dawn-built infrastructure is what makes any given decade's surface events possible; the fit is loose since Braudel's timescale is centuries rather than a fixed millennium and his "growth potential" accumulates gradually rather than through the sharper phase-transitions of Dawn/Day/Dusk. The slow coexistence of mechanized spinning with handloom weaving — an old and a new mode of production running side by side for two generations until the power-loom forces a sudden, visible rupture — resonates with history-machine's image of a paradigm assembling "silently and distributedly from independent components until it lurches into visibility," though here the lurch is a technological and economic dislocation rather than a change in how history itself gets written. The Fischer-Clark sector scheme, like Braudel's own asides about the division of political power and the division of commerce, is a sorting apparatus that makes a messy economy countable and comparable, which brushes against legibility-as-machine-core — though it is a retrospective analytic tool rather than a live administrative technology like the cadastre or the timetable.

Notable passages

The Kuznets-derived split between growth potential and "the way it actually happens" (c1) is the unit's conceptual spine, fusing Braudel's longue durée/conjoncture apparatus directly onto growth theory and settling, via the India/cotton counterfactual, that the industrial revolution's specific sectoral form was contingent even though growth itself was not. The putting-out system passage (c2) shows why an apparently backward arrangement was in fact a rational response to demand volatility, setting up the contrast with the factory. The passage introducing the "industrialist" (c3) marks the social pivot Braudel treats as real but insists is not identical to "true capitalism" being born. The Fischer-Clark sector passage (c4) shows Braudel adopting, and immediately qualifying, a still-standard economic classification. The reversal-of-geography passage (c5) states in one sentence the chapter's most dramatic empirical claim: that industrialization inverted, rather than reinforced, Britain's inherited core-periphery hierarchy — itself a variant of the core/semi-periphery/periphery apparatus that organizes this volume's account of world-economies, here applied at sub-national scale.
