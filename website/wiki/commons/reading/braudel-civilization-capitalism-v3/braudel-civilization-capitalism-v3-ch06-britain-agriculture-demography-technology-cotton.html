The Industrial Revolution in Britain, Sector by Sector

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel surveys the British industrial revolution sector by sector—agriculture, demography, technology, and cotton—while insisting throughout that this fragmented, stepwise method he is forced to borrow falsifies a change that was in reality total, synchronic, and mutually reinforcing rather than a sequence of discrete causal stages.

Braudel opens by surveying the scholarly deadlock over what "the industrial revolution" even names (Hartwell's synthesis, the 1960 Past and Present conference, the 1970 Lyon colloquium, Jacques Bertin's confession that he no longer knows what "industrialization" means), then commits—reluctantly—to the conventional sector-by-sector exposition (agriculture, demography, technology, cotton) that the rest of the chapter follows, even as he argues at length that this method misrepresents a change that was synchronic and interdependent rather than a "flight of steps."

Themes

A confessed methodological compromise. Braudel's real argument is not about Britain at all but about how history should be written about it. He rejects the sector-by-sector approach as a "fragmented model" borrowed from "the most traditional kind of political economy," one that makes agriculture, demography, technology and trade look like separate stages "affected one after another," when what is needed is a synchronic model of how sectors furthered or bottlenecked each other simultaneously. No such model exists, so he uses the old one anyway — an unusually candid admission of method-as-constraint rather than method-as-choice.

Agriculture fed the industrial revolution a market and a workforce more than a technology. The historians he canvasses (Flinn, Habakkuk, Bairoch, E. L. Jones) disagree on whether agricultural growth preceded, accompanied, or merely permitted industrial takeoff, and Braudel treats the mechanization story (seed-drill, threshing machine, Rotherham plough) as marginal until well into the nineteenth century. The real transformation was organizational: new crop rotations eliminating fallow, and above all Jones's "virtuous circle" in which cheap cereals pushed farmers toward livestock and forage crops, which increased manure, which raised cereal yields in turn. Enclosure and the concentration of land into large tenant-farmed estates (in sharp contrast to post-1789 France's fragmentation) produced a genuine agrarian capitalism — landowner-as-rentier, farmer-as-entrepreneur — integrated into a national market that both fed industrial towns and, through demand for iron implements, was "the iron industry's leading customer." The same countryside crisis that stagnated grain also incubated cottage industry (lace, straw-plaiting, nailing, hosiery), producing the "trained and malleable" proletariat that urban industry would later draw on.

Population growth is shown to follow industry as much as enable it. Braudel uses regional data (industrial counties rising from a third to 45% of population by 1831, earlier marriage ages in industrial Lancashire) and Tocqueville's 1835 travel account of Manchester and Liverpool to establish that demographic explosion and industrialization were "two mighty processes taking place side by side," then explicitly refuses to assume causation runs from population to output. Citing Phyllis Deane's reasoning about the divergence of birth and death rates after 1740, he leans toward the reverse: industrial output growth is what let the population expand rather than being checked by rising mortality.

Technology is demoted from cause to consequence. Against Marx's view of technology as the industrial revolution's primum mobile, Braudel builds a case (via Bairoch, Hyde, Chaudhuri) that inventions typically lagged behind, and were pulled into use by, existing price and demand conditions: Kay's flying shuttle (1730) waited thirty years for demand; coke-smelting, patented by Darby in 1709, was ignored for four decades until charcoal prices rose past coke's; the mechanized cotton industry emerged to match cheap, competitive Indian calico prices rather than from a technical breakthrough. Braudel's own gloss on this cumulative, contingent unfolding — that the revolution had "to invent itself as it went along" and that each new advance retroactively gave meaning to those before it — half-resonates with the history-machine pattern: a paradigm ("the industrial revolution") assembling silently and distributedly out of independent, often anonymous components (Darby, Hargreaves, Arkwright, Crompton, Wilkinson, Hyde's price economics) until it is named in retrospect, rather than driven by a single "great man" or a single technology, as Marx's reading implicitly assumed.

Cotton succeeded where its own antecedents had not. Braudel pushes back against the revisionist demotion of cotton (small absolute tonnage next to coal, John Hicks's label of it as "the last chapter" of an old textile order rather than the first of a new one). He grants the ancien-regime lineage — Indian calico imports, the hybrid fustian fabric, prohibitions on Indian cottons in England (1700, 1720) and France (1686) that a contraband trade cheerfully ignored — but insists the crucial difference is that this revolution succeeded: unlike earlier textile booms it converted into sustained, "continuous growth," underwrote a quarter (later half) of British exports, and its profits, even after margins collapsed from windfall to modest, financed the subsequent iron and railway buildout. "One cycle propelled the other on its way."

Notable passages

Jacques Bertin's lament that he no longer knows what "industrialization" means — railways? cotton? coal? gaslighting? — draws from Braudel not a narrower definition but a wider one: "industrialization, like the industrial revolution, means everything." That totalizing move half-fits world-machines's treatment of an era's deep infrastructure (institutions, technologies, assumptions) as a single analytic object rather than a bundle of separable causes — though where WMP treats that totality as the intended unit of analysis, Braudel treats it as an admission of analytical defeat, a phenomenon too large for "any simple, all-purpose, peremptory definition."

The agricultural "virtuous circle" (cheap cereals → more livestock → more manure → higher cereal yields → cheaper cereals) is the chapter's cleanest worked example of the synchronic, mutually-reinforcing causation that Braudel says the sector-by-sector method cannot capture — a small, self-contained case of several "sectors" (arable, pastoral, market price) driving each other rather than following a line of stages. It gestures, loosely, at the kind of non-teleological, mutually-constituting causation Configurancy names for agents and worlds, though Jones's circle is a narrow price-and-yield feedback loop rather than an open-ended co-emergence, and Braudel uses it descriptively, not as a general theory of change.

Tocqueville's 1835 description of Manchester — "a vision of hell," tenfold population growth, fifteen or sixteen people to a basement, Irish, Welsh and Scottish immigrants alongside women and children as "docile and underpaid" labor — gives the demographic statistics their human weight and anchors Braudel's broader claim that the industrial revolution assembled whatever manpower it needed by any means available, skilled or not, native or immigrant, adult or child.

The closing move from cotton to iron — "even if cotton did not directly influence the machine revolution ... profits from cotton certainly paid the first bills" — is Braudel's own synchronic point made in miniature: not sectors in sequence, but one sector's capital surplus becoming another sector's investment fund, the "flight of steps" he otherwise refuses collapsing into a single financed continuity.
