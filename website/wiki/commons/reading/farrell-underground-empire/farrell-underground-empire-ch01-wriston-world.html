Walter Wriston's World: How Escaping the State Built New Chokepoints

Commons · Underground Empire: How America Weaponized the World Economy · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Walter Wriston's crusade to free global finance from state control instead built the dollar-clearing system, SWIFT, the Internet's physical backbone, and the semiconductor supply chain into concentrated chokepoints that gave the United States more leverage over the world, not less.

Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman use the career of Citibank chairman Walter Wriston as the frame for the book's central paradox: a generation of business leaders who believed they were dissolving state power by building global markets and networks instead built centralized infrastructures — the Eurodollar market, the SWIFT messaging system, the physical Internet, the semiconductor supply chain — whose chokepoints eventually gave states, above all the United States, more leverage than they had ever had before. The unit moves from Wriston's ideological formation and his role remaking Citibank, through the Eurodollar market and the SWIFT/MARTI rivalry, to the physical geography of the Internet in Ashburn, Virginia, and finally to the globalized semiconductor industry built around TSMC and Qualcomm. Each case follows the same arc: an infrastructure grows in a decentralized, seemingly ungovernable way, and then, because network effects and stickiness reward whoever gets there first, collapses into a small number of critical nodes.

Themes

Wriston's ideology and its inversion. Wriston inherited a Hayekian faith in market freedom from his father Henry Wriston, a Mont Pèlerin Society co-founder, and spent his career arguing that information technology and mobile capital would "decentralize power" and erode the "choke points" of state sovereignty. The book's central irony, stated almost as a thesis sentence, is that Wriston and his peers were "centralizers by their nature": they built world-spanning networks that "centered on a few key choke points," and those choke points sat on U.S. soil, subject to U.S. surveillance and U.S. law. This is the book's governing move — take globalization's own self-description (borderless, decentralized, escaping government) and show that its physical and legal substrate did the opposite. The resonance with world-machines is direct: Wriston is describing what he takes to be the death of one world machine (state-centered "Modernity") and the birth of a new one built purely on market and information "liveness," but the text shows the new infrastructure re-encoding legibility-as-machine-core at exactly the points where flows must pass through identifiable, jurisdiction-bound nodes.

The Eurodollar market as accidental infrastructure. The Eurodollar market arose to solve a real problem (dollar demand outside the U.S., interest-rate caps inside it) and grew into "a legal gray zone" that made the dollar the de facto global currency "without anyone really planning it." Farrell and Newman's account of this — no central designer, components accreting independently (certificates of deposit, correspondent banking workarounds, Soviet risk-avoidance) until the system "lurches into visibility" in a crisis — reads as a textbook case of a history-machine: the 1974 Herstatt crisis is the moment the silent infrastructure becomes suddenly, catastrophically visible, revealing that "twenty to thirty private banks had become the 'de facto payments mechanism of the world.'" Every Eurodollar, however offshore, "had to be backed by a real dollar, sitting in a U.S. bank" — the pirate kingdom's buccaneers still had to "regularly provision themselves in the monarch's ports."

Standardization battles produce chokepoints even when they resist monopoly. The MARTI/SWIFT story shows that even a defeat for centralization produces a chokepoint. Citibank tried to make its proprietary MARTI system the mandatory standard for interbank messaging; other banks revolted, and the revolt catalyzed a multilateral, Belgium-based consortium, SWIFT, precisely to avoid that kind of capture. Yet SWIFT itself became an "obligatory passage point" for the entire global financial system — the very consolidation Wriston wanted for Citibank was achieved collectively instead, and proved just as exploitable once the "cops noticed." This is a sharper case for capture-resistance than it first appears: SWIFT's design successfully resisted capture by any single bank, but not by the state whose currency and banks underwrote the whole clearing system.

The Internet's founding myth versus its physical geography. Distributed-network theory (Paul Baran's Cold War design for nuclear-survivable command and control) promised a network with no exploitable center. In practice, the commercial Internet's growth — MAE-East, then Equinix, then AWS — concentrated around a single Northern Virginia corridor, Ashburn, because stickiness (colocation, buried fiber, cross-connects) rewards whoever is already there, "attracting more high-speed fiber, which in turn attracted more server farms, in a self-reinforcing loop." The 1996 hacker's line to Neal Stephenson — "Virtually all communications between countries take place through a very small number of bottlenecks" — is presented as prophetic and mostly ignored at the time. The chapter treats this as an unintended, emergent centralization, another instance of a history-machine assembling itself out of independent commercial decisions (Rick Adams's coup against the NSF, Adelson's "neutral exchange") until it becomes, in the book's own image, "a vast parabolic mirror, concentrating the Internet into a tiny point that is easy to observe and perhaps to exploit."

Semiconductors globalize design and manufacture apart — and each half becomes a chokepoint. The fabless/foundry split (Gordon Campbell's insight, Morris Chang's TSMC) let semiconductor design (concentrated in the U.S., via firms like Qualcomm and tool-makers Cadence and Synopsys) separate from fabrication (concentrated at TSMC in Taiwan). Industry self-description celebrated this as a "beautiful and extraordinarily complex global ecology" beyond any single country's control — an image that maps neatly onto Wriston-style optimism about world-machines-scale "divergence." But the chapter's punchline reverses it: TSMC is "a single point of failure for the U.S. economy" located ninety miles from a strategic rival, while U.S. control of design IP gives Washington a matching lever over everyone else — setting up the book's larger argument (developed later) about weaponized interdependence.

Notable passages

The chapter opens by quoting Wriston's own predictions almost verbatim — "shifting the tectonic plates of national sovereignty" — before immediately undercutting them with the observation that "when novel ideas succeed, they decay into stale clichés." This sets the ironic register the rest of the unit sustains: Wriston is taken seriously as a prophet of real change while being shown, repeatedly, to have misdiagnosed its political meaning.

The Herstatt anecdote is the chapter's clearest dramatization of latent infrastructure becoming suddenly legible: a failing German bank's frozen clearing account cascades through the interbank system until "the global financial system went into heart failure," forcing Wriston to personally reassemble a functioning payments network out of a phone tree of elite bankers — his own dry aside, "and it also makes us nervous," captures how even the system's chief architects hadn't understood what they had built.

The Ashburn material is the chapter's most concrete image of how ideology (the Internet as inherently decentered) diverges from infrastructure (a cluster of low-slung warehouses in Loudoun County carrying an outsized share of global traffic) — reinforced by the closing detail that a January 6 participant was later arrested for plotting to physically attack the same facilities, treating the metaphorical "chokepoint" as a literal target.

The TSMC/Qualcomm passage closes the unit on the book's real argument: the same interdependence that seemed to prove Wriston right — a globally distributed, mutually reliant industry with "sixteen thousand suppliers worldwide" — is simultaneously a set of loaded weapons pointed in both directions, since "U.S. intellectual property wound through the entire semiconductor production chain, like a fisherman's longline with barbed and baited hooks."
