The Empire of Wind and Light

Commons · Underground Empire: How America Weaponized the World Economy · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
America's underground empire of weaponized financial and technological interdependence emerged not from grand design but from ad hoc improvisation, and now, as it grows more visible and China responds in kind, it risks spiraling toward the same kind of catastrophic feedback loop that once nearly produced nuclear war and once helped precipitate World War II -- so Farrell and Newman close by arguing for Schelling-style strategic frameworks, new institutions, and a possible turn toward using the empire's coercive machinery for climate and anti-corruption goals instead of unbounded rivalry.

The book's final chapter pulls back from case studies to survey the underground empire in full: a digital-currency arms race between Facebook's Libra, China's CBDC, and a nervous Federal Reserve; a string of ad hoc improvisations -- Banco Delta Asia, Iran sanctions, the Rusal aluminum fiasco, encrypted fiber -- that hardened by accident into a structure of global control; and a closing argument that America now faces a choice between an accelerating US-China feedback loop of mutual fear (with echoes of the interwar sanctions regime that helped produce World War II) and a deliberate, Schelling-like effort to govern the empire strategically, perhaps even repurposing it toward climate and anti-corruption ends.

Themes

Mutual fear generates its own escalation, independent of anyone's actual intentions. The chapter opens with the Libra/CBDC episode as a case study in how empires of interdependence self-radicalize: Chinese officials "pattern-matched Libra to Sputnik," accelerated a digital yuan they feared would let the US extend its "one boss" currency dominance, which in turn triggered American panic about a "Sputnik moment" and calls for a US CBDC -- even though Libra was never a government project and the digital yuan itself proved unpopular with Chinese citizens. Farrell and Newman generalize this into the book's central closing claim: "As the underground empire became increasingly visible, its consequences became less predictable... American officials, like their Chinese equivalents, had difficulty understanding how their own actions helped feed the widening gyre." Neither side accurately models the other's constraints, so each escalation looks rational locally while compounding globally.

The empire was never designed -- it accreted. Against the outside view of "a relentless machine of domination, the product of decades of careful engineering," the authors insist that from inside it is "a haphazard construction lashed together from ad hoc bureaucratic decisions and repurposed legal authorities" that "emerged half by accident, as officials tried to deal with one damn problem after another." Banco Delta Asia's designation against North Korea inadvertently modeled the technique later used to sever Iran from global finance; NSA data-center intrusions provoked Google and Microsoft into pervasive encryption that blinded the agency's own "secret mirrors"; sanctioning Deripaska's Rusal nearly shut down an Irish aluminum refinery load-bearing for German car factories, a physical-supply-chain blind spot the US's financial maps couldn't see coming. This is history-as-emergent-infrastructure rather than history-as-master-plan -- a paradigm assembling in the dark until it becomes visible and, once visible, unstable history-machine.

Overreach without punishment taught the wrong lesson. Trump's administration was "both brutal and incompetent," designating ICC officials and threatening allies in ways no prior administration dared -- yet the dollar's dominance and US technological centrality survived intact. Biden officials drew the perverse inference that "mistakes wouldn't undermine U.S. power," licensing an even larger, more consultative, but ultimately more expansive campaign of sanctions and export controls against Russia and China. The 2022 Russian oil price cap is presented as an elegant-seeming instrument whose knock-on effects (Saudi Arabia reading it as a precedent-setting threat and cutting production) illustrate how instruments calibrated for one target reshape the incentives of bystanders who fear becoming the next one.

The China relationship risks repeating the interwar spiral, not just the Cold War one. Farrell and Newman invoke Nicholas Mulder's The Economic Weapon to argue that interwar sanctions and blockade-fears helped drive Nazi Germany and Japan toward war rather than deterring aggression -- "fear of sanctions helped spur Nazi Germany to embark on conquest so that it could secure the raw materials the Third Reich feared it would be denied." The explicit, uncomfortable analogy: "Like the World War II Axis powers, China is nationalistic, militaristic, and ruthlessly nondemocratic. It has reason to be paranoid: the world economic order is being retooled to try to hold it down." The chapter refuses to resolve whether China will acquiesce, retaliate economically, or turn to force over Taiwan -- and stresses that the U.S. government has no institutional apparatus (unlike the Pentagon's decades of deterrence theory) for reasoning about economic coercion at all, since responsibility is "scattered... across Treasury, Justice, Commerce, and other smaller outfits."

The proposed fix is Schelling's, not disarmament. The authors don't call for abandoning economic weapons but for thinking about them the way Thomas Schelling taught Cold War strategists to think about nuclear ones: game-theoretic, systematic, aimed at crisis management rather than victory. "The United States needs a framework like Schelling's today... the players are both blindfolded and are playing on a board whose shape they do not know." Concretely this means a Manhattan-Project-scale mapping of global economic networks, a dedicated US economic-security institution (Japan's 2021 ministry of economic security is the model), and eventually direct dialogue with adversaries, not just allies -- mirroring how Cold War stability required American and Soviet officials to actually talk.

A closing pivot toward repurposing empire rather than only restraining it. Sputnik led to a missile race, but also to satellites and space exploration; the authors ask whether today's spiral can likewise be "redirected inward" toward solving earthly problems. They point to the nascent EU-US green tariff deal on steel and aluminum, and to Peter Harrell's argument that the same tools built for economic warfare -- tariffs, investment restriction, sanctions designations -- could be redeployed against carbon-intensive economies and against corruption, producing a different feedback loop "in which national power reinforced global legitimacy and global legitimacy reinforced national power." This is explicitly offered as partial, imperfect, and carved out only where US and global self-interest overlap -- not a utopia.

Notable passages

The book's title metaphor resolves in the final paragraphs into an extended tunnel image: "There is no visible exit from the underground empire. Every tunnel that seems to lead out ends up turning back in on itself" -- business leaders seeking freedom from government "forged new manacles for themselves," and politicians who trusted markets to guarantee security instead watched "markets became battlefields." The book's very last line supplies the only note of possible ascent in an otherwise claustrophobic argument: "The roots of imperium go far too deep to ever fully be torn out. Yet even if we cannot escape the darkness underneath, we can strive to bend the growth of empire upward, toward the sun and air" -- the wind-and-light imagery of the chapter's title reappearing as the book's final gesture toward redemption without illusion.

The Mulder digression is the chapter's most historically loaded move: it reframes the entire US-China standoff not as a rerun of the Cold War (which the authors elsewhere find instructive but comparatively survivable) but as a possible rerun of the run-up to World War II, when "fear of sanctions" itself became a proximate cause of war rather than merely its instrument. Layering this onto the direct address to the reader -- "Or might it turn, as Nazi Germany turned, to military aggression, securing its interests through force and territorial gain?" -- gives the chapter its most urgent register.

The Schelling passage functions as the book's methodological credo: having spent the whole book describing a world-machine-scale infrastructure of financial legibility and control that grew without anyone designing it, the authors end by asking for exactly the kind of deliberate, theorized statecraft that built Cold War nuclear stability -- an implicit admission that the empire described in this book is a legibility machine whose builders never had a map of their own creation ("the players are both blindfolded and are playing on a board whose shape they do not know").
