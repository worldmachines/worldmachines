Hook's Captain: Corporations Trapped Between Empires

Commons · Underground Empire: How America Weaponized the World Economy · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Through the coerced tanker captain, Microsoft's decades of diplomatic improvisation, TSMC's fraying neutrality, and crypto's failed attempts to build money beyond state reach, the chapter shows businesses trapped between rival powers that have weaponized the same global networks companies once treated as neutral infrastructure.

This chapter is the book's central case-study chapter, tracing four businesses (an anonymous tanker captain standing in for shipping generally, Microsoft, TSMC, and the crypto ecosystem built on Ethereum) as each discovers that the global economic networks it relied on for profit have become instruments of state coercion. Brian Hook's blackmail email to an Indian oil tanker captain — pay-or-be-sanctioned — opens the chapter as a miniature of the position all these businesses now occupy: no longer free agents on an open sea, but vessels "boxed in by the demands of jealous states."

Themes

Businesses inherited Wriston's dream of stateless commerce, then lost it. The chapter's throughline is the collapse of the mid-century fantasy that global business could operate as a neutral, borderless space above sovereign politics. Walter Wriston (from earlier chapters) imagined firms sailing "piratical realms beyond the reach of sovereigns"; this chapter shows that dream souring as "clashing powers—the United States, China, Europe, and even Russia—press companies into their service." Firms are no longer choosing whether to engage geopolitics; they are choosing only how to lose least badly.

Legibility is the mechanism of capture. Hook's tactic — "mapping out … the pressure points" of Iran's oil shipping network — literalizes how a state converts a commercial network into a weapon: first render it legible (find the chokepoints, the captains, the insurers, the ports), then squeeze. The same pattern recurs with Microsoft (the NSA's ability to "listen to nearly everything," per Bowden), with TSMC (the Biden administration's "data hub" demanding supply-chain visibility "from across the federal government"), and with crypto (OFAC's targeting of Tornado Cash once it could no longer see through the mixer). This is legibility-as-machine-core weaponized: making a network visible to power is the precondition for controlling it, and the businesses in this chapter are all, in different ways, trying to resist or manage their own forced legibility.

Neutrality is not a stable strategy — it decays under pressure. Microsoft (Brad Smith's "Digital Geneva Convention," the aspiration to be a "trusted and neutral Digital Switzerland") and TSMC (deliberately built, per Morris Chang, as an even-handed "Switzerland of semiconductors" that would offer identical deals to rival customers) both tried to institutionalize impartiality as a business model and a survival strategy. Both were eventually forced to abandon it: Microsoft chose a side outright in the Ukraine war, becoming, in the New York Times's phrase, a wartime supplier "like Ford Motor Company" in WWII; TSMC was compelled to hand technology and data to the US, build a subsidized fab on US soil, and watch China treat its compliance as "treachery." Neutrality survives only as long as no great power needs to test it.

Crypto tried to engineer capture-resistance directly into infrastructure, and recentralization ate the project from within. Vitalik Buterin's warning about "base layer services" concentrating power, and blockchain's promise of alegal, "unstoppable" code (Gavin Wood's "forces of nature"), represents the most explicit attempt in the book to build capture-resistance as a structural property rather than a diplomatic posture — money and contracts that "cannot care as to whether its actions might be interpreted as legal … or illegal." Yet the chapter documents relentless recentralization: exchanges, wallets, and infrastructure providers (Coinbase, MetaMask, Alchemy, Infura) became new choke points; DAOs concentrated voting power in venture funds like Andreessen Horowitz; and when OFAC designated Tornado Cash, most crypto intermediaries capitulated instantly, revealing how thin the decentralization actually was beneath the ideology. Only code with no possible off-switch (Tornado Cash itself) proved genuinely resistant — and even that triggered a direct confrontation between sovereign power and "alegal" mathematics whose outcome the chapter leaves open.

The old order is dead and the new one hasn't arrived — a genuine interregnum for business strategy. The chapter's closing lines make this explicit: "After decades of imagining that they lived in a decentralized, borderless world, businesses again find themselves constrained by governments... it is difficult to see how" they escape. This is a corporate-scale gramsci-gap: the legitimating fiction of neutral global markets has collapsed, but no new settled framework — cooperative, coercive, or technological — has replaced it. Firms cope ad hoc: equivocation (TSMC), conversion (Microsoft), or continued flight toward ever-more-radical decentralization that keeps reproducing the very monopolies it was meant to escape.

Notable passages

The Brian Hook tanker-captain story is the chapter's controlling image: coercion delivered personally, informally, and irresistibly through a state actor who has mapped a commercial network's pressure points and can end a life's trajectory with an email. The captain's frozen indecision — circling for days before Hook "decided for him" — dramatizes the loss of agency that recurs at scale throughout the chapter.

The Bowden/Snowden material shows how thoroughly the surveillance apparatus depended on businesses' silence and governments' incentive to look away — "nearly everyone who knew anything had strong self-interested reasons to look away" — until Snowden forced disclosure and converted a hidden arrangement into an open, unmanageable "chasm between governments and the tech sector."

Michael Hayden's "invisible boundary" between a rights-respecting America and a "lawless outside world" is the chapter's clearest statement of the book's larger argument: American multinationals like Microsoft eventually found themselves classified with foreign targets, on the wrong side of a line their own government drew.

Morris Chang's rebuke of Thomas Friedman — "the world is not flat anymore" — crystallizes TSMC's tragedy: a company built entirely on the premise of frictionless, apolitical globalization, now forced to refute that premise's most famous popularizer while ceding the very neutrality that made it valuable.

The Tornado Cash confrontation is the chapter's speculative climax: a test of whether genuinely unstoppable, "alegal" code can survive a great power's will to control it, staged as a direct clash between sovereign authority and the crypto movement's most literal attempt at capture-resistance engineered into infrastructure rather than claimed as a diplomatic posture.
