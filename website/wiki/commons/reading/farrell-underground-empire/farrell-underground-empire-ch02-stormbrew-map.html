The STORMBREW Map

Commons · Underground Empire: How America Weaponized the World Economy · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
The STORMBREW leak reveals how the U.S. turned literal maps of internet cable choke points and SWIFT's financial-messaging network into instruments of empire, using NSA telecom surveillance and Treasury/OFAC dollar-clearing power to police, coerce, and cut adversaries like North Korea and Iran out of the global system.

A leaked NSA slide titled "STORMBREW at a Glance," showing the cable choke points through which the agency siphons global internet traffic, becomes the emblem for the chapter's real subject: how the United States turned the physical and financial infrastructure that carries the world's communications and money into an instrument of empire. The chapter braids two parallel stories that share a single logic — General Michael Hayden's NSA exploiting fiber-optic choke points after 9/11 (STORMBREW, FAIRVIEW, PRISM, the Mark Klein/AT&T "secret room" 641A), and Treasury officials led by David Aufhauser, Stuart Levey, and Juan Zarate discovering that SWIFT messaging data and the dollar clearing system could be weaponized to freeze, isolate, and eventually excommunicate whole countries (Banco Delta Asia, Iran) from the global economy. Both stories end on the same warning: having proven that centralized chokepoints can be dominated, the U.S. incentivizes everyone else — allies and rivals alike — to build alternative, less legible maps of their own.

Themes

Maps are not neutral description but a technology of sovereign claim. The chapter opens by explicitly linking the NSA's technical slide to Pope Alexander VI's 1493 bull dividing the New World and John Dee's Elizabethan maps asserting an "Arthurien" claim over the Americas. In each case, a map doesn't just represent territory — it manufactures a legal and practical basis for dominion by declaring some space terra nullius, a "nobody's land" that the powerful may rightfully seize. STORMBREW and its financial equivalents perform the same operation on the globalized economy: rendering the borderless flows of data and money into a legible grid of choke points that a single state can claim and control. This is the chapter's clearest resonance with the WMP concept of legibility-as-machine-core — the reduction of "blooming, buzzing confusion" into "lines on a page" is explicitly framed as what makes control at scale possible, echoing the modernity-machine's core operation, here redeployed as covert empire rather than public bureaucracy.

A legal line drawn to protect Americans became, after 9/11, a line redrawn to license total surveillance of everyone else. Hayden's own bridge-crossing hypothetical — bin Laden gains Fourth Amendment protection the instant he steps onto U.S. soil — captures a genuine pre-9/11 constraint. After the attacks, Hayden and Bush administration lawyers like John Yoo didn't abolish the U.S./non-U.S. line; they aggressively reinterpreted where it fell and how much "commingled" data could be swept up incidentally, producing programs like Stellar Wind. The chapter is careful to show this was not really a legal innovation but a physical fact given legal cover: American telecom dominance meant "the territory hadn't changed" even as the doctrine did, since most of the world's cheapest data routes ran through U.S. soil regardless of the two endpoints' nationality.

Corporate compliance, not martial law, is the actual mechanism of the underground empire. AT&T's "extreme willingness" to build room 641A, Verizon/MCI's cooperation with STORMBREW, and eventually Yahoo!'s capitulation under a $250,000-a-day fine threat show that the NSA's power depended on heavily regulated telecom firms and increasingly compliant platform companies choosing (or being coerced) to cooperate — a soft-power lever independent of any judicial warrant. The same pattern recurs with SWIFT: a nominally independent, Belgium-based cooperative caved to Treasury pressure not because of a valid subpoena it couldn't resist, but because its board of international banks needed continued access to the U.S. financial system.

Treasury weaponized designation and dollar-clearing dependency rather than diplomacy. The Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC), once an "obscure unit," became devastating because designation cut a foreign bank off from correspondent accounts at U.S. banks — and other banks, fearing contagion, would abandon the designated bank preemptively. Banco Delta Asia (2005) proved the mechanism worked with terrifying, disproportionate force: a $25 million freeze produced a bank run and made North Korean money globally "toxic," an effect U.S. officials themselves hadn't anticipated. Iran (2006–2015) then became the full-scale test: stripping U-turn exceptions, threatening banks directly rather than governments, and eventually forcing SWIFT itself to expel nineteen Iranian banks, contributing directly to Iran's concessions in the nuclear deal.

Success bred visible overreach, and overreach bred resistance. The chapter closes on its own hinge: Jack Lew's 2016 warning that sanctions power is "inextricably linked" to U.S. economic centrality, and that overreach could "ultimately drive business activity away from the U.S. financial system." Stuart Levey (by then at HSBC) and Christopher Hill's "sanctions doomsday machine that could not be turned back off" describe a policy that, once fear is manufactured, cannot easily be un-manufactured — a system whose very success is what motivates other states to build parallel, less legible financial and communications infrastructure of their own.

Notable passages

The opening meditation on cartography as imperial technology — moving from the papal bull's line "one hundred leagues west and south of the Azores" to John Dee's "iust Arthurien clayme" to the NSA's slide — establishes the chapter's governing metaphor before a single NSA program is described, so that STORMBREW reads not as a one-off scandal but as the latest instance of a centuries-old practice of drawing lines to license dominion over unclaimed space.

Hayden's own Niagara Falls hypothetical, delivered in 2000 to reassure Congress and then inverted after 9/11 to justify expanded surveillance, is the clearest single illustration of how a legal boundary functions: the same fact pattern (a foreigner crossing into U.S. jurisdiction) is available to argue either for restraint or for aggression, depending only on which outcome officials want the law to license.

Mark Klein's discovery of room 641A gives the surveillance story its ground-truth, human anchor — a technician piecing together, from misplaced manuals and a colleague's slip about "splitters... installed in cities along the West Coast," that a supposedly narrow terrorism program was in fact a wholesale duplication of internet backbone traffic, with AT&T's full and enthusiastic cooperation.

The SWIFT material — Lenny Schrank's blunt "We don't do subpoena" giving way, after 9/11, to reported eagerness ("What took you so long?") — captures how quickly institutional resistance to state surveillance collapsed once the underlying commercial dependency on U.S. financial access made resistance costly, a pattern that recurs almost exactly with Yahoo! and the platform companies.

The Banco Delta Asia episode is the chapter's proof-of-concept for financial choke-point warfare: a $25 million freeze against a minor Macau bank produced effects — a bank run, North Korean diplomats requesting direct contact, months of banks refusing to touch the money even after it was unfrozen — wildly disproportionate to the sum involved, demonstrating that the weapon's real power lay in contagion and reputational fear rather than the designated amount itself.

Jack Lew's valedictory warning and Christopher Hill's "sanctions doomsday machine" image close the chapter on its central irony: the same centrality that made U.S. financial and communications infrastructure globally dominant is what made its coercive use so effective, and so effective a use that it now supplies every other state a reason to build an alternative, less mappable system — the gramsci-gap-adjacent moment in which a hegemonic infrastructure's very success generates the incentives for its own circumvention.
