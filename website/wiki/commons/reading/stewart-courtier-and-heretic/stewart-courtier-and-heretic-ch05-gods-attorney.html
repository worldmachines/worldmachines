God's Attorney

Commons · The Courtier and the Heretic: Leibniz · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Chapter 5 lays out the young Leibniz's instrumental, other-directed "philosophy of philosophy" — a legalistic, conciliatory, reason-grounded theocratic project defined by its opposition to modern philosophy, in sharp contrast to Spinoza's solitary, truth-seeking monomania.

This chapter reconstructs the young Leibniz's earliest philosophical writings — letters, the Catholic Demonstrations, essays on motion, and the notorious "Egypt Plan" — to argue that Leibniz had, by age twenty-five, already fixed the entire "philosophy of philosophy" that would govern his career: an altruistic, conciliatory, legalistic, and reason-grounded theocratic project defined above all by what it opposes. Stewart builds the chapter as a systematic point-by-point contrast with Spinoza, whose portrait (developed elsewhere in the book) haunts every paragraph as the implicit foil, and closes by revealing that Leibniz secretly reached out to Spinoza at the very moment he was polishing this self-presentation for the Duke of Hanover — setting up the book's central confrontation.

Themes

Philosophy as instrument, not vocation. Where Spinoza treated philosophy as a personal path to salvation, Leibniz treats it as one tool among many for producing "the general happiness of the human race." His guiding maxim, "Justice is the charity of the wise," makes philosophy charitable rather than contemplative — addressed not to God or reason in the abstract but to named patrons, dukes, and fellow scholars, because his goal was "not necessarily to reveal the truth, but to get something done." This other-directedness even shapes the form of his work: nearly everything he wrote was a letter to somebody, a genre suited to influence rather than disclosure.

Philosophy practiced as advocacy — the chapter's title theme. Leibniz's defense of transubstantiation in the Catholic Demonstrations is Stewart's central case study. Leibniz does not argue the doctrine is true; he argues that the "insults of unbelievers and atheists" against it are fallacious — an innocent-until-proven-guilty legal posture. Stewart makes the analogy explicit: "Philosophical arguments are the moral equivalent of legal briefs," judged by believability, not belief. Leibniz's own habit of calling doctrines "advantageous" or "useful" rather than "true" — including doctrines central to his own system, like the immortality of the soul — confirms that he practiced philosophy as an attorney practices law, prosecuting a client's case rather than pursuing disinterested truth. Hence "God's Attorney."

The Empire of Reason as theocratic-but-rationalist politics. Leibniz's underlying project was ecumenical and political: reunite the Catholic and Protestant churches into one respublica Christiana, legitimated not by scripture or divine right but by "the eternal truths established by philosophy." Stewart credits this "Empire of Reason" — with its state duty to relieve poverty and promote welfare — as an early sketch of the modern welfare state, even as its ultimate aim (a single unified Christendom) is thoroughly medieval. The tension between a rationalist method and a theocratic goal is the chapter's political counterpart to its epistemological argument: reason recruited in service of an inherited order rather than as an independent standard.

The universal characteristic — legibility as machine. Leibniz's recurring dream, first mooted in The Art of Combinations, is a symbolic language so transparent that philosophical disputes could be resolved by mechanical calculation — an "arithmetical machine" that would let disputing philosophers simply say "Let's calculate!" This is Leibniz's clearest bid to make thought itself perfectly measurable and adjudicable, collapsing disagreement into computation — a vivid anticipation of legibility-as-machine-core and the ambitions later inherited by the modernity-machine: philosophy, in his ideal world, "could just as easily be turned over to a beautiful machine."

Omnimania versus monomania. Stewart's most vivid contrast: Spinoza compressed "a lifetime of insights into a single, adamantine volume," while Leibniz scattered himself across hundreds of unfinished projects — calculating machines, submarines, pneumatics, optics, longitude, natural and revealed theology — cataloged in his 1671 letter to Duke Johann Friedrich. Fontenelle's line that Leibniz "could manage all the sciences simultaneously" understates it, Stewart notes; the real number of simultaneous projects was "almost always an order of magnitude greater than eight." Leibniz emerges as an "omnimaniac" whose achievements, however extraordinary, were "meager indeed in comparison with his plans."

The seeds of the monadology. Buried inside the transubstantiation defense is Leibniz's first sketch of his mature metaphysics: since mechanistic physics cannot explain motion, all bodies must contain an incorporeal "concurrent mind" — in humans, the ordinary mind; in everything else, God's. This move elevates mind to the status of primary reality and makes the world "be-souled," anticipating the monad as "the indivisible atom from which all things are created." Stewart flags the argument's structure as a "deconstruction": exposing modern philosophy's failure to account for motion, then smuggling in a positive metaphysical doctrine as the only alternative — a pattern he calls the "founding gesture" of Leibniz's thought.

Self-interest disguised as the general good. The Egypt Plan — persuading Louis XIV to invade Egypt instead of Holland, framed as a new Christian Holy War — supplies Stewart's comic set piece. Behind the pious rhetoric lay Boineburg's unpaid French rents and Leibniz's own ambition to reach Paris, "the most knowledgeable and powerful city in the universe," and its republic of letters. Stewart reads this as paradigmatic: Leibniz repeatedly discovers "convenient concordance between the general good and his personal ambition," a pattern that raises doubt about how cleanly he ever distinguished the two.

A reactive philosophy, defined by its enemy. The chapter's closing argument is structural: Leibniz's system is easier to state negatively (what it opposes — mechanism, modern philosophy, atheism) than positively, and Stewart predicts that all these labels will eventually collapse into one proper name: Spinoza. This primes the book's architecture — Leibniz's mature philosophy will be legible only in opposition to the man he secretly wrote to at the very moment this self-portrait was being drafted.

Notable passages

The paragraph built around "Justice is the charity of the wise" establishes Leibniz's instrumental, altruistic self-conception against Spinoza's inward "essentially personal problem," giving Stewart's whole comparative method its first explicit statement. The "Let's calculate!" passage on the universal characteristic is the chapter's most quotable image of Leibniz's faith that clarity and mechanism could dissolve all philosophical conflict — a proto-computational fantasy that resonates with WMP's account of legibility as a machine's core drive. The "legal briefs" passage is the interpretive key to the chapter's title, making explicit that Leibniz measured philosophical arguments by believability, "in terms of what the jury decides, and not necessarily the truth." The Fontenelle/omnimaniac passage crystallizes the monomaniac-versus-omnimaniac contrast that structures the whole double biography's opposition between Spinoza's single book and Leibniz's hundreds of abandoned projects. The Egypt Plan passage shows Stewart's driest irony at work, tallying how one crusade proposal was designed to solve German security, church reunion, the fate of Egypt, and Leibniz's career all in a single stroke. Finally, the "reactive philosophy" passage functions as a hinge to the rest of the book: it names the entire remaining project as an argument that Leibniz's system cannot be understood except as a response to Spinoza, setting up the secret correspondence revealed in the chapter's final lines.
