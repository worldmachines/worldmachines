The Haunting

Commons · The Courtier and the Heretic: Leibniz · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Across four decades after The Hague, Leibniz's political maneuvering, church-reunion diplomacy, feuds with Locke and Newton, admiration for Chinese philosophy, and eventual defense against charges of Spinozism all reveal the same underlying anxiety: an obsessive, lifelong struggle against the ghost of Spinoza.

This unit surveys the last four decades of Leibniz's life — his court career, his genealogical hackwork, his church-reunion diplomacy, his feuds with Locke and Newton, his enthusiasm for Chinese philosophy, and finally the charges of Spinozism leveled against him in his own lifetime and after — and argues that all of these disparate, seemingly unrelated pursuits are governed by a single, permanent anxiety: Leibniz's inability to exorcise the ghost of the man he met in November 1676. The chapter's structure enacts its thesis, cycling through one arena after another (theology, epistemology, physics, sinology, and finally self-defense) in which Leibniz discovers, again and again, the same rival lurking beneath the surface.

Themes

The public erasure and private obsession. Leibniz spent his later career minimizing the 1676 meeting in public record — by the time of the Theodicy he reduced the encounter to a throwaway line about seeing "M. de la Court as well as Spinoza" on his travels, and lied outright about the extent of their correspondence. Yet the chapter argues this public diminishment is the mirror image of a private preoccupation that never let up: "the ghost of Spinoza was far from leaving the courtier-philosopher in peace." The chapter's central claim is that this anxiety, not ambition or piety, is the hidden engine of Leibniz's restless career.

Church reunion as failed containment. Leibniz's negotiations with Arnauld and then with Bossuet's circle over Protestant-Catholic reunion are read not as sincere ecumenism meeting sincere ecumenism, but as a mismatch: Leibniz pursued reunion as a bulwark against a coming metaphysical catastrophe, while his Catholic interlocutors pursued it as a vehicle for confessional conquest (they were, quite literally, praying for his and Sophia's conversion). Stewart frames Leibniz as prophetically "out of touch... only because he was too far ahead of his time" — his counterparts didn't yet grasp the civilizational threat Leibniz saw in the new philosophy, understanding reunion only as recycled medieval politics rather than "one front in a cosmic struggle between two forms of modernity." This framing of two rival modernities in friction — one legible, hierarchical, and church-bound, the other diffuse and irreducibly monist — gestures toward the kind of large-scale paradigm competition world-machines tries to formalize, though the chapter keeps its claim narrowly biographical.

Locke and Newton as disguised Spinozists. The chapter's most sustained argument is that Leibniz's two most famous philosophical rivalries — the five-hundred-page refutation of Locke's Essay in the New Essays, and the vicious calculus-priority war with Newton — are actually displaced battles with Spinoza. Locke's tentative suggestion that "some Systems of Matter fitly disposed" might think is, for Leibniz, functionally identical to Spinoza's doctrine that thought and extension are attributes of one substance; Newton's law of gravitational attraction implies matter can move (and by extension think) without a "mindlike principle," which again collapses into Spinozist substance monism. Stewart's account of the priority dispute reframes what looks like a squalid contest of egos as something with a deeper motive: "When Leibniz looked at Newton — no less than when he looked at Descartes and Locke — he saw Spinoza." The reasoning is presented as a fixed, almost compulsive inferential chain that Leibniz runs on every rival regardless of what they actually argued.

China as a laboratory for the same fear. Leibniz's genuinely admiring engagement with Chinese philosophy — his claim that the ancient concept of li essentially anticipates his own monadology — curdles into the same anxiety once he turns to "modern" Chinese thinkers, whom he accuses of a "bad" philosophy that reduces to the same brute-necessity monism as Spinoza's, right down to comparing them to the Averroists. China becomes, in the chapter's phrase, "a kind of laboratory experiment in modernization, a cautionary example of what might happen here at home, should Spinoza succeed" — evidence that Leibniz's fear operated as a portable template applied to any system, however distant, that seemed to converge on Spinozist monism.

"Stop Spinoza" as the master political motive. Leibniz's opposition to Louis XIV is reinterpreted not as balance-of-power realism but as a fear that French "atheism" — coded as Spinozism — was a "venom" spreading through Europe's elite via "fashionable books" and "men of high station," threatening "the universal revolution with which Europe is threatened." Stewart compresses this into a slogan: "The program at the core of all of Leibniz's political activities throughout his career can be summarized in a single slogan: Stop Spinoza." The image of ideas "stealing gradually" and "slithering" into elite opinion before erupting into visible crisis has an affinity with how a history-machine is described as assembling silently and distributedly before it lurches into view — here dramatized as Leibniz's paranoid perception of that very process underway in his own century.

The charge turned back on the accuser. The chapter closes by turning the argument on Leibniz himself: contemporaries and later commentators (Andala, Lange, Lessing, Herder, Russell) accused Leibniz of being a covert Spinozist. Leibniz's own 1714 reply — "it is precisely by means of the monads that Spinozism is destroyed," for Spinoza "would be right, if there were no Monads" — which Stewart glosses as "if he is wrong, Spinoza is right," is read as an admission that Spinoza, not any classical philosopher, was the only real rival system to his own. The chapter's final psychological suggestion is that Leibniz's ubiquitous sightings of Spinoza everywhere he looked were themselves symptomatic: "he perceived the influence of his rival everywhere because he mistook the tint in his own spectacles for a certain dark aspect of the outside world."

Notable passages

The chapter opens its analytical turn with the claim that Leibniz's four decades of frenetic activity — genealogy, politics, courtly flattery, mathematics — were unified beneath the surface by "a permanent anxiety" that "came to acquire a name." This sets up the rest of the chapter as a series of case studies (church reunion, Locke, Newton, China, the Spinozism charge) all demonstrating the same underlying compulsion, rather than as separate biographical episodes.

The Locke discussion is the clearest statement of Stewart's diagnostic method: he traces the exact logical chain by which Leibniz converts Locke's hedged empiricist conjecture into a full Spinozist heresy, concluding that the New Essays is "a covert assault on the man he met in 1676." The same method is then applied almost identically to Newton, where gravitational action-at-a-distance is read as smuggling in the same materialist premises, making Newtonian physics "a Trojan horse" for atheism — the repetition of the pattern across two entirely different domains (epistemology, physics) is what makes Stewart's argument that this is a fixed obsession rather than case-by-case reasoning.

The "Stop Spinoza" passage distills Leibniz's entire geopolitical program — his opposition to Louis XIV's absolutism, his fear of a Bourbon-dominated Europe — into a single anti-Spinozist crusade, treating the Sun King's court as the vector by which "the slithering Spinozists flourish" and threaten "universal revolution."

The China material shows the same reflex exported to an entirely different civilization: Leibniz's charge that "bad" modern Chinese philosophers reduce the li to a universal Substance is explicitly likened to "the opinions of Spinoza," confirming that his fear was not really about any particular rival philosopher but about a recurring structural threat he detected wherever monist metaphysics seemed to appear.

The chapter's final movement — Leibniz's own 1714 admission that failure of his monadology would vindicate Spinoza, and the subsequent chorus of accusations (Lessing's "I fear that he was himself a Spinozist at heart," Russell's remark that "Leibniz fell into Spinozism whenever he allowed himself to be logical") — culminates in Stewart's own psychological reading of the whole chapter: Leibniz's compulsive rediscovery of Spinoza in every rival system reflected his own suppressed proximity to the philosophy he spent his life refuting.
