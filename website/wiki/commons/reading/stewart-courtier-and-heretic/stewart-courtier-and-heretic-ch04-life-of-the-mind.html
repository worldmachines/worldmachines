A Life of the Mind

Commons · The Courtier and the Heretic: Leibniz · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Stewart reconstructs Spinoza's Rijnsburg-to-The-Hague years as the deliberate enactment of a philosophy born from a personal crisis of vanitas, arguing that his ascetic, materialist, and paradoxically sociable way of living was itself the argument for his system, and sets up his November 1676 encounter with Leibniz.

This chapter traces the roughly sixteen years between Spinoza's excommunication and his 1676 meeting with Leibniz, using his early Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect as a key to decode the deliberately austere, itinerant, and paradoxically sociable existence he built in Rijnsburg, Voorburg, and The Hague — arguing that for Spinoza the philosophical life was not a career but a total, self-consistent project whose ascetic economy, materialist psychology, and fraught fellowships were themselves a species of argument.

Themes

Philosophy as therapy for a metaphysical crisis. Stewart frames Spinoza's entire vocation as originating in the sensation the tradition calls vanitas — not ordinary melancholy but a "dire encounter with the prospect of a descent into absolute nothingness," a dread that success, pleasure, honor, and money are all merely the postponement or disguise of failure and death. This "dark night of the soul" is presented as the archetypal origin of philosophy itself (Socrates, Diogenes, Epicurus all belong to the same lineage), and its resolution — "supreme, continuous, and everlasting happiness," explicitly glossed by Spinoza as "blessedness" or "salvation" — becomes the sole stated purpose of the Ethics. Stewart is careful to note that this dread is not universal among philosophers and "does not attain prominence in the work of Leibniz," planting an asymmetry the book will exploit at the two men's meeting.

The rules of living as a practiced discipline, not a pose. From the vanitas crisis Spinoza derives three explicit "rules of living" — accommodate ordinary social custom, permit sensual pleasure only insofar as it preserves health, and acquire money only insofar as it sustains the body for contemplation — and Stewart marshals the biographical record (Colerus's inventory, the reported diet of gruel and watery beer, the two pairs of trousers, the refusal of Simon de Vries's annuity, the estate too small to interest his own sister) to show Spinoza actually lived by them for sixteen years. The chapter uses the Thales-of-Miletus anecdote and lens-grinding trade to make the same point from two angles: poverty was chosen, competent, and instrumental to the mind's freedom, not incidental hardship.

The materialist paradox: a "life of the mind" that denies the mind's independence. Stewart isolates what he calls the central paradox of Spinoza's existence — a philosopher whose entire life was organized around contemplation belonged to a system with "no space for an 'other' world," in which every mental operation "has its basis in a material process" and "desire is the essence of man." This is the book's clearest statement, in Spinoza's own terms, of the doctrine World Machines readers will recognize as Conatus — the striving that constitutes the human essence — and Stewart flags it as "the same one that would bedevil Leibniz," making it a structural hinge for the whole double-biography rather than a local curiosity.

A double sociability: Heraclitean toward the herd, Epicurean toward the fellowship of reason. The chapter's most sustained argument concerns Spinoza's social theory and practice: a stated policy of avoiding "the multitudes" while cultivating an inner "fellowship of reason" in which "Man is a God to Man." Stewart shows this policy working smoothly with ordinary people (the landlord's family, the villagers, even church attendance) but failing badly whenever Spinoza tried to induct a correspondent into the fellowship — the underground, quasi-devotional circle around Simon de Vries has a faintly cult-like, The Thicket-adjacent intimacy ("drawing the curtains, lighting the candles"), while the Blijenburgh correspondence curdles from effusive welcome ("deeply devoted to truth") to icy dismissal within two letters, and the Oldenburg friendship survives a near-fatal rupture over the Tractatus. Stewart uses these case studies to argue that "even the purest friendships always harbor some level of conflict" — a lesson explicitly aimed forward at the Spinoza-Leibniz relationship.

Reputation as a philosophical battlefield. The closing movement reframes Spinoza's austere lifestyle as itself contested terrain: seventeenth-century logic held that atheism implies vice, so Spinoza's manifest virtue was either suppressed by hostile contemporaries (who invented a "craven, worm-eaten syphilitic" caricature) or claimed by Spinoza himself as evidence against the charge of atheism. Leibniz, about to visit in person, is set up as the one contemporary too "clearheaded" to accept either fiction — he will simply see the room, the lathe, the buckles, and know "too much."

Notable passages

The vanitas passage early in the chapter is the interpretive key to everything that follows: Spinoza's philosophy is presented not as an academic position but as the resolution of an existential emergency, one he describes as "suffering from a fatal illness…foreseeing certain death unless he employs a remedy." Stewart's gloss on the Ethics's stated purpose — that its "principal—indeed, the sole—purpose" is "blessedness or salvation" — insists on reading Spinoza's rationalism in essentially religious terms, a framing that recurs throughout the book's comparison of the two philosophers.

The materialist-paradox passage ("How can one who denies the very existence of the mind lead a life of the mind?") is the chapter's philosophical crux and its most direct engagement with what World Machines would call conatus: desire as the essence of man, mental life as material process, with no "outside the cave." Stewart explicitly marks this as a problem shared with Leibniz, not unique to Spinoza.

The fellowship-of-reason material — "Man is a God to Man," the underground gatherings of the de Vries circle, and the escalating Blijenburgh correspondence — dramatizes the gap between Spinoza's theory of philosophical community and its practice. The Blijenburgh case in particular (from "deeply devoted to truth" to "I hardly believe that our correspondence can be for our mutual instruction") shows how rigidly binary Spinoza's category of "men of reason" could be, and how personally costly misjudging that category proved to be, given the grain merchant's eventual five-hundred-page rebuttal of the Tractatus.

The chapter's final paragraph, describing Leibniz's actual visit to the attic room with its lens-grinding lathe and four-poster bed, functions as a narrative hinge into the book's central meeting: all the biographical detail assembled in the chapter — the diet, the wardrobe, the shoe buckles — is redeployed as what Leibniz himself will personally witness, converting abstract argument about Spinoza's life into the concrete scene the book has been building toward.
