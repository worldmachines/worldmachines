The Hague, November 1676

Commons · The Courtier and the Heretic: Leibniz · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Matthew Stewart frames the secret 1676 meeting between Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz and Baruch Spinoza in The Hague as the hidden pivot of modern philosophy, with Spinoza embodying an affirming response to modernity and Leibniz a reactive one.

Stewart opens his double biography of Leibniz and Spinoza not with either man's life story but with the moment their lives intersected: a secret, multi-day meeting in November 1676 at Spinoza's lodgings in The Hague, weeks before Spinoza's death. The chapter establishes the book's central conceit — that this hidden encounter, which Leibniz spent the rest of his life denying and minimizing, was in fact "the defining event of Leibniz's life," and that the two philosophers' opposed characters and doctrines constitute two rival, still-unresolved responses to the birth of the modern world.

Themes

A double biography built on a single hidden meeting. Stewart's structural gambit is to treat the Hague meeting as the hinge of both men's intellectual lives — "Everything before points toward it for resolution; and everything after points back for explanation." The only direct trace of the meeting is a single sheet in Leibniz's hand, read aloud to Spinoza, containing a proof of the existence of God — a fittingly abstract and contested relic for an encounter that shaped, but never openly acknowledged, the metaphysics both men would go on to publish.

Public denunciation, private obsession. The chapter dwells on the gap between Leibniz's public posture — denouncing Spinoza's work as "horrible," "terrifying," "intolerably impudent" in letters meant to be seen — and his private conduct: annotating Spinoza's writings meticulously, corresponding secretly under flattering address ("celebrated doctor and profound philosopher"), and finally traveling to Holland expressly to see him, despite later claiming he was merely "passing through." Late in life Leibniz half-confesses, in the voice of a fictional interlocutor he chose not to publish, that he "once went a little too far, and began to lean to the side of the Spinozists." This pattern of disavowed intimacy is the chapter's engine: the book will spend its length reading Leibniz's mature philosophy as a suppressed argument with Spinoza.

Spinoza as the active response to modernity; Leibniz as the reactive response. Stewart's thesis, stated almost programmatically here, is that Spinoza was "perhaps the first to observe" the modern world well: a philosopher who derived God, ethics, selfhood, and politics from a universe of pure natural causation, "without purpose or design," and who arrives at "a liberal, democratic system of government suitable for an inherently fragmented and diverse society." Leibniz, by contrast, is cast as building "a strategy for recovering something of the old ideas about God and man by means of an analysis of the limits of reason" — the "paradigm for the reactive response to modernity." Stewart explicitly maps this pair onto later ideological categories: Spinoza anticipates secular liberalism, Leibniz religious conservatism. This is the book's organizing axis and reads as a direct historical instance of two rival responses emerging at the threshold of an epochal transition — the kind of fork Stewart says still structures "debates concerning the separation of church and state, the clash of civilizations, and the theory of natural selection" three centuries later. Readers working in the world-machines framework will notice the resonance with a gramsci-gap-like moment: an old theocratic legitimacy dying and a new secular one not yet settled, with Spinoza and Leibniz offering competing bids for what comes next.

Character as philosophy. Stewart insists the two men's biographies are not incidental to their ideas: "there are no two finer examples of the dictum that character is philosophy." The chapter's physical and social portraits — Leibniz the wigged, restless, ambitious courtier-genius who "never knew what to do with his arms"; Spinoza the ascetic lens-grinder living on raisins and milk gruel, owning almost nothing but a childhood bed — are offered as material embodiments of their opposed metaphysical temperaments, not mere color.

The century of transition. Stewart frames the seventeenth century itself as an age when "the theocratic order of the medieval era ceded to the secular order of modernity," an era of "glitter and strife" that contemporaries experienced as wickedness rather than the "century of genius" later historians would name it. This framing sets up Spinoza and Leibniz as figures standing exactly at a civilizational threshold, each theorizing what should follow the old order's collapse — an old-order-to-new-order transition that a modernity-machine-attentive reader will recognize as a Dawn-era negotiation dressed in seventeenth-century clothes.

Notable passages

The chapter's opening juxtaposes Leibniz's public letters denouncing Spinoza's work with his private, secret courtship of the man himself — a contrast Stewart uses to establish that the "official" record of their relationship is a screen. The paragraph beginning "None of it was true" punctures Leibniz's own later cover story (a chance visit, a few hours, idle "anecdotes") with the documented facts: a purposeful three-day visit, conversations "many times and at great length," and a single surviving page containing a proof of God's existence, penned in Spinoza's presence. The passage on Leibniz's belated, unpublished confession ("I once went a little too far...") is Stewart's strongest direct evidence for his claim that the meeting was "the defining event of Leibniz's life." Finally, the back-to-back thesis paragraphs on Spinoza as the "first and archetypal instance of the active response to modernity" and Leibniz as the architect of "the reactive response to modernity" state, almost as a manifesto, the interpretive axis the rest of the book will use to read both philosophers' systems against each other.
