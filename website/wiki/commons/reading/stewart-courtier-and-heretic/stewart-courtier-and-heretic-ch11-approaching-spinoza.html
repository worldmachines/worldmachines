Approaching Spinoza

Commons · The Courtier and the Heretic: Leibniz · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Leibniz's private Paris notebooks and his 1676 sea-and-canal journey toward The Hague show him drafting, in Spinoza's own logical form, the very substance-monism he simultaneously denounces and censors in himself.

This unit tracks Leibniz through the winter, spring, and autumn of 1676 as his Paris notebooks fill with fragments of a never-written "philosophy of everything," and as he finally, after eight months of stalling on his new post in Hanover, sails and canal-hops his way toward a face-to-face meeting with Spinoza in The Hague. The chapter's real subject is not the journey but the paper trail: notebook entry after notebook entry shows Leibniz reproducing Spinoza's arguments almost word for word, recoiling from them, and then reproducing them again — culminating in a shipboard draft that breaks off in mid-word at the exact moment it arrives at Spinoza's conclusions.

Themes

Leibniz's private notes converge on Spinoza's God almost by accident of vocabulary. The very title of Leibniz's planned masterwork, The Elements of a Secret Philosophy of the Whole of Things, Geometrically Demonstrated, mirrors what Leibniz would have called Spinoza's (still unpublished) Ethics. Stewart traces how Leibniz's Latin phrase "de summa rerum" slides between meaning "the universe" and meaning "God" — a lexical ambiguity that quietly imports Spinoza's central claim that God and the universe are one and the same substance. When Leibniz writes "the reason for God is God" and "an infinite whole is one," he is, in Stewart's reading, restating Spinoza's doctrine of God as causa sui and as an infinite-attribute Substance almost verbatim, without needing to have read the Ethics directly.

The oscillation is not resolved but repeated. On the same scrap of paper where he flirts with a Spinozist world-soul, Leibniz recoils and insists "God is not something metaphysical... but a certain substance, a person, a mind" — and sets himself the anxious task "it must be shown" that God is an agent who deliberates and chooses. This is not a single conversion narrative; the same cycle of attraction and recoil recurs in April, when Leibniz rejects Spinoza's mind-body doctrine outright ("Is the mind the idea of the body?... That cannot be") only to drift, a few lines later, back into language ("the origin of things from God is of the same kind as the origin of properties from an essence") that Stewart shows leads logically straight back to Spinozism.

The shipboard draft is the chapter's centerpiece: Leibniz re-derives Spinoza's Ethics from his own first principle. Composed while becalmed off Sheerness and drifting through the canals toward The Hague, "That a Most Perfect Being Exists" starts from Leibniz's own commitment to the principle of sufficient reason and arrives, by Leibniz's own admission that the steps are "easily demonstrated" and "manifest," at the conclusion that there is only one substance and all things are its modes — the opening propositions of Spinoza's Ethics in miniature. Leibniz misattributes the doctrine to Plato's Parmenides rather than to Spinoza, a diversion Stewart reads as evidence he already understood exactly whose ground he was standing on.

Self-censorship becomes the chapter's dramatic and philosophical climax. Mid-sentence, mid-word — "Essentia est pr…" — the draft simply stops. Stewart treats this break as more revealing than anything Leibniz completed: a moment when Leibniz "retreats from philosophy to the philosophy of philosophy," catches himself in the act of thinking like Spinoza, and refuses to let the thought finish, even alone in his cabin with no reader but himself. Thirty years later, in writing he withheld from publication, Leibniz all but confesses to the lapse. The chapter frames Leibniz less as a closet Spinozist with a fixed secret doctrine than as a mind containing genuinely incompatible commitments — orthodox providence and rationalist necessity — that could not be reconciled into "a single view of the world," and a temperament built around concealing whichever commitment was inconvenient in the moment. This lived instability — an old theocratic order not yet abandoned, a new rationalist metaphysics not yet allowed to speak its name — is its own miniature gramsci-gap, playing out inside one anxious, ambitious mind rather than across an era. The way the Spinozist argument assembles itself silently across scattered notebook entries before nearly surfacing as a single finished statement also has something of the logic of a history-machine lurching toward visibility, here interrupted by the author's own hand.

Leibniz's double game extends from metaphysics to politics. He tips off Spinoza, via a letter nominally from Tschirnhaus, that his own patron Huet — a man who believed Spinoza deserved to be "covered in chains and whipped with a rod" — is preparing a public refutation of the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus. Leibniz's editors later suppress this paragraph in the Latin edition of Spinoza's letters, evidently recognizing how compromising it was. Stewart's image of Leibniz as "the great chameleon" — courtier, calculus inventor, secret Spinozist, loyal servant of the theocratic establishment, all at once — is the chapter's summary portrait, sharpened by the sheer bureaucratic comedy of his eight-month delay in reporting to the Duke of Hanover while he pursued Spinoza's circle and a seat at the Royal Academy instead.

Notable passages

The February 11 notebook entry (paragraph beginning "There seems to be...some kind of most perfect mind, or God") is the first clear instance of Leibniz's Spinozism, glossed line by line by Stewart to show how each phrase — "world-soul," "the reason for God is God," "an infinite whole is one" — maps onto a specific Spinozist doctrine, even where Leibniz's phrasing is cruder than Spinoza's own.

The recantation on the same page ("God is not something metaphysical, imaginary, incapable of thought, will, or action... Rather, God is a certain substance, a person, a mind") shows the recoil arriving within paragraphs of the lapse — establishing the push-pull pattern the rest of the chapter tracks.

The shipboard draft's collapse — "An attribute is a predicate which is conceived through itself... Suddenly, the manuscript breaks off in midword, midsentence: Essentia est pr..." followed immediately by Stewart's own aside that what follows are "perhaps the most revealing" lines Leibniz ever wrote — is the chapter's turning point, converting a private philosophical lapse into a scene of genuine drama.

Stewart's own analytic verdict — "Leibniz was a Spinozist—at least at this moment—and he knew it," followed by the account of his strategy of concealment, diversion through "great thinkers like Plato and Parmenides," and the patient wait for Spinozism to "claim its rightful place in the sun" — is the interpretive key the whole unit is built around, explicitly naming the self-censorship the shipboard fragment only performs.

The Huet episode (the letter's suppressed final paragraph, and Stewart's comment that Leibniz was "willing to double-cross his ally Huet in order to forewarn Spinoza about a possible danger") extends the pattern of concealment from Leibniz's private metaphysics into his public alliances, showing the same operator running in both registers.

The chapter closes with Leibniz stepping off the canal boat on November 18, 1676 — "arms flapping, wig billowing, perfume dissipating in the autumn wind" — an image Stewart clearly intends as a comic, slightly absurd counterpoint to the philosophical weight of everything that has just been shown to be at stake in the meeting about to occur.
