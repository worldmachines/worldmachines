Leibniz's End

Commons · The Courtier and the Heretic: Leibniz · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Stewart narrates Leibniz's humiliating final decade of political house arrest, unpaid salaries, and diplomatic exile alongside his sunset metaphysical writings, arguing that the monadological optimism was an elegiac fiction masking a life that, contrary to Leibniz's own philosophy, was actually lived on Spinozist premises of self-interest, deception, and ontological insecurity.

This chapter narrates the last sixteen years of Leibniz's life (1700–1716) — his slow humiliation at the Hanoverian court, culminating in de facto house arrest, set against the serene metaphysical optimism of his final essays (the Monadology, the Principles of Grace). Stewart's argument is that the disjunction between these two Leibnizes is not incidental: the monadological "best of all possible worlds" was less an argument than an elegy for an ideal Leibniz privately knew was unrecoverable, and the courtier's undignified political and financial scrambling reveals a man who, despite explicitly repudiating Spinoza, had come — after their 1676 meeting — to live according to essentially Spinozist premises about selfhood, truth, and power.

Themes

Political humiliation and house arrest. Georg Ludwig, contemptuous of his "living dictionary," effectively imprisons Leibniz in Hanover by conditioning his freedom to travel on completion of the never-finished History of the House of Brunswick. Leibniz responds with an escalating pattern of secret trips, fabricated alibis (Karlsbad's waters, an imaginary abduction by the Empress), and increasingly desperate bids for better patronage in Vienna, Berlin, Paris, and finally London — each one failing. The ouster from the presidency of the Berlin Society of Sciences and the later suspension of his salary compound the picture of a once-central figure reduced to a supplicant "reviled in London, scorned in Berlin, ignored in Vienna and Paris."

Sunset metaphysics as elegy, not argument. The Monadology and Principles of Grace, written at sixty-eight, no longer "pretend to argue" — propositions follow one another "like the lyrics of a metaphysical ballad." Stewart weighs two readings: the literal one (Leibniz genuinely believes the universe has these harmonious properties) against a "second, more compelling" one, in which the monadological "City of God" is confessedly an unreachable ideal, "a place just over the next hill," offered wistfully rather than descriptively. The evidence for the second reading is Leibniz's private despair over a Europe he thought had fallen to a "spiritual epidemic," fated for "anarchy and revolution" — the same man who, when told of the Abbé de St. Pierre's plan for perpetual peace, could only imagine restoring the medieval Church as Europe's central power, reducing his life's ecumenical project to "little more than a pleasing diversion." This reads as a version of the decay-sequence: the unification-of-the-churches project has, by Leibniz's own late admission, slid from a functional goal into an ornamental fantasy he no longer expects to see realized — the gramsci-gap between a dying medieval-Christian order and a secular one he cannot yet fully name.

A self that must labor to persist. Stewart reframes Leibniz's vanity, greed, and compulsive self-promotion as symptoms of ontological insecurity rather than simple character flaws: because Leibniz did not actually trust that the self was the permanent, windowless substance of his own monadology, he had to fight constantly — for money, rank, praise, protection from "dangerous" rival philosophies — to keep his fragile identity intact. This is the book's sharpest resonance with Conatus: Leibniz becomes "that part of us that is always striving," but the striving here is not Spinoza's affirmative self-preserving power so much as an anxious compensation for a self Leibniz secretly believed to be as fragile as Spinoza said all selves are.

The verdict: a Spinozist life beneath an anti-Spinozist philosophy. The chapter's culminating thesis is that Leibniz's actual conduct enacted the premises of the philosophy he spent his life opposing: an "Empire of Reason" theory of politics gave way, in office hours, to raw self-interest and deception at every turn (from duping the Poles in 1669 to anonymous pamphleteering in 1704); a professed "principle of charity" gave way to an operating assumption that all people act from self-interest alone. Stewart frames the 1676 meeting with Spinoza as the origin of a self-knowledge that took Leibniz forty years to absorb: "Leibniz acted like a Spinozist—and yet he was nothing like Spinoza."

Death and an unmourned funeral. The deathbed scene — feverish talk of alchemical gold, a curt refusal of last rites ("Fool, what should I confess? I have stolen or taken from no one"), and a death attended only by a secretary and coachman — is followed by an inquest, an heir who inherits a small fortune, and a funeral that no one from the Hanoverian court attends. The Berlin Society and the Royal Society let his death pass unremarked. Stewart closes on the same image the chapter opened with, the "windowless monad," now turned into an epitaph: for all his influence on countless lives, Leibniz died "having touched no one very deeply at all."

Notable passages

The paragraph on the summer's end ("As the summer gave way to autumn...") compresses fifteen years of failed patronage-seeking into a single sentence of exile from every court that mattered to him, setting up the chapter's ironic contrast between public abjection and metaphysical serenity.

The "second, more compelling reading" of the monadology ("But, according to a second...") is the interpretive hinge of the chapter: it converts Leibniz's most famous optimistic doctrine into a confession of loss, comparing his "City of God" to "a place just over the next hill" — an ideal understood, at some level, as already unreachable.

The long paragraph on the fragile self ("Leibniz never quite lived in the world of monads...") is the psychological core of Stewart's argument, reading Leibniz's vanity and status-seeking as defenses mounted by a self that did not believe in its own monadological permanence.

The chapter's explicit thesis statement ("The stage on which Leibniz acted out his life...") names the book's central irony directly: Leibniz's practical life ran on Spinoza's premises about power, self-interest, and the passions, even as his declared philosophy insisted on charity, the Empire of Reason, and the immutable self.

The deathbed dialogue ("The secretary reminded his master...") dramatizes Leibniz's refusal to perform contrition or admit wrongdoing even facing death, a final flourish of the "master of managing perceptions" persona Stewart has been building throughout the chapter.

The funeral's aftermath ("As neither the court nor Leibniz's rich and recently widowed heir...") delivers the closing irony, contrasting the "six state carriages" that had honored Spinoza's death with Leibniz's unmarked grave and the silence of the very scientific societies he had founded, before the chapter's final line returns to the windowless-monad image as verdict on a life spent, in the end, largely alone.
