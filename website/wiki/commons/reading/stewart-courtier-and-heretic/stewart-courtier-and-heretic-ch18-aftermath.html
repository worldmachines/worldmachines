Aftermath

Commons · The Courtier and the Heretic: Leibniz · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Stewart's closing chapter tracks how posthumous reception distorted Leibniz and Spinoza into misleading caricatures before arguing that they represent modernity's two enduring responses—active affirmation versus reactive longing—which still structure philosophy today.

The book's final chapter steps back from the 1676 meeting to survey how history mistreated both philosophers after their deaths, then delivers Stewart's thesis in full: Spinoza and Leibniz are not merely two dead thinkers but the two permanent poles of the modern condition, one an "active" affirmation of a world without transcendence, the other a "reactive" longing for something beyond it—a longing Stewart traces forward through Kant, Hegel, Bergson, Heidegger, and postmodernism.

Themes

Posthumous reputations were reshaped by later needs, not evidence. Spinoza's dangerous political theology was suppressed and unacknowledged in his own aftermath, then in 1765 domesticated by Lessing and later canonized by Novalis as the "God-intoxicated man"—a mystical pantheist image that persists today and has erased "the political revolutionary who sought to overthrow theological tyranny." Leibniz fared no better: mocked by Swift and Voltaire in his own century, flattened by his self-styled follower Christian Wolf into "banality," then split by later admirers into two incompatible legacies—a romantic "literary" philosopher of the unconscious, and (via Russell) a founding logician.

Kant's rationalist/empiricist scheme is a historiographical distortion that hardened into orthodoxy. By grouping Leibniz, Spinoza, and Descartes together as "rationalists" opposed to Locke, Berkeley, and Hume as "empiricists," Kant obscured the far deeper opposition between Leibniz and Spinoza themselves. Hegel's fondness for triads and British philosophical pride entrenched the scheme further, so that "in philosophy classes to the present... Spinoza and the man who dedicated his life to expunging Spinoza's name from the world's memory are presented as happy partners." This is a paradigm that assembled quietly out of institutional convenience—Kant's need to professionalize philosophy, Hegel's taste for tripartite history, British self-regard—until it became the unquestioned default account, the kind of silently-assembled orthodoxy that only recent scholarship has begun to dismantle. In World Machines terms this reads as a small history-machine: a classificatory paradigm that lurches into dominance not because it is true but because it is convenient to enough constituencies at once.

The book's thesis: modernity has an active response and a reactive response, and they are still with us. Spinoza's active response "supplied the basic theory for the modern, liberal political order, as well as the underpinnings of modern science," teaching how to be moral in a secular society. Leibniz's reactive response—anxious about purposelessness, alienated, unwilling to "assume personal responsibility for happiness"—became, paradoxically, the dominant lineage of modern philosophy: Kant's noumenal self, Hegel's teleology, Bergson's élan vital, Heidegger's Being, and the postmodern critique of "phallogocentrism" are all, in Stewart's reading, restatements of Leibniz's original complaint against a disenchanted world. This bifurcation of one shared underlying condition into two enduring, mutually entangled orientations—one oriented toward what can be built and known now, one oriented toward what always lies just beyond—resonates with the world-machines framework's picture of coexisting orders in permanent friction rather than simple succession.

Two perennial philosophical personality types, not just two historical men. Stewart's closing meditation universalizes the contrast: Spinoza speaks for those who find "happiness and virtue are possible with nothing more than what we have in our hands," calmly attending to one's own deepest good and affirming "the totality of things such as it is." Leibniz speaks for those whose happiness depends on something beyond themselves, who long "to see our good works reflected back to us in the praise of others." This is a plainspoken restatement, in Spinoza's own terms, of what the Ethics calls conatus—the striving to persist by means of what one already has, without recourse to an external judge or a hidden truth—see Conatus.

A final, asymmetric verdict. Stewart does not split the difference. Leibniz's very flaws—"his greed, his vanity, and above all, his insatiable, all too human neediness"—are what made him "the philosopher of the common man," even its "first human being." Spinoza, by contrast, remains "a rara avis," marked by "eerie self-sufficiency" and "contempt for the multitudes." His teaching—that there is no hidden mystery, no other-world, only "the slow and steady accumulation of many small truths"—closes the book as "a philosophy for philosophers, who are as uncommon now as they have always been."

Notable passages

The chapter opens by naming the injustice of intellectual history directly: Spinoza's influence in the crucial decades after his death was "mostly negative and almost always unacknowledged," an invisible power exerted on contemporaries like Leibniz who could not publicly admit the debt. Stewart then traces the specific mechanism of Spinoza's later domestication—Lessing's 1765 discovery of the Opera Posthuma reinvented the seventeenth century's "most infamous atheist" as a "God-intoxicated" mystic, a persona that survives today at the cost of erasing the actual political revolutionary. The chapter's pivot sentence states the book's real argument plainly: once "suspect narratives" are set aside, "much of modern thought simply wanders in the space between the two extremes represented by the men who met in The Hague in 1676." The long passage on active versus reactive modernity then cashes this out concretely, crediting Spinoza with the liberal political order and modern science while tracing Leibniz's reactive lineage through the anxieties of a "needy humankind." The penultimate passage—"happiness and virtue are possible with nothing more than what we have in our hands" versus the longing "to see our good works reflected back to us in the praise of others"—is the book's clearest lay statement of the two philosophers' opposed ethical postures. The book's final passage delivers Stewart's last word on Spinoza: no hidden mystery, no other-world, "only the slow and steady accumulation of many small truths," ending on the line that closes the book, that his is "a philosophy for philosophers, who are as uncommon now as they have always been."
