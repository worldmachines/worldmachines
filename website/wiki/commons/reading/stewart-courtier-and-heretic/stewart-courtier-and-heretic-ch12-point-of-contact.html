Point of Contact

Commons · The Courtier and the Heretic: Leibniz · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Stewart reconstructs the November 1676 meeting itself, staging the personal and philosophical collision between Leibniz's needy vanity and Spinoza's self-sufficient indifference around a single surviving document: Leibniz's ontological proof and his claim that Spinoza pronounced it "sound."

Stewart finally stages the encounter the whole book has been orbiting: several days in November 1676 when Leibniz called on Spinoza in his rooms on the Paviljoensgracht in The Hague. Working from the single scrap of direct evidence that survives — a page in Leibniz's hand titled "That a Most Perfect Being Exists," annotated with a note about Spinoza's reaction — the chapter reconstructs both the intellectual content of their exchange (a critique of Cartesian physics that conceals a much deeper divergence about the reality of mind, and Leibniz's proof of God's existence) and the collision of personalities behind it: Leibniz's compulsive need for approval versus Spinoza's imperturbable self-sufficiency.

Themes

The evidentiary poverty of the encounter forces speculative reconstruction, which Stewart performs while flagging its own unreliability. Only one document survives from days of conversation — Leibniz's dated note and the "Most Perfect Being" proof itself — and everything else (the small talk, the mood, Spinoza's true reaction) must be imagined from context: Tschirnhaus's testimony, Lucas's biography, Leibniz's later letters. Stewart is explicit about this method, hedging even his most vivid reconstructions ("One may readily imagine then...") and closing the chapter by turning the same skepticism on Leibniz's own note: "we should perhaps leave open... whether or not this note, too, was less a statement of fact than an expression of its author's needs."

The physics discussion is a proxy for a metaphysical disagreement that runs far deeper than either man's stated topic. Leibniz records that Spinoza was surprised to have the flaws in Descartes's rules of motion pointed out — a critique that seems to place the two men on the same side against their common predecessor. But Stewart insists this apparent agreement conceals opposite aims: Leibniz undermined Cartesian physics to make room for a principle of activity he identified with mind, while Spinoza pursued the identical critique in order to abolish the very notion of mind Leibniz was trying to rescue. The chapter's recurring point is that shared vocabulary and even shared arguments can mask irreconcilable philosophical projects.

Leibniz's character is diagnosed as a self-love that is really a starved need for external validation, and this diagnosis is offered as the psychological key to the whole encounter. Stewart calls him "stupendously vain" but immediately complicates the charge: vanity's "other side... is a self desperately in need of love." The chapter reads Leibniz's insistence on extracting Spinoza's approval for the ontological proof — leaping from his chair, "demanding his listener's unconditional approval" — as an enactment of this deeper anxiety, one that Stewart claims also structures Leibniz's mature philosophy of a universe optimized for approbation (a world where "everything is for the best" and the philosopher himself is thanked by God and humankind alike).

Spinoza is drawn as Leibniz's temperamental photonegative: self-sufficient, indifferent, unmoved by the need to please or be pleased. Where Leibniz seeks validation, Spinoza (per Stewart) has "immunized himself to the influence of others" since childhood and stakes his happiness on "supreme self-sufficiency." The set-piece image of the debate's climax stages this contrast almost cinematically: "Spinoza sitting unmoved, deeply indifferent, perhaps silently contemptuous, the very incarnation of his own Nature-God; Leibniz pacing around the room, clinging to his proof, desperately shouting out his demands, the perfect representative of an ever needy human race."

The chapter frames the theological stakes of the whole book as a single question first posed here: was Spinoza's God actually a God at all. Leibniz's proof of "the subject of all perfections" and Spinoza's "substance consisting of infinite attributes" look, on a literal reading, nearly identical — but Stewart argues Leibniz arrived carrying two incompatible ideas of God at once: the philosophical God of reason he shared with Spinoza, and the "providential deity of orthodox religion" he wore "on his sleeve." The unresolved question of whether a divinity "stripped of anthropomorphic attributes and residing only in the here and now could be considered divine at all" is presented as the real subject of their meeting, beneath the courtesies and the physics talk.

Notable passages

The physics exchange (the equality of cause and effect passage) is the clearest textual evidence of what was actually said in the room, and Stewart uses it to pivot immediately from reportage to interpretation — arguing that the two men's shared critique of Descartes hid a fundamental disagreement about mind that neither may have made explicit to the other.

The description of the sole surviving document — "That a Most Perfect Being Exists," with Leibniz's own marginal note that Spinoza "thought it to be sound" after first contradicting it — anchors the entire chapter. Stewart treats this terse annotation as compressing "the essence of the two characters" and the dynamic between them, which is why nearly every subsequent claim in the chapter circles back to interrogating what that one word, "sound," could actually mean.

The portrait of Leibniz's vanity-as-neediness is the chapter's psychological centerpiece, explicitly linking his behavior in Spinoza's front room to the self-congratulatory cosmology of his mature philosophy — the argument being that the same drive that made Leibniz demand approval in 1676 later produced a metaphysics engineered to supply that approval eternally.

The staged confrontation — Spinoza motionless and contemptuous, Leibniz on his feet and shouting — is the chapter's dramatic peak and its thesis in miniature: two temperaments as much as two philosophies in irreconcilable contact.

The closing doubt about whether Spinoza truly approved the proof, or whether Leibniz's note simply expresses "its author's needs," refuses the reader any secure resolution, deliberately leaving the meeting's central fact — did Spinoza actually agree? — unresolved even as the chapter ends.
