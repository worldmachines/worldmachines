Method: coherence, influence, and what Rousseau read of Hobbes

Commons · Rousseau and Hobbes: Nature · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Douglass sets out his method: a defensible but non-dogmatic claim that Rousseau's political philosophy is coherent, a focus on why Rousseau engaged Hobbes rather than what he borrowed, and a probabilistic case that Rousseau knew Hobbes chiefly through De cive and the French reception.

This short preliminary section addresses three methodological problems before the study proper begins. First, whether Rousseau's political philosophy can be read as a coherent whole; second, how to treat intellectual influence, given that Douglass is studying Rousseau's engagement with Hobbes and "Hobbism"; and third, the evidentiary problem that there is no definitive proof Rousseau read Hobbes at all. Douglass answers with a moderate position on each and then reconstructs the editions of Hobbes available in French and Latin, concluding that Rousseau most probably knew De cive (likely in Sorbière's translation) plus the common references to Hobbes circulating in his day. Douglass is a historian of ideas here, not a machine theorist; the material bears on jean-jacques-rousseau scholarship rather than on World Machines concepts directly.

Themes

Coherence without a 'mythology of coherence'. Douglass assumes one can speak of Rousseau's political philosophy in general terms, but explicitly refuses to impose a "mythology of coherence" or to deny development between works. He treats free will and natural goodness as fully formed by the Discours sur l'inégalité, and the theory of the passions as the hard case, still changing up to Emile (1762). His fallback is that the idea of conflicting passions as a cause of misery, and of cultivating virtue through the passions, is consistent and "nowhere contradicted".

Reception, not derivation. Douglass reconstructs how Hobbes's ideas were understood and used by people who engaged him directly, which often differs from how Hobbes is read in scholarship today. He declines to claim what Rousseau took from Hobbes, since influence is hard to discern, and asks instead what Rousseau thought was at stake in targeting Hobbes. Resemblance between the two is explicitly not to be read as derivation "unless otherwise stated". Comparison is still called a fruitful enterprise, provided the problems each saw himself addressing are reconstructed from context.

Did Rousseau read Hobbes? Douglass admits Rousseau never referenced Hobbes by more than name, his correspondence is silent, and there is no definitive evidence he read him. He argues for probable familiarity with De cive from availability and from Rousseau's own references. Rousseau's limited English and painful Latin study make a French edition the likelier route. Douglass surveys the translations (Sorbière's De cive of 1649, du Verdus's editions, the De corpore politico translation whose attribution to Sorbière Noel Malcolm doubts) and library data (Glaziou's thirty-eight French libraries) showing De cive as the most widely held Hobbes text.

Scholars disputed. Marguerite Richebourg's claim that Rousseau drew on the French De corpore politico is called unsupported, and Bruno Bernardi's confident claim that he read it but not Leviathan is called problematical because the 'body politic' language could equally come from De cive. Robert Wokler's suggestion that Rousseau had no firsthand knowledge is taken seriously, but Douglass notes Wokler himself recognizes one textual clue.

The 'enfant robuste' evidence. The phrase 'un Enfant robuste' in the Discours sur l'inégalité is, Douglass reports, unique to the French De cive and absent from French commentaries before the Discours. Helvétius and the Encyclopédie articles 'Hobbisme' and 'Homme' (Diderot) pick it up later. Douglass judges it more probable that Diderot borrowed from Rousseau than the reverse, since Diderot's use occurs in a passage comparing the two men's states of nature, which he thinks would post-date the Discours (hedged: "fairly safe to assume").

A shifting view of Hobbes. Some commentators think Rousseau's opinion of Hobbes changed in the 1750s. Rousseau criticized Hobbes's religious intolerance in the letter to voltaire (1755) and the first draft of Du contrat social, but by 1762 called Hobbes the only Christian philosopher to see that political and religious authority must be united. Douglass says this suggests re-evaluation but does not tell us which texts Rousseau read.

Notable passages

The opening passage (c1) states the coherence stance and the rejection of a 'mythology of coherence'. The second (c2) grades which Rousseauian themes are stable and which, the passions, are not. The third (c3) is the key statement of the influence-versus-reception method and its caveat about resemblance. The fourth (c4) states the evidentiary problem candidly. The fifth (c5) works through the 'enfant robuste' argument and Wokler's and Diderot's roles. The sixth (c6) records Rousseau's shifting opinion of Hobbes on civil religion and the limits of what that shows. Together they justify Douglass's practical choice to cite De cive as Hobbes's principal work in the first three chapters and to flag any other Hobbes texts where an intermediary source is at least as plausible.
