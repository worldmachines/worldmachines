Reason, Passion, and the Limits of Enlightened Politics in Rousseau

Commons · Rousseau and Hobbes: Nature · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Douglass argues that Rousseau, siding with the Augustinian critique of Stoicism against Kantian and Stoic readings, held reason to be a judging, instrumental faculty that orders the passions rather than masters them, so that politics must move citizens through their passions, above all love of fatherland, and not through enlightened reason.

Douglass opens from the hope that republican institutions might order citizens' passions towards love of fatherland, so that virtue would not require duty to triumph over inclination. He then tests the assumption, which he attributes to Kant and to proto-Kantian readings of Rousseau such as Cassirer's, that virtue means reason mastering the passions. Placing Rousseau against the Augustinian and neo-Augustinian critique of Stoicism (Augustine, Senault, Vauvenargues), Douglass argues that Rousseau denied any opposition between reason and the passions. Reason is the faculty of judgement, and it works on the passions from within. Douglass's own thesis is that this has a political corollary: because men cannot order their passions by reason alone, politics has to speak to the heart.

Themes

Against the Stoic and Kantian readings (Douglass's thesis, disputing Cassirer and others). Douglass rejects the claim that Rousseau identified God, Nature, and Reason. In the Discours sur l'inégalité natural law is known 'anterior to reason', and reason later 'stifles' Nature. He grants that Rousseau's thought has Stoic-like features, quoting a commentator's phrase 'a strikingly original piece of secular Augustinian Stoicism', but he denies that Rousseau shared the Stoic view, as the Augustinian tradition read it, that passions are vices to be eradicated. Rousseau sides with Augustine, Senault and Vauvenargues: annihilating the passions would be to 'reform the work of God'.

Reason as judgement, against Helvétius. Rousseau accepts the Lockean critique of innate ideas but, against Helvétius's reduction of reason to sensation, holds that sensations are passive while an 'active principle that judges' forms ideas. Reason is nothing other than judgement, the faculty of comparing sensations. This grounds free will, which Douglass has stressed earlier as consciously resisting, acquiescing in, or choosing between inclinations. Rousseau concedes that will is largely shaped by education, surroundings and sensation, and Douglass adds that reason is not abstract, since its object is always some sensation or inclination.

Conscience as innate sentiment. Against the Pyrrhonism that Malebranche feared from Hobbesian and Lockean empiricism, Rousseau invokes conscience, 'an innate principle of justice and virtue', whose acts are 'not judgements but sentiments'. It is neither reason nor sensation. If made active through the will, it can guide judgement.

Instrumental reason, ordering rather than mastering. Reason orders the passions 'suitably to the nature of things'. It cannot fix its own ends independently of the passions, so Douglass describes Rousseau's view as instrumental (his characterization, hedged as 'may be viewed'). Misuse of reason yields conflicting passions and misery. Reason therefore cannot master the passions, and only passions can check passions. Douglass concludes that the differences between Rousseau and Kant outweigh the similarities.

Political consequence: politics must work through the passions. Rousseau had little faith that men can order their passions by reason themselves. He faulted the Abbé de Saint-Pierre for the 'false principle of perfected reason', the belief that men are led by enlightenment and not by passion. Modern political language has become 'more precise and less passionate' and speaks to reason and not the heart. In a well-ordered republic, government, legislator and public education must cultivate love of fatherland, and the passion to be reckoned on is love.

Notable passages

Passions and reason ordered, not opposed (c1, c2). Douglass draws the conclusion that the problem is conflict among passions, not between reason and passion, supported by Rousseau's argument that destroying the passions would make God contradict himself.

Judgement and free will (c3). The distinction between passive sensation and active judgement is Rousseau's defence against Helvétius's materialism and the basis of his account of freedom.

Conscience (c4). Rousseau's answer to sceptical empiricism is a sentiment, not innate ideas or abstract reason.

Instrumental reason and the rejection of Kant (c5). The clearest statement of Douglass's reading, aimed at the Stoic and proto-Kantian interpretations.

Speaking to the heart (c6). The political payoff, in Rousseau's criticism of Saint-Pierre and of modern political language.

Connections

This is a reading of jean-jacques-rousseau as a critic of a rationalist Enlightenment politics, which sits with counter-enlightenment framings, though Douglass makes his case through the Augustinian critique of Stoicism and does not use that label. Rousseau's programme of making a people one by cultivating civic love is comparable to designs for legible, unified political order such as the-legible-commonwealth, but here the mechanism is affective, not informational, and Douglass does not frame it as machine-like.
