Love versus fear: Rousseau's answer to Hobbes and the Augustinian tradition

Commons · Rousseau and Hobbes: Nature · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Closing the chapter, Douglass argues that Rousseau's principle of natural goodness, set against both Augustinian and Hobbesian pictures of post-lapsarian man, licenses a politics of love of fatherland where Hobbes (and neo-Augustinians like Nicole) could offer only a second-best politics of fear.

This closing section of the chapter summarizes Douglass's reading of the relation between free will, nature and the passions in Rousseau. He says he has dispelled proto-Kantian readings of freedom, virtue and reason, and has drawn attention to the Augustinian context, in which the central problem is cultivating the right type of love. Rousseau's natural goodness is set against both Augustinian and Hobbesian accounts of man; Hobbes and Rousseau agree on the problems (inflamed amour-propre) and on the need to cultivate passions rather than appeal to reason alone, but disagree on the threat (citizens who judge the sovereign, for Hobbes; citizens who are disengaged, for Rousseau) and on which passion politics should engage (fear versus love of fatherland).

Themes

The Augustinian frame. For Douglass, Rousseau generalizes natural amour de soi-même to the body politic and channels socially acquired amour-propre through love of fatherland. By denying that amour-propre is always sinful, Rousseau elides the antithesis between the earthly city of men and the city of God, so the right love can prevail in a well-ordered republic.

Hobbes read as Augustinian (Douglass's careful distinction). Douglass does not claim Hobbes intended an Augustinian view: Hobbes held that the passions are 'in themselves no Sin' and denied that man is naturally evil. But his state of nature resembles the Augustinian post-lapsarian condition, and Rousseau appears to have read him that way. The bridge is Pierre Nicole, a neo-Augustinian who used Hobbes's state of nature and taught that fear of death is the first brake on amour-propre. This is the Hobbism of the French moral tradition rather than a claim about Hobbes's own theology.

Shared problem, divergent diagnosis. Both thinkers hold that political right cannot rest on reason alone and that passions must attach men to the body politic. Hobbes fears individuals who think themselves judges of right and wrong; Rousseau fears civic disengagement and lack of virtue.

Fear as second-best. Rousseau concedes fear has uses but calls its purpose 'not to lead them to do good but to keep them from doing evil'. Douglass, following Nicole's and Rousseau's readings 'at least', says Hobbes's politics, like Pufendorf's morality, is that of fallen man: peace is the best achievable without the right love. Fear or love turns on whether nature is pre-lapsarian goodness or post-lapsarian war.

Positive amour-propre and the secular fall. If man is naturally good, politics should channel passions toward fatherland before corruption, focusing pride on political virtue. Rousseau's fall is secularized: Original Sin is replaced by luxury and entrenched inequality, so the republic suits only the naturally good not yet fully touched by modern social and economic conditions.

Notable passages

The opening summary (c1) states Douglass's thesis on love and the Augustinian context. The second passage (c2) is the most careful attribution: Hobbes denied natural evil yet gave the post-lapsarian picture Rousseau meant to refute. The Nicole passage (c3) supplies the historical channel. The fear passage (c4) contains the quoted contrast between restraining evil and inspiring good. The last two (c5, c6) give the positive programme and its limiting condition: a love-governed republic depends on natural goodness and on the absence of luxury and entrenched inequality.

For World Machines, this rhymes loosely with designs for making a people one (compare the-legible-commonwealth and legibility-as-machine-core), and situates jean-jacques-rousseau against a fear-based order, though Douglass is not a machine theorist and the fit is partial: Rousseau's is an affective, not an informational, unification.
