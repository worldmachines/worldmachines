Freedom Preserved: The General Will as the Citizen's Own Will

Commons · Rousseau and Hobbes: Nature · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Douglass argues that Rousseau preserves each citizen's freedom through equal legislative right and a general will that is the citizen's own will (dependence on law, not on men), and defends against the 'pluralistic reading' the view that Rousseau required the opposition between private and general wills to be overcome, though Du contrat social alone offers only a partial solution.

Douglass asks in what sense Rousseau's social contract preserves the freedom of every individual citizen, given that the collective freedom of the people (the inalienability of sovereignty) is a necessary but not obviously sufficient condition. He answers in three steps. First, equal legislative right: the contract creates no dependence on a superior will, and any representative sovereignty would violate the moral equality of citizens. Second, the general will, read as an objective but indeterminate common interest, is each citizen's own will qua citizen, so obeying law is dependence on things rather than on men (the Emile passage). Third, he takes up the hardest cases, the minority voter and the standing conflict between private and general wills, and argues against the 'pluralistic reading' (Wokler, with Cohen's evidence) that this conflict is healthy. His own position, stated as falling 'somewhere in-between' the pluralistic and the total-unity readings, is that the opposition must be overcome, though not by renouncing all private desires. He notes that Du contrat social does not fully solve the problem; the solution is deferred to the legislator and to the cultivation of morals and love of fatherland in later chapters.

Themes

Equality of legislative right as a condition of freedom. Douglass (reporting Rousseau) holds that no citizen depends on another's will within the sovereign body, because 'each citizen gives himself to all, he gives himself to no one'. Since each vote counts equally, representative sovereignty would introduce inequality of right and so violate the moral equality at the contract's heart. Equality matters because 'freedom cannot last without it'. This is the inversion of Hobbesian authorization that the book builds toward, though this unit does not name Hobbes beyond one phrase (freedom 'in the silence of the law').

The general will as objective common interest, and as one's own will. Douglass reads the general will through interests: it is the rule of the body politic articulating the common interest any people must have to form a society. It is objective (what is not in the common interest is not in accord with it) yet indeterminate (the content varies by people; 'objectivity does not entail universalizability'). Citizens are free under it because it is their own will qua citizen, even if they hold conflicting particular wills. He stresses that Rousseau examines both the nature of the general will and the conditions of its realization, and that failing to separate these causes confusion.

Freedom as dependence on things, not men. The Emile passage is the key statement: dependence on things has no morality and does not harm freedom; dependence on men is without order and corrupts master and slave; the remedy is 'to substitute law for man'. Law that emanates from the will each has as citizen makes obedience 'freedom'. This is Rousseau's problem of placing law above man, in Douglass's account.

Majority voting and the minority citizen. Majority voting is only the best means to realize the general will, not constitutive of it: the general will 'always remains droite' whether or not the vote tracks it. The minority citizen consents in two senses: he wills the procedure (the convention presupposed unanimity once, and he may leave), and, more importantly, he must come to recognize that he was mistaken about the general will's content. Douglass argues against a reading (which he attributes to another scholar) on which the general will's being one's own will does not depend on recognition; for Rousseau, consciousness of freedom is essential. The model is Pedaretus, delighted to find three hundred Spartans worthier than himself.

Against the pluralistic reading. Charvet criticizes Rousseau's solution as vacuous in ignoring the particularity of men's ends. Wokler defends a necessary, healthy tension between general and particular wills. Douglass examines the three passages that might support this: the 'naturally independent' life and freedom passage (silent on the conflict); Book I, Chapter VII on the man/citizen divergence (definitional, and it names the prevalence of private interest as the source of injustice and ruin); and the d'Argenson footnote on the sum of differences (plausibly read as opposition among private interests, not between private and general will). He concedes the footnote can support pluralism and claims only that it is not the sole plausible reading. He then marshals counter-evidence: repressive force should rise as private wills diverge from the general will, the state weakens when the general will 'is no longer the will of all', and 'no particular will can be ordered in the social system'.

Douglass's intermediate position. Agreeing with Cohen only partly, Douglass holds that the general will should be the dominant will but that private wills must not oppose it; overcoming the opposition does not require renouncing all private desires, though Rousseau occasionally suggests it might. This ties to nature as a normative standard (Chapter 2): harmony and order in the social state require no contradiction between private and general wills. Full resolution awaits the legislator, will-formation, and love of fatherland (next chapters), since Rousseau himself says politics and morals cannot be treated separately.

Notable passages

Equal right and no dependence on a superior. The passage arguing that the contract 'disregards any natural inequalities' and that any representative sovereignty would breach moral equality (c1). It contrasts Rousseau with the predecessor accounts of sovereignty the book has examined and links freedom to equality of legislative right.

The Emile passage on two dependences. Quoted at length by Douglass as Rousseau's clearest statement of freedom and dependence, and the source of the law-for-man formula (c2). It is close in spirit to the jean-jacques-rousseau problem of a republic combining the advantages of the natural and civil states, though this unit attaches no world-machine claim to it.

Obedience to self-prescribed law. Douglass's gloss that dependence on law is dependence on one's own will (c3).

The minority citizen's recognition. The most distinctive interpretive move in the unit: recognition, not mere consent, is required, and the minority voter actually changes his opinion (c4). Douglass hedges against readings that make the general will one's own regardless of recognition.

Private will versus general will. Douglass's settlement of the debate with Cohen and his reading of the 'wickedness' fragment (c5), and his closing statement of what freedom requires and what remains unsolved (c6).

Possible resonances (hedged)

Rousseau's general will, as a design for making a people one and its interests coherent to itself, is comparable to legibility programs such as the-legible-commonwealth, and the demand that citizens recognize the general will as their own resembles modern projects of aligned collective will. Douglass makes no such comparison, and Rousseau's account is normative and republican rather than administrative, so the parallel is loose. The unit also sets the terms for Berlin-style criticisms of Rousseau (see isaiah-berlin, counter-enlightenment), since the 'complete civic unity' reading is the one critics attack; Douglass distances himself from that extreme.
