Montesquieu Against Hobbes

Commons · Rousseau and Hobbes: Nature · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Douglass argues that Montesquieu, the leading French political thinker of the mid-eighteenth century, attacked Hobbes on two fronts, the state of nature and the reduction of justice to the sovereign's will, and thereby became a limited but real precursor and possible conduit for Rousseau's own critique of Hobbes.

Douglass takes Montesquieu as the most important French political thinker of the first half of the eighteenth century, and asks how far he opposed Hobbes and passed anti-Hobbesian arguments on to jean-jacques-rousseau. He works through the Troglodytes tale in the Lettres persanes (1721), the opening chapters of De l'esprit des lois (1748), Montesquieu's own later defence of that work, and a passage from the unpublished Mes pensees. His conclusion is deliberately measured. Montesquieu attacked Hobbes on two fronts: he took Hobbes's state of nature to be superfluous or mistaken, and he denied that justice reduces to the will of a sovereign. Beyond those passages there is, Douglass says, little evidence of sustained engagement with Hobbes, so Montesquieu's role in transmitting Hobbes to Rousseau was 'far more limited yet no less significant'.

Themes

The Troglodytes tale is a weak refutation of Hobbes. Douglass reports that the tale is often read as Montesquieu siding with Shaftesbury against Hobbes, on the view that man is naturally virtuous and justice is not artificial. He disputes this. The first half, in which the Troglodytes agree to obey nobody and destroy themselves, actually reinforces Hobbes's war of all against all. The tale is better read as aimed at the doctrine that private vice yields public benefit, associated with Mandeville. It only undermines Hobbes in a limited sense, by showing that virtue, and not self-interest or pleasure, can ground justice. Even so, virtue is rare (two survivors) and a king soon has to be elected. Douglass adds that elsewhere in the Lettres persanes Montesquieu ridiculed the whole practice of founding politics on an exit from the state of nature, which hits modern natural law generally and not Hobbes in particular.

State of nature as primitive condition, with the passions still not historicized. In De l'esprit des lois Montesquieu denies that natural man would be driven to fight: he would feel his weakness and be timid. He also holds that Hobbes gave men motives for attack and defence that arise only once societies exist, a point with antecedents in Cumberland and Barbeyrac and one Douglass calls 'now most commonly associated with Rousseau'. Montesquieu thus consolidates reading the state of nature as man's primitive or original condition, and not merely his condition absent civil law, which was the normative basis of Hobbes's and Pufendorf's case for sovereignty. Douglass is careful about the limits. Montesquieu does not historicize the passions as Rousseau will, and he accepts that a state of war develops alongside society, which prefigures the Discours sur l'inegalite and leaves him telling a Hobbesian story of how positive laws became necessary.

Justice founded on the nature of things, against conventionalism. The central purpose of the opening chapters, on Douglass's reading, is to contest the reduction of all justice and morality to positive law, a position widely attributed to Hobbes. Montesquieu's own defence of his book names Hobbes's a 'terrible system' that, 'like Spinoza', overturns religion and morality. Note that this is Montesquieu's characterization, which belongs to the eighteenth-century 'Hobbism' Douglass elsewhere distinguishes from Hobbes's actual views. Montesquieu holds that relations of justice preceded laws and derive 'from the nature of things'. Here he parts from Pufendorf and Barbeyrac, who ground obligation in the will of a superior, and instead reuses arguments from Malebranche and Leibniz (gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz). The move places Montesquieu inside the natural-law method while rejecting its voluntarism and conventionalism.

Rejection of contractualism and of authorization. Douglass argues that Montesquieu's differences from natural law are larger than the similarities, which are confined mostly to the short first book. Montesquieu rejects genetic accounts of political right and social contract theory 'tout court', because a contract would not really represent people who had no part in making it. In Mes pensees he targets Hobbes's doctrine of authorization, which Douglass calls unique to Leviathan: the claim that if the people authorize the prince, the prince's acts are the people's acts, so the people cannot complain. Douglass stresses that this text was unpublished and so unavailable to contemporaries.

Montesquieu as a forerunner of Rousseau. Douglass's closing claim is that Montesquieu criticized Hobbes on grounds shared with modern natural law, but by rejecting much of its approach he made criticisms that resonated against natural law in general. That makes him an important, if partial, forerunner of Rousseau's critique of Hobbes. The influence path is Douglass's inference from convergence and known influence, and he does not claim direct evidence that Rousseau took these particular arguments from Montesquieu.

Notable passages

The core state-of-nature refutation (c1) opens De l'esprit des lois and gives the savage who trembles and flees as evidence against Hobbesian aggression. Douglass's analysis of it (c2) is the most compact statement of what Montesquieu does and does not anticipate in Rousseau. The Troglodytes passage (c6) is where Douglass most directly corrects a common reading. Montesquieu's own third-person summary of his aim (c3) supplies the 'Hobbism' framing of Hobbes as subverter of morality. The passage on justice and the nature of things (c4) and the authorization passage (c5) carry the second and third lines of attack.

There is a faint resonance with the WMP interest in how political order is made legible and binding, since the authorization argument concerns whether a people can be read as one will through a representative (compare modernity-machine and Legibility). The fit is loose: Douglass is doing intellectual history of natural law and does not speak in machine terms, and Montesquieu's argument is a moral-realist one about justice, not a claim about infrastructure.
