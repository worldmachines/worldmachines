Harmony, Contradiction, and the Hobbesian Moment

Commons · Rousseau and Hobbes: Nature · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Douglass argues that for Rousseau natural goodness means harmony, the absence of contradiction within man and between man and his conditions, that the golden age is a normative model of that harmony rather than a return-worthy past, and that the Hobbesian state of war appears in the Discours sur l'inégalité only as a late product of inflamed amour-propre and inequality, deployed to expose Hobbesian justifications of society as illegitimate.

Douglass takes up two worries about the role of the golden age in jean-jacques-rousseau's thought: that it is an unrecoverable anachronism, and that the Discours sur l'inégalité is internally inconsistent, since it illustrates natural goodness in isolated natural man in part one but then treats social, tribal societies as the golden age in part two. His resolution, offered as his own reading, is that Rousseau's central evil is contradiction (psychological disorder within, or opposition between one's interest and one's condition or others' interests) and that natural goodness just is unity and harmony. The golden age then serves as a normative model of a life without contradiction. The unit closes by reading the apparent 'Hobbesian moment' in Rousseau's genealogy, the war of all against all that follows the rise of amour-propre, as a critical device against Hobbesian justifications of society and not as a concession to Hobbes.

Themes

Contradiction as the fundamental evil. Douglass reports that for Rousseau one of the greatest evils is contradiction, whether between conflicting internal passions or between internal desires and external condition. Happiness requires unity within and without. The theme is said to recur throughout Rousseau, and is put most explicitly in an unpublished fragment on public happiness (man divided is 'torn to pieces'; better to give him wholly to the state or wholly to himself).

Natural goodness is pre-moral harmony, not moral virtue. Douglass's reading is that natural man was good because his few wants were easily met, he was compassionate, and his dormant moral faculties could not clash with his physical side. To say man is naturally good is only to say that nothing in his nature causes conflict within himself or with others. This answers the consistency worry: goodness was exemplified in the isolated monad because that was the simplest case of unity, not because solitude is the ideal. Douglass does not present the reading as one Rousseau states in these terms.

The golden age is a normative model, not a destination. Because harmony can also hold in more developed states where desires are still limited and conjugal and patrimonial love bind people, the small societies of the golden age also live in accordance with nature. Douglass holds that Rousseau uses nature as a normative standard (closer, among his immediate predecessors, to Burlamaqui) and cares more about the polarity of accordance versus contradiction with nature than about nature versus society or artifice. It follows that a legitimate body politic must also be 'in accordance with nature' by not forcing man to alienate his life and freedom. The golden age cannot be regained, but the harmony it models is what an ideal body politic would have to restore. This is a distant relative of the noble-savage trope, but Douglass's Rousseau is not recommending a return.

Amour-propre and the making of contradiction. With agriculture, metallurgy, and inequality of fortunes, amour-propre is aroused, worth becomes the opinion of others, and 'to be and to seem to be' come apart. Douglass argues that amour-propre becomes a vice only when it drives man to live outside himself and sets it in constant opposition to amour de soi-même. Restoration of unity would require a condition in which the two passions no longer pull in opposite directions.

The Hobbesian moment, qualified. Douglass grants that the account is very Hobbesian in one respect: war results from unrestrained passions, and the problems that flow from inflamed amour-propre resemble those Hobbes associates with glory. But he says the differences are more telling. For Rousseau the state of war can arise only after equality has been destroyed, so the passions Hobbes attributed to natural man are the consequence of entrenched inequality. The war is the outcome of a genealogy, not its starting point.

Genealogy as critique. Douglass's thesis is that Rousseau uses the Hobbesian moment to demonstrate the illegitimacy of societies justified from a Hobbesian state of war: the rich exploit the horror of war to persuade the poor into a society that entrenches inequality. Against Hobbes and his followers Rousseau repeatedly argues that society cannot be justified solely by preservation and peace, since that would only hold if natural man were already unequal and warlike. The golden age, not the state of war, is the measure of political states.

There is a loose rhyme with the WMP interest in how an era's deep order is legitimized (see modernity-machine), but Douglass is doing intellectual history of a natural-law dispute, and the connection should not be pushed.

Notable passages

The fragment on contradiction (c1). The clearest statement that human misery is contradiction 'between nature and social institutions, between the man and the citizen', with the remedy of unity.

Natural goodness as pre-moral harmony (c2). Douglass's gloss that natural goodness pertained to the simplicity of physical condition and passions, so that 'The first movements of nature are always good and right.'

Nature as normative standard (c3). Douglass separates the descriptive use of nature (owed to Hobbes and Pufendorf) from the normative use (nearer Burlamaqui), and quotes Emile on not confounding the natural in the savage and civil states.

Amour-propre and contradiction (c4). How commercial society makes each man's interests conflict with others' and divides amour de soi-même from amour-propre.

The Hobbesian moment and its limits (c5). Douglass's central comparison: war from unbridled passions is Hobbesian in form, but it presupposes destroyed equality.

Genealogy against the Hobbesian justification (c6). The payoff: the Hobbesian moment exposes existing political orders as fraud by the rich, and the golden age is the measure for all political states.
