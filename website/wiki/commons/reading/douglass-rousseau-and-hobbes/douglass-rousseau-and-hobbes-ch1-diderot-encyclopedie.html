Hobbes in the Encyclopédie: Diderot, Barbeyrac, and the Hobbes Rousseau Inherited

Commons · Rousseau and Hobbes: Nature · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Douglass argues that the Encyclopédie mostly recycled Barbeyrac's four charges against Hobbes (after the Prades affair made denunciation urgent), that Diderot's 'Droit naturel' nonetheless introduced the general will as a universal answer to a Hobbesian 'violent interlocutor' which Rousseau set out to revise, and that by mid-century Barbeyrac's translations and sociability doctrine had steadily degraded Hobbes's reputation in France.

This unit closes the chapter on the French reception of Hobbes before Rousseau. Douglass's own thesis is that the philosophes, when they turned to politics, deferred to Barbeyrac and Burlamaqui and added little of originality; the Encyclopédie reinforced established criticisms of Hobbes rather than producing new readings. He then singles out Diderot as the exception that matters for Rousseau: the 1753 article 'Citoyen' shows real insight into Hobbes, and the 1755 'Droit naturel' introduces the general will in reply to a 'violent interlocutor' generally identified (following Wokler) as Hobbes, or Hobbes as Diderot understood him. A short section, 'Hobbes Before Rousseau', then summarizes the whole pre-Rousseau image of Hobbism: attacks on his first principles, on justice reduced to the sovereign's will, on the state of war, and on his rejection of natural sociability, with Barbeyrac as the chief shaper of the 1750s Hobbes.

Themes

The Prades affair and defensive denunciation. Early Encyclopédie references to Hobbes concerned materialism and atheism (alongside Epicurus and Spinoza); after the 1752 condemnation of the Abbé de Prades's thesis, which included the charge of false notions of the law of nature and so of Hobbism, later contributors more often denounced Hobbes on morality and politics. Prades himself accepted Hobbes's state of war only as a picture of post-lapsarian man, the neo-Augustinian line of Nicole, and that was still not enough to save him.

Barbeyrac's four charges, repeated. Boucher d'Argis (1755) and Jaucourt ('Morale', 'Injustice', 1765) rehearse Barbeyrac's list: self-preservation as the purpose of society, the state of nature as war, unlimited royal authority in state and religion, and justice determined only by the sovereign's will. Burlamaqui replaces Pufendorf as the culmination of the natural law tradition. Jaucourt's articles on government and sovereignty do not mention Hobbes at all, and borrow Pufendorf's account of sovereignty via Barbeyrac and Burlamaqui. Douglass's judgment is that this simplified Hobbes and evaded the real problem in the tradition, the obligatory force of natural law before positive law; the charge that Hobbes denied natural law holds only if his definition of justice is wrongly taken as coextensive with his laws of nature. This is the Hobbes/Hobbism distinction the book keeps insisting on.

Diderot as partial exception. The Pensées philosophiques (1746) joke about Cartouche the bandit misses Hobbes's distinction between injustice (wrong) and loss, and 'Hobbisme' (1765) was largely lifted from Brucker. But 'Citoyen' correctly sees that Hobbes collapses the distinction between subject and citizen, and uses the moral/physical force distinction that Pufendorf and later Rousseau would also use.

The general will born against Hobbes. In 'Droit naturel' Diderot first insists that free will is necessary for morality, then answers the violent interlocutor not by appeal to free will but by shifting the question of justice from private wills to the general will of the whole species: universal, accessible to reason, applicable in nature and in civil society alike. Douglass reports Riley's claim that Malebranche stands behind both Diderot and Rousseau (Douglass presents this as a possibility, not settled), and concludes that Rousseau found both Diderot's general will and his refutation of Hobbes deficient. Rousseau's own early usage in the Discours sur l'économie politique already confines the general will to particular bodies politic. This is Douglass's setup for later chapters on Du contrat social. The general will as a way of making a people one has a loose kinship with the legibility programmes discussed under the-legible-commonwealth and legibility-as-machine-core, though Douglass is not a machine theorist and nothing in the unit draws that link.

Hobbism before Rousseau. Commentators (Bayle, Barbeyrac) granted Hobbes's genius while attacking his foundations: materialism (Malebranche, Leibniz), justice as will (Malebranche, Leibniz, Montesquieu, against which they set eternal laws or relations prior to positive law), and self-love or amour-propre as the root of justice. The state of nature was once adopted (Nicole, Bayle) and then attacked from two directions: Montesquieu, and the sociability tradition (Pufendorf via Barbeyrac, Cumberland translated 1744, Shaftesbury translated by Diderot 1745). Douglass's conclusion: the Hobbes Rousseau met was a much diminished figure, and it is wrong to say no one engaged Hobbes between Bayle and Rousseau.

Notable passages

Boucher d'Argis and the four charges (c1). The most compact statement of the received Hobbism, and evidence that the Encyclopédie simply relayed Barbeyrac while promoting Burlamaqui.

Douglass's diagnosis of the simplification (c2). The unit's central interpretive claim: the philosophes evaded the question of natural law's obligatory force without civil sanctions by claiming Hobbes denied natural law.

The general will in 'Droit naturel' (c3). Diderot's universal, species-wide general will, the version Rousseau will reject.

Rousseau's departure from Diderot (c4). Douglass marks how, already in the Discours sur l'économie politique, Rousseau moves the general will inside the body politic.

Diderot, Malebranche and Rousseau's double refutation (c5). Douglass's summary of why the Diderot article matters for reading Rousseau against Hobbes.

The degraded Hobbes (c6). The closing claim that Barbeyrac did more than anyone to shape the Hobbes Rousseau encountered. See also jean-jacques-rousseau for the wider Rousseau context.
