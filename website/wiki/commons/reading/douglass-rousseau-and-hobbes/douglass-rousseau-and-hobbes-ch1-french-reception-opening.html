Chapter 1 opening: why Rousseau's Hobbes was the French Hobbes

Commons · Rousseau and Hobbes: Nature · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Douglass opens Chapter 1 by arguing that Rousseau's critique of Hobbes can only be read against the eighteenth-century French and Genevan reception of Hobbes (the 'Hobbism' of Bayle, Barbeyrac, Montesquieu and Diderot), and sets out the selective, contextual method and scope of the chapter.

This short opening of Chapter 1 (headed by an epigraph from Diderot's 'Hobbisme': no one argues with greater rigour, but do not go beyond his first principles if you do not wish to follow him everywhere) frames the whole chapter. Douglass argues that scholarship on Rousseau and Hobbes has been too theoretical and ahistorical, and that Rousseau's critique of Hobbes must be read through the Hobbes as characterized in eighteenth-century France and Geneva, not only the author of De cive and Leviathan as read today. He sketches the earlier seventeenth-century French reception (the Mersenne circle, Sorbière, Verdus), contrasts it with the later political and subversive reputation, and then states his method: selective, contextual, deliberately without a grand narrative, and focused on works written or published in French.

Themes

Against ahistorical Hobbes-Rousseau scholarship. Douglass's own thesis: because few scholars think Hobbes mattered to French authors between Bayle and Rousseau (a view he reports, and implicitly challenges), the Hobbes–Rousseau literature has proceeded at a 'predominantly theoretical and ahistorical level'. That yields a 'less nuanced understanding' of Hobbes's place in Rousseau's thought.

Hobbism as rhetorical weapon. Hobbes's name carried great rhetorical weight, perhaps surpassed only by Spinoza's; the two were presented together as the most subversive philosophers. Responses tended to be authoritative dismissal rather than detailed critique. Protestations against Hobbism often concealed real influence or affinity, some of which, Douglass suggests with a hedge ('appear to have been'), only Rousseau recognized or exposed. This keeps apart the actual Hobbes and the caricature circulating in France.

Rousseau as sophisticated commentator. Douglass resists the temptation to treat Rousseau's critique as naive misrepresentation: seen through the lens of Bayle, Barbeyrac, Montesquieu and Diderot, Rousseau is one of Hobbes's most sophisticated commentators. Rousseau sometimes followed existing interpretations and sometimes rejected or inverted them.

The earlier, scientific Hobbes. Following Quentin Skinner's work on Hobbes's correspondence with Mersenne's circle, mid-century France welcomed Hobbes chiefly for mechanistic and geometrical explanation, with less interest in his politics, though translators such as Sorbière and Verdus praised him as a political authority. Noel Malcolm (reported, agreed with) notes the decline of that non-political reputation from the mid-1650s. Douglass stresses this image differs crucially from the one later French readers developed. (This mechanistic Hobbes is a faint rhyme with the machine vocabulary of world-machines, but Douglass is not a machine theorist and the connection is only verbal.)

Method and scope. The chapter is not a survey of all references to Hobbes. It is selective: thinkers Rousseau named, whose ideas he evidently knew, or whose works were so widely disseminated that ignorance of them is improbable. Douglass explicitly disclaims a 'grand narrative', offers 'threads' that lead to Rousseau, and defines 'French' reception by language (works in French or French translation), while acknowledging that many readers had Latin and other languages.

Notable passages

The gap in scholarship (c1). The opening claim that Rousseau's criticisms of Hobbes in the Discours sur l'inégalité, Du contrat social and Emile are the evidence of concern, alongside the report that no major French thinker engaged Hobbes in depth between Bayle and Rousseau, and the diagnosis of ahistorical scholarship.

Hobbism and Spinoza (c2). Hobbes and Spinoza as paired subversives; dismissal instead of critique; hidden affinities.

Rousseau as sophisticated commentator (c3). The central reframing: the Hobbes at issue is the one characterized by Bayle, Barbeyrac, Montesquieu and Diderot.

Skinner, Sorbière and Malcolm (c4). The earlier scientific reception and its decline.

Purpose and method (c5, c6). The contextual background 'strikingly absent from existing scholarship' and the refusal of a grand narrative.

For World Machines, the resonance is modest: the chapter concerns how a thinker's public image (Hobbism) diverges from his texts, relevant to jean-jacques-rousseau and to the Enlightenment's self-image, but it makes no claims about machines or legibility.
