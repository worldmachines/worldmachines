Introduction: the book's argument in three themes (nature, free will, the passions)

Commons · Rousseau and Hobbes: Nature · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Douglass sets out the book's interpretive thesis that Rousseau's political philosophy is best understood through three interlocking themes, nature as a normative standard, free will, and the ordering of the passions, developed largely in opposition to (and sometimes in affinity with) Hobbes.

Douglass says his aim is twofold: to reconstruct Rousseau's engagement with Hobbes in historical context, and to show that doing so yields a better understanding of Rousseau. He advances an interpretation built on three themes whose relationship "often appears contradictory" and which he aims to render coherent: nature as a normative standard, the centrality of free will, and the cultivation of the passions in the body politic. The unit sketches each theme in turn, positioning Douglass against several scholars (Strauss, Melzer, Cooper, Masters, Simpson, MacLean, Riley) and against proto-Kantian readings of Rousseau.

Themes

Nature as a normative standard (Douglass's thesis, against Strauss, Melzer and Cooper). Douglass reports a reading, popularized by Strauss, on which Rousseau abandoned any basis for political right in nature. He reports that Melzer finds no natural goodness in the Du contrat social, and that Cooper holds nature to be a regulative standard that the citizen of a well-ordered republic does not meet. Douglass argues instead that nature supplies a normative standard throughout Rousseau's philosophy and that a well-ordered republic would satisfy it. Rousseau uses nature descriptively (natural man, the state of nature) and normatively (inalienable gifts of nature; what is just is so by the nature of things). Natural goodness was set against Augustinian and Hobbesian accounts of man as naturally evil, with corruption caused only by certain social relations. The standard is "indeterminate": met by the pre-agricultural societies Rousseau called 'best for man' and potentially by a republic whose institutions forestall corruption. This appeal also lets Rousseau resist the idea, often associated with Hobbes, that justice reduces to convention.

Free will (Douglass's thesis, against Masters, Simpson, MacLean and Riley). Hobbes was a materialist who denied free will (Douglass quotes Leviathan calling talk of it Absurd); Rousseau was a dualist who affirmed it, and Douglass claims his political thought "makes little sense" without it. He defends this against readings that detach the Savoyard vicar's 'Profession de foi' from Rousseau's own views (Masters), pointing to Rousseau's repeated endorsements and to his defence of Emile against Beaumont. An early unpublished fragment professing indifference is explained as a not-yet-formed view, superseded by the Discours sur l'inégalité. Douglass holds that Rousseau's account of legitimate contract presupposes free will as what distinguishes moral from physical force. He reports Riley's worry that cultivating wills through passions clashes with free will as autonomy, and Riley's proposed 'end of political time' resolution, which Douglass finds insufficient given Rousseau's pessimism about the body politic. The challenge is to show that cultivating passions is consistent with preserving freedom, which requires dispelling proto-Kantian readings.

The passions and reason (affinity with Hobbes). For Rousseau, reason's role is to order the passions and keep them from conflicting, not to master them, which Douglass says is closer to Hobbes and later Hume than to Kant. So nature and free will are best read in opposition to Hobbes, while the passions show notable affinities with him; both stressed ordering the passions to secure political unity.

Amour-propre and a secularized fall (Douglass's reading). Rousseau gave amour-propre a more specific sense than earlier French moralists but kept its neo-Augustinian moral and theological connotations. The Discours sur l'inégalité is presented as a secularized Augustinian fall, with Original Sin recast as entrenched inequality and luxury, which inflame amour-propre against amour de soi-meme. If a republic were built on pre-lapsarian passions, amour-propre could be brought into accord with amour de soi-meme and the citizen's life could accord with nature. Douglass's cautious framing is that this is a preliminary sketch to be defended in the book.

Notable passages

The opening statement (c1) gives the three-theme thesis and its aim of coherence. The passage on Strauss, Melzer and Cooper (c2) states the central claim that a well-ordered republic can meet nature's standard, and the following passage (c3) explains natural goodness against Augustinian and Hobbesian pessimism and the indeterminacy of the standard. The free will passage (c4) sets Hobbes's materialism against Rousseau's dualism and lists the scholarly neglect Douglass aims to correct. c5 gives the reason/passion relation and the Hobbes-Hume-Kant alignment. c6 gives the secularized Augustinian story and the summary of the republican vision. Resonance with World Machines is slight: the ordering of passions to secure political unity is loosely comparable to designs for making a people one (see modernity-machine, Legibility), though Douglass is not a machine theorist and the fit is only partial. On the person, see jean-jacques-rousseau; the 'natural man' here is not the noble-savage trope, since Rousseau's state of nature is historicized and man's constitution is 'irrevocably altered'.
