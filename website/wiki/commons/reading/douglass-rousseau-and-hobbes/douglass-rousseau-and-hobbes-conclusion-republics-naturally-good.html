Republics for the Naturally Good: Douglass's Conclusion on the Limits of Rousseau's Republic

Commons · Rousseau and Hobbes: Nature · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Douglass concludes that Rousseau's republican vision was suited only to naturally good peoples not yet corrupted by commerce, luxury and inequality, and so was never a live option for large modern commercial states, though his critique of commercial society remained pointed enough to demand Adam Smith's reply.

Douglass closes the book by returning to Rousseau's ‘Lettre à Mirabeau’ dilemma (the most austere democracy or the most perfect Hobbism) and asking whether Rousseau's political proposals suited ‘the children of Adam’. His answer, which he presents as following from the interpretation advanced throughout the study, is no. Rousseau's rejection of the Hobbesian state of nature and of Hobbesian man means his republic was designed for men not yet fully corrupted by modern commercial society. Douglass supports this with Rousseau's criteria for receptive peoples in Du contrat social, the Corsican preference for agriculture over commerce, the small-republic restriction, and the shifting hopes for Geneva. He then separates the obsolescence of Rousseau's republican vision from the continuing force of his critique of commercial society.

Themes

The Mirabeau dilemma revisited (Douglass's thesis). Rousseau accused the Physiocrats of studying reason rather than the ‘play of the passions’ and proposing systems for ‘the people of Utopia’. Douglass grants that Rousseau himself was centrally concerned with the passions and with how institutions could cultivate virtuous citizens, and that he tried to deflect the charge of utopianism (Lettres écrites de la montagne). Douglass nevertheless concludes that the republic was unsuitable for the children of Adam. Rousseau's own pessimism in the Mirabeau letter, where a government placing law above man ‘will never be found’ and the best hope is the most perfect Hobbism, is read as consistent with this. Douglass hedges that whether such pessimism was warranted is a separate question.

Republics only for the naturally good. Hobbes's state of nature is populated by the children of Adam, while Rousseau's republic is for men yet to be corrupted by the pursuit of private wealth and utility. Luxury and commerce foster individualistic passions incompatible with the passions and mores a well-ordered republic needs. This is the book's central contrast between nature (Rousseau's natural goodness vs. Hobbes's state of war) and the passions (individualistic vs. civic).

Against the moderate-and-modern reading (Hanley). Douglass argues against Ryan Patrick Hanley's reading of Rousseau, alongside Adam Smith, as ‘both moderate and modern’. He concedes two of Hanley's points: that Rousseau grounded proposals in specific social and economic conditions, and that he saw little hope of reviving ancient virtue among the already corrupted (the ‘when a man is dead there is no point calling Doctors’ remark on sumptuary laws). He argues that these very points show the republican vision was inapplicable to large commercial states such as France and England.

Criteria for receptive peoples; Corsica; agriculture against commerce. Du contrat social gives criteria for a people capable of receiving good laws: a common bond, self-sufficiency, and a mix of stability and docility. Rousseau thought Corsica the only European country left that might meet them, and his Corsican proposals insist on an agricultural economy. Agriculture and commerce are incompatible: finance and taste for wealth breed inequality and extinguish love of fatherland (already argued in the Discours sur l'économie politique).

Prevention, not remedy; small republics; Geneva. Everything ‘beyond physical necessity is a source of evil’, so the republic's economy is designed to prevent wealth accumulation. Freedom, once lost, is not recovered. The proposals are for small republics, and also for low levels of economic development. In 1755 Rousseau still held out hope for Geneva because its citizens were not yet irredeemably corrupt, but became pessimistic in the 1760s. By the logic of this argument, once luxury arrives the passions turn individualistic and ‘fatherland and citizen’ should be ‘effaced from modern languages’.

Obsolete vision, live critique. Douglass distinguishes Rousseau's republican vision (obsolete for modern states) from his republican critique of commercial society, which its defenders, above all adam-smith, felt bound to answer. Since Rousseau offered no route back for modern states, the response was all the more necessary. The Physiocrats were blinded by faith in reason, whereas Rousseau's attention to the passions left him aware of how hard it would be to revive republican ideals among ‘the fallen, Hobbesian, men of modernity’.

Notable passages

The opening passage (c1) restates the Mirabeau dilemma and gives Douglass's verdict that Rousseau's republican vision was ‘ultimately unsuitable for the children of Adam’. The second (c2) ties this to the rejection of the Hobbesian state of nature and argues against Hanley. The third (c3) sets out Rousseau's criteria for receptive peoples and the Corsican and agricultural argument. The fourth (c4) covers small republics, Geneva and the irreversibility of corruption. The last (c5) separates obsolete vision from continuing critique and closes the book.

Resonances with World Machines

The argument that a form of political order works only for peoples with a common bond, self-sufficiency and shared civic passions, and cannot survive commerce and luxury, loosely rhymes with WMP thinking on how the modernity-machine reshapes the populations it governs, and with the republican-virtue tradition of niccolo-machiavelli. The fit is partial: Douglass offers no machine theory, and his claim is a historical-interpretive one about Rousseau's intended audience. The Rousseau–Smith exchange over commercial society places both in the wider debate that includes adam-smith and the scottish-enlightenment. Rousseau's natural goodness is also not the noble-savage trope, since it is a fragile, preventable-corruption condition of citizens in small states. See also jean-jacques-rousseau.
