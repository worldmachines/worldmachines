A Stroll Around Town (1415 CE)

Commons · Majapahit: The History of an Empire That Was · updated 25 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Drawing on the Ming translator-official Ma Huan's eyewitness notes from Zheng He's fourth treasure-fleet voyage, the chapter reconstructs a walking tour of 1413–15 Majapahit — its markets, keris-craft, unwalled cityscape, hydraulic royal temple, religious pluralism, and a witnessed sati — as the only foreign first-person account of the empire at its height.

The chapter reconstructs Majapahit through the eyes of Ma Huan, a Chinese Muslim translator who sailed with Admiral Zheng He's fourth treasure-fleet expedition (1413) and left the only surviving foreign eyewitness account of the empire — walking from the port of Tuban through Gresik and Surabaya to the capital itself, then touring its markets, temples, crossroads, and royal palace before witnessing a widow's sacrificial burning and glimpsing King Wikramawardhana in person.

Themes

An anomalous source. Ma Huan is less a soldier than an "anthropologist" avant la lettre — fluent in Arabic and Persian, systematically recording weather, diet, custom, law, and economy across three of Zheng He's seven voyages. His notebook, later published as The Overall Survey of the Ocean's Shores, is the chapter's entire evidentiary spine: nearly every observation about daily Majapahit life (the keris taboo, the head-touching prohibition, market prices, the king's dress) traces back to his own footnoted jottings, making this unit as much a portrait of an observer's method as of the place observed.

A capital that needs no walls. Ma Huan is struck that Majapahit has no defensive perimeter — he reads it as a "sign of strong, confident rulers." Instead the city organizes itself around ritual and ceremonial nodes — the catuspatha crossroads with its Ganesha statue, the alun-alun square, the red-brick palace gates — rather than a hard legible boundary. Authority here is projected through consecrated infrastructure and processional space rather than fortification, a different register of ordering the world than the wall-and-ledger legibility of contemporaneous world machines elsewhere; see legibility-as-machine-core.

Hydraulic kingship. The Candi Tikus bathing temple, discovered only in 1914 under a rat infestation, turns out to be a literal engineering of divine legitimacy: an irrigation system channels water through a miniature replica of holy Mount Semeru before feeding rice paddies whose harvest only the king's family may eat. Sanctity is piped, not merely proclaimed — infrastructure made to carry cosmological authority is a recurring motif worth flagging against legibility-as-machine-core.

Plural but not boundless tolerance. Ma Huan finds Buddhists, Hindus, and a resident Muslim trading community coexisting without apparent friction — a genuine multiplicity of ontologies resonant with divergence-machine variety-over-standardization. But his tolerance has a limit: unidentified ascetic or animist figures, wild-haired and minimally clothed, are read by him as practitioners of "devil worship," showing that even a relatively catholic observer's framework has an edge past which unfamiliar variety reads as threat rather than diversity.

The violence under the color. Against the chapter's vivid sensory catalogue of markets, food, dance, and moonlight processions sits a sharply reported darker register: judicial execution by rattan-binding and rib-stabbing, debt bondage, corpses left to river or dog, and — narrated in Ma Huan's own words — a sati in which enslaved or bonded women dance themselves into a funeral pyre. The chapter does not resolve or soften this tension; it simply lets the eyewitness's own account carry it.

Female authority at court. The inner palace sanctum is guarded not by men but by the bhayangkari, an elite female guard corps, a detail Ma Huan — well-traveled across the Islamic and Sinic worlds — finds unprecedented. The text ties this to the historical prominence of Queens Tribhuwanatunggadewi and Gayatri a century earlier, suggesting an institutionalized (not incidental) female presence in Majapahit statecraft.

Notable passages

The keris description (the knife every male carries "from little boys of three years to old men of a hundred years," wrought in steel with gold, rhinoceros-horn, or ivory hilts) is Ma Huan's most granular material-culture observation, and the companion note on the taboo against touching someone's head — punishable by knife — shows him registering local sensibilities as serious social law rather than curiosity. The unwalled-city observation is the chapter's clearest structural claim about how Majapahit projected power. The Candi Tikus irrigation passage is the unit's best example of infrastructure literally encoding religious-political authority. The sati passage, quoted at length in Ma Huan's own words, is the chapter's most morally weighted moment and the fullest verbatim record of an actual ritual observed rather than reported secondhand. Finally, the physical description of King Wikramawardhana — unkempt-headed or crowned with gold leaves, barefoot, dagger tucked in his waistband, riding an elephant or ox-cart — is the only close eyewitness physical description of a Majapahit monarch to survive from any source, foreign or domestic.
