Conservatives and the Politicization of Religion before 1848

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Clark argues that pre-1848 conservatism was an oppositional defence of natural, particularist, devolved authority against both liberal constitutionalism and bureaucratic modernization, and that the era was not one of secularization but of religious sentiment coming unstuck from ecclesiastical authority and flowing into every political camp.

Two sections of Clark's chapter on the 'conjectures of order'. The first shows that conservatism was not simple loyalty to the state: conservatives often opposed state authority, and their thinkers (Savigny, Haller, de Maistre, Adam Müller, Lancizolle, Gerlach, Donoso Cortés) defended a 'natural', paternalistic, historically grown and devolved order against the social contract, written constitutions and centralizing reform. The second traces how, after the Great Disruption, religious feeling detached from church institutions and was recombined with nationalism (Mickiewicz), radicalism and socialism (Lamennais, Considerant, Cabet, Mazzini), liberal constitutionalism (Constant), noble Pietism, ultramontane political Catholicism and dissenting movements (German-Catholics, Friends of Light). Clark's conclusion is that this was 'not in any straightforward sense an era of secularization'.

Themes

Conservatism as opposition, not status quo. Clark opens by denying that conservatives simply endorsed the current state of things. Prussian conservatives resisted official reformers, Baden nobles boycotted a constitution, French legitimists opposed the July Monarchy (whose liberal elite became 'in a sense, the new conservatives'), and in 1848 the Spanish absolutist paper La Esperanza cheered the fall of Louis-Philippe alongside French radicals. The 'current state of things' could change and strand its partisans.

The critique of the social contract and of abstraction. Haller attacked Hobbesian contract theory as historically unspecifiable ('where was the contract archived?') and as implying that power delegated could be withdrawn; authority was instead natural, like paternal authority. Clark notes that liberals such as Martínez de la Rosa and Dahlmann also rejected an anarchic state of nature: 'Martínez and Dahlmann found reason in the state of nature, Haller found power.' De Maistre (see joseph-de-maistre) saw revolution as a providential lesson in which actors are swept along by 'circumstances', and written constitutions as a symptom of instability; the resonance with edmund-burke and the counter-enlightenment-and-nationalism tradition is real, though Clark does not draw it explicitly here.

Nested, non-standardized order versus the universal state. Müller's image of the Hausvater at the head of concentric 'states' (family, guild, city, principality) and of revolutionaries as taxidermists stuffing cadavers with 'imaginary rights' is a compact statement of anti-legibility: conservatives defended particular liberties against levelling, homogenizing uniformity. This half-fits the WMP frame of legibility-as-machine-core and modernity-machine (compare contested-uniformity-bayly), though these conservatives were often also the special pleading of an agrarian elite, as Clark stresses, so the defence of opacity was interested.

Devolution as politics and geopolitics. Carlist resistance to centralization, Gioberti's federalism versus Mazzini's unitarism, and the Swiss cantons show conservatism and liberalism as rival conceptions of 'liberty' rather than freedom versus despotism. Metternich's weak confederal arrangements (Germany, Switzerland, Italy) were meant to keep the centre weak and Europe stable, and made coordinated national reform 'virtually impossible'.

Fragility and contagion as conservative diagnosis. Lancizolle saw the 1830 'magic words' from Paris stirring the world, and predicted that liberal regimes, lacking a legitimating logic and dependent on 'armed masses', would be swept away by the forces that raised them. Clark calls this 'hostile, but not stupid' and notes the irony that a divinely ordained order looked so vulnerable. The political/social revolution distinction, which radicals read as hope and some conservatives as a reason to concede nothing, hardens the idea that 1789 never ended. Revolution is tied to the sin of pride (Gerlach), while Donoso Cortés could not explain how a tactical struggle could become restoration.

Religion unstuck from ecclesiastical authority. Clark's central claim in the second half: after the Great Disruption religion 'passed like a spiritual power surge across Europe', energizing political commitments in new combinations because it had been partly loosed from theological and ecclesiastical institutions. Religious framing raises stakes, tying politics to collective salvation and perdition, and Clark quotes Harold Bloom's 'spilled poetry'.

Prophetic re-inscription of scripture in politics. Mickiewicz's Books of the Polish Nation casts Poland as the crucified 'Christ among nations'; Lamennais, a former ultramontane and papalist, broke with Rome after Mirari Vos and produced Paroles d'un croyant, a publishing sensation that fused religious language with the Social Question and drew Gregory XVI's Singulari nos. Weitling promised a socialist messiah. This connects with romanticism-and-political-danger and nationalism, and with the sacralized nation in benedict-anderson's frame, though Clark's emphasis is on scriptural form and pastoral reception rather than imagined community.

The religious left, and liberal religious sentiment. French radicals (Saint-Simon's New Christianity, Considerant, Cabet, Blanc) separated 'true' Christianity from the institutional Church, resonating with popular Catholics who, per the clergy, exercised 'a kind of popular sovereignty with respect to religion'. Constant distinguished 'free' from 'sacerdotal' religions and thought religious sentiment favoured liberty; Mazzini urged Pius IX to become a 'priest of love' and fused God, people and nation into a 'sacred trinity'.

Institutional revival alongside disembodied faith. Clark insists institutional religion was not dying: clerically disciplined Catholic revival (the 1844 Trier pilgrimage of half a million), ultramontanism, political Catholicism (Görres) and Pietist east-Elbian noble evangelicalism grew in parallel with the German-Catholics (60,000 members by 1847, tied to Blum and Struve) and the Friends of Light, 'halfway between sect and party', concentrated in areas that later became radical centres. Religious revival and secularizing initiatives 'inadvertently shaped and reinforced each other'. Clark ends by imagining, without supplying, a colour-coded map of religious sentiment against political orientation, and predicts only 'an opalescence of hiatus, differentiation and flux'.

Notable passages

Conservatives against the state (c1). The opening paragraph dismantles the assumption that conservatism means the defence of the existing order, giving Savigny's appeal to the 'consciousness of the people', Baden's boycotting nobles, and the legitimist and Esperanza cases. It is the key to reading conservatism as a position within a contested field rather than a fixed pole.

Müller on the nested order (c2). The most vivid conservative statement of the anti-universalist case: an 'absolute state, an absolute law, [and] an absolute reason' is a chimera, and the republic 'one and indivisible' violates nature and God by destroying guilds, cities and provinces. Useful for WMP as an account of why standardization was experienced as violence by those who benefited from local opacity.

Lancizolle's diagnosis (c3). Contagion from Paris in 1830, the contradiction between 'the people' and a narrow franchise, and the prediction that liberalism would be swept away by armed masses. It anticipates the liberal-radical fissure that Clark treats later as a driver of 'entropy', though here it is a conservative's anticipation, not Clark's own account.

Religion as spiritual power surge (c4). Clark's thesis statement for the religion section, with a grid metaphor that invites (with hedging) comparison to how an order's infrastructure can be overloaded by a new energy; that comparison is ours, not Clark's.

Sentiment without sacerdotalism (c5). Clark's synthesis of the French left, Constant's distinction and the priest from Bayasse: religion unstuck from authority, with popular believers choosing beliefs and rituals for themselves.

Not an age of secularization (c6). The closing empirical sweep, from Pest priests to the Zurich culture war and Metternich's suspicion of Hungarian Protestants, ending with the claim that revival and secularization fed each other. Relevant to history-machine-style reading of distributed components assembling a shift, and a caution against the simple Modernity-as-disenchantment story in modernity-machine; compare empires-of-religion-bayly for the global side of religious revival.
