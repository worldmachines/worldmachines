Berlin and Milan in March 1848: news, crowds, and the withdrawal of armies

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Clark narrates how news of Paris and Vienna set off the March 1848 insurrections in Berlin and Milan, where improvised publics, rumour, hesitant courts and social divisions inside the crowd turned a crisis of confidence into the withdrawal of two armies, with the revolution making revolutionaries rather than the reverse.

Two linked narratives of the March 1848 uprisings. In Berlin, news of Louis Philippe's abdication and then of Metternich's fall pulls the public into cafes, reading clubs and the improvised 'parliament' of the Tents. A crowd and a garrison contest the city's space through ritual and provocation. The king concedes reforms on 17 March. Two accidental musket discharges on 18 March, taken by the crowd as a massacre, unleash a night of barricade fighting with heavy losses. Frederick William then orders the troops out and emerges with his prestige largely intact. In Milan, seen partly through the diary of the Austrian envoy Hübner, a city already alienated from imperial rule (the tobacco boycott, a native civil service that is 'terrorized' or 'traitors') rises on 18 March on the strength of Vienna's news. The Milanese fight without a plan, a committee or the rumoured Piedmontese rifles. They improvise communications, force Radetzky to withdraw on 22-23 March, and enjoy a brief harmony of 'brotherhood' before the narratives diverge.

Themes

Contagion through news and print. Paris is the signal for democrats and radicals; Vienna is the signal that shakes the Prussian court. An extra edition of the Vossische Zeitung, newspapers read aloud from chairs, and a news sheet posted in a Milan cafe carry the shock. Cattaneo adds that real events were 'amplified by an electric current of rumour'. The synchrony is informational: each capital reads the previous one as an omen. The shock also arrives late and thin, as when Hübner is left without instructions from a Metternich who may no longer be Chancellor.

Institutions improvised under pressure. Berlin's Municipal Assembly, already a liberal channel, 'began to mutate into a protest rally'. The Tents took on 'the contours of an improvised parliament', with voting, resolutions and elected delegations. In Milan the emergent executive around Mayor Casati was 'merely an extension of the municipal administration', composed of conservative civil servants. Existing local bodies become vehicles of revolution, and the insurgents improvise ziplines, an orphans' postal service and balloons. Clark's line 'it was the revolution that made revolutionaries, not the other way around' is the note's strongest general claim.

Fissures inside the crowd. By 13 March workers at the Tents drew up their own petition for a Ministry of Labour, while on 18 March the wealthy at the front celebrated and 'proletarians and workers' at the back said the concessions were of no use to 'poor people'. Boerner sees an implicit division of labour in which artisans fight and the bourgeoisie, who own the houses, offer passive support. This anticipates the liberal-radical and social-versus-political splits that Clark later treats as the entropy of the revolution. In Milan the parallel split is between Cattaneo's republicanism and the Piedmontese monarchists, whom he accuses of solving 'only the problem of war'.

Court hesitation and the collapse of confidence. Berlin's leadership is divided between Pfuel's tact and Prince William's hard line, and the king is buffeted by conflicting advice. He concedes too late, misreads the mood ('you always see things too negatively!'), and then withdraws the army, a decision Clark judges shrewd because it preserved his reputation. Hübner's diary supplies the Austrian mirror image: a governor on holiday, officers in the Scala, a civil service that no longer obeys. Read with gramsci-gap in mind, this is a moment when old authority has lost its grip before anything new exists, though Clark describes a specific tactical retreat rather than a general interregnum.

Legibility and illegibility of the crowd. Both armies are trained for a formal, legible confrontation (the riot act, three drum signals, the cavalry advance) that crowds learn to game. The crowd 'thinking with its ears' misreads two accidental shots as a massacre. The state's attempt to correct the record with a linen banner ('A Misunderstanding!') is futile. Milan's insurgents are 'invisible and hidden behind walls', a fighting style that the Austrian forces cannot resolve. This touches the WMP concern with how a public becomes readable or unreadable to the state, cf. legibility-as-machine-core, though Clark does not frame it this way.

Structure imposes similar solutions. Boerner rejects the idea that 'Polish or French emissaries' taught Berlin to build barricades: the city's material texture imposed the solution. This is a small but explicit argument against conspiracy explanations, and a hedge against reading synchrony as coordination. The Milanese uprising is likewise 'a conspiracy without conspirators'.

Nation and its complications. Cattaneo argues that Lombardy was conquered, not merely occupied, and that Italy was 'the captive' of 'the retrograde ideas of its own princes'. The Milan section links to nationalism as a contested political programme rather than a settled identity, and the euphoric closing tableau (strangers embracing, 'all were included in the bond of brotherhood') shows the fleeting unity Clark elsewhere sees dissolving.

Notable passages

Berlin's electric shock. The opening reports the Vossische Zeitung extra and the street reaction, with the witness recall that 'it was as if people had received an electric shock' (c1). It sets the news-driven, synchronous frame.

Ritualized confrontation. The crowd and the soldiery as 'collective antagonists' in a struggle for city space, with the riot act and drum signals turned into a game the crowd could replay (c2).

The Schlossplatz on 18 March. The king's announcements, the class geography of the square, the chant 'Soldiers out!', and the cavalry advance (c3), followed by the accidental shots and the futile linen banner (c4). Together they show a crisis produced by misreading and contingency more than by intent.

Boerner on houses and barricades. The 'implicit division of labour' between workers and bourgeoisie (c5), the passage most relevant to class fissures within the revolution.

Cattaneo's rumours. The exchange about non-existent rifles and a non-existent directing committee, closing on the claim that the revolution made the revolutionaries (c6). It is the note's best statement of the improvisation and the gap between rumour and reality.

The king's withdrawal, Prince William's outburst and the death toll (Berlin over 300 protesters and about 100 soldiers dead, against about fifty in Vienna) are in the unit but not curated; the Milan improvisations (zip lines, balloons, orphan couriers) are likewise only summarized here.
