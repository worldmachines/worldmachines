After 1848: exile, dispersal and the foreign country of the present

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
After 1849 the defeated revolutionaries were scattered as exiles whose families were punished with them, and Clark traces their diverging inner and political paths, ending in Belgioioso's turn to women's education, Bach against Széchenyi, and Jókai's returned poet who is a stranger in the post-revolutionary present.

Clark opens the chapter on the aftermath with the human wreckage of the revolutions: tens of thousands of political refugees, punished as families, scattered across Ottoman, Swiss, British, American and colonial destinations. He follows how individual exiles found their way out of revolution (persisting, adapting, withdrawing, converting) and closes with three case studies of post-revolutionary inner life: Cristina di Belgioioso's turn to women's education, the Bach/Széchenyi stand-off, and Mór Jókai's novel Political Fashions, whose returned poet Pusztafi is an exile in his own country.

Themes

Repression exported people rather than only silencing them. Clark gives figures (50,000+ from the Italian states, 80,000 from Baden in the 1850s, about 10,000 French republicans) and stresses that states actively financed or encouraged emigration of the compromised, so that one historian speaks of an 'exiled generation'. Repression here is a circulation of bodies across jurisdictions, and refugees were moved on again by Piedmont and the Second Empire.

Refugee policy as reputation politics. The Ottoman refusal to extradite is presented as partly Tanzimat-reformist principle and partly an attempt to project an 'enlightened and progressive' image to Britain and France, a bet that paid off in 1853. Clark's claim, not a general law: liberal treatment of refugees carried geopolitical returns.

Exile as collective punishment, borne unevenly. Formally aimed at individual men, exile fell on wives and children (Kossuth's family, Guyon's, Perczel's), and women carried the burden of survival in exile. The petitioners' question about why the family should be the practical unit of punishment exposes a gap between the state's stated legal logic and its practice.

Dispersal of energies, fragmentation of purpose. 'The dispersal of revolutionary energies now manifested itself as a dispersal of people.' Exile deepened prior rifts (Kossuth's rivals, Italian pamphlet wars). Kossuth's proposed Hungarian colony near Izmir shows the attempt to preserve a 'corporate being' against atomization. Some exiles rebuilt networks (clubs, newspapers, Deroin's correspondence); others found new careers (Schurz).

Many roads out. Mazzini 'kept running the same political algorithms', Lamartine accommodated ('I look the other way'), Hess turned to Zionism, Proudhon drew a lasting hostility to the state. Bach, a former democrat, became the architect of neo-absolutism, so the revolution's beneficiaries could embody counter-revolution.

Politics displaced, not abandoned. Belgioioso, after the Roman siege, turned from institutional politics to literary work and the education of women. Clark's reading is that this substituted one form of politics for another, outside 'the masculine machinery of political reform'.

Homelessness in the post-revolutionary present. Füster's parody of Arndt's anthem and Jókai's Pusztafi both express estrangement from a settled new order. Clark reads Jókai as capturing the 'watershed character' of the revolutions, while noting the novel abstains from adjudicating whether the fault lies with the returned hero or with a society that suppressed memory.

Notable passages

Ottoman liberality (c1). The 14 September 1849 memorandum argued that extradition 'would harm the reputation of the Ottoman Empire'; Clark ties it to pro-Ottoman opinion in the Crimean crisis. Worth attributing carefully: the memo's words are Ottoman officials', the reputational-calculus reading is Clark's.

Scale of exile (c2). A concentrated inventory of displacement and the states' managed-emigration devices. Loosely resonates with the WMP interest in how an order handles what it cannot integrate, though Clark makes no such generalization; the pattern of expulsion across a continent of separate states is his own descriptive claim.

Family as unit of punishment (c3). The clearest case of the gap between formal legal theory (individual offenders) and administrative practice, and of gendered burden.

Belgioioso's pivot (c4). The chapter's most explicit argument about the persistence of politics in a different register. It sits alongside intellectual-vs-revolutionary only loosely: Belgioioso is neither withdrawing nor turning revolutionary but relocating her politics.

Füster's fatherland (c5). Arndt's 'What is the German's fatherland?' inverted into a catalogue of expulsions. It bears on nationalism: the national idea fails to find any territorial home in the post-1849 settlement, though this is a lament by a radical, not Clark's own thesis.

Pusztafi, the exile at home (c6). Clark's concluding gloss on Jókai. It rhymes with the interregnum feeling of the gramsci-gap (old world dead, new one uncomfortable), though it is an individual's experience of a settled counter-revolutionary order rather than a structural interregnum, and Clark does not frame it so. Bach as former democrat turned centralizing interior minister is a small instance of the modernizing counter-revolution Clark develops elsewhere in the book, though this unit only states it in outline.
