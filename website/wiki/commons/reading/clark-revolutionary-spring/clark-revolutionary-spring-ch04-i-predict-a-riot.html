Palermo, January 1848: the Sicilian revolution as first detonation

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Clark reconstructs the Palermo revolution of January 1848 and its Neapolitan spillover, arguing that a poster-triggered riot succeeded because the Bourbon regime lost confidence in itself, foreign consuls and navies constrained and abetted the insurgents, and agrarian change had loosened peasant deference.

Clark opens the chapter on the detonations of 1848 with Sicily, where the first revolution of the year began in Palermo on 12 January and, against the odds, drove the Bourbon garrison out by early February. The unit reconstructs a revolt triggered by a fictitious poster and the anticipation of a riot, set against months of "seething anticipation" (Messina's failed rising of September 1847, demonstrations after the Swiss liberal victory, Pius IX's example). It then explains the insurgents' success through regime irresolution and collapsing confidence, the mediating and arming role of foreign consuls and navies (Britain above all, also the United States), Austrian alarm at the fragile "European concert", the fear and plebeian violence that ran alongside elite liberal leadership, the agrarian changes that loosened peasant deference, and the spillover into Naples, where Ferdinand II promised a constitution.

Themes

Happenstance and anticipation as trigger. Bagnasco's placard invoked a "revolutionary committee" that did not exist; the announcement of a revolt drew curious crowds whose presence supplied the occasion, and Clark sets Lamartine's later remark about the Paris boulevards beside it. Clark generalizes cautiously: "an element of happenstance" attached to every spring revolt's moment of combustion. Read with care, this is a claim about how expectation itself can function as a mechanism, not a claim that the outbreaks were causeless; the unit stresses that Palermo "was no bolt out of the blue."

Too little too late. Ferdinand II's incremental measures (tax relief, ministerial replacement, the 11 August 1847 edict) crossed a threshold after which "concessions no longer mollify but only embolden the political opposition." Individually easy to master, the tumults collectively "suggested an irreversible momentum."

Contagion through connectedness. The cries of "Viva Pio Nono!" in Messina, the Piedmontese and Roman liberal letter widely reprinted in the press, the Swiss news setting off demonstrations, and the Neapolitan king's failed attempt to "disconnect" his kingdom from the peninsula show political life propagating through print, example and rumour. Clark gives Bagnasco's placard as directly motivated by the ascendancy of Pius IX. Marginally resonant with the WMP interest in contagion and synchrony, though Clark makes no systemic claim here.

Regime failure of nerve. The garrison was better prepared and reinforced by 6,000 troops, yet withdrew. Clark attributes this to friendlessness among elites and masses, hesitancy (de Sauget, possibly for political reasons Clark treats as insufficient to explain the outcome on its own), and a court that argued for "saving the monarchy by satisfying the demands of the age." His formulation: "the collapse of the regime's confidence in itself preceded and facilitated the revolution raised against it." This is a nice case of an official ideology and apparatus outliving its own legitimacy, loosely rhyming with the gramsci-gap (old legitimacy dying), though Clark is describing a single court and army, not an era.

Great-power entanglement. Foreign consuls acted as a "kind of moral tribunal"; war steamers were "floating chunks of extra-territorial sovereignty." Britain mediated, armed insurgents, and defended the 1812 constitution; Palmerston favoured Sicily but warned against satellite status; the US sought base rights; Austria pinned hopes on a joint démarche until Paris rose ten days after Metternich's letter. Clark's point is that local tumult was "caught up ... in the gears of the relations among states."

Social fear and plebeian violence. Revolution combined elite liberal leadership with armed craftsmen's corporations and peasant squadre; Malvica's contemptuous description is his, not Clark's. Elite participants (per Edgcumbe and Crispi) joined once "the mob became more to be feared than the government," a pattern Clark says will recur across Europe and that anticipates the later liberal-radical split. Clark ties peasant disinhibition to post-1815 land reform, which produced a new landholding class that eroded the old debt-bound deference.

Spillover. News from Sicily brought Naples' cafe liberals and lazzaroni onto the streets; a peasant uprising in Cilento panicked the government; on 27 January Ferdinand promised a constitution.

Notable passages

Poster and crowd (c1). The placard, the fictitious committee and the curious crowds show a revolution generated by anticipation, with Lamartine as a parallel witness.

Threshold of reform (c2). A compact statement of the too-little-too-late dynamic.

Loss of confidence (c3). Clark rejects blaming de Sauget alone and locates the collapse in the court itself; the most portable analytical sentence of the unit.

Foreigners and warships (c4). The consuls' moral authority and the extra-territorial gunboats as spaces of negotiation and refuge give the Sicilian revolution an international dimension that Clark elsewhere develops into the global 1848. This is geopolitics as scaffolding around a local revolt; nothing here is a machine theory.

Land reform and violence (c5). The Bourbon attempt to build a loyal middle class by dismantling noble jurisdiction and commons instead produced rent-collector landowners and a less deferential peasantry, a reforming state whose modernizing measures yielded illegible and ungovernable consequences. Loosely related to legibility-hardens-hierarchy-bayly in spirit (state reform reshaping rural hierarchies) though Clark does not use that vocabulary.

Fear of the mob (c6). Elite liberals feared victory of the people more than the Bourbon troops, an early instance of the fissure Clark later calls entropy.
