Hecker and the First Baden Uprising: radicals break from the liberal revolution

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Clark narrates the radical split from the liberals at the Frankfurt pre-parliament and Friedrich Hecker's failed April 1848 Baden uprising, arguing that its military fiasco was transfigured into radical memory and celebrity while exposing the gap between republican networks and a population that had not yet been reached.

Clark follows Baden, the first German state to see serious unrest in 1848, and the lawyer Friedrich Hecker, whose leftward swerve in 1846–7 ended in the radical walkout from the Frankfurt pre-parliament and an armed republican insurrection declared on 12 April 1848. The rising was crushed within a week at Kandern, with Herwegh's and Sigel's columns broken up soon after. Clark then follows the afterlife of the episode: memoirs, the Mazzinian cult of splendid failure, the diaspora careers of the participants, radical celebrity, the hate-filled monarchophobia of the Hecker Song, and Robert Blum's dissent from Hecker on procedural and moral grounds.

Themes

Two theories of how a new order is born. The liberals imagined the new order emerging through cooperation with the old (Gagern: ‘not to destroy, but to build’), routing authority through a Committee of Fifty alongside the 1815 Confederal Assembly. The radicals modelled themselves on 1789 and demanded a permanent, concentrated executive (Struve: ‘The old authority is a corpse!’). Clark frames the dispute as a question of how radically an administration must break with the past to count as unequivocally new, even reducing it to the choice between ‘after’ and ‘while’. This is a small, vivid instance of Clark's wider theme of the fissuring of the liberal–radical coalition. It rhymes with the WMP question of how a legitimacy interregnum is bridged (see gramsci-gap), though Clark offers no machine-level theory and this is one episode, not a pattern he generalizes here.

Networks that feel like the whole population. Republicanism was rare in Germany but strongest in Baden. Clark notes that ‘political networks’ can ‘come to seem all-encompassing to those who live and think within them’. Hecker's petitions and deputations did not translate into men in the field: urban supporters shrank back, villagers were hostile or had been persuaded by liberal and conservative propaganda that the rebels were a ‘horde of Huns’, and only the women were sometimes braver than the men. Häusser is quoted saying the question of monarchy or republic ‘had not yet touched the masses’. This is a gap between the activists' image of the people and the people themselves, and a gap in what the radical movement could read of its own society (compare legibility-as-machine-core, loosely, and nationalism for the surrounding German national question).

Military washout, mnemonic triumph. About eighty insurgents and twenty-three soldiers died. Yet memoirs by Hecker and Amalie Struve began the transfiguration into a ‘golden chapter of radical memory’ at once. Clark ties this to Mazzini's view of splendid failures, while stressing the differences: the Baden insurgents were their own propagandists, natives rather than emissaries of a London mastermind, and they survived. The later fates of Hecker, Sigel and the Struves in the United States add irony, above all Hecker's later anti-feminist essay after Amalie Struve's part in his campaign.

Radical celebrity as a mobilizing mechanism. Clark argues that celebrity was integral to political mobilization: Hecker's costume gave neologisms (Heckerhut, Heckerbluse, Heckerbart), the Struves' vegetarianism made them recognizable, and hostile journalists and cartoonists countered by targeting persons rather than issues (the Herwegh-under-Emma's-skirts poem). The link to contagion through print and image is Clark's, and the resonance with a mass-media-driven public is only implied.

A zero-sum, socially thin radical vision. Hecker's memoir blamed monarchy for everything, including popular inertia, and Clark calls it strangely lacking in social nuance. The Hecker Song's imagery of guillotines and princely blood, later adapted as an SA marching song, is noted flatly. Clark does not treat this as a general verdict on radicalism.

Radical dissent within radicalism. Robert Blum, unequivocally a radical, valued the procedures of representative democracy and refused to walk out; he saw Hecker's uprising as a betrayal of ‘the People’. The radical camp was thus not unitary; procedural loyalty split it as much as the liberal–radical line did.

Notable passages

Liberal versus radical scenarios (c1). Clark sets the two plans side by side and gives Hecker's demand for ‘permanence’ its 1789 template.

The ‘after’ versus ‘while’ vote (c2). The radicals demand a purge as a precondition; the liberals treat the Confederal Assembly as a useful administrative holdover.

The uprising and its defeat (c3). Clark explains why Baden was the place, and why Hecker's confidence in a tensed and ready population was an illusion.

The washout becomes memory (c4). The reputation of Hecker overshadows the moderate revolution of his liberal contemporaries in present-day regional memory.

Celebrity (c5). Costume, recognizability, and the counter-image of the ‘diabolical alter ego’ of the bourgeois householder.

Blum's line (c6). Blum's letter to his wife condemns the uprising as a crime against the People.

Much of this unit also touches romanticism-and-political-danger and intellectual-vs-revolutionary loosely, as the radical style is theatrical and self-mythologizing, though Clark does not put the case in those terms.
