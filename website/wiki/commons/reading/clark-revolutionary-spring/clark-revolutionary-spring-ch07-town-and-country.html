Town and Country: the peasant black box of 1848

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Clark argues that the peasantry was the black box of 1848: urban revolutionary governments in France, Lombardy, the Veneto, Hungary and Wallachia misread, neglected or only half-resolved the land question, so rural unrest never fused with urban revolution and the counter-revolution could win the countryside.

Clark turns from the urban Social Question to the rural majority of Europe, whom he calls the black box of the 1848 revolutions. He shows a countryside that was stratified, local and in flux rather than a homogeneous mass, and whose protests (against dues, hunting rights, tax registers, agents, better-off peasants and Jewish traders) often had little to do with constitutions or parliaments. He then works through case studies. Lombardy and the Veneto had exploitative tenancy and a republican indifference to it. France had the 45 per cent surtax. Hungary had April 1848 emancipation laws that left grey areas. Wallachia had the Islaz land promise and the Property Commission. He closes on the revolutionary commissars, who preached to peasants instead of listening to them, and on Hans Kudlich, a radical who did understand the countryside. The recurring point is that new urban leaders lacked knowledge, institutional grip and time, and that the counter-revolution and post-revolutionary regimes took over the agrarian question.

Themes

The countryside as illegible to the revolutionaries. Most Europeans worked the land, under regimes that "defied and still defy generalization". Clark rejects Marx's image of peasants as homologous potatoes (Marx's phrase, attributed to him by Clark, not Clark's own claim) and stresses stratification, such as the landowning peasant versus the landless labourer, and new middlemen such as Lombard agents and tenant farmers. Liberals and radicals from newspapers, clubs and commerce replaced landowning elites who at least knew the countryside. This resonates with the theme of what a state or new elite can and cannot read (legibility-as-machine-core), though Clark's version is about a revolutionary leadership's ignorance rather than about the state's administrative legibility drive. Clark also notes that landlords' agents kept all credit and debt records, so peasants could not audit their accounts.

Local grievance, not national politics. Protest was aimed at particular persons and local institutions. Clark asks whether Lombard and Venetian peasants in the National Guard supported the revolution or repudiated Austrian taxes and conscription. He keeps the divide between town and country porous: peasants came to town on market days and carried news back, and revolutionary institutions such as National Guards reproduced themselves even in small villages (the Votice guard with wooden guns).

Different land histories produce different demands. Where commons had been privatized, peasants demanded their restoration. Where commons had been protected, the landless demanded partition. In the Two Sicilies, rural unrest turned on land registries and rent records. Where nobles were the perceived enemy, peasants looked to the state as ally, which is the Habsburg pattern from Galicia in 1846 that Vienna exploited in 1848.

Policy error as a machine for counter-revolution. The Provisional Government's 45 per cent surtax financed the Comptoir National and a real "revolution in banking", but fell on the peasantry and tied the revolution to taxation. Belgium's progressive forced loan is Clark's foil. The republican governments of Venice and Milan neglected peasant demands (bread prices, contracts), and peasant enthusiasm faded by July. Clark's argument is that the urban leaders' fiscal and social choices shaped the counter-revolution's opening, not that peasants were inherently reactionary.

The gap between legal act and social process. Hungary's April laws abolished labour services and 'rusticity' but left urbarial-land definitions, wine tithes, noble privileges and the more than half of peasants on lords' personal land untouched. The result was protest, a state of siege and executions, and full resolution only by 1896. Clark generalizes: emancipation by stroke of pen versus emancipation as a generations-long social process, as with slavery and Jewish emancipation. The same holds in Wallachia, where the Islaz text was "imprecise and contradictory".

Improvised institutions under pressure. The Wallachian Property Commission, a public, half-landowner and half-peasant body, was unique in 1848 and produced striking theatre (the landowner who begged forgiveness) but the landowners drifted away and the issue was deferred to an Assembly that never met. Clark compares it to truth and reconciliation commissions. This resonates with institutions improvised under pressure, though it is one small case and Clark does not generalize it into a theory.

Monological revolution. Commissars in France and Wallachia acted as evangelists ('priests of the constitution') and read disorder as apolitical. Clark's reply is that clan rivalries and rage at foresters were what rural politics consisted of, and that revolutionaries needed to be "anthropologists". Kudlich supplies the insider's version of the same critique. Nationalist arguments also appear: Bălcescu ties smallholding to national cohesion and defence, which touches nationalism but only as one radical's argument.

Notable passages

The black box (c1). Clark opens by setting rural Europe's scale (three quarters of the French population, more than seven in ten across much of the east and south) against its diversity and against the genre-painting picture of unchanging scenery. It frames everything after it.

The forty-five centimes (c2). The clearest case of an urban policy choice, driven by Garnier-Pagès's conviction that agricultural France was of "inexhaustible fecundity", alienating the countryside from the liberal order. Attribution matters: the fecundity belief is the minister's, and Clark's own claim is about the consequences.

Hungary's grey areas (c3). Kossuth's clarifying order on 19 April is called an acknowledgement rather than a solution. The passage ends on the gap between legal act and social process.

The Property Commission (c4). The peasants who "refused to recognise property as sacred until they had some of their own" (James Morris, quoted by Clark) and the repentant landowner's speech. Clark then reports the commission's collapse.

Listening versus preaching (c5). The passage pivots from Commissaire Léoutre's report of 'not motivated by politics' disorders to Clark's judgement that the commissars failed to see what rural politics was.

Kudlich (c6). Kudlich's 'Kudlich-Gesetz' passed on 7 September 1848 with no time to implement it, and his verdict on radical theorists is quoted by Clark as the closing indictment.

What does not fit the World Machines frame: Clark offers no machine model, and the chapter is a historian's case-by-case account. Its closest rhyme is the gap between official revolutionary ideology and social reality, and the post-revolutionary state's later handling of the land question ('a process that tended to favour the landowners'), which suggests reconstruction rather than restoration, though Clark states this only briefly here.
