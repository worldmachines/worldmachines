Weavers: textile revolts and the making of the Social Question, 1831–1844

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Clark uses the weavers' revolts of Lyons (1831, 1834), Brno and Prague (1843–44) and Silesia (1844) to show that pre-1848 labour protest was already organized, politically saturated and culturally resonant, yet did not fuse with radical politics, and that its main effect was to accelerate the political differentiation of the left.

Clark opens the pre-1848 story with a cluster of textile-worker uprisings. The révolte des canuts in Lyons (November 1831) and its bloodier repeat in April 1834 anchor the chapter; Brno (1843), Prague (1844) and the Silesian weavers of Peterswaldau and Langenbielau (1844) supply variations. Each is triggered by falling wages or orders, but each differs in organization, gender composition, state response and afterlife. Clark's main claim is that the weavers were neither faceless starvelings nor simple recruits to republicanism: they had their own organizational culture, print outlets and moral economy, and the radical left's politics and worker activism converged without intertwining. Yet the conflict over resources gave off, in his phrase, a 'negative energy' that sped political differentiation, and the events became a lasting cultural and literary reference point (Lamennais, Sand, Heine, Marx, Hauptmann, Kollwitz).

Themes

Association as organizational technology. The Lyons masters built a cellular Mutual Aid Society (companies of at most twenty, syndics, a central office, a grand council), shaped by the legal asymmetry Clark stresses: the Le Chapelier Law of 1791 barred workers from associating while merchants could still cartelize and form chambers of commerce. Association was the charismatic idea of the era, and the shared geography of weaving households let masters and compagnons cooperate despite a divide of property. This is a case of a social order improvising counter-institutions under legal and economic pressure.

Social, not political? Contested description. Desbordes-Valmore called the 1831 revolt an uprising of hunger with no politics in it, and Clark says middle-class commentators liked to raise such tumults to tragedy by insisting on a purely social motive. He disagrees: the weavers' world was saturated with politics, they were largely literate, and they had reading clubs, radical lecturers and their own journal, L'Écho de la Fabrique, which replaced the alienated third-person perspective of the Social Question with a lexicon drawn from the weavers' own experience (and from Saint-Simonianism, cf. henri-de-saint-simon). The question of who gets to describe the workers, and in whose vocabulary, recurs through the chapter; it bears on how new classes become legible, or are made legible in the wrong terms, to elites and to the state (cf. legibility-as-machine-core, though Clark does not use that frame).

Convergence without fusion. Monfalcon thought the 1834 revolt had turned from industrial to party-political. Clark grants that republicans infiltrated Lyons after 1831, but shows that the fighters were mostly local, that only five of 108 arrested in one arrondissement were republicans, that the weavers kept to a traditional moral economy, and that they refused to let the Paris trial become a platform for republicans. The republican Lagrange spoke of proletarians and prostitution in the stereotyped terms of the Social Question, which the weavers did not use of themselves. This anticipates the later fissuring of the liberal-radical-social coalition, though Clark himself only says the two politics had not intertwined by 1834.

Repression, escalation and memory. The 1831 retaking was nearly bloodless; the 1834 one, under Thiers, used artillery and massacre. In Silesia, troops fired on a crowd at the Dierig house and killed eleven, including bystanders. Repression fed cultural resonance: lithographs of defendants, essays and novels by Lamartine, Balzac, Hugo and others, Lamennais's pamphlet, Sand's political awakening at the trial, Heine's Weavers' Song, and later Lafargue, Hauptmann and Kollwitz. News of Silesia crossed Prussia within days despite the censors, a small instance of contagion through print; Clark treats these as separate episodes that a later public assembled into a story, so any resonance with history-machine is loose.

Variation across the textile belt. Brno's unemployed weavers turned on employed fellows, which Clark reads as unemployment having a deadening, diffusing effect on political awareness where falling wages generate indignation. Prague showed higher organization (delegations, strike headquarters, machine-breaking against the perrotines) and a week-long general strike. Silesia was rawer: linen and cotton workers with no mutual society, no journal and no republican network, whose destruction of company books nonetheless became the object of Marx's reading. Machine-breaking never halted technological change, but Prague employers quietly raised wages and the provincial government issued minimal labour guidelines.

Gender and the exclusions built into association. Women were absent from the 525 Prague arrests, yet women then led a stone-throwing crowd. Clark rejects fear or domesticity as explanations and argues that men-only mutual societies, plus laws banning women's societies, excluded women from strike support and from the schooling in collective action that association provided. Working-class associational culture thus brought new forms of gender inequality and segregation; the argument sits alongside Clark's remark on Lyons that women and children died in numbers as civilians in 1834 without any woman being arrested or tried.

Intellectuals interpret the weavers. Left Hegelians and Friedrich Wilhelm Wolff sharpened their categories (proletariat versus monopoly of capital) in response to Silesia, and Ruge and Marx split over whether it was a hunger riot or a theoretically conscious revolt (cf. intellectual-vs-revolutionary; Clark attributes the readings to Ruge and Marx and notes Marx's rhetoric almost sounds like Prussian patriotic pride).
