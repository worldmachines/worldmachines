From common enterprise to fault lines: vagabond sovereignty in Paris, Berlin and Vienna, spring 1848

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Clark opens his chapter on the 'entropy' of 1848 by showing how, in Paris, Berlin and Vienna in spring 1848, the unity of the February and March days dissolved into liberal–radical antagonism, with authority dispersed among clubs, militias and improvised bodies until moderates reclaimed the initiative in Paris and Berlin while the Habsburg court, protected by its multi-front empire, prepared its counter-revolution.

Clark opens Chapter 7 with Philippoteaux's painting of Lamartine rejecting the red flag at the Hôtel de Ville (25 February 1848), reading it as a picture of a revolution suspended between 'already complete' and 'yet to end', and of a crowd whose unity cannot be assumed to survive the next morning. He then frames the chapter's questions (who holds authority, whether the revolution is over, why the countryside was hard to mobilize, what happens to liberal–radical solidarity when nationalisms collide) and turns to the first case study, 'vagabond sovereignty': the interval in which the authority released by the fall of the July Monarchy had not yet been recaptured by any single institution. The unit follows this dispersal through Paris (clubs, the labour question, National Workshops, the Luxembourg Commission, rival police forces, the 16 April demonstration, the 23 April elections and the Rouen rising), Berlin (clubs, flying squads, the Crown Prince affair, Held's balcony about-turn) and Vienna (strikes, cat-concerts, the Pillersdorf constitution, the May radicalization and the Security Committee), and closes on the Habsburg court's flight to Innsbruck as a sign of the monarchy's structural strength rather than weakness.

Themes

Speed and amplitude of the unity-to-conflict transition. Clark grants that all great events unite and divide, but says what was unusual in 1848 was how fast the 'oceanic immersion in a collective self' gave way to 'anxiety and distrust'. The chapter's title metaphor, entropy, is announced through images (wasps dispersing, dust motes in a sunbeam) and Clark argues that the apparently random dispersal still shows a pattern. This is his framing, and it is a historian's metaphor, not a model.

Vagabond sovereignty. With the old authority gone and no single institution having recaptured it, sovereignty 'flowed unpredictably' through the mobilized population: an armed mechanic dictating a decree to a government, clubs styling themselves representative bodies, the Luxembourg Commission becoming a 'workers' parliament', and a proliferation of overlapping militias and police (Caussidière's Montagnards, Sobrier's armed headquarters, Berlin's ten flying squads, Vienna's Academic Legion). This resonates with the WMP interest in institutions improvised under pressure and in the Gramsci-style interregnum (gramsci-gap), though Clark does not frame it as a legitimacy interregnum in any theoretical sense and his sovereignty is a descriptive, not a formal, category.

Two revolutions in one. For liberals (the tricolour) the revolution was an accomplished event needing stabilization and political reform; for radicals (the red flag) it was a process of social transformation only just begun. Labour is the test case. The government answers the demand for the organization of labour with a commission with no implementing power and with National Workshops designed to palliate and neutralize the unemployed, not to reorganize work; yet the Luxembourg Commission unexpectedly becomes a hub for organized workers. This bears on the gap between official ideology and social reality and on how a new class becomes legible, and is managed, by an improvised state.

Rumour, forgery and polarization. The 16 April crisis turns on a fabricated 'manifesto' attributed to Ledru-Rollin in L’Assemblée Nationale, on rumours of a Committee of Public Safety under Blanqui, and on Louis Blanc's counter-claim that the rumours were engineered. Clark compares the forgery with a 'deepfake' and hedges carefully: each side treats its own fears as authentic and the opponent's as manipulated, and Blanc's account is 'itself the fruit of hearsay and fear'. Relevant to contagion through print and to how information conditions shape breakdown; the modern analogy is Clark's own.

Divergent trajectories, common fragility. Initiative slips to the right in Paris (National Guard ballot of 8 April, 16 April, the violent elections of 23 April, Limoges, Rouen) and Berlin (the Crown Prince affair, Held's balcony reversal, absence of cohesive radical leadership), but to the left in Vienna (the Pillersdorf constitution outdated within weeks, the storm petition, the Security Committee). Even so, the Viennese victory is judged hollow: no revenue, soaring unemployment, hoarded silver, fleeing wealth. Rouen shows the social question undercutting the electoral one ('it’s better to die by a bullet than to perish of hunger').

Imperial structure as a resource for counter-revolution. In Clark's argument the Habsburg court's flight to Innsbruck is not a defeat. Because Vienna was one seat in a multi-national empire, the crown could exploit a plurality of fronts, rely on peripatetic, loosely constellated aristocratic authority, cultivate peasants (Galicia) and national movements against each other, and launch Windischgraetz and Radetzky while the capital radicalized. This anticipates the modernizing and structural character of counter-revolution treated later in the book, and offers a WMP-relevant contrast between distributed, legible-to-no-one authority on the revolutionary side and a redundant, multi-node authority on the imperial side, though Clark argues this only as a claim about the Habsburg monarchy.

Notable passages

Liberal versus radical definitions of the revolution (c1). Clark's compact statement of the schism: accomplished event and political reform versus ongoing process and social transformation, with labour as the acid test.

Marche, the decree and vagabond sovereignty (c2). An armed mechanic bangs his rifle on the floor and a decree guaranteeing labour is drafted and signed 'with mixed feelings' by every minister; Clark names the condition 'a vagabond sovereignty flowing unpredictably through the mobilized population of the capital'.

National Workshops (c3). The moderates' answer to Blanc: palliation and neutralization of an 'combustible reserve of inactive men' under a determined opponent of socialism, with 100,000 signing up within two months.

Rumour, propaganda and the 16 April clash (c4). Why a pre-cleared demonstration met fixed bayonets, and Clark's hedge on Blanc's fake-news claim.

Held's about-turn (c5). A crowd-pleasing poseur reverses the plan on the balcony, and Clark uses the episode to point to the absence of a cohesive radical leadership cadre; whether Held was an agent 'is not important'.

The court's flight as strength (c6). The Habsburg monarchy's capacity to operate 'in peripatetic mode' and to remain 'a free agent' capable of launching a counter-revolution as the revolution deepened in the capital.

Adjacent WMP context: nationalism (the Habsburg use of Croatian, Serbian, Romanian and other national movements against Hungarian and Polish autonomy, only glimpsed here and developed elsewhere in the chapter), legibility-as-machine-core (the workshops and commissions as attempts to make and manage a newly politicized working class, though Clark uses no such language), and history-machine (only loosely: Clark's dust-mote image suggests hidden pattern, but he does not present a distributed assembling paradigm).
