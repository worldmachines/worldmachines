The July Monarchy's eclipse and the moderate turn in Italy

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Clark shows the July Monarchy's apparent 1840s stability (Guizot's franchise-freezing moderation, defused Bonapartism, railway boom) eroding under a hostile caricature-driven press and a radicalizing banquet campaign, then turns to Italy, where Gioberti-style moderatism – elitist, technocratic, harmony-over-conflict – emerged as a reformist centrism that anticipated the post-1848 order.

The unit has two halves. The first traces how the French constitutional monarchy of Louis Philippe and Guizot, apparently consolidated in the 1840s, was hollowed out from beyond the tiny 'legal nation': a hostile caricature-driven press and a spreading banquet campaign carried opposition into public space until the attempt to ban the banquets in February 1848 'would trigger a revolution'. The second half moves to the Italian states, where Clark describes a 'moderate' turn around 1840 (Minghetti, Gioberti, Balbo, d'Azeglio, Manin's Venice) away from conspiracy towards gradual, elite-led reform, and argues that its 'limited politics' had a real futurity.

Themes

A regime frozen at the 'optimal moment'. Guizot, Clark's portrait of a liberal with conservative instincts marked by his father's execution in 1794 and Protestant memory of persecution, rejected popular sovereignty and rested authority on a class with the 'capacity' to govern. His 1843 advice to the excluded, 'enrich yourselves', became the slogan by which the regime was denounced as a class regime. Clark calls this policy of 'constitutional inertia' the regime's greatest liability. Guizot embodies the ambivalent pathos of actors who try to arrest change once their own rights are secured.

Real consolidation, thin base. The 1840s brought receding threats (jailed insurrectionaries, waning legitimism, a domesticated Napoleonic myth, the pageant of the 1840 'return of the ashes' that produced 'no spark'), farcical Bonapartist coups by Louis Napoleon in 1836 and 1840, railway-driven growth to 1846, and parliamentary management by patronage. Clark's point is that these successes all sat inside the narrow 'legal nation'; outside it the picture was 'less rosy'.

The press as a contagion channel and a war of images. The 'civilization of the journal' shaped opinion; prosecutions (the 1835 press laws, the Tribune's 111 lawsuits) mainly underlined the press's importance. Philipon's pear caricature, defended in court as a sign for 'power', not the king, turned recognition of the code into conspiracy, and shifted invective onto ministers and bourgeois society. Clark quotes an analyst that 'representation does not have to convince, it has only to occur'. This resonates with WMP interest in how legitimacy decays symbolically before institutions fail (compare decay-sequence's move from functional to performative to allegorical), though Clark makes no such claim and the fit is loose. The regime also became less visible as assassination attempts cut public appearances.

Banquets: an improvised public infrastructure. Liberal banquets began as cohesion-building sociability within the franchise; republicans invented the cheaper 'democratic banquet'. The 1847 campaign was decentralized, had no agreed programme, evaded association laws, and drifted from franchise reform to toasts to 'the organization of labour' and 'the problem of the proletariat'. Lamartine warned of a 'revolution of contempt'. The political nation was widened but stayed masculine, with women in 'reserved' galleries. The form is a case of institutions improvised outside legal categories, which the state could neither legibly classify nor easily suppress (cf. legibility-as-machine-core, used here as an analogy only).

Italian moderatism. Minghetti recalled a turn around 1840 from sects and insurrection to a 'road more serious, practical and safe'. Gioberti's Primacy (about 80,000 copies) fused papacy, nation, faith and modernity in a federal Pope-led vision, valued 'moderation' as a precondition rather than a product of conflict, and rejected Hegelian dialectics; Clark judges it placed 'harmony and social peace above liberty'. Balbo, d'Azeglio, the Bologna Agricultural Society and Venetian associations pursued administrative reform and capacity-building. Clark stresses that moderatism was 'an attitude' with no party or manifesto, resting on a composite noble-bourgeois elite, worried about crime and unemployment from below. The 'nation' here is a Catholic, elite-managed one, resonating with nationalism debates, though the Giobertian version is providential and anti-dialectical rather than Herderian.

Moderatism's futurity. Clark's own claim: moderatism linked Italian liberals with Guizot's France and the Spanish moderados; it began from 'the current balance of political power', and was 'elitist, technocratic, modernizing and unashamedly counter-revolutionary', prefiguring the reformist centrism after 1848. This foreshadows the book's later argument about counter-revolution as modernizing reconstruction rather than restoration (compare history-machine on orders assembling quietly before they become visible; the parallel is suggestive, not Clark's).

Notable passages

Guizot's 'enrich yourselves' (c1) states the regime's philosophy of arrested change and its rhetorical undoing. Philipon's pear defence (c2) is the sharpest statement of the press's symbolic power. The banquet campaign (c3) shows opposition escaping the legal frame and radicalizing, ending on the fuse of February 1848. Gioberti versus liberalism (c4) is Clark's clearest analysis of moderate ideology as harmony-over-conflict. Minghetti's sea change (c5) records the elite turn from conspiracy. The closing assessment (c6) is Clark's thesis on moderatism's future.
