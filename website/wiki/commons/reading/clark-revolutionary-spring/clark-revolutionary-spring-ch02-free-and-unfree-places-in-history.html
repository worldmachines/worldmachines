Slavery as Metaphor, History as Current: Two Preconditions of 1848

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Clark argues that pre-1848 Europeans used slavery as a root political metaphor while averting their eyes from actual slavery, so that abolitionism stayed elite and stalled until February 1848 fused metaphor and fact, and that the same generation experienced history as an irreversible current in which everyone, radical, liberal and conservative, was in ideological flux.

The unit has two sections. In 'Free and Unfree', Clark shows that European radicals, socialists, feminists, nationalists and conservatives constantly invoked slavery to describe unfreedom (of women, Poles, Italians, Jews, workers), yet almost never connected the word to the chattel slavery that was expanding in the colonies of the continental powers. He follows the thin, elite, gradualist French abolitionism of Victor Schoelcher and Cyrille Bissette up to the eve of the revolution, and ends by noting that February 1848 would suddenly join 'the metaphor and the thing'. In 'Places in History', he turns to a shared sense among contemporaries that history was a fast, irreversible current ('the spirit of the age'), and to the striking mobility of individual political commitments in the 1830s and 1840s, closing with a reflection on periods of stabilization versus periods of flux.

Themes

The metaphor without the thing. Slavery had become 'one of the root metaphors of western political philosophy and language', but for critical writers it was 'either a metaphor, or the phenomenon of a bygone age, or a theoretical abstraction'. Weitling makes chattel slavery a remote prelude to the worse 'modern slavery of money'; Clark reads this as speaking of slavery 'in order not to speak of it'. Clark cites a recent re-reading of Hegel in which the lord and bondsman dialectic drew on the Haitian revolution, so that Hegel 'thought with Haiti rather than writing about it' (Haiti 'written in invisible ink'). This is a gap between the language available to a political culture and the social facts it could not bring itself to see, a Clark-level instance of ideology and reality diverging; it is a historian's observation, not a machine theory.

Abolitionism was British, elite and slow. Slave-trading and slave use grew in the colonies of the continental powers; the Pope's 1839 condemnation was ignored; in Portugal, abolitionism was read as a British strategy against national interest. There was no continental counterpart to British mass petitioning. In France, petition drives yielded fewer than 9,000 (1844) and 12,395 (1846-7) signatures, and Bissette's Revue des Colonies had 250 subscribers. Still, by the later 1840s a broader press front had formed, and Schoelcher moved from gradualism to immediate emancipation. Clark's point is that accumulated sentiment might have gone nowhere without the discontinuity of February 1848, which 'deposited' abolitionists near power. Clark stresses the difficulty of moving from theory to facts (Bissette: slavery 'was at the same time a fact and a principle') and the social conformity that converts newcomers in slaving societies (Schoelcher).

History as an irreversible current. Across the political spectrum, contemporaries pictured history as torrent, chariot, wind or flood (Démar, Bernard, Gerlach, Metternich). Liberals saw constitutions as 'the foreseen future' and a moderating guarantee against repetition of 1789-94; conservatives (Chateaubriand, Le Quotidien) saw cyclical repetition. Even the resisters felt the 'ambient pressure for change'. The cascade of 1820-23 constitutional revolutions, all adopting the Cadiz constitution, and their suppression by international intervention foreshadow the 1848 pattern (Clark does not draw the comparison here).

Flux of commitments. Bernard, Lamennais, Mazzini, Marx, Donoso Cortes, Thorbecke, Tocqueville and an anonymous artisan foreman each moved across ideologies. Clark's inference is that 'everyone was unusual', that the political field of the 1830s-40s was unsettled and loosely bounded rather than sorted into stable camps. He places this in a typology of periods of stabilization versus periods of flux, and says 'Our own age is one' of flux.

Resonances (hedged). The slavery-as-metaphor gap and the state or elite reluctance to see what it is committed to describing sit alongside legibility-as-machine-core and legibility-hardens-hierarchy-bayly, though Clark is concerned with moral vision, not administrative legibility. The 'spirit of the age' as a convergent path into modernity echoes the pull character of the modernity-machine, though contemporaries here disagree about whether the current is progress or repetition. The closing contrast between stabilizing and flux periods, and the personal ideological drift, loosely rhymes with the interregnum idea in gramsci-gap, but Clark only offers a historian's periodization, and does not claim an old order dying while a new one cannot be born. Joseph de Maistre appears briefly as the thinker who called revolutionaries 'the slaves of history' (joseph-de-maistre); Herzen, Saint-Simon and Paine are not discussed here beyond the Saint-Simonian milieu (henri-de-saint-simon).

Notable passages

The catalogue of slavery metaphors (c1). A dense list from Démar to Börne to Leroux that shows slavery as a shared root metaphor across feminists, nationalists, Jews, and socialists, establishing the thesis of the section.

Weitling and Hegel (c2). Clark's sharpest interpretive claim: unfreedom was thought and named while the real slavery, and Haiti, went unseen. The Hegel reading is a recent scholarly claim that Clark reports, not his own discovery.

No continental counterpart (c3). Explains why continental abolitionism stalled: national interest, elite narrowness, and the absence of an evangelical mass base.

The discontinuity of February 1848 (c4). The French abolition story culminates in Schoelcher's frustration ('people have been saying this for a quarter of a century and still the slaves are in their chains') and the sudden conjunction of 'the metaphor and the thing'.

Everyone under pressure (c5). Gerlach, Niemojowski, Martinez de la Rosa and Metternich show that even the defenders of order experienced irreversible motion.

Stabilization versus flux (c6). Clark's closing typology of historical periods and his reading of the 1830s-40s as an age of flux.
