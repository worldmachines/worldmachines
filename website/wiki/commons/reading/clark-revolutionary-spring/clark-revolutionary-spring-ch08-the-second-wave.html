The second wave: Rome, the Reich constitution campaign and the erosion of the middle

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Clark narrates the 'second wave' of revolution from autumn 1848 to summer 1849 (the Pope's flight and the Roman Republic, then the radical, better-networked Reich constitution campaign and its suppression in Saxony, Prussia and Baden) and argues that liberals, frightened of the left, drifted toward order while the radicals were in fact ameliorative social democrats rather than communists.

Clark opens with the Pope's cloak-and-dagger flight from Rome (24 November 1848) after the assassination of Pellegrino Rossi, and follows it into the Roman Republic of 1849: its constituent assembly elected by universal male suffrage, its humane and secular constitution, its charismatic figures (Mazzini, Garibaldi, Belgioioso, Fuller) and its money troubles. He then widens to the 'second wave' as a European phenomenon. Radicals, not liberals, dominate; they are better planned and networked (the Märzverein, Republican Solidarity, Saxon clubs). Liberals, frightened by the June Days and by clubs they read as a 'state within the state', drift toward the forces of order, hollowing out the political middle. The German question (greater- versus lesser-German, the king of Prussia's refusal of the crown) turns the radicals into defenders of the Frankfurt constitution, producing the insurrections at Iserlohn, Dresden and Baden, all crushed by armies that stayed loyal. Clark closes by arguing that second-wave radicalism was ameliorative and proto-social-democratic, and that the spectre of 'communism' was a way of not seeing what radicals actually wanted.

Themes

Myth outruns the man. Clark argues that the 'liberal and national pope' was not built by Pius IX or his circle but was 'a cultural and emotional phenomenon in its own right'. When the allocution of 29 April 1848 broke the spell, that mythic capital collapsed, and the same inchoate spirituality later flowed toward the anticlerical Garibaldi and the near-priestly Mazzini. This is a picture of legitimacy resting on a public's projection rather than on institutional fact, a loose echo of world-machines-style questions about what holds an order together, though Clark does not frame it that way. It also touches benedict-anderson's imagined communities and nationalism as quasi-religious sentiment.

Institutions improvised under pressure. The Roman Republic is a case of provisional government building lay courts, ending the Inquisition, abolishing the death penalty and guaranteeing religious equality within weeks, while running on 'debased fiat coinage and inflated paper money'. Clark stresses these 'inconspicuous successes' against the horror stories spread by enemies, and the election boycott inaugurated by papal excommunication. Mazzini's remark at St Peter's ('it is so beautiful to the eye') marks the limit of a secular nation-religion against the Church's aesthetic and moral hold, a nice qualification of romanticism-and-political-danger and of Mazzini's own project.

The fissure of the liberal-radical coalition (Clark's entropy). Liberals are a 'saturated power': they treat social life as a private domain the state must not bind itself to (Lamartine's competition-rewarding-liberty line), regard clubs as an 'anti-parliament', and move toward the conservatives out of fear. Sigismund Stern, quoted by Clark, attributes bourgeois panic after the June Days as the foothold for counter-revolution. Election results in Saxony, Prussia, France and Württemberg show the middle eroding (Strauss finds no one to build a moderate party with). This fits the wider theme of bourgeois fear, cf. bourgeoisophobia and the-bourgeois-experience, though Clark's account is class-political rather than psychological.

Second-wave radicalism: organized, deep, but militarily thin. The left learned from spring: mass associations (950 Märzverein clubs with about 500,000 members; 350 clubs in Republican Solidarity; 75,000 Saxons in 280 associations), with a working-class base (68.3 per cent of the Waldheim prisoners were artisans and labourers). What it lacked was command structure and troops: Bakunin's cigars and doomed trees, Wagner's retreat from the steeple. The decisive resource of counter-revolution was army loyalty, sustained by soldiers' belief that they defended order against 'anarchy', made plausible by Jacobin symbolism (the Mountain, red flags, Phrygian caps).

The imperial constitution as integrative symbol. Radicals who had mocked parliaments ('Das Reden nimmt kein End') backed the Frankfurt constitution because it was the 'written distillation of the March revolution' and became a choice between revolution and counter-revolution once princes dissolved their parliaments. Committees of safety appear as improvised local government. The refusal of the crown showed that national unification would proceed under monarchical, not parliamentary, auspices, an anticipation of the counter-revolution's capacity to take over the national project (Clark's larger modernizing-counter-revolution argument, developed elsewhere; note only hinted here).

Legibility failure: communists that were not. Clark's closing claim is that 'communism' functioned as a way of not seeing radicals' actual, ameliorative demands (credit institutes, subsidized timber, lower taxes, no war on property). This is a gap between official ideology and social reality, resonant with legibility-as-machine-core in reverse: a new political public was rendered illegible to the propertied by a threat-label. Read as a hedge: Clark is a historian and this is his interpretive claim, not a machine-theoretic one.

Notable passages

The myth of the liberal pope (c1). Clark's claim that the pope became 'a way of thinking about the world' independent of his actions.

Roman Republic's achievements (c2). The catalogue of secular, humane reforms and the enemies' horror stories, plus the excommunication of voters.

Liberal satiation (c3) and the withering of the middle (c4). The two passages that carry the liberal-radical fissure argument, with Stern's testimony on bourgeois terror.

Reich constitution campaign (c5). Why radicals defended a liberal document, and why it became a proxy for revolution versus counter-revolution.

Democ-socs (c6). The reclassification of second-wave radicals as ancestors of social democrats, and the 'not seeing' function of the communist spectre.
