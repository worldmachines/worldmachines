Roma Emancipation in Wallachia and the Four Emancipations Compared

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Clark traces the abolition of Roma slavery in Wallachia in the summer of 1848, from Provisional Government proclamation to bureaucratic bottleneck and Ottoman–Russian reversal, then generalises across the four emancipations (slaves, women, Jews, Roma) to argue that each advanced in advocacy, contracted with the revolution's ebb, and never merged because racial, sexual and anti-Jewish discrimination were separate, non-reducible fundaments.

The unit has two parts. The first follows Roma slavery in the Danubian Principalities: a 1843 sale notice for 200 families, the earlier freeing of state and Church slaves (1843–47), and the fate of the 40,000–50,000 privately owned Roma when the Wallachian Provisional Government proclaimed manumission on 26 June 1848. The proclamation set up a Commission, liberation tickets that doubled as identity cards, and compensation machinery. The machinery choked, owners resisted, and the Ottoman–Russian intervention annulled everything on 28 September 1848. The second part steps back. Clark starts from a Mazzinian speech in Rome that linked Jewish emancipation to O'Connell and to abolition, and then compares the four emancipations (enslaved Africans, women, Jews, Roma). He finds a common chronology (1790s stirrings, an advocacy boom in the 1830s–40s, a further push in 1848, then narrowing) and argues that the four never fused, because the rationales of discrimination behind them were distinct.

Themes

Proclamation is not implementation. The Roma case is Clark's clearest example of the gap between a decree and a working emancipation. The Commission for the Liberation of the Slaves is a small, improvised body that Clark calls a 'stupendous achievement'. It printed forms, set deadlines, and issued certificates. It was then swamped: forms did not reach the counties, owners could not document slaves 'who had been in their family for generations', and tens of thousands of people set off to claim liberty. The unit names the same three questions that faced the French colonies: who compensates, whether emancipation is immediate or gradual, and how to stop freed people leaving their employment. This is an institution improvised under pressure and outrun by the scale of what it was asked to do. It resonates with the history-machine picture of a paradigm lurching into visibility, though Clark makes no such claim.

Legibility cuts both ways. Private Roma slaves were poorly legible to the state, since 'their names were not recorded in the cadastral surveys'. Emancipation had to make them legible before it could free them. Liberation tickets doubled as identity cards and, 'tellingly', recorded the name of the former owner. Owners had to prove the existence of their slaves in order to be compensated. This fits legibility-as-machine-core and legibility-hardens-hierarchy-bayly loosely: the state's instrument of freedom is also its instrument of registration, and the paperwork carries the old hierarchy forward. Clark himself only notes the detail ('tellingly'); the legibility reading is ours and should be carried as a hedge.

Counter-revolution as reversal. The 28 September 1848 decree annuls revolutionary acts, orders the tickets 'returned and destroyed', and restores the pre-11 June condition. Here counter-revolution is a literal rollback, and it hits the least powerful first: 'Roma emancipation was the first casualty of the counter-revolution in Wallachia'. Process resumed only in 1855. Compare the modernising counter-revolution argued elsewhere in the book: in this case the reversal is plain restoration, and the revolutionary machinery was destroyed along with the tickets.

Cultural rehearsal without swallowing. European elites rehearsed the four emancipations in opera, ballet, novels and plays (Nabucco, Nathan the Wise, Amazon ballets, Haiti-revolution dramas, idealised 'loyal gypsy' sketches). Clark's judgment is that these works 'allowed European cultural elites to keep chewing on emancipation without ever quite swallowing it'. The cultural production is a form of contagion and preparation, but also a way of consuming the idea safely. He also notes an irony in Zauli-Sajani's speech: an advocate of universal emancipation who never mentions women, though his wife was a novelist.

A shared chronology of expansion and contraction. Advocacy networks (Bissette, Schoelcher, Voilquin, Fürst, Riesser, Romanian liberals) thickened in the 1830s–40s and intensified in 1848. Fürst's remark that 'what matters is not the result, but rather the activities that gave birth to this outcome' shifts value from outcome to process. After the ebb, decrees were deactivated, women were again excluded from political organisations, and creed-based equality was dropped. Formerly enslaved women moved from a system where they and men were interchangeable chattels to one of male authority. Jewish emancipation tracked liberal nation-building (the 1866 Romanian constitution restricted citizenship to Christians) and eroded when liberal energies waned.

Connected but not reducible. Garrison, Heinzen and Douglass saw the struggles as one. Clark argues they did not merge: racial or ethnic difference, sexual difference and the Jewish predicament were 'separate fundaments', deeply built into modern European culture and made to seem natural. The revolutions also released countervailing forces (competitive resentment, xenophobia, misogyny, fear of disorder, the zeal to discipline). Clark refuses the verdict of failure, pointing to real advances in advocacy, while stressing the recalcitrance of racial and sexual inequality. For World Machines, this bears on why a single liberating logic ('progress') does not carry all excluded groups at once. It touches modernity-machine assumptions about convergence, though Clark is describing plural, non-convergent rationales of exclusion, closer to divergence than to a single arc, and does not use that frame.

Notable passages

The passage on the categories of Roma slavery (c1) gives the scale (about 200,000 by 1830, 40,000–50,000 private slaves in 1848) and the missing cadastral record. The account of the June proclamation (c2) shows the state improvising an emancipation bureaucracy, with tickets that served as identity cards. The passage on the gap between proclamation and implementation (c3) ends with the Ottoman commissioner's decree of 28 September that annulled the whole process. The cultural-rehearsal passage (c4) is Clark's most compressed statement of how elite culture handled emancipation as spectacle. The narrowing passage (c5) carries the comparative argument: expansion, contraction, and the long tail into 1866, 1914, 1933 and 1855. The closing passage (c6) states the thesis that the emancipations did not fuse, and guards against reading 1848 as simple failure.
