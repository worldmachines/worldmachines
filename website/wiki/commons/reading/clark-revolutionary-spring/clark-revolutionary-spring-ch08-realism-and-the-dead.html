Realism born of counter-revolution, and the dead of 1848-49

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Clark argues that the counter-revolution's victory of armies over networks produced a cross-ideological 'realist' turn (Marx and Engels, Bismarck, Droysen, d'Azeglio, Rochau) that treated power rather than ideas as the basis of politics, then closes the narrative with the deaths of the revolutionaries, famous and anonymous.

Clark's first insight about the closing phase of the revolutions is that transnational revolutionary networks, though real (Sicilian recruiting, French volunteers on both sides at Rome, a 'diplomacy of the peoples'), were small and horizontal, and lacked the vertical structures and resources to resist the territorial armies of Prussia, France and Russia. He then traces how this outcome reshaped political thought: Marx and Engels in 1850, Bismarck after Olmütz, Droysen, d'Azeglio and Rochau's Realpolitik all moved from ideals and abstractions to a 'pragmatics of power'. A short excursus (Angeloni, Barbès) shows that this disenchanted science of forces began on the far left and migrated to the right and centre. The section headed 'The Dead' then gives the revolutionaries the last word: Mameli, Manara, Bassi, Blum, Elsenhans, Petőfi, Ciceruacchio, and the anonymous dead, including cholera victims spread by counter-revolutionary armies.

Themes

Hierarchies beat networks. Clark's own formulation is 'Towers prevailed over squares. Hierarchies beat networks. Power prevailed over ideas and arguments.' The revolutionaries' networks were successful as networks but lacked vertical resources; the counter-revolution drew on armies whose loyalty had never been seriously in question. This rhymes with the WMP interest in synchrony and contagion through print and news, though Clark's point is the limit of such contagion when it meets organized force. It half-fits a legibility-as-machine-core reading (state capacity as the decisive asset), but Clark does not make that argument himself.

The realist turn as an idea in motion. Marx and Engels (1850, in the Neue Rheinische Zeitung, as Clark reports) saw the words of the revolutions as words, and read outcomes as compelled by force and by the return of 'industrial prosperity'. Bismarck welcomed Olmütz as clarity; Droysen wrote that realities triumphed over ideals; d'Azeglio learned that 'there is nothing more serious than power!'; Rochau likened the law of power to gravity, while also urging that new social forces (national sentiment, parties, the press) deserve acknowledgement. Clark stresses that these were different realisms (cultural, military, economic) but all superseded radical and liberal rhetoric. Rochau's realism was both an explanation of failure and a way to manage the consequences of the revolutions' success, which connects to Clark's wider theme of counter-revolution as reconstruction rather than mere restoration; this unit only hints at that theme.

Idea mobility, not influence. The Angeloni/Barbès excursus is explicit that this is not a genealogy: none of the later figures probably knew Angeloni. The claim is that a realist temperament migrated from margin to mainstream under turbulence: 'What had once been on the margins became mainstream; what had once been esoteric became ordinary.' Compare, loosely, the gramsci-gap as an interregnum in which old legitimacies die, though Clark's realists are not describing an interregnum but a settled new pragmatism.

Revolution as performed narrative. Manara's wish to 'bring ’48 to a serious conclusion' is read by Clark as the completion of a story, not military success; the revolution, like the nation, was a 'melodrama' whose stage deaths were also real. This links the 1848 actors' narrative self-understanding to the book's earlier theme of them as ardent storytellers. Dandolo's contrasting view (the last eight days were useless slaughter) is kept as a dissent.

Human cost and its ecology. The anonymous dead (40,000-50,000 in the Hungarian war, the June Days, the Castelnuovo massacre) and the cholera evidence, which Clark says suggests that counter-revolutionary armies crossing borders spread the disease more than the revolution did, give a material and epidemiological register to the political story.

Notable passages

Networks versus armies (c1). Lists the Prussian, French and Russian interventions and draws the network/hierarchy conclusion.

Marx and Engels on force (c2). Clark reports their view that ideology carried little weight and that political actors were 'forced' at each turn; the attributions are Marx and Engels's, not Clark's.

Olmütz, Bismarck and Rochau (c3). The contrast between Sybel's grief and Bismarck's welcome, with Droysen, d'Azeglio and Rochau, is the heart of the 'birth of realism' claim.

Angeloni and the migrating realist temperament (c4). Clark's methodological caveat about influence and his image of ideas travelling across an 'archipelago of arguments'.

Manara and the serious conclusion (c5). Death as narrative completion, with Dandolo's dissent.

The anonymous dead and cholera (c6). The close of the counter-revolution narrative: 'Grass has grown over them all.'
