Fluid ideologies and the radical women's attack on the power of the father

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Clark opens the 'conjectures of order' by showing that pre-1848 ideologies were fluid, unbounded constellations rather than party programmes, then makes radical women's critique of patriarchy (Démar, Voilquin, Deroin, Tristan and others) his first case study of a dissenting vision that exposed the gender order as constructed but proved the most resistant to change.

Chapter 2 opens with Clark's claim that the political map of 1830s-40s Europe was not yet the tidy spectrum of left, centre and right: 'liberalism', 'socialism' and 'conservatism' were newly circulating, unstable terms; there were no disciplined continental parties, only networks and an 'archipelago of texts and personalities'. He then devotes the opening section, 'It's a man's world', to one dissenting current: the radical women of France and beyond. Centred on Claire Démar's 1833 tract My Law of the Future (and her suicide days after writing it), Clark follows the Saint-Simonian and Fourierist milieux, the secession of women from Enfantin's 'church', the Gazette des Femmes' legalistic exposure of the gap between the 1830 Charter and the Civil Code, the political self-fashioning of Anneke, Tristan, Aston and Sand, and the women's fiction that carried the argument. He closes by arguing that these women saw the gender order as a social construction, and that it was the structure of inequality most resistant to the revolutions to come.

Themes

Ideologies as protean constellations. Clark stresses that political arguments were 'infiltrated in unpredictable ways by economic discourses, patriotic claims and the language of religious belief', and that appeals to the past were rhetorical tools of legitimacy rather than memory. He explicitly compares this 'fluxy, non-linear and hazy' intellectual life to our own time, when the 'great disciplining identities of modern politics' are dissolving. This is Clark's own comparison, offered with some hedging; it sits loosely beside WMP's interest in what happens when an era's organizing categories lose their hold (compare gramsci-gap), though Clark makes no such theoretical claim.

The power of the father as the deepest structure. Démar argues that patriarchal power, not monarchy, religion or capital, is the foundation of every other inequality, transmitted through childhood discipline and anchored in marriage. Clark notes that the law itself wrote obedience into marriage (Napoleonic Civil Code, 'the woman owes obedience to her husband'). The claim that this structure sits beneath the visible political order rhymes with WMP's idea of deep infrastructure beneath surface politics (world-machines), though Démar's argument is a moral-political one, not a machine-style periodization.

Emancipation as slow, distributed cultural revolution. Démar contrasts the 'three days of gorgeous sunshine' of July 1830 with a revolution in mores that 'takes place at all hours, in all places'. Clark also draws out her claim that women's liberation is the precondition of the proletarians' and 'of all', though he notes she is 'vague on how exactly the causation might work'. Note the echo of the history-machine idea of change assembling silently and distributively, but Démar is imagining a programme, not describing a process.

Utopia moves into the future; voice matters. Clark contrasts Démar with Fourier: she wants equality and autonomy, he wants 'harmony' and gratified 'needs' organized by administrators ('sexual minimum wage', Courts of Love). Clark reads Fourier as 'bossy and controlling' and observes that in this era 'Utopia' shifted from an impossible place to a possible future place. For Démar, 'Who was speaking was as important as what was being said.' Saint-Simon's movement (see henri-de-saint-simon) here appears as an unstable religious-reformist sect whose men would not grant women equal rank.

Gap between constitutional principle and legal practice. The Gazette des Femmes set Article 1 of the 1830 Charter against Article 213 of the Civil Code and asked whether, if the government would not say 'les françaises', half the population was already living in a republic. Clark calls this 'forensic irony' exposing 'the double standards on which liberal patriarchy was built' and shows a regime whose official ideology of equality did not match its own legal machinery. Clark also flags the irony that the nominal female editor was a front for a male journalist.

Making the private legible as systemic. Tristan proposed to break 'the reign of privacy' so that servitude scattered across 'myriad individual biographies' could 'become visible as a form of systematic oppression', using private experience as political currency. This bears on how a previously illegible social group becomes visible, but here the visibility is claimed from below by the group itself, the opposite of the state-imposed legibility discussed in legibility-as-machine-core.

Diffuse movement, real tension, poor prospects. The advocacy stayed ideologically diffuse, split between civil-legal reform and change in behaviour and selfhood; the association of emancipation with sexual licence alarmed many women (Němcová, Gatti de Gamond, Otto). Clark's verdict: sexual inequality was 'the most resistant to change'; in 1848 the 'fortified architecture of women's civil and political inferiority held firm'.

Notable passages

Pre-1848 ideologies without parties (c1). Clark's short methodological corrective: the terms and the party formations we take for granted had not yet crystallized, so the chapter should be read as a survey of conjectures, not camps.

Démar on the power of the father (c2). The opening image of a 'colossus' that centuries of revolution had failed to topple, with the catalogue of places (academies, laws, armies, factories, taverns, intimacy) where it operates.

Démar's future and the 'deeper' revolution (c3). Experimental cohabitation, unions of indefinite duration, the slow revolution of mores, and the chain of emancipation running from her sex to 'slaves, proletarians and children'.

The Gazette's constitutional arithmetic (c4). The clearest instance in the unit of a dissenting group turning the regime's own text against it.

Tristan's naming of the private (c5). Self-fashioning and the politicization of private experience as new form of voice.

Closing verdict (c6). Gender order as constructed and contingent, hardest to change, and the note on 'wayward lives and beautiful experiments' where freedom was 'a hard-won personal experience, not a political fact'. Clark's comparison with African American women in early-twentieth-century American ghettos is his own analogy.

Other figures Clark folds in but which are not curated: Herzen's reading of Sand (see alexander-herzen), J. S. Mill's later reflection on Fourier and the Saint-Simonian critique of marriage, and Guépin's medicalized account of bourgeois girls' frustration.
