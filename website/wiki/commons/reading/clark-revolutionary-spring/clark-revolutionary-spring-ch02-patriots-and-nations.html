Patriots and nations: nationalism as feeling, before 1848

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Clark argues that pre-1848 nationalism was above all a feeling, built by patriotic historians, philologists, clubs and print out of a contested mix of inheritance and invention, radical in implication yet socially thin, overlapping with other loyalties, and only about to reveal its power to mobilize and divide in the revolutions.

Clark opens with the claim that early- to mid-nineteenth-century nationalism was 'above all a feeling, rather than a set of principles or arguments', one that expressed itself first as a particular relationship to the past. He follows the work patriots did on that relationship: historians such as Michele Amari re-sculpting medieval revolts into popular insurgencies, poets and songwriters (Foscolo, Petőfi, Becker, Runeberg), the Rhine crisis of 1840 and Musset's parody reply, and a transnational solidarity fed by prison memoirs like Pellico's Le mie prigioni. He then turns to the infrastructure of patriotism: periodicals, miscellanies, guidebooks, academies, lexicons and language reform (Jahn, Gaj, the Matice česká, Hungarian and Romanian societies), balls and costume, and gymnastic and burgher clubs, and to the fact that women could participate in patriotism in ways they could not in liberalism or radicalism, illustrated at length by the diaries of Dragojla Jarnević. The chapter closes on limits: nationalism was mostly an elite, literate, urban affair (250 subscribers to the leading Romanian paper in Transylvania), competed with religion, region, dynasty and status, and had rival mental maps whose conflict would only appear once revolution broke out.

Themes

Nation as feeling and relation to the past. National memory 'was about making some things feel near and others remote'. Serbian epic song, Foscolo's ruined graveyard Italy, and Petőfi's 'we were slaves until now' show patriotism as an emotional lens rather than a doctrine. Clark's stress on affect echoes benedict-anderson's imagined communities, though Clark is more interested in the social texture (costume, balls, songs) than in a theory of the imagining.

History as ideological work. Amari chose history because it 'released contemporaries from the tyranny of the present, implicitly posting a question mark over the present order of things'. Retelling the Sicilian Vespers made insurrection look old and deep and the Bourbons look shallow and alien. The censors' violent response (banned book, sacked censors, exiled publisher who died) shows the state treating a rewritten past as a live threat. This is a small-scale case of legitimacy being contested by re-narrating the past, which sits near history-machine only loosely; Clark's point concerns deliberate patriotic authorship, not silent distributed assembly.

Constructivist versus primordialist, refused. Clark declines to adjudicate: nations drew on histories and traditions, yet 'the intensity and social depth of modern nationalism marked a new point of departure'. The invented national costumes (Pest tailors, Jahn's old-German dress, the Slovanská Lípa commission) are his exhibits. Compare the wiki's nationalism and counter-enlightenment-and-nationalism pages; Herder's linguistic nationhood (johann-gottfried-herder) is the intellectual background, though Clark cites Arndt and Jahn here rather than Herder.

Reactive surges. Patriotism 'surge[d] and ebb[ed] in response to historical pressures' (the 1840 Rhine crisis). The Prussian reaction to the Paris republic in February 1848, cancelling leaves for the Koblenz garrison rather than arresting democrats, shows the state reading the revolution as a national-security event before a domestic one.

Transnational patriot solidarity and print contagion. Greek, Polish and Italian causes drew volunteers from front to front through the 1820s and 30s, and Pellico's prison memoir went through 165 French editions. Rumour, partly false (the anniversary beatings), circulated as 'the currency of patriot solidarity'. This is an early instance of the news-and-print circulation that later spreads the 1848 cascade, though Clark does not make that link in this chapter.

Knowledge, language and standardization. Patriotism began with gathering knowledge of folklore, music and land; language became the core project (Arndt: 'The only real boundary of a people is language'), with lexicons, purism (štampa replaced by tisak) and academies. Language standardization is a legibility project in miniature, a resonance with legibility-as-machine-core and modernity-machine, though here it is pursued from below by cultural entrepreneurs against a dominant elite language (German, Latin, Greek) rather than imposed by a state; and Clark warns that the link from linguistic refinement to national politics was 'not always direct' (the Slovene farming periodical).

Radical implication, conflicting maps. Exalting the nation 'did not necessarily propose, but it did imply, a kind of popular sovereignty'. Polish, German, Ukrainian, Hungarian and Illyrian mental maps clash with each other and with the political geography, foreshadowing the national conflicts of the revolution.

Purity versus composite nations. Jahn's ethnic purity ('The more mixed it is, the more it resembles a criminal gang') is set against Kogălniceanu, Dahlmann and Amari, who argue that nations are composite and that blood kinship recedes over time. Clark warns against reading Jahn only through what came later.

Inclusion of women. Patriotism was 'more inclusive than either liberalism or the various forms of radicalism'. Women were mothers, allegories, recruited readers and conspicuous consumers of patriotic dress; Jarnević's diary traces a German-speaking Croat learning Croatian, gaining company, purpose and a public role.

Limits: elite, thin, plural. Fries in 1816: German patriotism 'is and must be a matter for educated people'. Peasants had 'no rights, no property, and no country' (Eötvös), which helps explain the 1846 Galician catastrophe and Mazzini's failed insurrections. Local loyalties (Trieste, Sicily, Dalmatia) and multilingual habits (Arnold) show national feeling as 'an evolving field of awareness' competing with religion, status, city and dynasty. This bears on how nations and publics were partly illegible to the state and to each other, and on the gap between the ideology of a nation and the social reality of who felt it; compare the-mass-as-fiction only as a loose analogy.

Notable passages

Amari and the uses of history (c1). The clearest statement of how a past can be re-narrated to delegitimize a present order. Note Clark's hedge that Amari's retelling was 'often fanciful'.

Inherited or invented (c2). Clark's refusal of the primordialist/constructivist binary, backed by the costume examples.

Radical charge and rival maps (c3). The nation implies popular sovereignty and sets incompatible mental maps against each other; this is Clark's forward pointer to the national conflicts of 1848 and the 'entropy' that follows.

The tiny patriotic cosmos (c4). The sociology of print: 250 subscribers, a small literate urban stratum, a corrective to overreading nationalism's reach.

What Jarnević gained (c5). Clark's reflection on why patriotism drew women in when liberal and radical politics did not.

Nationalism as one field among others (c6). The chapter's conclusion: national feeling as evolving, plural and situational, dominant only under war or revolution.
