Religious civil strife in Switzerland and the radicalization of Hungarian opposition, 1830s–1847

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Clark shows how, in the 1830s and 1840s, religious quarrels in Swiss cantons and press-driven oppositional politics in Hungary polarized societies and radicalized moderate reform well before 1848.

Two pre-revolutionary case studies of polarization. The first section follows the Swiss cantons after 1830, where constitutional reform fused with confessional conflict: Aargau's monastic dispute (1835–41), the Zurich "Züriputsch" of 1839 provoked by the appointment of David Friedrich Strauss, and the Valais civil wars of 1840 and 1844. The second follows Hungary, where the Pressburg Diet, an antiquated noble assembly, became the arena in which Széchenyi's gradualism was outflanked by Wesselényi and then by Kossuth, whose newspaper and manuscript reports built a national public. Clark's point in both is that frustrated moderation, sacred commitments and new channels of communication pushed political societies toward mutually unintelligible camps.

Themes

Religion deepens the abyss. In Aargau, Zurich and the Valais, constitutional questions became entangled with the sacred. Clark generalizes that these cases revealed "a deepening of religious tensions, not just in Switzerland, but across the continent", and that when one group's most sacred things are held in contempt by another, conversation becomes impossible. Note the axes vary: Catholic against Calvinist in Aargau, conservative against liberal Protestants in Zurich, north against south within a single Catholic canton in the Valais.

Improvised counter-institutions. The Zurich "committees of faith" began as petitioning bodies and became a "government-in-waiting"; clergy-led armed columns drove out the executive. The Valais northerners formed an illegal clerical counter-government. Institutions improvised under pressure, and mobilized by preachers, are a recurring pattern Clark stresses; the Swiss cases anticipate the parallel structures of 1848 (a resonance, not a claim Clark makes here).

Moderates outflanked. The mediators are the casualties: Hegetschweiler shot while mediating, Barman outflanked by Young Switzerland, Széchenyi shunned by Metternich and overtaken by Wesselényi and Kossuth. Clark calls Metternich's refusal of Széchenyi "a major blunder" because he was the Austrians' best pre-1848 chance of "a non-revolutionary path of change". This is the same fissuring dynamic (moderate versus radical) that later drives the book's argument about entropy, seen here in embryo.

A parliament that is not a parliament. The Hungarian Diet represented only the Magyar nobility and gentry, and delegates were envoys with county instructions, yet it became a forum for dissent. Wesselényi pushed it from grievance politics to principled stance, using a lithographic press despite the ban.

Print, samizdat and the making of a public. Kossuth's handwritten Parliamentary Reports, then the Municipal Reports, then Pesti Hírlap (4,700 subscribers, perhaps 100,000 readers) created a "national clearing house for critical opinion". Persecution produced martyrs (Wesselényi, Kossuth) who "helped to focus political commitments". Resonates with benedict-anderson on print and imagined community, though Clark does not invoke him and his emphasis is on tactics and personalities. The Magyar-nationalist dimension (Széchenyi warned it risked alienating non-Hungarian nationalities) links to nationalism.

Social roots: getting richer, feeling poorer. Clark explains the gentry's radicalization by rising expectations outrunning income, and notes that many young liberal leaders (Eötvös, Szemere, Kossuth) were financially precarious. This is his own interpretive claim, offered as a partial cause ("in part").

Repression and cooptation backfire. Austrian prosecutions and electoral intimidation "discredited the monarchy" and raised the temperature. Clark treats Metternich's licensing of Kossuth's paper as an unexplained gamble and offers three hypotheses, hedged with "perhaps", including splitting the reformist bloc and falling back on peasants and non-Magyar nationalities.

Notable passages

Zurich and Strauss (c1). Protests from the pulpits, committees of faith and a near-unanimous petition (39,225 to 1,048) force the Great Council to retreat, yet the unrest continues.

Religion and incommensurability (c2). Clark's distilled lesson, with Hegetschweiler as the man who understood it.

Széchenyi's Hitel (c3). The gradualist programme of credit, trust and a new creditor elite, and Clark's characterization of him as conciliator.

Gentry expectations (c4). The socio-economic explanation of Hungarian liberal radicalization.

Kossuth's samizdat (c5). Public sphere building and the martyr function of imprisonment.

Metternich and Pesti Hírlap (c6). The puzzle of Metternich's magnanimity and the paper's effect on Hungarian opinion. For World Machines readers, this bears on how an imperial order that thinks in geopolitical terms misjudges domestic opinion; the fit with legibility-as-machine-core is loose (the Austrian police monitor the opposition but cannot steer it), so treat it as a hedged analogy only.
