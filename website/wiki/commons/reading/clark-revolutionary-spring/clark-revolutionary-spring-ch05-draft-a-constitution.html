Constitutions as the medium of 1848: preventive, revolutionary and counter-revolutionary

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Clark argues that the constitutions of 1848 were versatile, improvised instruments of pre-emption, stabilization and counter-revolution that, through their ubiquity, turned constitution-making from a liberal end into a state-building means, marking a shift from the age of 'constitution' to the age of 'administration'.

Clark surveys the wave of constitutions that every revolution-struck state produced in 1848–9, sorting them into three types: those granted pre-emptively by governments (Piedmont, the Netherlands, Denmark), those drafted by elected assemblies to stabilize the revolution (Switzerland, France, Frankfurt), and those imposed afterwards by resurgent monarchs (Prussia in December 1848, Austria in March 1849). He works through detailed cases: the panicked, four-meeting drafting of the Statuto Albertino under the shock of news from Naples; the French committee writing under the sound of civil strife and dropping the 'right to work'; the Dutch shift of power to an elected Second Chamber; the Prussian and Austrian cases in which 'a constitution was used to kill a constitution'. He then argues for the constitutions' communicative dimension, their afterlives (Danish, Dutch, Swiss and Italian texts still shape their states; Frankfurt echoes in Weimar and the Basic Law), and finally their ambivalence: constitutions became a general-purpose tool of stabilization, even for conservatives, and the age of 'constitution' gave way to the age of 'administration'.

Themes

Constitutions as versatile technologies, not a single liberal programme. Clark stresses that 'Constitutions had always been versatile, and they remained so in 1848.' The same form served to fend off revolution, to capture and cool it, and to bury it. This is a claim about function over ideology: what a constitution did depended on who wrote it, under what pressure, and for what purpose.

Pre-emption is still a product of revolution. Even 'pre-emptive' constitutions came from 'an atmosphere of fear and emergency'. The Piedmontese court moved from 'lead [bullets] and the noose' to a statute because ministers argued that a constitution would strengthen rather than weaken the crown, an argument Clark links to Camphausen's in Prussia. Improvisation under pressure (bits of the Cadiz, French and Belgian texts 'cobbled' together) is the characteristic drafting mode.

Constitutions as stabilizers and instruments against the left. The French drafters sought 'something cool and inert' to conserve a liberal understanding of what had been achieved; the right to work was inserted and removed as street politics shifted, and social-amelioration language was finally moved into the non-binding preamble. The 'Social Question' proved hard to translate into questions about the form of government, and the constitution became a means of delegitimating radical challenges.

Nation- and state-building work. Beyond rights, constitutions defined executive-military relations, broadened franchises (deepening 'the purchase of politics on the population'), and built scaffolding for unions not yet existing (Frankfurt, Switzerland, the April Laws, Piedmont's leadership claim). Clark also notes their limits: no constitution gestured towards women's emancipation, and franchises remained narrow (the Dutch electorate was slightly smaller than before, though it now voted directly).

Communication from elites to the governed. The texts were idiosyncratic, addressing the masses in paternal (Pillersdorf, Statuto), liturgical (Islaz, after Lamennais) or teleological (French preamble) registers.

Afterlives and the conservative conversion. Even rejected or rescinded texts fed later ones. Crucially, the events of 1848 exhausted the conservative view that written constitutions were satanic disruptions; post-1848 conservatives, Bismarck above all, used them for stabilization. Clark reads this as the sign of 'an unexpected emptiness': an end that became a means, with Lorenz von Stein's dictum marking the turn from 'constitution' to 'administration'.

Resonances with World Machines (hedged)

The pattern in which the counter-revolution takes over the very instrument of the revolution and reuses it as a stabilizing mechanism fits the theme of counter-revolution as modernizing reconstruction only loosely, and Clark does not put it in machine terms; the nearer fit is with how an order is reassembled in new form after breakdown, and with legibility-as-machine-core: franchises, budgets, and the state's reach into the population are made formal and legible, and Stein's 'administration' points to that. The gap between the constitutions' grandiloquent preambles and their non-binding social clauses echoes the difference between official ideology and social reality, though Clark's frame is legal-political rather than infrastructural. The modernity-machine and world-machines framings are analogies only; Clark makes no such claim. The Herderian and nation-building strand (nationalism) appears where constitutions supply scaffolding for unions that did not yet exist.

Notable passages

The three types (c1). Clark's typology (preventive, stabilizing, imposed) and the observation that constitutions were versatile frame the whole section.

Piedmont: fear and improvisation (c2). Charles Albert's 'fury and terror' at news from Naples, the Council of State's argument that a constitution would protect the crown, and the added prospect of Italian leadership show a pre-emptive text that was a product of revolution.

France: capturing the revolution (c3). Barrot's recollection of civil strife audible through the windows dramatizes the gap between the stabilizing intent of the drafters and the street.

Austria: a constitution to kill a constitution (c4). The Pillersdorf, Kremsier and Stadion texts show all three types in one empire and the weaponization of the form.

The apogee of liberal politics and the conservative conversion (c5). Constitutions delegitimated the left, diluted their link to progressive politics, and were embraced by conservatives.

Emptiness and the age of administration (c6). Clark's closing assessment, with Stein's formulation.
