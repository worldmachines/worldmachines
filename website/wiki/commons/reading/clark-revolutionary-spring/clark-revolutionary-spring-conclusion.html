Against the verdict of failure: 1848 as consequential chaos and a mirror for the present

Commons · Revolutionary Spring: Fighting for a New World · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Clark closes by rejecting the 'success or failure' verdict on 1848, arguing that the revolutions were chaotic but deeply consequential, produced a post-revolutionary synthesis that excluded the popular classes while absorbing enough of the revolution to stabilize the state, and rhyme strikingly with a present in which high modernity is waning.

Clark's Conclusion opens with the revolutionaries' self-conscious mimicry of 1789 and the sceptics (Tocqueville, Marx) who read that mimicry as emptiness, then dismantles the question of whether the revolutions 'succeeded' or 'failed'. He argues that revolutions are sums of dissonant intentions, that 'failure' can be reminted as success (Hungary 1867, Romania, the French Third Republic, Bismarck), and that 1848 transformed the actors who passed through it: liberals, radicals, conservatives, Catholics, socialists and women's advocates all learned modern politics. The post-revolutionary synthesis excluded the popular classes and delayed universal suffrage, yet gave states new tools of commodities, communications, money, law and policing. He traces effects on Italian and German unification, on Russia and the Crimean War, then turns to the present: the questions and structural problems of 1848 persist, liberals and radicals failed to hear each other, and as high modernity wanes, movements from Occupy to the Capitol storming look less strange against 1848. The book ends on a hedged note that a coming revolution might look like 1848.

Themes

Mimicry as felt historicity, not emptiness. Tocqueville and Marx read the revolutionaries' cross-references to 1789 as parody. Clark answers that being dwarfed by the past is 'a mode of reflexive awareness, a form of felt historicity': there was no single memory of 1789, only a multitude of examples drawn on selectively. This is Clark's argument against those two contemporaries, whose claims remain theirs.

'Success' and 'failure' are the wrong categories. Revolutions are 'the sum of many potentially dissonant or even contradictory intentions', so the mythical ideal of a generative moment never obtains. Clark prefers to measure effects, and notes that failure was later reminted as success by former 1848ers, and that even the counter-revolutionary Bismarck counted the revolution an enabling condition of his career.

Consequence through apprenticeship and institution-building. The revolutions were a 'collision chamber' in which every camp learned the techniques of modern politics: constitutions and chambers for conservatives, confessional politics for Catholics, welfare-oriented socialism, universal suffrage for radicals, coalition trade-offs for liberals. Government itself absorbed the lessons of the upheaval.

The post-revolutionary synthesis. Order was rebuilt on the continuing exclusion of the popular classes and an abiding liberal suspicion of democracy, while digesting 'just enough of the revolution' to proof states against further upheaval. This is the book's clearest statement of counter-revolution as modernizing reconstruction rather than restoration. Socialism gained pragmatic platforms but lost the pre-1848 breadth of speculation about the good life, leaving a reformist majority and revolutionary minority.

Geopolitical afterlives depend on management of the aftermath. Italian and German unification are consequences of 1848 not because of new nationalisms but because monarchs learned the potency of nationalism and nationalists the indispensability of state power. Different post-revolutionary state-building (federal Germany, Piedmont's railway-driven fusion of Italy, reformist Wallachia) produced different outcomes; Russia's turn towards a distinct path fed the Crimean War and later realignments.

Liberals and radicals failed to listen; both missed the countryside. Radicals saw who was being left behind, liberals valued discursive mediation of interests, and their mutual denunciation is called a central tragedy of 1848. Neither made sense of rural society. Clark handles counterfactuals warily (Ginsborg: fun but futile) but uses them to expose design flaws: loss of cohesion under democratic conditions, failure of dialogue, hardened orthodoxies, inability to prioritize.

The present as a rhyme. As 'modernity' (industrialization, ideological parties, nation and welfare state, secularization, mass media) goes into flux and the left-right axis fails, Clark reads recent movements against 1848: improvised theatricality, unstable leadership, fused ideologies, the Ottawa convoy's carnivalesque insurrectionary logic. He explicitly declines to equate them with Louis Blanc's reasoned reformism.

Resonances with World Machines

The claim that the 1850s order digested just enough revolution to survive, and that legibility of the poor and the countryside was the blind spot, fits the WMP interest in orders reassembled in new form; see legibility-as-machine-core and nationalism for the state-nation fusion, and legibility-hardens-hierarchy-bayly for the exclusionary side of state consolidation, though Clark offers no machine theory. His statement that the things 'we used to call modernity' are 'now in flux' loosely rhymes with modernity-machine in Dusk and the interregnum of the gramsci-gap, but it is a historian's impressionistic sketch, not a claim about a thousand-year machine. The point that the revolution assembled itself from polyvocal, uncoordinated vectors echoes history-machine in spirit, again without any shared vocabulary. Clark's Saint-Simonian 'idlers' and 'industrials' echo through Douthat's Virtuals and Practicals (see henri-de-saint-simon), a comparison that is Clark's, not a WMP finding.

Notable passages

c1, felt historicity. Clark's rebuttal of Tocqueville's and Marx's charge of mimicry, using the Puritans and Mosaic scripture as analogy and noting Marx's own ambivalence.

c2, revolutions as sums of dissonant intentions. The storm analogy and the argument that no actual revolution meets the mythical standard; 1848 was marked by polyvocality and lack of coordination.

c3, the collision chamber. The catalogue of what each political camp learned, the empirical core of the claim that chaos was still consequential.

c4, the post-revolutionary synthesis. The exclusion of the popular classes and the state's new toolkit of commodities, communications, money, law and policing; the heart of the book's counter-revolution-as-modernization thesis.

c5, end of modernity. Clark's account of why 1848 now feels close: the institutions of high modernity are waning, and the left-right map fails.

c6, closing. The final hedged forecast that a coming revolution might look like 1848, and the reversal that the people of 1848 could see themselves in us.
