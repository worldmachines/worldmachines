Printing's Parting of the Ways: Scripture and the Book of Nature

Commons · The Printing Revolution in Early Modern Europe · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Printing pushed Bible study and natural philosophy onto opposite paths—entangling Scripture in ever-multiplying translation variants and confessional fracture while freeing science through standardized pictures, diagrams, maps, and mathematical notation—and Eisenstein closes the argument by insisting the press was an overdetermined but genuinely revolutionary force, not “just a tool.”

This unit closes out a chapter comparing the Reformation and the Scientific Revolution: it argues that although both movements could be described in the same rhetoric of purifying "corrupt copies" to recover "perfect originals" (Bishop Sprat's parallel between the Anglican church and the Royal Society, both "passing by the corrupt copies and referring themselves to the perfect originals"), and although the two communities briefly shared personnel and problems (astronomers fixing the date of Easter, humanists chasing Greek and Hebrew for both Bible and nature), printing drove Scripture and science apart once it took hold. Vernacular Bible printing multiplied doctrinal variants, hardened confessional lines, and never delivered the "pure original words of God" it promised; scientific publication, by contrast, replaced words with standardized pictures, tables, and equations that let observers pool data across language barriers and converge on consensus — Newton's "few elegant, simple universal laws" against the endlessly proliferating polyglot Bible. The unit ends by defending printing's status as an independent historical force against an instrumentalist "it's only a tool" view, closing on Eisenstein's own formula: the press "laid the basis both for literal fundamentalism and for modern science."

Themes

A shared starting point, forced apart. Eisenstein first grants the surface parallel — corrupted texts, purification, "Reformation" claimed by both church and Royal Society — then insists it doesn't survive contact with what printing actually did: "Scriptural and scientific traditions had taken a “like course” in the age of scribes. By the time of the Reformation, however, they had come to a parting of the ways." Before print, "how the heavens go" and "how to go to heaven" were the same inquiry (Erasmus and Copernicus both dated old records and old festivals); after print, celestial mechanics raced ahead of calendrical piety.

"Image to word" for religion, "word to image" for science. Her governing formula for the whole divergence: Protestant reform runs from "image to word" (iconoclasm, vernacular Bible reading), but the rise of modern science requires the reverse, "word to image" — printing reduced translation problems and bridged university and artisan cultures for natural philosophy in a way it never could for Scripture, whose communications shift instead "permanently fragment[ed] Western Christendom along both geographic and sociological lines."

Standardized pictures as an engine of consensus. Identical diagrams beneath diverse vernacular names, uniform maps and globes, logarithm tables and slide rules gave scattered observers a common measure; Eisenstein calls these "silent instructors" that let "nature's language" be "emancipated from the old confusion of tongues." This is her version of a legibility argument — fixity and uniform notation are what let a "large-scale pooling of talents" cut across old frontiers, resonating with legibility-as-machine-core and Legibility even though her frame is a specific historical mechanism (shared diagrams, tables, maps) rather than a general theory of state control.

Print as overdetermined, not monocausal. Rather than crediting a single driver — capitalist profit, evangelical mission, the printer's own hunger for fame, or rulers extending their charisma — Eisenstein insists these motives converged and reinforced one another: "the use of early presses by Western Europeans was “overdetermined.” The convergence of different impulses proved irresistible, producing a massive irreversible cultural “change of phase.”" This is a machine-construction argument in miniature: infrastructure assembling from many independently-motivated pushes until it tips into an irreversible new regime, the kind of dynamic that underwrites modernity-machine construction during its Dawn.

Answering the "just a tool" critique. In the unit's final movement Eisenstein quotes — in order to reject — an instrumentalist position from unnamed humanist critics of technology: "The powers which shape men’s lives may be expressed in books and type, but by and of itself printing . . . is only a tool, an instrument, and the multiplication of tools and instruments does not of itself affect intellectual and spiritual life." That is the view under attack, not her own. Her reply, and the unit's closing line, is that intellectual and spiritual life "were profoundly transformed" and that the press "laid the basis both for literal fundamentalism and for modern science" — an agent of change, in her careful phrase elsewhere in the book, not a sole or simple cause, which is the same hedge behind legibility-as-precondition-not-cause.

Vernacular print and the nationalizing of Scripture. In passing, Eisenstein notes that "Vernacular Bibles authorized by Protestant rulers helped to balkanize Christendom and to nationalize what had previously been a more cosmopolitan sacred book" — a direct anticipation of benedict-anderson's print-capitalism account of nationalism, though her interest here is confessional fragmentation rather than the rise of imagined national communities as such.

Notable passages

  • The parting of the ways (c2). The paragraph pair stating her core thesis plainly: shared scribal-age inquiry pulled apart once print accelerated one side (celestial mechanics) while leaving the other (calendrical/Scriptural reckoning) behind.
  • "Word to image" (c1). The single clearest statement of the chapter's organizing reversal, explicitly framed as resisting any one-formula account of the communications shift.
  • Silent instructors (c5). Vesalius and Galileo's pictorial/geometric preference, Fontenelle's "reign of words" had ended, and Thomas Elyot's 1531 endorsement of figures over lecture — concrete evidence for how standardized images displaced words in scientific communication well before the Scientific Revolution proper.
  • Overdetermined causation (c3). Her methodological payoff: printing's triumph wasn't reducible to capitalism, fame, piety, or statecraft alone, but to their irreversible convergence.
  • The "only a tool" rebuttal (c4). The unit's climax: she stages the instrumentalist critique in a block quotation, then answers it directly, ending on the book's most quotable line about literal fundamentalism and modern science sharing one root.
