Defending the Printing Revolution: Phase Change, the Long Revolution, and a Fragmented Europe

Commons · The Printing Revolution in Early Modern Europe · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Continuing the 2005 Afterword, Eisenstein answers critics by distinguishing her “communicative spectrum” argument from an all-or-nothing “impact” model she never held, replaces “impact” with a “cooperative transition” or “change of phase” metaphor, unpacks “revolution” into an abrupt-replacement sense and a long-range-irreversible-process sense that she traces through Gesner’s 1545 universal bibliography into modern information overload, argues that printing’s explosive fifteenth-century spread across Europe depended on political fragmentation and pre-existing trade networks rather than nation-state consolidation, and closes by defending “shift from script to print” against the misreading that handwriting was an early casualty of the press.

This stretch of the 2005 Afterword finds Eisenstein working through a list of critics' objections one by one: she reframes apparent contradictions as differences of emphasis, defends her "communicative spectrum" framing of speech/writing/print against readings that flatten it into naive technological impact, replaces the discarded "impact" metaphor with a physics-derived "cooperative transition"/"change of phase" model, unpacks the double meaning of "revolution" she intended for her title, traces the shift from an economy of scarcity to an economy of abundance through Conrad Gesner's 1545 universal bibliography, argues for a multivariable explanation of why print exploded in fragmented Western Europe rather than centralized China, the Ottoman empire, or Muscovy, and finally defends her phrase "shift from script to print" against the charge that it wrote off surviving handwriting.

Themes

Print altered the balance, not the existence, of speech and writing. Eisenstein insists her disagreement with recent scholarship is one of emphasis: she was never claiming print eliminated oral and written coexistence, only that printing shifted the balance between "interactive modes." Her test case is mobilization speed and reach: a "handwritten pasquillade" could not synchronize scattered publics into "a relatively simultaneous response" the way a printed one could — "the thirteen clocks to strike as one" — and an "imagined" society like "Mr. Spectator's Club" is misclassified as oral communication when it was, in fact, entirely a print-dependent "virtual" community. The imagery of print synchronizing dispersed audiences into one argument half-rhymes with old-mother-press's own myth of the press marching "letters into columns" until scattered villages "woke up arguing the same argument" — though Old Mother Press is deliberate mythopoeia and Eisenstein is making a historical claim about simultaneity and reach, not building a personification.

Dumb preachers, silent instructors, mute orators. Print gave a channel to those denied a platform: recusant preachers and Marian exiles who kept preaching "without speaking" from Continental printing shops, Huguenot exiles running periodicals after the revocation of the Edict of Nantes, teachers building an "imaginary classroom far larger than an individual classroom could be," and a new figure she coins here, the "mute orator" — political tribunes who lacked oratorical gifts but reached "a people dispersed" through the press where orators once reached "a people assembled." She extends this to Condorcet, who believed print's "cold reason" would supplant the "power of eloquence" in politics — a hope the French Revolutionary press itself refuted, since print proved just as compatible with demagoguery and religious fanaticism as any oral medium ("open books sometimes go together with closed minds").

"Impact" discarded for "cooperative transition" / "change of phase." The chapter's central methodological move: Eisenstein regrets ever using "impact," rejects the "metaphor of billiard balls colliding," and instead reaches for physics and chemistry — a "cooperative transition" or "change of phase" in which the same elements persist while "the whole is transformed into a different state." This directly answers John Barnard's claim that "the printing press was not, on its own, an agent of change" since "print, politics and religion were inextricably linked": Eisenstein calls this a non sequitur, since hand copying was also linked to politics and religion in medieval Europe — what changed after printing was "the character of the linkage itself," not the mere fact of linkage. "Agent," for her, never meant sole cause; historical change is "indeterminate" and "compatible with movement in diverse directions," as the divergent reactions to vernacular and polyglot Bibles show.

Two senses of "revolution," and scarcity giving way to abundance. She intended both meanings of the title's key word: the conventional sense of an abrupt, decisive change (hand copying replaced by printing as the "chief" mode of book production within decades) and the sense of a "long-range irreversible process" whose effects intensify over time, as with an "industrial revolution" or Raymond Williams's The Long Revolution. She traces the long-process sense through a scarcity-to-abundance reversal: medieval Europe needed roughly ten scribes to supply one reader, a ratio inverted within a century of print. The turning point is Conrad Gesner's 1545 Biblioteca Universalis — the first, and last, serious attempt at a truly universal bibliography of the Commonwealth of Learning — after which bibliographies specialized and multiplied until "bibliographies of bibliographies of bibliographies" had to be compiled, an early self-parodying instance of the condition named in printing-press-and-information-overload and information-overload-across-eras: abundance, she notes pointedly, "can be no less dispiriting than scarcity."

Why fragmented Western Europe, not China, the Ottoman empire, or Muscovy. Eisenstein rejects any single-factor explanation, weighing alphabetic-versus-ideographic script alongside religion (the Ottoman ban on printing the Koran or any Arabic-script text; slow, church-sponsored Russian printing) and, above all, politics: the absence of a consolidated central authority let printers exploit rivalry among bishoprics, free cities, and petty principalities competing for prestige and revenue, so that major book-production centers (Venice, Antwerp, Amsterdam — the Venice cluster likened to a "Silicon Valley" of short-lived "dot-com" startups) diverged from political capitals. This is a direct complication, not a confirmation, of the standard print-and-nationalism story associated with benedict-anderson: here "nation building worked at cross purposes with the rapid expansion of early modern printing industries," since print-capitalism's takeoff depended on the political fragmentation nation-states would later erase. Layered onto this are economic and craft preconditions — manuscript and cloth-trade networks, paper, credit, metallurgy — that had been "incubating" since the fourteenth century; "a convergence of many different impulses and motives" is what let presses install themselves "in fewer than five decades" from the Atlantic to Montenegro to Stockholm. That image of long-separate components silently maturing before suddenly locking together into a continent-wide effect rhymes with history-machine's account of paradigms assembling from independently developing pieces until they "lurch into visibility" — though Eisenstein's is a historian's multivariable causal analysis with named preconditions and precipitants, not a claim about paradigm-level shock.

Defending "shift from script to print": handwriting was not a casualty. Eisenstein pushes back on the reading that her title phrase implied handwriting's death. Letter-writing, ledger-keeping, dictation, and hand copying itself all persisted and in some ways intensified after print (printers circulated model letters and bookkeeping manuals more than scribes ever had); she cites Reader's Digest founder DeWitt Wallace copying passages by hand in the New York Public Library, her own pre-photocopier habits, and Anthony Grafton's line, "Scholars remained scribes for a long time. Some of us still are." She also defends calling Petrarch and his contemporaries "scribal scholars": not to demean them but to register, against a later generation's "enormous condescension," the real handicap of total dependence on hand-copied texts — a handicap Poliziano and later humanists no longer faced once printed materials were available.

Notable passages

  • The communicative-spectrum passage (c1) states Eisenstein's core methodological correction: a claim about altered balance among media, not about print's sole existence, illustrated by the pasquillade and Mr. Spectator's Club.
  • The dumb-preachers/mute-orator passage (c2) is her most vivid statement of print substituting for oratory and pulpit access denied by "unfriendly regimes."
  • The cooperative-transition passage (c3) is the chapter's key model-building move, replacing "impact" with a phase-change metaphor and setting up the Barnard rebuttal.
  • The Gesner/bibliographies-of-bibliographies passage (c4) grounds the long-revolution sense of the title in a concrete case of scarcity flipping into overload.
  • The nation-building-at-cross-purposes passage (c5) makes the counter-nationalism argument explicit: political fragmentation, not consolidation, powered print's early spread.
  • The preconditions-and-precipitants passage (c6) is the unit's clearest statement of multivariable historical causation and the closest rhyme with a history-machine-style convergence of independently maturing components.
