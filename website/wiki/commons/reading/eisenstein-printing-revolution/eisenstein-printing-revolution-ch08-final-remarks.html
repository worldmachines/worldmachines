Some Final Remarks: Irreversibility, Overload, and the Limits of Grand Narratives

Commons · The Printing Revolution in Early Modern Europe · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Closing the book, Eisenstein argues that the print revolution's cumulative processes are irreversible and still gathering momentum in the computer age, that the information-overload problem print inaugurated has only intensified rather than been resolved by newer media, and that Hegelian and Marxist grand narratives wrongly assume such contradictory historical trends must resolve into a dialectical end rather than persist indefinitely.

In this short closing chapter, Eisenstein first admits the limits of her study (it stops at the wooden handpress and says nothing of steam presses, telegraphs, Linotype, typewriters, or later media) and then defends the book's central claim against unnamed critics: the fifteenth-century printing revolution set cumulative, irreversible processes in motion that are still gathering momentum in the computer age. She surveys contemporary analogues of print institutions surviving under new technical skins, argues that the "knowledge industry" and its overload problems keep expanding rather than resolving, and closes by rejecting Hegelian and Marxist historical schemes that would force print's contradictory legacies toward a dialectical end, insisting instead that contradictions like bigotry-and-toleration or national-fixity-and-cosmopolitan-learning can stand unresolved indefinitely.

Themes

Irreversible, cumulative momentum. Responding to "contrary views," Eisenstein insists the early-modern printing revolution set off cumulative processes in the mid-fifteenth century that "have not ceased to gather momentum" all the way to "the age of the computer printout and the television guide" — an explicit continuity thesis linking Gutenberg's press to modern data technology as one unbroken cumulative arc. This reads as modernity-machine infrastructure still gathering momentum well past its Dawn, though Eisenstein frames it as one continuous historical process rather than a discrete construction-to-dominance machine cycle.

Overload as print's enduring by-product, not a solved problem. New media do not relieve the pressure print created; they compound it. Library overload persists, and "an enhanced capacity to store and retrieve, preserve and transmit, has kept pace with an enhanced capacity to create and destroy, innovate and outmode." She traces the "chaotic appearance of modern Western culture" to the "duplicative powers of print" as much as to newer technologies, and personifies the problem via the "voracious appetite of Chronos" (feared historically) now surpassed by "a monstrous capacity to disgorge." This is squarely the printing-press-and-information-overload and information-overload-across-eras thesis: overload is not a side effect of the digital age but a five-centuries-old condition print inaugurated and every subsequent medium has intensified.

Institutional continuity beneath technological churn. Even as movable metal type and nineteenth-century publishing institutions are "rapidly undermined," their functional descendants reappear: commercial copy centers spring up on campuses much as stationers' stalls once did near medieval universities, and professors acquire word processors to bypass university presses. Eisenstein reads this as the same institutional logic recurring under new technical skins, not as rupture.

Against grand dialectical narratives. Eisenstein diagnoses a recurring "impulse to end tales that are still unfolding" as an inheritance from Hegel and Marx, whose schemes "point logical dialectical conflicts toward logical dialectical ends" and disallow "an indefinite prolongation of fundamentally contradictory trends." Against this she insists the print revolution's contradictions were never resolved: it "fanned the flames of religious zeal and bigotry while fostering a new concern for ecumenical concord and toleration," and it "fixed linguistic and national divisions more permanently while creating a cosmopolitan Commonwealth of Learning" spanning the globe. The nationalizing half of that pairing is the nationalism thesis associated with benedict-anderson (print fixing vernacular and national boundaries), held here in the same breath as its opposite, an internationalizing Republic of Letters — her point is that both effects are real and neither cancels the other. This refusal to force a single resolution onto a standing contradiction half-rhymes with world-machines's picture of eras defined by unresolved friction rather than dialectical synthesis, though her "contradictory trends" are two effects of one process, not three coexisting infrastructures — a narrower, more historically specific claim than the world-machines architecture.

Epistemic humility about unfinished business. Some consequences of Gutenberg's invention are now available for retrospective analysis, "more than could be seen in francis-bacon's day," but others "are still unfolding" and "impossible to gauge at present." She warns against "premature leaps in the dark" and against a modern habit of writing off, by "premature obituaries," problems that recur for later generations. The chapter ends on an admission of incompleteness: the "gulf that separates the age of scribes from that of printers" has yet to be fully probed.

Notable passages

  • The opening defense of irreversibility (c1) is the chapter's thesis statement, aimed at unnamed critics of the book's continuity claim.
  • The library-overload and duplicative-powers passage (c2), together with the Chronos and premature-obituaries aside (c3), make the case that overload is print's own long-running legacy rather than a novel digital-age complaint.
  • The Hegel/Marx passage (c4) is the unit's most explicit historiographical statement: a rejection of teleological grand narratives in favor of standing, unresolved contradiction, illustrated by print's simultaneous nationalizing and cosmopolitanizing effects.
