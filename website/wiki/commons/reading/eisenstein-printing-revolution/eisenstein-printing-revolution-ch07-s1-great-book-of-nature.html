The Great Book of Nature and the Little Books of Men

Commons · The Printing Revolution in Early Modern Europe · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Eisenstein argues that the cliché of Renaissance natural philosophers abandoning "the little books of men" for direct study of "the great book of Nature" obscures how printed texts, illustrations, and maps made systematic observation possible at all.

Opening Chapter Seven, Eisenstein argues that print's role in the Scientific Revolution has been underrated even relative to its grudging acknowledgment in Reformation history, then spends the section dismantling the commonplace that Renaissance natural philosophers turned away from "the little books of men" to read directly in "the great book of Nature." She traces the two-books (Scripture/Nature) metaphor from medieval sermon literature through Sir Thomas Browne, engages the Curtius-versus-Burckhardt debate over its medieval pedigree, and argues that the identical old metaphor concealed a wholesale transformation in what "reading nature" required once printed maps, illustrations, and cross-referenced compendia existed. She extends the argument to scientific illustration (why sixteenth-century woodcuts suddenly improved) and to cartography (feedback loops between mapmakers and map users), concluding that access to expanding, duplicated libraries — not liberation from books — was the precondition of the era's advances in natural history and mapmaking.

Themes

  • The "book of nature" cliché is a category error, not a discovery. Eisenstein's central move is to ask how the "great book of Nature" could ever have been investigated except "by means of the 'little books of men'" — the dichotomy historians invoke to explain the Scientific Revolution's origin is itself the thing that needs explaining away.
  • Old metaphors persist while their referents change. She partly credits and partly corrects Ernst Curtius's rebuttal of Burckhardt: the "two books" trope's medieval ancestry is real, but that fact doesn't defang her claim, since Browne's "universal publick Manuscript" and Saint Bernard's book of nature share words but not referents — one is monastic allegory about field labor, the other assumes durable printed texts, star catalogues, and diagrams.
  • Empiricist "away from books" rhetoric was itself a print-enabled genre. The Paracelsus and Campanella manifestos, and the Royal Society's charge to "study Nature rather than Books," were disseminated and reproduced as books — an irony she catches via Olschki: writers who preached direct observation "know natural phenomena only from books."
  • Print, not the rejection of print, explains the sixteenth-century leap in scientific illustration. Before print, images degraded through copying exactly as texts did, and accurate naturalistic drawing was a "marginal activity" (literally, decorating manuscript borders) rather than a vehicle for technical demonstration. What changed was the new capacity to keep a master draftsman's work "intact in hundreds of copies," not a sudden new appetite for observation.
  • Cartography as a case study in fixity, feedback, and cumulative correction. Against Kenneth Boulding's assumption that manuscript world maps already had a "uniform spatial reference frame," Eisenstein marshals evidence that hand-copied maps from 300–1300 CE show degradation, not "orderly development." What Boulding rightly identifies — a "process of feedback from the users of maps to the map maker" — was only achievable once printed, duplicated maps let scattered readers check, correct, and return corrections to publishers; this is a clean instance of the book's "data collection" feature, and edges toward legibility-as-precondition-not-cause and Legibility: uniform, reproducible maps are a legibility technology, though Eisenstein is careful to credit print as precondition and enabler rather than sole cause of the resulting "closure of geographic space," which she notes still took centuries and many lives to achieve — a gradual, contested-uniformity-bayly-style process rather than an instant switch.
  • Reorganization and library expansion as the real engine of the era's data collection. Atlases like Ortelius's Theatrum (with alphabetical index and continually expanding editions) and libraries like Aldus Manutius's (which Erasmus said had "no other limits than the world itself") depended on breaking "the tyranny of major authorities inherent in small libraries." This is print as modernity-machine infrastructure in miniature: the same expansion of duplicated, indexed material that enabled cumulative science also anticipates the management problem named in information-overload-across-eras, though here Eisenstein's emphasis falls on indexing and cross-referencing as solutions rather than on overload as a burden.

Notable passages

Eisenstein sets up her argument by quoting an unnamed authority's claim that the voyages of discovery should have "turned attention from the little books of men to the great book of Nature," then answers it herself: "Yet how could the 'great book of Nature' be investigated ... without exchanging information by means of the 'little books of men'?" — this rhetorical question is the section's thesis in miniature, and the framing sentences around the quoted claim are where her own position lives, not the claim itself.

She locates the historiographical stakes in the "two books" metaphor's persistence: after weighing Curtius's correct-but-insufficient rebuttal of Burckhardt (the metaphor is medieval, not a Renaissance invention), she insists that "the persistence of old metaphors often masks major changes" — Browne's and Saint Bernard's identical words describe incompatible objects, separated by the shift from script to print.

On scientific illustration, her own analytic claim — not a quotation from another historian — is that what made the "sixteenth-century revolution" possible was not new observational zeal but the fact that "for the first time the work of skilled draftsmen could be preserved intact in hundreds of copies of a given book," reversing centuries of scribal image decay.

She turns the empiricists' own rhetoric against their self-image, endorsing Olschki's observation that writers who invoked the "Away from books!" motto in fact "know natural phenomena only from books" — evidence that even avowedly anti-bookish natural philosophy was a publishing phenomenon, and that the virtuosi were "not entirely consistent in attacking the written word."

Citing, and partly correcting, Kenneth Boulding, she credits him with identifying the "process of feedback from the users of maps to the map maker" as the true source of a printed map's authority, even while disputing his separate assumption that manuscript maps already possessed a uniform reference frame.

Finally, Erasmus's tribute to Aldus Manutius — whose library "had no other limits than the world itself" — and George Sarton's claim that a great library is "just as much a scientific instrument as ... the telescope or cyclotron" crystallize her argument that expanding, cross-referenced print collections were themselves the scientific apparatus of the era.
