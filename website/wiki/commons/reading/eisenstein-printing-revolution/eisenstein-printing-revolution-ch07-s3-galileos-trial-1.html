Another Look at Galileo's Trial: Publication, Confession, and the Geography of Early Modern Science

Commons · The Printing Revolution in Early Modern Europe · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Historians debating where the Scientific Revolution took off have neglected publication itself: Protestant presses printed and defended Copernican astronomy almost without interruption from Rheticus through Galileo's Sidereus Nuncius, while Catholic presses, briefly reactivated by Galileo's 1610 sensation, were shut back down by the 1616 Index, and even the Reformation promise that everyman could read the "book of Nature" collapses once Galileo insists that book is written only in mathematics.

Despite its title, this installment of "Another Look at Galileo's Trial" is not yet about the 1633 trial itself; it is Eisenstein's brief against every existing account of where and why the Scientific Revolution took off, on the grounds that all of them ignore the printer, the publisher, and the "geography of the book." She surveys the standard candidate explanations — the Italian Platonic revival, Wittenberg-versus-Padua Protestant-priority arguments, Frances Yates's Hermetic-Cabalist thesis, the demystifying-materialist reading, and the eclectic multivariable compromise — and argues that none goes far enough because none asks who actually got a given work into print, in a given region, at a given political moment. She then builds her own case from the confessional geography of Copernican publishing: Protestant presses carried pro-Copernican work almost continuously from Rheticus onward, while Catholic presses stayed largely inactive until Galileo's Sidereus Nuncius (1610) briefly reversed the pattern, only for the 1616 Index decree to close the window again. The unit ends on a different but related problem: the analogy between the Protestant "priesthood of all believers" and the ideal of "everyman his own scientist," an analogy she endorses only partway before Galileo's own words undercut it.

Themes

Publication, not just research, has a geography, and that geography is a missing variable in histories of science. Eisenstein's opening move is methodological: "to understand the circumstances confronting seventeenth-century scientists we need to assign more significance to the act of publication and to the role of intermediaries who got the printing done." Because the distribution of printing industries can be mapped with real precision, and printing centers track "the fixing of religious and dynastic frontiers," the outcome of dynastic and religious wars becomes a variable in the history of science, not an "extraneous political event." This is a variant of her general thesis about print as infrastructure: the press is not a neutral conduit for pre-existing scientific ideas but a resource whose access was rationed by confession and politics, in ways that read like an early, low-tech instance of the modernity-machine's legibility-and-control apparatus being applied to who gets to publish, though her claim is narrower and more historical than a machine-theoretic one.

A confessional asymmetry runs through Copernican publishing that theological statements alone can't explain. Eisenstein's strongest evidence is the printing history of Copernicus's own book: "as a churchman Copernicus was aided by several Catholic prelates and he had his major treatise dedicated to a Catholic pope. Nevertheless the actual work of publishing De revolutionibus was done under Protestant auspices" — a paradox that recurs with Rheticus's First Narration, with Bruno's treatises (printed in London by the Protestant printer John Charlewood despite fictitious Venice and Paris imprints), and with Galileo's own Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, which reached print only through the Protestant intermediaries Elias Diodati and Louis Elsevier. Against this she sets a rebuttal of "vulgar" equivalence-drawing between Protestant grumbling and Catholic censorship — the claim that Luther's contempt for Copernicus "strangled" German science is false on its face given Rheticus, Reinhold, Maestlin, and Kepler — because "theological statements do not represent the same kind of resistance to change as do measures taken to prevent publication." Catholic and Protestant theologians held nearly the same hedged, hypothetical view of a moving earth; only Catholic printers were later forbidden to publish it.

Galileo's Sidereus Nuncius is the exception that proves the rule, and the 1616 Index closes it again. The 1610 Starry Messenger was an "overnight sensation" that briefly reactivated Italian and even Jesuit presses (the discoveries reached a Jesuit missionary in Peking within five years) and pulled the Republic of Letters' attention back toward Italy from the Protestant north. But the windfall lasted "only five or six years": the 1616 Congregation of the Index decree against vernacular Copernican writing made such publication "risky for Catholic printers and thus all the more profitable for Protestant ones" — the same forces that had suppressed Catholic vernacular Bible-printing now closed down Catholic scientific publication.

The Reformation's "everyman his own priest" analogy extends to science, then breaks under Galileo's own mathematical elitism. Eisenstein traces a real but overstated analogy — Palissy's "I have no book but heaven and earth and it is given to everyman to know and read this beautiful book," Culpeper's vernacular medical translations meant to make "everyman his own physician," Hooykaas's naturalists calling on "the unlearned" to report observations — to the Protestant doctrine that everybody should "read the book of Scripture for himself." She credits Palissy's public lectures for influencing francis-bacon, but immediately qualifies the analogy with Galileo's own insistence that the "book of Nature," although "always open to public inspection, was not really given to everyman to know and read": it is written "in the language of mathematics," a code that grew only more technical across the seventeenth century, so that "isaac-newton was as incomprehensible to the average mechanic as Thomas Aquinas." Where Scripture-reading had one credentialing barrier (Latin) traded for a promise of universal access, the book of Nature substitutes a new, narrower priesthood of mathematical specialists. This inverts rather than confirms legibility-as-machine-core: the press does not make nature newly legible to a lay public so much as it enshrines a technical code that fences legibility off to the initiated few — worth flagging as a genuine but partial rhyme, since Eisenstein's "specialists" are credentialed by training rather than by any centralizing state apparatus.

Print turned readers into data-suppliers, and the motives behind that were never simply pious or simply mercenary. Atlas- and herbal-authors solicited notes on coastlines and plants from their readers, "a form of data collection... in which 'everyman' could play a supporting role," and instrument-makers used prefaces to advertise their shops alongside professions of service to God and country. Eisenstein resists reducing this to either Hooykaas's genuine-piety reading or a "vulgar Marxist" profit-motive reading: publication was "fueled not by love of God or Mammon but by love of God and Mammon." The artisan-author claiming both public benefit and personal reputation (the same dynamic she traces elsewhere under print-culture-and-the-individual-author, where print fixes an author's name to a text) is one more case of print's capacity to braid altruism and self-interest into a single institutional practice.

Notable passages

The paragraph on the "geography of the book" is the unit's methodological thesis statement: publication and its intermediaries deserve as much weight as doctrine in explaining regional science, because printing-center distribution can be correlated with "religious and dynastic frontiers."

The Copernicus passage is the unit's sharpest single piece of evidence for that thesis: a Catholic churchman's book, dedicated to a pope and aided by prelates, was nonetheless printed under Protestant auspices — patronage and production pulled in opposite confessional directions.

The Sidereus Nuncius paragraph narrates the one clear counter-case (a Catholic-press sensation) and its short half-life, ending with the 1616 Index closing the door Galileo had briefly opened.

The two-part Galileo quotation on the "book of Nature" ("Philosophy is written in this grand book... It is written in the language of mathematics") is the pivot where Eisenstein's own "everyman" analogy is checked by Galileo's mathematical elitism, capped by her own line about Newton and the new technical priesthood.

The data-collection passage shows print's readers turning into an early observational network — a minor but concrete instance of the standardization-and-feedback dynamic Eisenstein treats elsewhere as one of print's defining "features."
