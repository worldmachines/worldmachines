Introduction: From Two False Diagnoses to a Question No One Had Asked

Commons · The Printing Revolution in Early Modern Europe · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Eisenstein recounts how her dissatisfaction with Bridenbaugh's and McLuhan's overload narratives led her to ask, and find unanswered, what the fifteenth-century shift from script to print actually changed, then narrows her Western-European, elite-literate-culture inquiry and insists printing was an agent, not the sole cause, of change.

Eisenstein opens the book not with a thesis but with an intellectual autobiography: she traces her turn to the history of printing back to two overload narratives she found symptomatic rather than diagnostic — Carl Bridenbaugh's 1962 lament over historians' "collective amnesia" and Marshall McLuhan's The Gutenberg Galaxy — and from her dissatisfaction with both derives the founding question of her life's work: what did the fifteenth-century shift from script to print actually change, given that, to her surprise, no one had ever surveyed it?

Themes

Overload misdiagnosed as amnesia. Bridenbaugh's AHA address blamed a "run-away technology" for severing historians from the past and diagnosed a "collective amnesia," equating the decline of a "common culture of Bible reading" (itself only five centuries old, and never universal even after Gutenberg) with the fate of Western civilization writ large. Eisenstein reframes the same evidence: the profession's trouble was not forgetting but recall — an unmanageable volume of data "impinging on us from so many directions and with such speed" that ordering capacity was "strained to the breaking point." She proposes a rate-of-publication explanation where Bridenbaugh reached for a media-technology one, prefiguring the book's recurring theme of print-driven printing-press-and-information-overload and its modern echoes (information-overload-across-eras).

McLuhan as a second symptom, not a cure. marshall-mcluhan's The Gutenberg Galaxy struck her as the mirror image of Bridenbaugh — mischievous rather than alarmed, but equally a symptom of print-culture overload rather than a diagnosis of it, its own incoherence testifying to the problem it claimed to explain. What she credits McLuhan with is not an answer but a well-posed question: what were the actual effects of the shift from script to print? Her surprise at finding "not even a small literature" surveying that shift is the origin point of the entire project — a genuine gap, not a rhetorical one.

A deliberately narrow, unfashionable scope. Eisenstein stakes her project against the "current vogue for 'history from below'": she is concerned with a shift within literate culture (scribal to print), not the shift from oral to literate culture that occupies Africanists and social historians, and her subject is explicitly the unpopular "high" culture of Latin-reading professional elites transacting within the Commonwealth of Learning. She is respectful of the historiographical trend she is setting aside — she calls its new fields of inquiry valuable and enriching — but candid that her book will not serve its purposes. Geographically she confines herself to Western Europe (reserving "print culture" for post-Gutenberg developments there specifically), while chronologically she reaches back to Alexandria and medieval scriptoria whenever the argument requires it.

Printing as an agent, not the agent. The methodological center of gravity for the whole book: Eisenstein insists printing is "an agent, not the agent, let alone the only agent, of change," precisely to head off the charge of monocausal, technologically determinist reductionism that dogs any book organized around a single innovation. Her multivariable-interaction argument — that she is not substituting one variable for many but explaining why many long-present variables "began to interact in new ways" — is the hedge that should travel with every claim later in the book about print's effects. It resonates with a legibility-as-precondition-not-cause-style caution: an infrastructural shift can be necessary without being sufficient, an enabling condition rather than a sole engine. Read against the World Machines frame, this is also the right way to hold the press as a piece of the modernity-machine's Dawn infrastructure — Eisenstein would resist any claim that a single technology built that machine on its own, even the one she spent a career on.

Notable passages

The Bridenbaugh critique (c1) and the "recall rather than oblivion" reframing (c2) together do the real diagnostic work before the book even begins: they replace a moralized narrative of memory-loss with an empirical one about information volume and processing capacity, the seed of the printing-and-overload line the book returns to repeatedly. The McLuhan passage (c3) supplies the project's origin question and its evidentiary justification — the absence of any prior survey of print's consequences is what licenses a decade-long study. The scope passage (c4) preempts an obvious objection (why not write about oral and popular culture instead?), and matters because it tells the reader what kind of claims to expect from the rest of the book: about elite, Latin, written culture, not about literacy's spread among the many. The closing methodological passage (c5) is the disclaimer she asks to be judged against: not "printing caused modernity" but "these variables began to interact in new ways" once print entered the mix — the frame that should discipline how her later claims about standardization, fixity, the Reformation, and the Scientific Revolution get used downstream.
