The Reach of Standardization: From Errata to Individualism

Commons · The Printing Revolution in Early Modern Europe · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Standardization across errata, maps, notation, typography, costume, and architecture produced not only replicable sameness but, as its dialectical counterpart, a heightened sense of diversity and ultimately of individual selfhood, exemplified by Montaigne's Essays.

Eisenstein defends standardization as a feature of print culture that deserves study on its own terms, careful to avoid two opposite errors: crediting early print with modern-style identical "standard editions," and underestimating how much scribal culture genuinely could not do. She traces standardization's reach across editorial correction, calendars and reference works, visual and notational systems, typography and calligraphy, architecture and dress, and — the section's most striking claim — the emergence of a felt sense of individual selfhood as standardization's dialectical counterpart, exemplified by Montaigne's Essays, before closing on the routinized "brainwork" of compiling reference guides.

Themes

Standardization is a matter of degree, and its evidence is the new capacity to correct. She rejects the notion that early printed copies were all identical, but treats that qualification as secondary to standardization's real ramifications. Her sharpest evidentiary marker is the errata sheet: "Erasmus or Bellarmine could issue errata; Jerome or Alcuin could not" — a new capacity to locate an error with precision and transmit the correction simultaneously to scattered readers, illustrated darkly by the compositor's dropped "not" that produced the "wicked" Bible of 1631, a standardized error no scribe could have propagated at scale. She endorses Sarton's formula — printing "made it possible for the first time to publish hundreds of copies that were alike and yet might be scattered everywhere" — while insisting the operative standard is "sufficiently uniform" (enough for cross-regional scholarly correspondence), not perfect identity.

Standardization's reach extends far past text. Calendars, dictionaries, ephemerides, maps, charts, and musical or mathematical notation all became exactly, repeatably reproducible in a way hand-copying could not sustain; she suggests exact repeatability transformed the mathematical quadrivium more than the literary trivium. The same logic reached calligraphy (specimen books stripping scribal "hands" of idiosyncrasy), typography (replica-cast type, precisely fitted woodcuts), and eventually the whole narrative of Western art history, which she says collapses into "only a series of masks for two categories, the classical and the nonclassical" once standardized deviation from classical canon became visible and namable ("Gothic," "barocco").

Uniformity and diversity are "two sides of the same coin." Standardized maps, boundaries, and costume manuals did not just spread sameness — a Seville dress-pattern book making "Spanish" fashion visible throughout the Habsburg Empire — they also sharpened perception of difference, hardening regional and social types ("noble, burgher, peasant") vivid enough to survive "on dolls, in operas, or at costume balls." The more standardized the typical image of a town, head, or plant, the more clearly idiosyncratic departures could be perceived by observant draftsmen — a dialectic she applies equally to the Nuremberg Chronicle's reused woodcuts and to Reuwich's fresh engravings for Breydenbach's Peregrinatio.

Individualism is standardization's most consequential by-product. "The more standardized the type, indeed, the more compelling the sense of an idiosyncratic personal self" — a claim she grounds in Montaigne, whose Essays exposed a "solitary singular self" against the ideal types (princes, courtiers, merchants, husbandmen) that other printed books proliferated. Montaigne's informal essay also solved a genuinely new rhetorical problem: how to address readers who were no longer an assembled crowd but scattered, solitary individuals — intimacy rather than oratory — while reassuring each isolated reader that a felt singularity was "capable of being widely shared."

Standardized images did social and political work too. The "exactly repeatable pictorial statement" reinforced legibility of rank and place: repeated costume images hardened class and regional divisions, while a printed royal portrait — unlike a coin's crude stamp — let "an alert Frenchman... recognize and halt Louis XVI at Varennes," turning the sovereign's face into public, replicable information.

The compiling printer routinized a new kind of cognition. Master printers doubled as indexer-abridger-translator-lexicographer, extending scribal-era summulae into an "avalanche" of how-to literature with no scribal precedent. Compiling maps, tables, dictionaries, and grammars fostered what she calls an "esprit de système" — Ortelius likening his atlas to a "well furnished shoppe" where everything has its place — tying the printer's own editorial habits directly to early capitalist "efficient planning, methodical attention to detail, and rational calculation."

Notable passages

  • The errata / "wicked" Bible passage (c1) is her sharpest evidence that standardization is a real, new capacity rather than an illusion of scale.
  • The Sarton passage (c2) states the section's governing standard — "sufficiently uniform," not identical.
  • The standardization-to-individualism passage (c3) is the section's most striking claim, with Montaigne as its worked example.
  • The rhetorical-address passage (c4) extends the individualism argument into genre history: the informal essay as a technology for reaching a scattered readership.
  • The repeatable-image / Varennes passage (c5) shows standardization operating visually and politically, not only textually.
  • The esprit de système / Ortelius passage (c6) ties the printer's compiling routines to a new, systematized way of organizing knowledge.

For World Machines: standardization reads here almost exactly as modernity-machine's core operation of Legibility avant la lettre — identical maps, boundaries, calendars, and costume manuals rendering the world readable and orderable well before the state apparatuses WMP usually cites — though legibility-as-precondition-not-cause would caution that Eisenstein's own material shows this legibility infrastructure doing divergent work (hardening stereotypes and dynastic surveillance in one direction, producing Montaigne's private self in another) rather than one determinate effect. The individualism thesis pairs directly with print-culture-and-the-individual-author's claim that print "produced" solitary selfhood as a historical artifact rather than discovering it — Eisenstein supplies a concrete mechanism (michel-de-montaigne's Essays as the standardized type's dialectical negative) for a claim that note makes more generally. The costume-manual and boundary-map material, hardening regional and social stereotypes into durable "paper" identities, resonates with benedict-anderson's account of print manufacturing collective self-recognition, though Eisenstein's stereotypes here are social and regional rather than Anderson's specifically national imagined community.
