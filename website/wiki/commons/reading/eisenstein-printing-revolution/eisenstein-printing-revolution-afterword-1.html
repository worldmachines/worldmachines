Afterword: Revisiting the Printing Revolution — Answering the Historians of the Book

Commons · The Printing Revolution in Early Modern Europe · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Opening the 2005 Afterword, Eisenstein recounts how her thesis emerged against a historiography that had ignored printing entirely, then sorts fair from unfair criticism—defending the scale and speed of the fifteenth-century shift from scribal to print production against gradualist book historians while conceding that speech and script persisted alongside, rather than being superseded by, print.

Opening her 2005 Afterword, Eisenstein narrates the historiographical vacuum her book was written into—a graduate training in quasi-Marxist social history that had no place for a "communications revolution"—and then uses the now-established field of book history, which her own work helped call into being, as the vantage point from which to sort legitimate criticism (evolutionary continuity, the codex-to-screen longue durée, orality's persistence) from criticism aimed at positions she never held (a "McLuhanesque view," a dismissal of bibliographical scholarship). The bulk of the unit is a point-by-point defense of the "revolution" label against gradualist book historians, built from the scribe-to-printer transformation, the economics of the master-printer's shop, and the coexistence of print with manuscript and speech.

Themes

A once-invisible topic became visible only because a later communications technology revived interest in it. Eisenstein opens with a methodological aside as much as a memoir: "the writing of history... entails a dialogue between past and present," and the Internet's arrival is what gave her book "an unexpectedly long lease on life," even though the innovation on her mind when she wrote it was only the photocopier. Her graduate-school reading lists on medieval and early modern French history barely mentioned printing at all, and Henri Berr's planned volume on the invention of printing—meant to close the Middle Ages and open the modern world—"remained unwritten." This is offered as evidence that her original title, naming printing an "unacknowledged revolution," was accurate for the moment she wrote, even if, as she now admits, "the once-neglected topic is attracting so much attention that the title of my first chapter seems to be somewhat out of date."

She separates being misread from being wrong. Eisenstein is careful to distinguish objections she accepts from ones built on a misattribution. The sharpest example: she is charged with a "McLuhanesque view of history" and with dismissing bibliographical scholarship by saying "we need to think less abstractly, more historically and concretely"—but that comment, she clarifies, targeted marshall-mcluhan's "typographical man," not book history at all. The distinction matters for how the rest of the unit should be read: she wants credit for provoking a field while being held accountable only for the claims she actually made, not for the "exaggerated claims" of "media analysts" who get folded into her position by association.

Against the longue durée and the "book format" frame, she reasserts scale and speed via nonbooks and the scribe-to-printer metamorphosis. One line of gradualist criticism reframes print as a minor "blip" inside a scroll-to-codex-to-screen history; another (book history's own frame) stresses how little the book's physical format changed. Eisenstein's counters are concrete: nonbook mass production (indulgences, maps, proclamations, woodcuts) is exactly what a book-format-centered history misses, and the scribe who became a printer—Peter Schoeffer is her paradigm case, set against Vespasiano da Bisticci, the manuscript dealer who "closed shop"—"did not undergo a gradual change but experienced a veritable metamorphosis." She grants a continuity thesis built on Italian cartolai (manuscript bookdealers who supplied paper, ink, and capital to early printers) real force, but insists it applies only to "deluxe, hand-illuminated volumes" and does not erase what contemporaries themselves called a "stunning novelty," "almost literally overwhelming": turning out hundreds of copies at once rather than one at a time. She also corrects the record on two frequently miscited pieces of evidence—a Febvre and Martin passage about "hundreds of copies" that referred to one small manual order, and an overestimated "pecia system" of medieval university copying—both invoked to make scribal output look more print-like than it was.

The master printer's shop was a different kind of institution, not a faster scriptorium, and the evidence for that is economic. Eisenstein leans on entrepreneurial detail rather than rhetoric: Petrus Maufer's 1477 production of a 1,000-page Avicenna commentary (6,800,000 pieces of type, four presses, five months, labor strikes survived) reads, in her account, as structurally continuous with Robert Darnton's study of the 1778 Société Typographique de Neuchâtel wage book three hundred years later—"the master printers of 1477 were closer to the men of 1788 than they were to their contemporaries who provided hand-illuminated, hand-copied books to an elite clientele." This is her strongest single piece of evidence that the printer's shop, not the book's format, is where the metamorphosis shows up, and it resonates—loosely, since her claim is about a change of institution rather than a change of state capacity—with the modernity-machine's account of new infrastructures reorganizing production and labor around scale and repeatability.

Her actual project was never book history but the disruption of "Western Christendom," a debt she traces to McLuhan despite disagreeing with his method. Eisenstein states plainly that her first articles predate book history as a field and were never meant to contribute to it; her unit of study was the broader, "currently unfashionable" question of periodizing the Renaissance, the Reformation, and the discrediting of Ptolemaic/Galenic/Aristotelian authority. She credits marshall-mcluhan's 1962 Gutenberg Galaxy with alerting her to this dimension of change even though McLuhan "did not share my concern... for solid evidence, chronological order, or appropriate context"—a debt acknowledged and immediately hedged, exactly the move she asks her own critics to make with her.

Conceding gradualism in reading, she still insists repeated encounters with reorganized texts changed reading habits—a legibility-adjacent claim she keeps modest. She grants that silent reading predates print, that manuscripts and printed books were shelved and bound together undifferentiated by fifteenth-century readers, and that Silvia Rizzo is right that neo-Latin terminology drew no sharp line between them. But she rejects the inference that print therefore had no effect: "the reorganization of texts was not as obvious an innovation as the introduction of the codex... [but] repeated encounters with title pages, arabic numerals, running heads, alphabetical arrangements... probably affected the mental habits of readers." She concedes these elements were "derived from scribal practices" and puts her emphasis instead on frequency and repetition—a claim that rhymes with legibility-as-machine-core's account of standardized, navigable text as an infrastructure for readerly control, though her version is about incremental habituation in individual readers rather than a centralizing apparatus, and she explicitly declines the stronger claim that printing was necessary or sufficient on its own—an "agent," in her recurring word, whose effects depended on what else was in play, close to the qualified reading legibility-as-precondition-not-cause gives to infrastructure elsewhere in this wiki.

Increased output produced its own reading crisis, met by new note-taking and proofreading practices. Eisenstein links the eighteenth-century "lesen revolution" (intensive giving way to extensive reading) to an earlier, "just now beginning to be explored," early modern information overload: Ann Blair's image of scholars surrounded by disordered piles of books and manuals teaching readers how to take notes, keep commonplace books, and cut-and-paste. She adds a specialist case largely missing from that literature—the printing-house "corrector" or proofreader (Gabriel Harvey, who read for John Wolfe)—as evidence that print culture required its own new intensive reading skills, not only extensive skimming. This is a direct historical instance of the pattern named in printing-press-and-information-overload and information-overload-across-eras: a flood of new material outrunning existing filters, followed by invented tools (bibliographies, note-taking manuals, the corrector's trained eye) to re-establish orientation.

She defends a narrower, specific claim against critics who charge her with ignoring orality altogether. Donald McKenzie, Harold Love, and others argue for a "communicative spectrum" in which speech, script, and print are inseparably mixed, and fault "monistic models (such as mine) that artificially isolate print." Eisenstein's reply concedes the substance—reading aloud to "hearing publics... was, indeed, facilitated by the new abundance of texts," psalters and ballads spread orally-inflected repertoires, and both Protestants and Catholics valued the living preacher's presence over "the inanimate word in print"—while holding her line at a narrower point: she is not describing the whole communicative spectrum, only how printing changed it, and she maintains that "the scripts and books of the seventeenth century" differ from "those of the fourteenth" in ways that a spectrum model risks flattening.

Notable passages

The "unacknowledged... no longer" passage marks the pivot the whole unit turns on: the field she once had to justify founding has now overtaken her own title.

The McLuhan-misattribution passage is the unit's clearest boundary-drawing move, separating a real intellectual debt (Gutenberg Galaxy) from a mislabeling she rejects (the "McLuhanesque" charge, the misapplied bibliography quotation).

The "stunning novelty" passage is her strongest concession-and-rebuttal in one move: gradualists are right about the deluxe hybrid trade, wrong to let it stand for the whole shift from producing books singly to producing them by the hundred.

The Maufer/Darnton comparison is the unit's best piece of hard evidence for an economic, institutional metamorphosis: two master printers three hundred years apart share more with each other than either shares with hand-copy producers of their own era.

The "reorganization of texts" passage states, in her own qualified terms, the closest thing in this unit to a legibility claim: repetition of title pages, numerals, and running heads reshaping readers' habits without her claiming print invented any of these elements.

The closing orality passage shows Eisenstein's characteristic move against her critics: concede the phenomenon, narrow the claim, and hold that a "communicative spectrum" account risks erasing a real historical difference between manuscript-era and print-era "scripts and books."
