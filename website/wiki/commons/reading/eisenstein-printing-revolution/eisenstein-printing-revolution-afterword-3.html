Answering the Critics: Supersession, Standardization, and the Printing Press as Agent of Change

Commons · The Printing Revolution in Early Modern Europe · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
In the closing pages of her 2005 Afterword, Eisenstein answers critics (McKitterick, Duguid and Nunberg, Adrian Johns, Michael Warner) on manuscript persistence, the relative-not-absolute character of print standardization, and the charge of technological determinism, then ends the book by casting the printing revolution as a multigenerational “business of time” beyond any single agent’s control.

In this closing stretch of the 2005 Afterword, Eisenstein trades narrative for direct rebuttal, working through the reviewers who challenged the first edition point by point — David McKitterick on “no sudden break,” Paul Duguid and Geoffrey Nunberg on the fallacy of “supersession,” Adrian Johns on standardization and the reliability of print, and Michael Warner on technological determinism — before closing the entire book with a short meditation on the printing revolution as a “business of time” too large for any single human agent to have willed.

Themes

Manuscript persistence does not refute the transition. She concedes at length — citing Margaret Ezell, Harold Love, Julia Crick, and the antiquary Robert Cotton — that hand copying survived deep into the age of print, sometimes for clandestine reasons (she compares it to Russian samizdat) and sometimes because coterie authors, especially women writers and poets, used manuscript circulation to evade the “stigma of print.” But she insists this coexistence is compatible with, not a refutation of, her thesis: manuscript production “assumed more ‘specialized functions’ and never again resumed its central role.” Against McKitterick’s claim that speaking of a transition from manuscript to print is “misleading,” she holds that the shift was real and, crucially, “all in one direction” — toward accumulation and abundance rather than the scarcity and repeated loss of the scribal economy. She likewise rejects the Duguid/Nunberg-flagged doctrines of “supersession”: print did not replace manuscript any more than engraving replaced drawing, but it did “arrest and then reverse the process of loss, corruption, and erosion” that hand copying had always entailed.

Standardization is relative, not absolute — and that hedge is the whole argument. Answering Adrian Johns’s evidence-heavy case for the multiformity of early printed output (misprints, piracy, “no two copies identical” First Folios), Eisenstein does not deny the multiformity — she argues Johns is refuting a claim she never made. Early printed products were never as standardized as later mechanically or photographically reproduced ones, but they were still “more standardized than were hand-copied ones,” and that comparative, relative gain is what her book actually claims. This is a clean instance of legibility-as-precondition-not-cause: standardization here functions as an enabling condition for later, stronger legibility regimes rather than as a totalizing, already-achieved order, and the dispute is structurally the same shape as the contested-uniformity debates elsewhere in the corpus (cf. contested-uniformity-bayly), where a historian’s claim of partial, directional uniformity gets read by a critic as a claim of total uniformity and knocked down on that straw-man basis. Evidence she marshals for the real, if partial, gain: errata slips, the fixing of single errors across a whole print run (the “Wicked Bible,” the “britches” Bible), and readers like Pierre Bayle who could assert “with absolute confidence” that a printed date was wrong — confidence that presupposes trust in the print record, not endemic distrust of it.

The “textus receptus” is the mechanism of cumulative scholarship. Independent of accuracy, the first printed edition of a classical text — however “riddled with errors” — did something no manuscript copy could: it froze one version of the text as a shared reference point, a “textus receptus,” so that generations of scholars (her example is the sequence of defective Pliny editions and the Perotti controversy) could argue about the same passages. The duplication of error, far from being purely a defect, made errors visible to many eyes and thus correctable — feedback from a distributed readership improving successive editions, precisely the fixity-enables-cumulative-change dynamic the World Machines model associates with legibility technologies, though hers is a slower, scholarly-quarrel-driven version of it rather than a bureaucratic one.

“Agent of change,” not sole cause — the fight with Warner over determinism. Michael Warner’s charge that calling the press an “agent of change” wrongly privileges technology over culture draws her most careful hedge: she agrees printing and culture are “fused,” not discrete entities, but insists that “in this restricted sense” — the specific manual and cognitive skills presswork demanded, distinct from scribal skills — “the character of the tool(s) determined the character of the new labor force.” She also rebuts Warner’s claim that the press’s transformative reputation is a later, retrospective construct, pointing to contemporary colophons and sixteenth-century celebration of print as a “divine art.” This is exactly the hedge worth carrying forward when treating print as infrastructure of the modernity-machine’s Dawn: Eisenstein is willing to say the tool shaped the labor force “in a restricted sense,” never that it single-handedly authored the outcomes built on top of it.

The master printer’s shop as the real protagonist. Against critics who fault her for crediting printers and authors too much or too little, she reaffirms her deliberate focus on exceptional master-printer/author collaborations (Vesalius and Oporinus, Erasmus and Froben, Conrad Gesner and Christopher Froschauer) as the institution that let learned authors achieve what had been “out of reach” for even the most gifted scribal-age professionals — while granting that the ordinary, “cramped, dirty, noisy” print shop deserves more attention than her selective account gives it.

Information overload is old, and print made it structural. Almost as a coda and a self-critique (“Perhaps it should have been given more emphasis in my book”), she notes that the continued output of hand-copied books alongside a rising tide of printed ones only compounded an “unprecedented information overload” that early modern scholars already felt — a “diachronic” side effect of print’s preservative capacity that critics tend to overlook. This is squarely the terrain of printing-press-and-information-overload and information-overload-across-eras: abundance and preservation, not just distribution, driving the overload.

Closing note: the printing revolution as a “business of time.” The book’s very last passage, quoting Bishop Sprat on the new astronomy needing to be “not only an enterprise of one season… but a business of time,” makes an explicit claim about scale: no human being and no single generation, however gifted, could carry so vast a collaborative enterprise to completion. That is a genuine, if partial, rhyme with history-machine — a process that assembles across independent contributors and generations without a single controlling author, visible only in aggregate — though Eisenstein’s frame is a cumulative scholarly/printing enterprise rather than a silent paradigm-assembly, and she treats it as a subject for the discipline of history rather than a machine-construction dynamic as such.

Notable passages

The rebuttal to Johns on standardization (c2) is the load-bearing methodological move of the excerpt: she is not disputing his evidence, she is disputing what that evidence is evidence for. The textus receptus passage (c1) supplies the mechanism — fixity plus distributed correction — that lets “riddled with errors” first editions still function as engines of cumulative scholarship. The Warner exchange (c3) is where she is most explicit about resisting strict technological determinism while still defending “agent of change” as a description with real, if bounded, force. The master-printer passage (c5) names the institution and cast of characters (Vesalius/Oporinus, Erasmus/Froben) she considers the true protagonists of the book. The information-overload aside (c6) is a rare moment of self-critique, flagging a theme she now thinks her original book underweighted. And the closing passage (c4), ending the entire book on “Bon voyage!,” reframes the printing revolution one last time as a transgenerational “business of time” no individual could have authored — the final statement of her insistence, throughout the Afterword, that print is an agent embedded in collaborative, cumulative processes, not a prime mover acting alone.
