The New Process of Data Collection: From the Corrupted Copy to the Improved Edition

Commons · The Printing Revolution in Early Modern Europe · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Print's succession of editions converts scribal culture's drift toward corruption into a cumulative feedback loop — printers, editors, and networks of correspondents (Estienne's Bibles, Ortelius's Theatrum, Mattioli's Dioscorides, Gesner's bibliography) collect and credit reader-supplied corrections and new data across successive editions, producing an open-ended “knowledge explosion” that Eisenstein attributes to typographical fixity rather than any new scholarly virtue.

Eisenstein argues that print did more than freeze texts in place: by letting printers, editors, and their correspondents feed corrections and new data back into successive editions of reference works, it converted the “immemorial drift” of scribal corruption into a cumulative, self-improving process — turning atlases, herbals, Bible editions, and bibliographies into open-ended collective projects rather than isolated artifacts.

Themes

Reversal, not merely arrest, of scribal drift. Robert Estienne's Bibles and Ortelius's atlases show printers “were also able to improve on themselves” across editions — a structural possibility scribal copying never had, since each manuscript copy could only degrade relative to its exemplar.

Print could still corrupt data — but it made corruption visible and thus correctable. Eisenstein does not romanticize the press: she cites early Bible woodcuts and a sequence of herbals (1480s–1526) that grew steadily more distorted, “a remarkably sad example of what happens to visual information as it passed from copyist to copyist.” Her real claim is more precise than “print improves accuracy”: under “ignorant printers driving to make quick profits,” data still got garbled faster than before; only “under the guidance of technically proficient masters” did the same technology let “fresh observations… be duplicated without being blurred or blotted out.” Print is an agent that cuts both ways, and the direction depended on who controlled the press.

Feedback networks made publication a cooperative, self-correcting enterprise. Ortelius solicited criticism and new maps for the Theatrum from cartographers who “stumbled over themselves to send him their latest maps,” expanding it across at least 28 editions (by 1598, continuing to 1612) and crediting contributors by name. Mattioli's commentaries on Dioscorides absorbed correspondents' specimens edition after edition — this is literally how the horse chestnut, lilac, and tulip entered European botanical gardens. Eisenstein treats naming and crediting contributors as a technique enabled by print's capacity for wide, identical, repeated distribution, not a new moral achievement.

An open-ended knowledge explosion, unlike the finite libraries of the past. Because “the sequence of improved editions and ever-expanding reference works was a sequence without limits,” it could not be lost the way the Alexandrian Library or Matthias Corvinus's collection were destroyed — a point she draws from Conrad Gesner's own dedication to his Bibliotheca universalis (1545). She reaches for the word “feedback” — “ugly and much overused” but doing real work — to name what changed structurally between scribal and print-based data collection.

No moral triumphalism: mixed motives, not new virtue, explain the shift. Eisenstein explicitly refuses the reading that honest citation reflects printers or scholars becoming more virtuous than “greedy adventurers” or self-serving schoolmen. Crediting sources became more “satisfying to mixed motives after printing,” since publicity, fame, priority disputes, and (for artisan-authors advertising their workshops) plain commerce were all served by the same act of disclosure. She keeps the incentive structure of the new medium distinct from any claim about human character improving.

Typographical fixity as the deeper transformation behind the “scientific revolution” narratives. Closing the unit, she takes on Thomas Kuhn's claim that “no potentially revolutionary changes occurred in the data available to astronomers” until decades after Copernicus's death — countering that “the data available” to all book readers was being transformed across exactly those decades, via cartography, chronology, law, and bibliography alike, each domain now able to build on the work of predecessors “instead of trying to retrieve scattered fragments of it.” Her summary claim: “Typographical fixity is a basic prerequisite for the rapid advancement of learning.”

Notable passages

The Estienne/Ortelius opening states the core reversal thesis in miniature (c1). The herbal-degradation passage (c2) is the unit's key hedge, showing corruption could accelerate under print and specifying the actual mechanism (skilled oversight, not the technology alone) that turned that risk into gain. The Ortelius sequence (c3) is her paradigm case of a feedback-driven “cooperative enterprise,” down to the count of editions and their vernaculars. The Gesner/feedback passage (c4) supplies the vocabulary — “feedback,” “open-ended,” “without limits” — that names the pattern across fields. The mixed-motives passage (c5) shows Eisenstein at her most characteristic: resisting a moralized history of print in favor of an incentive-structure account. The closing Kuhn exchange (c6) is where her disagreement with another historian is sharpest, and where the unit's argument cashes out in her broader thesis about fixity.

For World Machines: this is the “data collection” feature of print culture — feedback from readers converting a corrupted copy into an improved edition — that functions as infrastructure for the modernity-machine's Dawn. The bibliographies, atlases, and reference works described here are also legibility-as-machine-core technology (standardized, cross-referenced, cumulative), though Eisenstein's own hedge about “ignorant printers” versus “technically proficient masters” is worth carrying forward: the infrastructure's effect depends on who runs it, which rhymes with legibility-as-precondition-not-cause's argument that legibility infrastructure's effects are set by what is coupled to it rather than guaranteed by the infrastructure alone — though that synthesis is about later fiscal/bureaucratic installations, not print. The knowledge “amassed” to the point of needing “new surveys” (c4) is a small-scale instance of the pattern named in printing-press-and-information-overload: overload met by a new organizing technique, not just raw accumulation.
