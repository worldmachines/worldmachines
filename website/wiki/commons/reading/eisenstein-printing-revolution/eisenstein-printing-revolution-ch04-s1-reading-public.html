The Rise of the Reading Public: Divergent Effects of a Silent Revolution

Commons · The Printing Revolution in Early Modern Europe · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Eisenstein corrects McLuhan's abstract 'typographical man' by tracing how the shift toward silent, solitary reading affected different social groups and even the same reading public unevenly, deepening a reading/hearing divide, dispersing communities into an atomistic reading public even as it forged new translocal loyalties, and handing centralizing states a duplicative tool for standardizing rule.

Opening the chapter on the "expanding Republic of Letters," Eisenstein takes issue with Marshall McLuhan's "typographical man" — not to reject his central insight that print reshaped psychology and society, but to insist its effects were plural, uneven, and historically specific rather than a single uniform transformation. Drawing on Paul Saenger's finding that silent reading predates Gutenberg, she reframes print's role as intensifying and institutionalizing an already-emerging practice rather than inventing it, then traces how the shift toward silent, solitary reading affected different social groups — and even different corners of the same reading public — in contradictory ways: deepening the divide between a lingering hearing public and an emerging reading one, unsettling the relationship between pulpit and press, dispersing communities even as it forged new impersonal, translocal loyalties, and equipping centralizing states with a duplicative tool for extending and standardizing rule.

Themes

Correcting McLuhan without discarding him. Eisenstein credits McLuhan for making "us more alert to the possibility that the advent of printing had social and psychological consequences," but faults him for "glossing over multiple interactions that occurred under widely varying circumstances," trading his metaphorical abstraction for her own historically concrete, group-by-group method. She uses Paul Saenger's demonstration that "habits of silent reading developed during the Middle Ages" to correct McLuhan's (and his sources') overstatement of medieval orality: printing did not introduce silent reading, but it did make "silent instructors" — books read alone — increasingly pervasive and "elaborately institutionalized." marshall-mcluhan is named directly; this is Eisenstein's recurring move of using and correcting him rather than simply following him.

Reading did not simply displace hearing. Classroom lectures, sermons, and orations persisted and were even amplified — "priests and orators both benefited from the way their personal charisma could be augmented and amplified by the printed word." Printed publicity let evangelists and demagogues "practice traditional arts outdoors before an expanded hearing public." The relationship between spoken and printed word was reciprocal and transformative, not simply substitutive.

A durable reading/hearing divide, itself reshaped by print. Most rural villagers "belonged to an exclusively hearing public at least until the nineteenth century," yet what they heard had "been transformed by printing two centuries earlier": the oral storyteller was replaced by "the exceptional literate villager who read out loud from a stack of cheap books and ballad sheets," producing a "fairly sleazy 'popular' culture" of mass-produced vernacular romances well before nineteenth-century mass literacy. Reading and hearing publics coexisted, separated by "a psychological gulf," each transformed on its own terms.

One innovation, opposite effects. Members of the same reading public, confronting the same innovation in the same place and time, were affected in "markedly different ways": Bible printing launched trends toward both modernism and fundamentalism; the identical silent, solitary posture of devotional reading also served "scandal sheets," "lewd Ballads," and "corrupted tales in Inke and Paper." This underwrites her secularization narrative — the periodical press ultimately displacing the pulpit, "nothing sacred" becoming the journalist's creed — without collapsing it into a single causal story.

Dispersal, individualism, and new translocal loyalties. A reading public was "more atomistic and individualistic than a hearing one": Malesherbes could say in 1775 that "men of letters are in the midst of a dispersed people," and Walter Ong's thought experiment (an audience fragmenting into solitary silent readers, then needing to be "reassembled into a collectivity") captures how duplicated, identical messages substituted an "impersonal link between people who were unknown to each other" for face-to-face assembly. Yet the same dispersal cut both ways: "even while local ties were loosened, links to larger collective units were being forged" — mass-circulated ruler portraits let "a reigning dynast to impress a personal presence on mass consciousness," seeding the "nationwide extension" of a leader's charismatic appeal. This is close to benedict-anderson's account of print-forged imagined communities and resonates with nationalism, though Eisenstein's frame here is social-psychological rather than Anderson's specifically national-print-capitalism argument. The atomized, silently-reading interior self she describes also anticipates what infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness names, though she is documenting the social mechanics of solitary reading rather than theorizing bourgeois subjectivity as such.

Print as an engine of state standardization. Rulers exploited the new medium directly — Henry VIII and Cromwell using "both Parliament and press to win public support for the Reformation," Louis XIV waging a Klaits-documented "psychological war" through pamphlets, Richelieu founding the Gazette as the Estates General went unconvened. But the "duplicative powers of print" cut toward both crown and opposition: government regulation extended bureaucratic reach even as "a greater uniformity began to characterize provincial demands, with the circulation of model petitions and lists of grievances." This is Legibility machinery in a strict sense — duplication standardizing both rule and resistance — though Eisenstein, per her usual hedge, treats print as an agent enabling this uniformity rather than its sole cause, close to the caveat in legibility-as-precondition-not-cause. The pattern of uniform demands answered in kind also rhymes loosely with the contested, uneven uniformity traced in contested-uniformity-bayly, though that account concerns a later, global setting.

Notable passages

The opening critique of McLuhan (c1) states Eisenstein's method for the whole chapter: praise the insight, reject the abstraction, replace it with group-specific history. The Saenger paragraph (c2) is her precise statement of print's relationship to silent reading — institutionalization, not invention. The rural-villager passage (c3) shows the hearing public itself being transformed by print at one remove, through the literate villager and cheap ballad sheets. The Bible-printing passage (c4) is her clearest evidence that identical innovations produce divergent, even opposite, effects within a single public. The Malesherbes/Ong passage (c5) supplies the argument that a reading public is structurally more dispersed and individualistic than a hearing one — the passage that most directly anticipates imagined-community and inwardness readings of print. The Richelieu/Cromwell passage (c6) shows print's duplicative power serving both centralizing rulers and the provincial assemblies that opposed them.
