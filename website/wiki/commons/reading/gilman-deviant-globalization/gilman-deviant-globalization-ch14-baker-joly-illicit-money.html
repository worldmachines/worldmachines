Illicit Money and the Offshore System: Sizing, Mechanisms and Containment

Commons · Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Baker and Joly argue that illicit financial flows of roughly $1 trillion to $1.6 trillion a year, mostly moved through falsified trade pricing and sheltered by tax havens, drain developing countries, fuel crime and terrorism, and can be contained (not eliminated) by a few transparency measures.

Chapter by Raymond Baker and Eva Joly (first published in The New York Review of Books, 2009), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).

Baker and Joly open with the Obama administration's May 2009 crackdown on offshore tax havens and argue that it misses the larger problem, money from criminal and other illicit sources moving through a "shadow" financial network. They size the flows with World Bank and other estimates, show that most of the money moves through falsified trade invoices rather than through drug cartels or corrupt rulers, trace the offshore system to decolonization and the rise of multinationals in the 1960s, set out its costs for development and security, and close with a short list of policy steps. Their stance is explicitly one of containment: "curtail this activity, not to try to stop it, which would be impossible." The authors cite estimates (the World Bank, Friedrich Schneider, Global Financial Integrity, the WTO) and note that the figures are hard to obtain and only loosely reliable, though they say the estimates fall within a "surprisingly narrow range."

Themes

Scale and composition of illicit flows. The authors cite the World Bank for $1 trillion to $1.6 trillion a year, about half of it out of developing countries. Their central analytic claim is that the popular image is wrong: organized crime is only about a third of the total and theft by corrupt officials about 3 percent, while international trade is the vehicle for some 60 to 65 percent. They suggest up to $2 trillion of the roughly $40 trillion in annual trade may be illicit money. These are the authors' reported estimates, not verified here.

Mispricing as the master mechanism. Baker and Joly describe two forms of false invoicing (rewriting at a tax-haven office; or a verbally agreed false price sent directly) and illustrate with a photocopier example of transfer pricing within a multinational. They cite Pak and Zdanowicz for systematic under pricing of US exports and offer country cases: Russia ($200 to $500 billion since the early 1990s), China, Nigeria, and Venezuela's state oil company. The same mechanism serves tax avoidance by firms and flight of stolen capital by elites, so the legal and the illegal blur.

The offshore system as designed infrastructure. The authors date the takeoff to the 1960s: forty-eight countries gained independence and some new leaders (Mobutu, Marcos, Suharto) sought foreign accounts, which Western bankers served, while corporations became multinational and "tax planning" became normal. Havens, with secrecy laws, trustees and disguised corporations, now control an estimated $6 trillion and specialize by market niche (Jersey for London, Panama for the US, Cyprus for Russian money, the British Virgin Islands for Chinese capital). They note that much haven activity is legal. This resonates with the volume's thesis that deviant and mainstream finance share the same infrastructure; the authors, however, frame it as a harm to be curbed, not as a development route.

Development and distributional costs. Against $50 to $80 billion a year in aid, they set $500 to $800 billion leaving poor countries, so that "for every $1 handed out across the top of the table, the West has been receiving back up to $10 under the table." They add that the system shifts tax burdens from large businesses to ordinary workers and widens inequality, and ask how Western states can promote the rule of law while tolerating it.

Security consequences. Cartels, racketeers, arms dealers, human traffickers and militant groups all use the same offshore mechanisms. They argue the Financial Action Task Force (1989) has not curtailed drug supply, cite estimates of al Qaeda funding and alleged Iranian Revolutionary Guard smuggling, and claim Saddam's illicit finance helped create the impression that he held weapons of mass destruction. That last claim is the authors' own contention and is stated with strong causal confidence.

Containment, not elimination. Proposals include automatic tax-information sharing (the EU Savings Tax Directive), country-by-country corporate profit reporting, knowing beneficial owners, the Patriot Act's shell-bank ban, Grassley's and Levin's US bills, two-signature commercial invoices, letters of credit, and a World Bank focus on cutting flight capital. They note that the shadow system "will now try to circumvent the new law" and that the first two G-20 meetings left it in place.

Notable passages

The sizing paragraph (c1) frames the problem and its uncertainty. The composition passage (c2) is the chapter's counterintuitive claim that trade, not crime or kleptocracy, is the main channel. The mispricing passage (c3) gives the mechanism and a worked transfer-pricing example. The 1960s origin story (c4) ties illicit finance to decolonization and multinationals. The aid-versus-flight comparison (c5) states the development argument. The closing World Bank paragraph (c6) gives the 25 percent benchmark. The authors also cite Treasury estimates that 99.9 percent of money US law bars from entering is accepted on first deposit, a statistic they report without further detail.

Resonances

The offshore system is a case of strategic illegibility to state eyes: secrecy jurisdictions, nominees and false invoices exist to defeat state accounting, and the remedies (beneficial-ownership registers, country-by-country reporting, automatic information exchange) are legibility projects, cf. Legibility and legibility-cuts-both-ways. That much of the opacity serves wealthy firms and elites echoes illegibility-as-elite-defense. Baker and Joly's picture of a financial network that states can partly police by controlling chokepoints such as correspondent banks parallels weaponized-interdependence, though they are policy advocates, not theorists, and make no such claim.
