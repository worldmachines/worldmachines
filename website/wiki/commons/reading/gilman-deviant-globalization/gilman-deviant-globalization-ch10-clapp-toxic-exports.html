Toxic Exports: Leaks in the Basel Regime and the Persistence of the Hazardous Waste Trade

Commons · Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Clapp argues that the Basel Convention and its regional and national companions curbed direct waste-for-disposal exports to poor countries but left cracks (a recycling loophole, weak enforcement, non-party states, e-waste and ship-breaking, and relocation of waste-generating industries) through which the toxic waste trade continues.

Chapter by Jennifer Clapp (first published in SAIS Review 22:1, 2002, as "Seeping Through the Cracks"), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).

Clapp opens with the 2006 dumping of toxic oil sludge around Abidjan from the Trafigura, a ship chartered by a Swiss-based trading firm after, she reports, disposal in Amsterdam was quoted at over US$300,000. She uses it to illustrate a global waste business: about 440 million tons of hazardous waste are generated worldwide, an estimated 10 percent crossing borders. She then traces how, before the late 1980s, waste flowed on regulatory differences between countries, and how developing-country outrage produced the 1989 Basel Convention (in force 1992), regional bans (Bamako, Waigani, Izmir, an EU regulation) and national laws. Her main argument is that these rules cut disposal exports but shifted the trade into legal or semi-legal channels: recycling, reuse, ship scrapping, e-waste and foreign direct investment. She closes with three policy prescriptions: universal commitment to Basel and the Ban Amendment, reduced waste generation through regulation rather than voluntary measures, and binding rules on multinational investment in toxic industries.

Themes

Regulatory arbitrage as the engine. Clapp states that wastes were shipped from countries with high disposal costs and strict regulations to ones with low costs and weak regulations, and that the trade persists as long as disposal rules differ among countries. This is a close fit with the editors' idea of profiting from legal differences, though Clapp frames it as an environmental-regulation problem, not as deviant globalization.

Regulation displaces rather than eliminates the trade. After the early-1990s rules, Clapp argues, exporters moved from disposal to recycling, a loophole that allowed transfer to continue legally. Wastes labelled recycling or reuse are often unrecyclable or leave hazardous by-products. The same pattern recurs with ships, which are technically in use when exported and often not declared waste until they arrive, and with e-waste exported as reusable though much is broken.

Weak treaty architecture. Clapp points to the Ban Amendment (adopted 1995) stalling because the ratification threshold was miscounted (three-fourths of parties at adoption, still 18 short as of March 2008), to non-party states such as the United States, to the JUSCANZ group opposing the Amendment, and to violations by parties (a Japanese shipment to the Philippines, India's continued imports). Some cases, such as Abidjan, involved parties to Basel and Bamako, and she judges the nighttime, deceptive operation virtually impossible to prevent.

Moving the factory instead of the waste. Clapp argues that waste can also move via foreign direct investment: Japanese industries relocating in the 1970s, the US-Mexico maquiladora zone, chemical MNCs shifting to Asia (Bayer acknowledging European regulation as a factor), and double standards as at Bhopal. She hedges that the extent of pollution havens is debated, saying some argue they are more elusive in practice than in theory, while holding that for toxic waste there appears to be a clear movement.

Policy conclusion. She calls for full adoption and implementation of Basel and the Ban Amendment, tighter regulation (noting that government regulation is the primary motivator for firms, per a survey she cites, and that voluntary measures are less stringent), and a binding global agreement on transnational firms' environmental practices, with a first step of requiring home-country standards abroad plus public disclosure.

Notable passages

The recycling loophole. Clapp's central mechanism: rules curbing waste for disposal pushed exporters toward waste sent for recycling, with examples such as UK spent mercury sent to South Africa and lead-acid batteries, plastic and scrap metal in Southeast Asia and Latin America.

The Ban Amendment arithmetic. The ratification threshold was presumed to be 62 but turned out to depend on three-fourths of the parties at adoption, leaving the Ban unenforced; a vivid case of institutional fragility.

Enforcement and non-parties. Clapp discusses ships and the Ivory Coast case: the dumping was carried out in deception and covered by none of the formal prohibitions in practice.

E-waste and toxic ships. Declaring goods as reuse, or not declaring ships as waste until arrival, exploits the legal category boundary. Clapp notes that customs officials lack knowledge of second-hand electronics.

Maquiladoras. Under the La Paz agreement waste was supposed to return to the US, yet in the early 1990s less than three percent of producing firms did so; after NAFTA the figure rose to 25-30 percent, while enforcement of NAFTA's anti-pollution-haven provision remained difficult.

Closing the investment channel. Clapp's final prescription on binding rules for FDI in toxic industries and the claim that remaining cracks allow the trade to thrive.

Fit with World Machines

There is little direct resonance with machine theory; Clapp is a policy analyst writing about treaty design. The weak echo is that a regime of formal rules is routed around through category definitions (waste versus reuse versus raw material), which loosely rhymes with the Legibility theme of formal categories failing to capture practice, though Clapp does not argue this herself and the fit is partial. Within the volume's frame, the chapter is an example of legal trade in the same infrastructure that carries ordinary goods, since containers and ships carry waste labelled as something else.
