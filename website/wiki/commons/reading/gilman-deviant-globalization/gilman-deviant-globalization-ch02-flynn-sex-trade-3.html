The Great Sex Migration (1): Zina and Veronica, and Moldova

Commons · Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Flynn opens Part 2 of his GQ sex-trade reportage with two Moldovan sisters lured, indebted and coerced into prostitution in Russia, then argues that trafficking's scale is unknowable, and uses Moldova's post-Soviet collapse to explain why women there are such easy prey.

Chapter by Sean Flynn (first published in GQ, Aug. & Sept. 2005, "The Sex Trade", Parts 1-2), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).

Flynn opens Part 2 with a narrative of two impoverished Moldovan sisters, Zina and Veronica, from the village of Costesti. A woman from their past recruits them with promises of market or window-washing work in Krasnodar, Russia, and "Gypsy" associates of hers supply papers and train fare. On arrival they are told the jobs do not exist, that they owe a debt, and that they will be prostitutes; beatings and death threats end their refusal and they are sold to a strip club. Flynn then pulls back to the problem of measurement: official estimates of trafficking are, by the admission of a State Department ambassador, an "intelligent guesstimate". He narrows the lens to Moldova, then to Costesti, using IOM counts, a local aid worker and a senior anti-trafficking policeman, Ion Bejan, and closes the unit with Bejan's account of how the Soviet collapse left Moldova the poorest country in Europe.

Themes

Trafficking as deception plus debt plus violence. Flynn presents the sisters' case as coercion by "deception, threats, and violence" rather than by "circumstance or poverty", yet the recruitment works only because of poverty: the recruiter returns repeatedly for two weeks until the sisters judge that going is the way to feed their mother. Remittances from villagers working in Lisbon, Moscow, London or Dubai make migration look normal and successful, so the lure borrows credibility from legitimate labour migration. This is Flynn's reporting, not a theoretical claim, but it fits the editors' point that deviant and mainstream migration flows share channels.

The number problem. Flynn lists competing figures (the US State Department: 600,000-800,000 people trafficked across borders yearly, 80 percent women and girls; UNICEF: 700,000 to 2 million; ILO: $27.8 billion yearly profits from sex trafficking; FBI: $9.5 billion for the transnational trade). He calls them "impossible to verify", quotes Ambassador John R. Miller calling any estimate an "intelligent guesstimate", and says the figures serve journalists, policymakers and grant dispensers who tolerate ambiguity poorly. A hedge worth carrying: Flynn states that trafficking victims are "surely a minority" among the tens of millions in the global sex trade.

Invisibility of victims. Flynn argues victims cannot be counted because they are indistinguishable from other sex workers and have reasons for silence: fear of the trafficker, distrust of corrupt police, no money or passport, and shame that turns captivity into apparent complicity. This is an informal sociology of an illegible population; it resonates with the project's Legibility theme from the opposite direction, since here it is the state's failure to see that sustains the trade, though Flynn does not frame it that way.

Zooming from global to village. He narrows from global estimates to Moldova (IOM assisted 1,571 trafficked Moldovan women from 2000 to May 2005, which IOM's Martin Wyss calls "only a fraction") to Costesti, where the Compasiune agency has taken in fifty-three women and girls since 2000. A Western diplomat's advice to multiply known counts by five or ten is informant opinion, not data.

State collapse as the supply side. Through Bejan, Flynn gives a causal story: Soviet breakup, asset stripping by former apparatchiks, a breakaway Transnistria, mass unemployment (about 6,000 jobless in Costesti), unfarmable privatized land, and impoverishment. Flynn leaves the link to trafficking implicit at this point in the text. This echoes the editors' picture of deviant globalization flourishing where state-led development collapses, though the chapter is journalism and does not make that argument itself. It also loosely rhymes with the late modernity-machine losing its hold, with the caveat that Flynn is describing one post-Soviet state's transition, not a world machine.

Notable passages

Recruitment narrative. The recruiter's two-week persistence and the sisters' reasoning (who will care for Mama, and how can they if they have no money) show how coercion is preceded by apparently voluntary consent. The subsequent debt claim and beatings are summarised here plainly rather than quoted.

The competing estimates. Flynn gives the State Department, UNICEF, ILO and FBI figures together, which makes the divergence visible.

The honesty about estimates. Miller's "guesstimate" quote and Flynn's remark that the honest figure would be "a lot" are a rare candid statement of the evidence limits.

Invisible victims. The paragraph on why a trafficked woman will not tell clients or police is the chapter's clearest analytical statement on why the trade is hard to measure and police.

Moldova's transition. Bejan's recollection, as reported by Flynn, of kleptocratic privatization, Transnistria and the leu photocopies gives the structural background.

(The unit also reports a series of harrowing victim accounts relayed by Bejan; these are not curated.)
