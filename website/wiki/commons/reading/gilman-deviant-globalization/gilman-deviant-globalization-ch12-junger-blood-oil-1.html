Blood Oil (part 1): MEND, the Niger delta, and the politics of oil poverty

Commons · Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Junger reports from Nigeria's Niger delta on how MEND's small-boat militancy threatens global oil supply and on the corruption and resource-curse poverty that feed it, framing it with the Oil ShockWave simulation.

Chapter by Sebastian Junger (first published in Vanity Fair, Feb. 2007), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).

Junger opens with the June 2005 Oil ShockWave simulation, in which unrest in Nigeria combines with a cold snap to push oil past $80 a barrel and threaten the US economy and military. He then reports that from January 2006 the Movement for the Emancipation of the Niger Delta (MEND) acted out something close to that scenario: kidnappings, pipeline bombings, an offshore rig raid and urban attacks that cut Nigerian output. The middle of the unit is first-person reportage of a tense meeting with MEND fighters in an Ijaw village, mediated by the online spokesman Jomo Gbomo. The final section, "Poverty and Corruption", argues that the militancy grows out of a state and an oil economy that have stolen or wasted most of the wealth and left the delta without basic services.

Themes

Cheap asymmetric leverage over a global chokepoint. Junger argues that scale defeats the Nigerian state: Shell's thousands of miles of pipeline and dozens of fields cannot all be guarded, and even deepwater rigs are not safe. He writes that "MEND had shown that 20 guys in speedboats could affect oil prices around the world." The Oil ShockWave scenario shows how thin the global supply cushion is. This is the shadow side of global infrastructure, seen from the insurgent side; it loosely parallels the weaponized-interdependence reading of networks, though Junger is a journalist and does not theorize it.

A low-tech, networked insurgency. MEND runs its campaign on a weak cell signal and $3 phones, with a pseudonymous email spokesman whose identity is unknown. Junger also reports ritual authority (the war god Egbesu, chalk paint, amulets) as part of how its fighters operate. He reports these as what Ijaw informants and an anthropologist told him, not as established fact.

Resource curse and corruption as the root cause. Junger cites economists' "resource curse" and the World Bank's claim that 1 percent of Nigerians capture most oil wealth. He cites an estimate of $300-400 billion stolen or misspent since 1960, and describes governors in the delta states, who receive roughly $6 billion a year, as largely accused of corruption. Junger's own claim is that this "starved" society is "cannibalizing itself": police shake-downs, collapsed roads, deadly air travel, slums. Note that these figures are estimates he attributes to others.

Complicity of foreign firms. Junger notes that oil companies are "long thought" to pay local youth gangs, reports Jomo's claim that Agip offered MEND $40 million (which an Agip spokesman denies), and lists Halliburton/KBR bribery admissions and an FBI investigation of a US congressman. These are allegations or admissions he attributes to named parties, not his own findings.

Grievance as political claim. MEND's demands (prisoner releases, $1.5 billion in restitution, 50 percent of oil, development aid) and Owei's rhetoric ("We need to be made to feel like human beings") position the militants as claimants on the state. This echoes the volume's theme of criminal insurgency and failed state-led development, though this section does not use that vocabulary.

Notable passages

Oil ShockWave scenario. The simulated loss of 800,000 barrels a day, combined with a demand surge, shows how fragile a system at near-maximum production is.

The scale problem. Junger explains why the Nigerian military cannot guard the creeks and why offshore moves do not solve it, and shows the 2006 attacks reaching the sea and the city of Port Harcourt.

Meeting MEND. The village scene, in which Jomo hands Junger a phone through a gunman and then orders his release, shows MEND as a decentralized network with a coordinating voice.

Resource curse and stolen wealth. The opening of "Poverty and Corruption" sets out the economic argument and its figures.

Cannibalizing society. Junger's catalogue of checkpoints, broken roads and unburied dead is his evidence for a hollowed-out state.
