Global Sex Trafficking: Disparity, Flows and Coercion

Commons · Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Seager's short atlas-text frames the global sex trade as a multi-billion-dollar industry driven by economic disparity that globalization has heightened, with shifting source and demand regions and widespread coercion.

Chapter by Joni Seager (first published in The State of Women in the World Atlas, 1997), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).

This is the brief text frame of an atlas chapter whose maps and charts (Figures 3.1 to 3.4: trafficking flows in the Western and Eastern Hemispheres, regional prosecutions in 2006, and a marriage-introduction site count by country) were not extracted. Seager argues that the sex trade is a multi-billion-dollar industry in which women's bodies are commodities, sustained by economic disparity between men and women and between regions, and that globalization has sharpened that disparity.

Themes

Disparity as the engine. Seager argues that the trade thrives on inequality at every scale. Source and demand regions shift with economic fortune: she says Eastern Europe becomes a major source region as poverty deepens, while rising wealth in China and Malaysia increases male demand. She names large trafficking circuits within East and Southeast Asia and from Central and Eastern Europe into Western Europe. This is a regulatory and economic arbitrage story told from the supply side, and it fits the editors' view that deviant trade rides on mainstream globalization, though Seager herself does not use that frame.

Coercion and deception. Seager describes recruitment by false pretences, sale of girls by poor families, and increasingly outright kidnapping, and says the trade is sustained by high levels of coercion and systemic violence. These are her own assertions in this extract; no figures or sources appear in the extracted text.

Demand-side commentary. She says the AIDS/HIV epidemic fuels demand for younger girls seeking supposedly safe partners. She also quotes the introduction to a marriage-introduction website (goodwife.com), a customer-side voice voicing a preference for a more traditional woman. That quote is the website's own text, not Seager's argument; it works as an example of gendered demand in a globalized market.

Notable passages

The opening analytical paragraph on disparity and shifting source and demand regions carries the chapter's thesis. The paragraph on lures, family sales and kidnapping gives her account of recruitment mechanisms. The goodwife.com epigraph-like quotation shows the demand side in the market's own words. Little here connects to WMP machine concepts; at most it is a data point on the shadow side of global flows (compare farrell-underground-empire, which reads networks from the state's side), and the text itself is a 1997 atlas caption-style overview, not a theory.
