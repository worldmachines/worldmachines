Violence, demand and the Lacey Act in the stolen-timber trade

Commons · Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Khatchadourian reports that illegal logging is sustained by violence, conflict-financing and Western demand, follows Sascha von Bismarck's EIA investigation of Wal-Mart's opaque wood supply chain and the campaign for a Lacey Act amendment, and ends with a night-time attempt to catch loggers in the Russian taiga.

Chapter by Raffi Khatchadourian (first published in The New Yorker, 6 Oct. 2008), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).

This is the middle stretch of Khatchadourian's profile of Sascha von Bismarck of the Environmental Investigation Agency (E.I.A.). It opens with the violence that Khatchadourian says supports illegal logging: killings staged as suicides, murdered activists, and "conflict timber" funding warlords and regimes. It then turns, via von Bismarck's argument, to demand: Western and rising Asian consumption, and an economy that undervalues wood. A case study follows of E.I.A.'s undercover work in Dandong, China, tracing Russian oak and poplar into toilet seats and cribs sold through Wal-Mart. Next comes the legal story: the absence of any forest treaty, the weakness of CITES, and the passage of a Lacey Act amendment, including an exemption won by biotech lobbyists. The unit ends with von Bismarck and a young Russian policeman chasing a logger in the Ussuri taiga.

Themes

Violence and organized crime as the enforcement layer. Khatchadourian reports killings in Russia, Peru and Brazil, mostly staged as suicides or occurring in official spaces, and presents them as one of the "most disturbing aspects" of the trade. He then describes how the U.N. coined "conflict timber" in 2001 for the DRC, and cites Burma, Cambodia and Liberia. Under Charles Taylor, logging concessions went to warlords, arms deals were run through a timber company, and 2003 U.N. sanctions on Liberian timber were followed by the regime's collapse. The causal claim that the sanctions brought Taylor down is an unnamed American official's, not Khatchadourian's own finding. The moral framing (von Bismarck's "unwitting financiers of this crime") is von Bismarck's.

Demand-side and price argument. Von Bismarck argues, and Khatchadourian develops with figures, that illegal logging is as much a demand problem as a supply problem. Most wood is used locally as fuel, but stolen timber returns to the West as finished goods, and US per-capita wood use has risen since the mid-1960s. Chinese and Indian paper consumption is growing. Von Bismarck's view is that an economy which treats wood as cheap and inexhaustible accepts black-market supply readily, and one estimate (uncited by name) puts the depression of global wood prices by illicit timber at as much as sixteen per cent. This is a clean statement of moral and regulatory arbitrage running through mainstream consumer markets: the illicit flow is dual-use with the legitimate one, and the buyer's indifference is part of the mechanism.

Opacity of supply chains. The Dandong case shows legal and illegal wood merged downstream by middlemen, relabelling and supplier churn. Wal-Mart's practice of dumping suppliers at high frequency, in von Bismarck's account, "created incentives for suppliers to get bad wood". A Huafeng manager says big clients never ask where wood comes from. The PIERS customs database is the lucky break that makes the chain readable at all. This resonates with Legibility as a theme, though here the point is retailer-chosen illegibility rather than a state strategy, and Khatchadourian does not frame it in those terms.

Governance gaps and the extraterritorial fix. Khatchadourian notes there is no forest treaty (the Rio talks in 1992 faltered over who pays), that over eighty per cent of forests are state-controlled, and that CITES works as an "emergency room" for a few species and leaks in practice. The proposed answer is the Lacey Act amendment, which makes importing wood taken in violation of another country's laws illegal, with no innocent-owner defence. This uses US law to enforce other states' rules, which loosely echoes weaponized-interdependence, though that concept is about state leverage over network chokepoints, while this is a lobbying coalition of NGOs, industry and retailers. Khatchadourian flags the weakness himself: foreign forestry codes can be vague and contradictory.

Industry politics. The coalition forms because some American firms say illegal wood undercuts them (Demorest; an industry association's billion-dollar estimate), while the Bush Administration declines to support the amendment. Monsanto and BIO secure an exemption late on. Von Bismarck calls this "a big, eye-opening moment in terms of how government works".

Notable passages

Conflict timber (c1). The paragraph that links timber to civil war and arms: Khatchadourian cites the U.N. term, then Burma, Cambodia and Liberia, ending with the sanctions claim attributed to a US official.

Undervalued wood and indifference (c2). Von Bismarck's argument, as reported, that structural undervaluation and murky chains make illegal supply easy to absorb. Note the hedge: the sixteen per cent figure is "according to one estimate".

PIERS and supplier churn (c3). How a customs database lets E.I.A. trace Russian oak to Wal-Mart suppliers, and how supplier turnover cuts both ways.

The missing treaty (c4). The only trade treaty is CITES, and the Texas ramin shipment shows its weakness in practice.

The Lacey Act design and its limit (c5). The no-innocent-owner provision, the comparison to illegal art, and the admission that foreign rules may be unclear.

The closing taiga chase is dramatic reportage with little analytical weight and is summarised here rather than quoted. Smirnov's remark that nobody locally profits from the forest's disappearance is a rare local-development counterpoint, in tension with the book's view that deviant trades can be a route to development for the poor, though Smirnov is speaking about gang bosses and Russia's far east specifically.
