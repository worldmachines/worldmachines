Snakehead Economics: Payment, Routes, Corruption and Saturation

Commons · Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Keefe describes how Fujianese snakehead smuggling is financed and organized (down payments, big and little snakeheads, relay routes, the $35,000 fee), argues that its 1990s boom rode on globalization and on corruption in the weakest states, and reports that the trade has slowed through market saturation rather than Chinese prosperity, though it could revive.

Chapter by Patrick Radden Keefe (first published in World Policy Journal, 2009), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).

This unit continues Keefe's account of Chinese human smuggling from Fujian Province to the United States. He sets out the commercial structure of the trade: down payment plus balance on arrival, a hierarchy of "big" and "little" snakeheads, and routes that shifted from bribed visas and recycled passports to chartered fishing boats and then to long multi-country relays. He argues that the 1990s explosion of the trade coincided with, and exploited, globalized commerce and the weakest links in the regulatory system, chiefly corrupt officials in Thailand, Central America and elsewhere. He closes with his own interview-based inquiry into why the trade has declined (his informants say saturation, not prosperity) and a hedged forecast that economic downturn could revive it.

Themes

Smuggling as a contract business, not slavery. Keefe argues that the common belief in years of indentured servitude is a misconception: snakeheads have no wish to track debtors for years, so they allow a grace period of 48-72 hours for family and friends to pay the balance. He reports that American authorities have sometimes charged hostage taking, but that Fujianese customers generally perceive the process as "the fulfillment of a contract." Debt shifts to relatives and friends who helped pay. Keefe also notes that the down payment explains why it was often the working and middle class, not the poorest, who left.

Entrepreneurial organization and logistics. Keefe describes "big snakeheads" (often Overseas Chinese who see themselves as entrepreneurs and run licit businesses) supplying capital and contacts, and "little snakeheads" recruiting locally, often requiring a guarantor in the US. As the business grew, accompanying guides gave way to subcontracted local guides and fixers working in a relay. He frames human cargo, in the snakeheads' minds, as "a form of cargo like any other," subject to economies of scale; the 1991 Taiwanese drift-net ban supplied idle ships, moving the business from a retail to a wholesale model.

Globalization as enabling infrastructure. Keefe states that the snakehead boom was "no accident": snakeheads exploited the volume and variety of international trade and transport, modern communications, supply-chain-style distribution networks and "friction-free" capital flows, including an underground banking network used by Sister Ping (who he says, citing the FBI, accumulated some $40 million). This is a journalist's version of the dual-use infrastructure point at the heart of the editors' framing. It resonates loosely with the state-side reading of networks in farrell-underground-empire and weaponized-interdependence; Keefe, however, writes about criminal use of open networks, not state chokepoint control.

The weakest-link logic of regulation. Keefe's most analytic claim is that any international regime to control clandestine trade is only as good as its least vigilant member, and that the more harmonized the rest of the system, the higher the payoff to the one country willing to cheat, offering "an illicit back door into the licit international system." He cites an observer's term "geopolitical black holes" for such hubs and gives examples: Bangkok airport officials, the Honduran cash-for-citizenship law (a ring made nearly $20 million selling passports), fired immigration directors in Panama, Belize and Guatemala (1995), the arrest of US official Jerry Stuchiner (1997), and a 2008 bribery arrest of a US immigration lawyer. Milosevic's lifting of visa requirements for Chinese citizens is his example of geopolitical shifts being exploited. This is regulatory arbitrage in the editors' sense, though Keefe does not use that term.

Adaptability from loose structure. Keefe argues the business is adaptable because of loose-knit organization and diverse international contacts, rerouting through Moscow, Ukraine, Slovakia, Prague, Belgrade and Central America as conditions change. A related point about illegibility to states is implicit but not made by Keefe; compare Legibility only as an outside lens.

Saturation, not prosperity, and a hedged outlook. Officials say the trade has been sharply curtailed by stiffer sentences and detention of asylum applicants; Keefe's interviews in New York's Chinatown suggest arrivals continue weekly, with the fee reaching $75,000, usually payable in full up front, with a return to air routes and Central American landings. Asking whether Chinese growth will end emigration, he reports that his Fujian informants mostly said the decline reflects market saturation (everyone likely to leave already has; Wenzhou has supplanted Fuzhou as a source). Informant Lin Li says growth changed who leaves: "If you can do anything here, you stay." Keefe cautions that the labor market is sensitive, that laid-off construction workers are returning west, and that the snakeheads, "entrepreneurial to the end," will probably lower prices if downturn pushes people out again. This is forecast by a journalist, written in 2009, and not a finding.

Notable passages

The passage on big and little snakeheads gives the organizational model and the guarantor mechanism that addresses the trust problem of deferred payment. The passage on the 1991 shift from planes to ships shows commodity logic applied to people. The passage on debt and the 48-72 hour grace period corrects a popular misconception and is a good example of Keefe distinguishing his own analysis from American authorities' charges and from customers' own perceptions. The two passages on globalization and weakest-link corruption carry the chapter's central argument. The final curated passage on market saturation is an informants' explanation, reported by Keefe as the prevailing view among those he spoke with in a two-week trip, not an established causal finding.

Keefe's chapter is journalism: the figures (fees, $40 million, $20 million) are cited from officials or reporting, and the saturation thesis rests on a limited set of interviews.
