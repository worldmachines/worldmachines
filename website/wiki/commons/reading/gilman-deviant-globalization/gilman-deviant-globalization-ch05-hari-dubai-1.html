Dubai's Hidden Underclass: Debt, Bonded Labour and the Mall Economy

Commons · Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Hari's 2009 reportage portrays Dubai as a debt-fuelled, tax-free boomtown whose skyline rests on a hidden, passport-confiscated migrant labour underclass, with expats, a pampered Emirati minority and the mall economy each looking away.

Chapter by Johann Hari (first published in The Independent, 7 Apr. 2009), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).

Hari opens with the cracks in the Dubai image after the 2008-09 crash (stalled cranes, half-finished towers, leaking luxury hotels) and calls the city a product of "credit and ecocide, suppression and slavery." He then reports in turn on a ruined Canadian expat, the history of the city, the migrant construction workers of the Sonapur camps, and the malls where Emiratis and visitors avoid seeing any of it. Most of the evidence is interview testimony from the people he met; the framing ("slaves", "con-job", "medieval dictatorship") is Hari's own polemical voice, and several figures (the 971 Indian deaths, the 300,000 men in Sonapur) are reported claims rather than audited counts.

Themes

A boom state built on credit and imported, unfree labour. Hari argues that the sheikh did not build the city; a foreign underclass did, and still does. Recruiters in Bangladesh promise 40,000 takka a month and charge a 220,000 takka visa fee. On arrival, according to the worker Sahinal Monir, the employer takes the passport and pays about a quarter of the promised wage. Debt at home (sold land, loans from lenders) plus the confiscated passport makes exit impossible. Hari says this is nominally illegal but that he met nobody for whom the rules are applied, and he accuses the authorities of complicity. The mechanism (up-front fees, debt, documents held, penalties for striking) is the chapter's most analytically useful content.

Legal asymmetry: debt is criminal. Karen Andrews's account (as Hari relays it) is that Dubai has no concept of bankruptcy, that employers must report resignations to banks, and that unpaid debts freeze accounts and bar departure. Her husband was jailed after a trial in Arabic with no translation. This is one expat's story and she is a stressed informant, but it shows the same legal logic that binds the poor workers also catches middle-class foreigners.

Three Dubais and strategic not-looking. Hari sorts the city into expats, Emiratis and the foreign underclass, "hidden in plain view." Segregation is spatial (camps an hour out of town, small buses instead of cattle trucks because expats found the trucks unsightly) and psychological: a 17-year-old Dutch visitor says "I try not to see." The malls, in his telling, are air-conditioned places where time does not pass and the brands are those of home, so the global consumer economy is visibly the other face of the labour economy. This fits the volume's broader claim that mainstream and deviant globalization share infrastructure, though Hari himself makes no such theoretical argument here.

The Emirati bargain and its defence. Locals, about 5 per cent of the population by Hari's figure, are described as living in a "Santa Claus state" funded by land rents, soft taxes on foreigners and residual oil. Ahmed al-Atar defends it by contrast with his grandfather's poverty. Sultan al-Qassemi, a self-described reformist, minimises the abuses ("30 or 40 cases a year"), insists workers can leave, rejects strikes as inconvenient, and says that Dubai's "export isn't oil, it's hope." Hari presents these as informants' justifications and rebuts them with the passport and wage-withholding facts; he reads Sultan's ordering (animals, shampoo, workers) as revealing.

Fragility. The recession has stopped pay, cut power to camps, and emptied malls; Hari calls Dubai "a living metal metaphor for the neo-liberal globalised world that may be crashing." Dated 2009, this is reportage of a moment, not a forecast.

Resonances (loose). The spatial hiding of labour and the habit of not looking rhyme with illegibility-as-elite-defense and with Legibility seen from below (workers whose documents are held are fully legible to their employers and invisible to everyone else), though Hari is not making a legibility argument. The chapter's ordering of a gleaming mall economy over a concealed labour base loosely echoes the layered economy of braudel-three-storey-house, with the caveat that Hari is a campaigning journalist, not a Braudelian. The "Dubai model" as a late globalization showpiece also touches globalisation-as-inevitability-rhetoric.

Notable passages

The framing paragraph. After the crash, Hari says the "secrets of Dubai are slowly seeping out" and offers his thesis: a city built on credit, ecocide, suppression and slavery.

Debt law. The account of no bankruptcy, accounts frozen on resignation, and a travel ban shows how the legal regime traps even the privileged.

Three Dubais. The short statement of the city's class structure and the culture of not looking.

Sahinal's recruitment. The recruitment-fee and wage-substitution story, the clearest account in the unit of how the labour pipeline works.

Illegal in theory, complicit in practice. Hari's claim that the abuses are supposed to be illegal, that he met no one for whom the rules held, and the reported suppression of death counts (Human Rights Watch quoted on a "cover-up", the Indian consulate told to stop counting).

The Santa Claus state. Ahmed's account of the Emirati welfare bargain and Hari's analysis of where the money actually comes from.

Sultan on strikes and leaving. The exchange where exit and voice are both closed to workers but defended by a local liberal.
