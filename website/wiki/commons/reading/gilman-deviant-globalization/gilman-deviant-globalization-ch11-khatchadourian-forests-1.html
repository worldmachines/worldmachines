The Stolen Forests (1): Suifenhe, E.I.A., and the Illegal Timber Chain

Commons · Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Khatchadourian opens his New Yorker account of the covert war on illegal logging by following Environmental Investigation Agency director Alexander von Bismarck into Suifenhe, the Chinese rail town where Russian timber (perhaps half of it illegally cut) enters the global furniture chain, and by sketching how E.I.A. built its undercover method, from the ramin trade to a plan to leverage Wal-Mart.

Chapter by Raffi Khatchadourian (first published in The New Yorker, 6 Oct. 2008), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).

Khatchadourian, a reporter, opens on Suifenhe, a Manchurian rail town through which more than five billion pounds of Russian wood a year pass into China, and accompanies Alexander von Bismarck of the Environmental Investigation Agency (E.I.A.) and an associate he calls Wu De as they enter the town under false identities as buyers for a front company, Axion Trading. He pauses to profile von Bismarck and E.I.A., gives a short history of deforestation and of illegal logging in Indonesia, the Philippines, Papua New Guinea and Brazil, traces how E.I.A. built its method on the ramin trail, explains how China's 1998 logging ban turned it into the world's biggest log importer, and describes von Bismarck's plan to follow Russian wood to Wal-Mart shelves. The unit ends with a hidden-camera meeting at the Longjiang Shanglian timber group, where a manager casually describes mafia protection money and cash payments in Russia, but the investigators fail to get him to admit that his own wood is illegal.

Themes

A black market fused with the legitimate supply chain. Khatchadourian's framing claim is that if a US-sold item was made in China from oak or ash, the wood probably came through Suifenhe, and that because, by his figure, as much as half of Primorski Krai hardwood is harvested in violation of Russian law, any given piece there is likely illegal. He calls it “a destructive black market merging into the global economy”. This is close to the editors' thesis that deviant and mainstream globalization share infrastructure. Note that the argument is the author's and sources are official: Russia's Minister of Natural Resources is quoted describing “an entire criminal branch” of the trade.

Regulatory displacement. Khatchadourian's account of China is a story of one jurisdiction's protection exporting the harvest elsewhere. After the 1998 Yangtze flood (over three thousand dead, over thirty billion dollars in damage) some Party officials blamed over-quota cutting, logging was banned across much of China, and the country began buying wood abroad, “often from ‘high risk’ countries”. He compares this to the behavior of developed countries that destroyed their own forests, profited, and then protected what remained by importing indiscriminately. He attributes the Russia-China flow to Russia being “in a state of political and economic anarchy”, and says the greatest illicit wood traffic is now “thought to be” Russia to China (his hedge).

State weakness and corruption as enabling conditions. A fifth of the world's wood comes from countries that cannot enforce timber laws; governments long avoided acknowledging illegal logging because their own officials made it possible. Examples he cites are the Philippines, Papua New Guinea (auditors describing loggers as “robber barons”), Brazil (1998 announcement that most logging was beyond the law) and Indonesia (the government's own estimate of seventy per cent illegal, a World Bank loss figure of three and a half billion dollars a year, and a park boundary illegally redrawn for a timber baron). In Suifenhe, a distance from the central government and proximity to Russia produced a violent shadow economy of opium and ephedrine smuggling and mob clashes, which Khatchadourian illustrates with anecdotes reported by an American trader.

Investigation as leverage, not prosecution. E.I.A. is a nine-hundred-thousand-dollar-a-year nonprofit whose operatives pose as buyers, film with hidden cameras, and then use the footage to pressure companies and governments (“leverage” is the office's favorite word). In the ramin case, von Bismarck's footage led Taiwanese officials to raid a crib factory, led J. C. Penney and Wal-Mart to pull ramin goods, put the factory on a US watch list and, later, helped bring a federal indictment. The Wal-Mart plan inverts the supply chain: pick the retailer with the greatest consumer-side clout, then trace backward through suppliers it takes care to obscure. This is the author's reporting on E.I.A.'s own account; the Taiwanese manufacturer now maintains his wood was legal.

Corruption as ordinary cost of business. In the Longjiang Shanglian meeting, the manager treats mafia protection money, police who are “like the mafia”, and suitcase cash as line items, and says Chinese firms edge out Japanese ones because the Russian climate is “dark”. These are statements by an interested informant, reported as spoken, not verified by Khatchadourian in this unit. Notably, the same man shuts down the question of legality of the timber itself by saying they have proper documents, so the investigators obtain evidence of enmeshment with corruption but not of illegal supply. The paperwork is the legibility layer that launders the origin.

Possible WMP resonances (half-fits). The documentary shield that makes illegal wood look legal (false Malaysian paperwork for ramin, “proper documents” in Suifenhe) rhymes with the project's treatment of Legibility and illegibility-as-elite-defense, though here the paper does not hide from the state but is the state's own instrument, bought or forged. The way a regulatory crackdown in one place (China's 1998 ban) reroutes the trade toward weaker jurisdictions is a form of arbitrage in the sense the editors use, and loosely echoes weaponized-interdependence's attention to chokepoints, though Khatchadourian is not a theorist and makes none of these links himself.

Notable passages

The framing claim (c1). The paragraph beginning “Chances are good...” carries the chapter's central assertion about the reach of the Suifenhe trade and quotes Russia's Minister of Natural Resources on a criminal branch of the industry.

Weak states and illegal logging (c2). A short survey of countries where laws were not enforced and where governments avoided admitting illegal logging because their officials were complicit.

Following ramin (c3). The model of E.I.A.'s method: undercover contact from trafficker to manufacturer to retailer, then notification of customs and companies, with measurable results and the manufacturer's disputed denial.

China's pivot to imports (c4). The causal account of how the 1998 flood and logging ban made China the largest log importer and exporter of finished wood products and drew in Russian wood.

The Wal-Mart strategy (c5). Why von Bismarck picked a retailer, and what chain he intended to chart.

Cash and protection (c6). The manager's description of payment in suitcases of cash and his boast that Chinese buyers dominate where Japanese ones cannot.
