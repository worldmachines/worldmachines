Wine, the girl from the G-Spot, and the failure of enforcement in the Philippine sex trade

Commons · Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Flynn's reportage contrasts Wine, an adult Manila club hostess financed by debt-funded Japan migration, with a 13-year-old girl in an Angeles City bar whose 2002 rescue raid, like later actions against foreign club owners, changed little because the business is too big and enforcement too weak.

Chapter by Sean Flynn (first published in GQ, Aug. & Sept. 2005, as "The Sex Trade", Parts 1–2), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).

This stretch of Flynn's reportage runs two profiles in parallel. Wine is a Manila club hostess, a young mother who financed a stint as a "cultural entertainer" in Japan through debts to her manager. The second is a girl who started in an Angeles City bar, the G-Spot, at 13. Flynn recounts her family collapse, her short time in the bar, the November 2002 raid that removed her and other minors, her year in a shelter run by the PREDA foundation, and her return to the bars. Flynn then widens to the structure around such cases. He covers the US bases' legacy, foreign owners, and how arrests of club owners fail to translate into prosecution. The sexual content of the original is not reproduced here; the note summarises it plainly.

Themes

Economics of the individual hostess. Flynn reports Wine's arithmetic in detail. On her best night she grosses about 2,000 pesos, owes 100 for laundry, 200 for makeup and about 300 for food, and nets roughly 1,400 pesos (about thirty dollars). He also notes that many girls claim to be 19 and two weeks into the job. Flynn presents this as the best job Wine has had. Her earlier work (cosmetics, banana cart, cockpit cashbox) paid worse. That is a development-style point about alternatives, though Flynn does not frame it theoretically.

Debt as the price of crossing borders. Wine's route to Japan ran through a manager, six months of training, an Artist Record Book test and housing, all on credit. Flynn glosses her word "credits" as debts. After six months in Kyoto she cleared 40,000 pesos, about $700. This is a legal-visa channel (the text mentions 70,000 cultural-entertainment visas) with debt attached. Flynn reports her account of staying clear of the dates other girls did, and notes her pronoun slips, so the reader is told how far to trust it.

Push factors and the mundane path in. The girl's story is Flynn's illustration of how it happens: a father's suicide, a violent stepfather, a mother with few resources, relatives who cannot afford another mouth, and then a decision to work in a bar. Flynn writes the line that this is how a 13-year-old girl walks into a bar and gets a job. The girl's account is her own testimony as reported by Flynn, and details such as which man owned the club come from her recollection plus Flynn's matching of a photograph.

From military demand to self-sustaining industry. Aurora Javate-de Dios of the Coalition Against Trafficking in Women (an informant, not Flynn) says the closure of the US bases did not end the trade, because the culture and infrastructure drew other investors. Flynn's gloss is that supply is no longer a reaction to specific demand but a self-sustaining industry that creates its own demand, now serving Koreans, Brits, Australians and Germans as well as Americans. This is the closest the passage comes to the volume's argument that deviant markets outlive the conditions that created them, and that infrastructure is value-neutral.

Enforcement as theatre. Flynn repeats the move of changing the details (different dates, more girls, more arrests) and concludes that it does not matter, because arrested girls are replaced and 17-year-olds turn legal at 18. Javate-de Dios says the laws exist but are almost always breached in practice. Flynn's own evidence is the bail of 30,000 pesos for Jarrell, no prosecution in almost three years, and Richard Agnew's eventual freedom, a return to managing bars and a 2005 police plaque for his contribution to tourism. Agnew denies owning the clubs, and Flynn notes his name was on none of the legal papers and that he says his admissions were coerced. Flynn's tone is that the Philippine criminal-justice system, in Javate-de Dios's words, 'sucks', and that the business is too big and the money too good. Corruption is the cited mechanism, and it is reported as allegation and implication by Flynn rather than proven.

Opaque ownership. Flynn notes that the legal papers are fuzzy and that fuzziness extends to legal responsibility. Ownership is largely foreign and hidden behind managers. This rhymes with the World Machines interest in strategic illegibility (illegibility-as-elite-defense), though Flynn is a journalist reporting a specific local arrangement, not theorising it. It also fits the editors' broader claim that state capacity is hollowed out where the trade is lucrative, but the chapter itself does not make that argument.

Notable passages

Wine's night ledger (c1). The opening paragraph gives the unit economics of a hostess shift and the parenthetical about girls who all claim to be 19 and two weeks in.

Credit financing of migration (c2). The training, test and ticket costs show how a legal visa regime and private lenders combine to produce debt before the first paycheque.

After the bases (c3). Flynn's reversal of the demand story: the industry now creates its own demand and is international.

Why the arrests do not matter (c4). The paragraph that states the structural verdict of the chapter, with Javate-de Dios's quote and the line that the business is too big and the money too good.

Agnew and the plaque (c5). The closing note about an alleged club owner, charges dropped, and a police chief's plaque is the sharpest piece of evidence for enforcement failure, flagged by Flynn himself as one of the only details that matter.

The text is dated reporting from 2005 and its claims about outcomes should not be extended beyond the unit.
