Challenging liberal views of globalization, and what is to be done

Commons · Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The editors argue that deviant globalization undermines both naive and nuanced liberal views of globalization, diagnose why prohibitionist policy fails (moralizing, progress-seeking, denial), and propose five principles and two cautions for managing rather than eradicating it.

Chapter by the volume's editors Nils Gilman, Jesse Goldhammer & Steven Weber (written for this volume), in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).

The second half of the Conclusion has two movements. First, the editors say their thesis is uncomfortable for both camps of liberal globalizers: the zero-sum cheerleaders who welcome weakened states, and the more nuanced liberals who accept a regulatory state, because deviant entrepreneurs are already inside the global market and erode the states that development depends on. Second, they ask what is to be done. They call most current policy dysfunctional, with the US "war on drugs" as the extreme case, and give three reasons for it. They then offer a modest alternative: no perfect solution exists, so states should play an iterated game of strategic interdependence, guided by five principles and two cautions. They end by saying there are "analytic solutions" but no policy solutions.

Themes

Liberal globalization is stretched, not refuted. The editors say the most avid advocates treat state weakening as a net good, which assumes a zero-sum game between states and markets. They call that position "hard to justify in historical terms". More nuanced liberals face a different problem. Deviant globalization replaces sixty years of liberal development in the Global South with "unfettered economics and exploitation that causes dry rot in states". The editors paradoxically describe illiberal groups as "ultra-liberal" free-market entrepreneurs, free of moral qualms and regulatory constraints. They put the point as a question: whether it is possible to be a global liberal in the early twenty-first century.

Not failed states but a self-reinforcing synergy. The editors reject the "failed states" framing, which implies states can and want to be fixed. In their account, weak states and strong deviant entrepreneurs, sometimes joined by high-level personal political connections, form a dynamic that keeps extending deviant globalization. Metropolitan states also lose the ability to decide winners and losers in the global political economy.

Three sources of policy failure. (1) Moralizing: once actors are labeled "global criminals", governments stop managing and start to "prohibit, target, capture, punish, strike, and kill". That ignores the system and creates arbitrage opportunities, and the editors compare it to partly effective antibiotics selecting for resistant organisms. (2) Progress-seeking: democratic politicians need visible progress, while deviant entrepreneurs show progress by making more money despite governments. Fences and armoured vehicles provoke better smuggling routes and IEDs. (3) Denial: the "growing pains" story that the mainstream economy will outgrow the deviant one is called analytically flawed, since deviant markets are probably growing faster. Inconsistent policy, which mixes all three, hands deviant entrepreneurs a profitable environment.

Neither war nor surrender. The editors take libertarian calls to stop doing harm seriously, but call "do nothing or declare war" a false choice. They propose iterated-game thinking, in which neither side can defeat or control the other. The goals are to curb the worst industries, redirect deviant energy into less harmful channels and put deviant know-how to public use.

Five principles. (1) State policy cannot eliminate deviant globalization, so regulate it, for example by making polluters pay. (2) No state can impose uniform standards or prices on moral offensiveness, so let advocates of different moral standards bargain through institutions. (3) Bargaining needs transparency about costs, benefits and preferences, including to domestic constituencies. (4) Regulation should err on the side of permissiveness, with particular care for the most vulnerable participants. (5) The global political economy rewards rapid innovation, so encourage legitimate innovation to crowd out deviant innovation, with stem-cell research against the organ trade as one example. The editors say the state need not be the arbiter or even the first mover in this game.

Two cautions. Do not try to turn back the clock: anti-globalist coalitions of moralists, unions, environmentalists and cultural protectionists pursue what the editors call a fantasy of autarky. Do not do nothing: the Global North will not outgrow the problem, and the Global South's reliance on it for development tends to hollow out states. The editors say the North and South are both complicit in the conditions that join deviant supply and demand.

Notable passages

The passage on synergy between weak states and deviant entrepreneurs (c1) restates the volume's thesis against the failed-states vocabulary. The three-reasons sequence (c2) is the editors' diagnosis of prohibitionist failure, with its ecological analogy of antibiotics and adverse selection. The statement of the alternative (c3) says there is no perfect solution and calls for an iterated strategic-interdependence game. Principle 2 (c4) gives the volume's view on moral arbitrage and competing moral standards. Principle 4 (c5) says to err on the side of permissiveness. The closing passage (c6) distinguishes policy from analytic solutions and says managing deviant globalization means learning to live with demand for repugnant products and services.

Resonances

The argument that prohibition selects for adaptive, ruthless actors echoes World Machines interest in how control efforts generate the variety they aim to suppress, which is close to the divergence-machine, although the editors argue as policy analysts and use no such framework. Their claim that the state loses the ability to sort winners and losers sits near the modernity-machine in its Dusk phase and the failing legibility project (see Legibility), but they do not use that vocabulary and still give states a regulatory role. Their critique of the zero-sum state-versus-market story rhymes with globalisation-as-inevitability-rhetoric, although the editors criticise a specific liberal claim and not the rhetoric of inevitability. Their "strategic interdependence" framing is related to what weaponized-interdependence describes from the state's side, but is not the same argument.
