The Illicit Abalone Trade in South Africa: Four Conditions of a Boom

Commons · Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Steinberg argues that South Africa's illicit abalone (perlemoen) boom of the 1990s, which brought the wild stock to the brink of commercial extinction, rested on four necessary conditions: a collapsing rand, pre-existing Chinese organized-crime trade routes, porous borders, and the political consciousness of colored fishing communities.

Chapter by Jonny Steinberg (first published as Institute for Security Studies report no. 105, 2005), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).

Steinberg opens by telling how one endemic marine snail, Haliotis midae (perlemoen), prized in East Asia, went from a quota-managed fishery (quota introduced 1970, 615 tons by 1995) to near commercial extinction within about a decade of the early 1990s. Poaching had always existed but was containable until South Africa's transition to democracy; by the late 1990s it was, in his account, a multi-million dollar industry run by street gangs on the shore and transnational criminal enterprises on the routes to East Asia. He reports that by 2002 enforcement confiscated more abalone per year than the commercial fishery harvested, and that enforcement authorities feared the fishery would be unsustainable by 2006 if the 2004-2005 season was as bad as the last. He frames the paper as partly a post-mortem and insists on following the whole value chain from the water's edge to East Asian ports rather than only border smuggling. This unit covers the four-factor explanation of the market's rise, the border and air-route evidence, the drying process, and the opening of the fishing-community argument.

Themes

Currency arbitrage and barter. Steinberg argues the rand's slide (about three to the dollar in December 1992, thirteen in December 2001) made a dollar-priced commodity whose costs were all paid in rands "a proverbial gold mine". Contraband traders, unlike licit exporters, barter: they export a dollar-denominated good from a weak-currency zone and swap it for imports, gaining on the way out and skirting the weak currency on the way in. He cites Ted Leggett's picture of the illicit economy as a bazaar and Joseph Roth's fictional border traders. The evidence he offers is inferential (stable cocaine and heroin prices despite the falling rand; abalone-for-methaqualone barter, with seizures linked to abalone businesspeople), and he says outright that nobody knows what proportion of the catch is bartered. Cannabis exports to the UK in the 1990s are his parallel case.

Trade routes, not commodities. Chinese organized crime (Hong Kong triads 14K and Wo Shing Wo, and a Taiwanese group, the Table Mountain Gang) was present for a decade before abalone took off, but went undetected because apartheid-era policing had no concept of investigating criminal organizations; they were found once the police built the institutions to see them (an Organized Crime Intelligence Unit, 1991). Steinberg's general claim is that "contraband traders specialize in trade routes rather than commodities", so one route hosts abalone, counterfeits, drug precursors, guns, people and possibly diamonds depending on margin and risk. He hedges that the counterfactual cannot be tested.

Border control as a missing function. Ports are not searched without intelligence, so Steinberg says precise audit is impossible, but TRAFFIC citing Hong Kong import records (200,000kg frozen and over 100,000kg dried perlemoen from Mozambique, Namibia, Tanzania, Swaziland and Zimbabwe, January 2002 to June 2003) implies more entered Hong Kong than the entire legal quota. Twelve investigators from six agencies agree that most goes over land borders or by light aircraft; in 14 monitored controlled deliveries only two left through commercial ports. Once across the border the abalone is, as he puts it, in juridical territory where carrying it without a permit is not an offence. He attributes the failure to a border function that muddled through coordination and where nobody "took ownership" of abalone.

Dried abalone as a logistics technology. Drying removes the smell, allows disguise, permits indefinite storage and bulk accumulation, and shrinks mass to about a tenth. Drying factories form a line from Cape Town to Limpopo and have grown from cupboards to whole houses.

Community legitimacy. The fourth and, Steinberg says, most interesting condition is the "mutation in the socio-political identities" of colored fishing communities. Apartheid closed the sea to them (the testimony of Gadija Tommy), the post-1994 expectation of open access collided with distrust of the ANC, and poaching was widely seen as entitlement. He reports abalone money visible in Hawston housing and half-empty schools (legend as told to him), and a broad web of participation. He qualifies this: a 1995 survey of 42 Hawston residents found 80% said marine poaching was wrong, so tolerance was complex rather than collective political defiance. He leaves open whether a faster fishing-rights reform would have helped.

Resonance with World Machines (partial). The weak-rand barter story and trade-route logic echo the editors' regulatory and moral arbitrage, and the dependence on existing infrastructure rhymes with the shadow side of global infrastructure (farrell-underground-empire, weaponized-interdependence, though those authors read it from the state side). The apartheid police's inability to see organized crime until it built the institutions to detect it, and the unmonitored ports and airfields, are a small case of state illegibility (Legibility, legibility-cuts-both-ways). Steinberg is a policy analyst and does not frame any of this as machine theory.

Notable passages

Collapse of containment (c1). Steinberg dates the break from containable poaching to organized industry to the democratic transition, and reports that confiscations exceeded the legal harvest by 2002 "under the nose of a hapless enforcement regime".

Bartering (c2) and the Cape Flats drug economy (c3). His mechanism for how a weak currency favors contraband, then its local expression: Chinese precursors swapped for abalone and abalone swapped for drugs, leaving every serious drug player needing abalone. The traffic-jam quote is an investigator's colorful remark, not the author's finding.

Routes over commodities (c4). The most general analytic claim in the unit, hedged by his own admission that the counterfactual is untestable.

Hong Kong import arithmetic (c5). TRAFFIC's figures, which Steinberg converts using the one-tenth shrinkage ratio to show exports exceeded the whole legal quota; the TRAFFIC authors' conclusion is theirs, with the phrase "almost certain".

The politics of entitlement (c6). The "potent combination" of expectation and fear that made poaching socially tolerated.
