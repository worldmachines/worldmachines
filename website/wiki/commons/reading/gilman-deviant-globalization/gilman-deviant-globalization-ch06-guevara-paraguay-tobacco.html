Paraguay's contraband cigarette industry: a smuggling hub built on tax arbitrage and weak regulation

Commons · Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Guevara, Rehnfeldt and Soares report that Paraguay, with minimal regulation, state complicity and a border geography of rivers and piers, became a top producer of contraband cigarettes (68 billion made in 2006, up to 90 percent allegedly smuggled), tracing the trade through the career of smuggler Roque Silveira, the Tri-Border Area and the factory owners who say they merely fill the void left by Big Tobacco.

Chapter by Marina Walker Guevara, Mabel Rehnfeldt and Marcelo Soares (first published by the Center for Public Integrity / ICIJ, 2009), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).

The authors, reporting for the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists, argue that landlocked Paraguay has become one of the world's leading sources of contraband cigarettes within about fifteen years. They open in the Brazilian border city of Guaíra, where a large share of residents live off smuggling, then follow the trade to Ciudad del Este and the Tri-Border Area, the Itaipú lake piers, a US smuggling ring and Brazilian prosecutions built around the figure of Roque Fabiano Silveira ("Zero Um"). They then turn to the factories themselves, notably Tabesa, and to the manufacturers' own defence that they are only replacing the smuggling that British American Tobacco and Philip Morris once ran. The authors rely heavily on named officials (customs chiefs, prosecutors, federal police), court records and the factory owners' own words; allegations against Silveira and Tabesa are reported as allegations by police and prosecutors, and Silveira declined comment.

Themes

Scale and the production-consumption gap. Guevara, Rehnfeldt and Soares cite a study by the Uruguay-based NGO CIET: Paraguayan factories made 68 billion cigarettes in 2006, more than 20 times domestic consumption, and law enforcement officials say up to 90 percent of output (worth an estimated $1 billion) disappears into the black market. Experts estimate Paraguay accounts for 10 percent of the world's contraband tobacco; flows to Brazil are put at 20-30 billion sticks a year, while legal exports to Brazil are, per a Brazilian customs chief, zero. These are expert and official estimates, not audited figures.

Regulatory arbitrage as the business model. The trade works because Paraguay's cigarette taxes are among the world's lowest while Brazil's and Argentina's are much higher. The authors report that the pattern was first run legally-on-paper by the multinationals: in the 1990s BAT and Philip Morris subsidiaries exported billions of cigarettes to Paraguay that were smuggled back. When Brazil raised export taxes in 1999, dozens of Paraguayan factories opened, many Brazilian-owned. Paraguayan manufacturers (Tabesa's CEO José Ortiz, Cosmopolita's Sidronio Talavera) disclaim responsibility for what happens downstream, which the authors let stand as the industry's own moral framing: Ortiz says of the multinationals "We are replacing that market they abandoned."

State weakness, complicity and the institutional hollow. The authors report (citing officials who speak confidentially) virtually no industry regulation, prosecutors whose seizures are reversed by judges and customs officials, politicians, bankers and soccer-club barons who are in the business, and a historical base in the Stroessner dictatorship's underground economy. On the Brazilian side, they report a customs agent allegedly paid $8,000 a month to wave loads through (later murdered), 700 arrests in 2007 but two convictions in Guaíra, and a municipal economy where lookouts, storage-renters and river "paseros" earn about $300 a week. The picture is of states that can see the trade but cannot or will not close it: new piers open as fast as old ones are shut.

Opacity and illegibility as operating conditions. Investigators say the proliferation of more than 2,600 registered, obscure brands ("Dirty," "Fidel," "Hamlet," "Opus Dei") makes tracking harder, while Ciudad del Este's financial system and its linkage of factories to money-exchange houses defeated US undercover agents trying to follow laundered proceeds. This resonates with Legibility: the smugglers' advantage lies in what the authorities cannot read, though the authors are journalists describing enforcement failure, not theorising legibility, and the chapter says little about why particular state actors choose not to look.

Hybrid legal-illegal firms and mobile infrastructure. The same factories serve a legitimate domestic market and, allegedly, the smugglers; plants range from state-of-the-art German-equipped facilities to truck-mounted "mobile" or "submarine" factories. Distribution rides ordinary infrastructure (vans over the Friendship Bridge, then motorboats from over 300 makeshift piers when Brazil tightened the border in 2005; containers hidden in plywood bales to Ireland; ports at Miami, Norfolk and Baltimore). This fits the editors' wider framing of deviant globalization as sharing mainstream channels, though that framing is the editors' and not this chapter's.

Adjacent harms. The authors report that smuggling channels are being used to supply weapons and munitions to Brazilian gangs such as the PCC (a claim attributed to the Foz do Iguaçu customs chief), and that Paraguay is regarded by US officials as a money-laundering centre for drugs, arms and cigarette proceeds.

Notable passages

The production gap (c1). The 68 billion figure, the 20-times-domestic-consumption ratio and the 90 percent black-market share anchor the chapter's claim that Paraguay's industry is built for export-by-smuggling.

Impunity by design (c2). "Smuggling is made easy" is the chapter's title claim, here attributed to officials speaking confidentially: no regulation, insulated traffickers, and political and sporting elites in the business. The President Lugo anecdote shows reform attempts stumbling.

Laundering opacity (c3). The US prosecutor's admission that agents could not find the money they helped launder is among the strongest evidence in the chapter for the financial system's impenetrability.

Whack-a-mole at the border (c4). The Brazilian customs chief on piers ("We close one pier, and two more pop up overnight"), a shoreline of nearly 1,000 miles and zero legal exports show the enforcement asymmetry.

Filling Big Tobacco's shoes (c5). The account of BAT and Philip Morris's 1990s round-trip exports and the post-1999 factory boom gives the history behind the industry and the manufacturers' counter-narrative.

"My job is to supply the market" (c6). Ortiz's disclaimer of responsibility, plus his "parents and grandparents of the creature" line, shows how a producer frames downstream smuggling as someone else's problem.
