Angeles City and the Bar-Girl Economy: The Sex Trade as Commodities Market

Commons · Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Flynn opens his two-part GQ report on the sex trade in the Angeles City bar scene, arguing that a largely uncountable, supply-and-demand-driven commerce flows women from poor places to rich ones and male sex tourists the other way, and that the Philippines tolerates it through legal euphemism because it earns money and jobs.

Chapter by Sean Flynn (first published in GQ, Aug. & Sept. 2005, "The Sex Trade", Parts 1-2), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).

This is the opening of Part 1 of Flynn's magazine feature. It begins in a Nagoya airport smoking lounge, where Western men heading to Manila trade stories about Filipino bar girls, and then moves into the G-Spot Lounge in Angeles City, where Flynn describes a 13-year-old girl on stage with a false birth certificate claiming she is 19, hired without scrutiny by the bar manager. Flynn then steps back to a global frame: the number of prostitutes worldwide is unknowable, the trade runs as a market of insatiable demand and seemingly endless supply, and two streams of people cross the planet in opposite directions. He grounds this in the Philippines (bars at Balibago, Makati, Quezon City and Subic Bay), explains why the law is not enforced, and closes with a second figure, a 23-year-old "client's liaison entertainment officer" in a Manila club who insists she is not a prostitute.

Themes

Uncountability. Flynn argues that the global sex trade resists counting because it is "largely underground and extremely fluid". His example is Thailand, where the government says 75,000 prostitutes and aid groups say nearly 2 million, ranges he calls "virtually useless as an accounting tool". He then says the exact numbers are irrelevant to participants; what matters is demand and supply. This is a journalist's judgement, not a measured finding.

Commodities-market framing and moral arbitrage. Flynn calls the trade "as pure a commodities market as pork bellies or soybean futures" and says that with easy travel and weak borders "both sides of the trade ... have become industrialized". The two counter-flowing streams (women from poor regions into rich cities; men from the First World into the Second and Third) are a vivid picture of arbitrage across differences in wealth and in law, which fits the editors' deviant-globalization thesis, though Flynn himself does not use that vocabulary.

Coercion versus choice, hedged. Flynn acknowledges stories of brutal trafficking and of naive recruits, but says "the great bulk" of the business is prosaic: women "willingly" selling a marketable asset. He immediately qualifies that "willingly" is "terribly relative", given the middleman's cut, and that "oppression is also a relative term" for people with few options. That is his own framing; he does not cite a study for the proportion.

Legal fiction as state accommodation. Prostitution is illegal in the Philippines, so the bars function through the "bar fine", framed as compensation to the club, which Flynn calls "a ridiculous semantic wink". He reports an anonymous Western diplomat saying that stopping it would mean arresting half the senate. The state is neither absent nor opposed; officials, airport guards and hotel drivers are in on it. This is a mild case of the state-and-illicit-economy entanglement the volume stresses, and a loose rhyme with legibility-cuts-both-ways, though Flynn offers no theory of it.

Economic embeddedness and development. Flynn says there is "no financial incentive to enforce the laws": sex tourism feeds a $2 billion tourist trade (he notes that no one knows the share), and bars supply jobs in a country where nearly half live on under $2 a day and where more than 10 percent of GDP is remittances. Japan's tens of thousands of "cultural entertainer" visas are given as an example of a transparent euphemism. The trade is thus woven into mainstream tourism and labour-export infrastructure rather than being separate from it. The legacy of the US bases at Clark and Subic explains how the scene became established; Flynn's own account is anecdotal and impressionistic.

Norms and demand. Flynn says Thailand and the Philippines have long local traditions of paid sex, citing studies (more than half of Thai men paid to lose their virginity; more than 400,000 visit brothels daily) and an activist, Aurora Javate-de Dios, who describes a norm of two kinds of women. Note that these are cited figures and an informant's view, not Flynn's own research.

Notable passages

The passage on counting (c1) sets out the data problem and the 75,000 versus nearly 2 million Thai estimates. The passage on willingness and industrialization (c2) is Flynn's central interpretive claim. The two-streams passage (c3) is the chapter's big-picture map, from the stratosphere view of opposing flows to the cultural-demand argument. The bar-fine passage (c4) shows the legal fiction and the complicity of the wider tourism sector. The economics passage (c5) lays out why enforcement has no incentive: tourist revenue, overseas-worker remittances and poverty. The G-Spot scene, which describes a child, and Wine's club scene are summarised here rather than curated; the former is Flynn's illustration of how age checks fail in practice, and the latter shows a tiered market, with a higher bar fine and a "not a prostitute" self-presentation.

For World Machines this unit offers little beyond a ground-level view of informal economies embedded in tourism and labour export; the nearest echo is Braudel's shadowy infra-economy (braudel-three-storey-house), but Flynn is a reporter and makes no such claim.
