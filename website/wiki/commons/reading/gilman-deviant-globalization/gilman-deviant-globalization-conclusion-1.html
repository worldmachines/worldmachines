Deviant Globalization as Development, and as a Challenge to the State

Commons · Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The editors argue that deviant globalization is a form of Southern development born of neoliberal state hollowing-out, and that it breeds new political actors who erode state power while preferring weak states to controlling them.

Chapter by the editors (Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber), written for this volume, in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).

The opening of the Conclusion, under an epigraph from Canguilhem on studying pathology to understand the normal, states why deviant globalization matters and then argues two of its three promised questions: development and the state. The editors say it matters for three reasons: its innovative energy, its challenge to the classical liberal view of the state as the organizer of economic and political development, and the dysfunctional policy that follows from misunderstanding it. They then argue that deviant globalization is itself a form of development for the Global South, that it is not a benign or heroic one, and that it grows into a new kind of political actor which degrades state power. A sidebar, "The Tribulations of Modernist Development", traces the neoliberal hollowing-out of Southern states as a precondition for the rise of deviant globalization. (The third question, on policy, falls outside this unit.)

Themes

Deviant entrepreneurs as innovators. The editors compare the Cali Cartel's Rodríguez Orejuela brothers with Brin and Page and say deviant entrepreneurs do marketing, strategy, organizational design and financial analysis like any others, invoking Adam Smith's animal spirits, Christensen's disruption and Schumpeter's creative destruction. The claim is that disgusting goods do not make the entrepreneurial lessons less real.

Deviant globalization is development. Both liberals and Marxists, the editors say, treat illicit economies as parasitic diversions from productive use. They reply that the poor are already running "radical experiments in actual development" (renting bodies, selling organs, manufacturing drugs, taking toxic waste) behind the back of the World Bank, the IMF and development NGOs. They hedge: they deny neither coercion nor externalities, and the claim is only that participation is rarely the worst available choice. Some host states, such as Thailand, the Philippines and Cuba in the sex-tourism example, tacitly build it into their developmental strategy, tolerating "social or moral pollution" as others tolerate physical pollution.

It is a different and ambivalent kind of development. The editors list four contrasts with NGO-style development: less transparency, less formal organization, weak contract enforcement, and no durable public goods because profits go untaxed. Each comes with a compensating gain (new degrees of freedom, flexibility, alternative enforcement, jobs and capital). They explicitly reject a Robin Hood reading: deviant entrepreneurs are "opportunistic parasites" enacting an ultra-libertarian development that rejects the public good.

A new political actor that perpetuates state weakness. Deviant entrepreneurs wield political power through money, violence and, most controversially, private provision of security, health care and infrastructure (Hezbollah, MEND, Mexican narco-traffickers, Brazilian favela syndicates). They do not begin as political actors, but their success turns state weakness into a positive feedback loop they have reason to preserve. Most do not want to take over the state, since that would oblige them to serve "ungrateful and low-profit clients"; they want autonomous zones. The editors therefore say deviant globalization thrives not in truly failed states but in weak, well-connected ones.

Neoliberalism as precondition. In the sidebar, the collapse of Communism also ended the shared Cold War idea of development as a postcolonial state responsibility. The Washington Consensus ("stabilize, privatize, and liberalize", quoting Rodrik) and structural adjustment left states as hollowed shells. This produced survival entrepreneurship ("you are on your own") and dismantled regulatory capacity, so that the triumph of neoliberalism was "a critical (perhaps even essential) prerequisite" for deviant globalization. The editors add that no stable equilibrium is likely: legitimacy erodes, local people shift loyalty to the entrepreneurs, and the state may implode, as they say happened in Colombia in the 1980s and Zaire/Congo since the 1990s and as may be happening in Mexico.

Notable passages

The opening passage (c1) sets out the three reasons and refuses to call bad policy outcomes "unintended consequences". The development passage (c2) is the core reversal of the parasite view, and c3 gives the editors' own qualification that the result is ultra-libertarian rather than heroic. The state passages (c4, c5) give the three forms of political power and the claim that cartels want zones of autonomy and not sovereignty. The sidebar passage (c6) supplies the historical mechanism.

Resonances

The sidebar's account of the collapse of the postcolonial state-development consensus rhymes with the late-stage decay of the modernity-machine, and the proliferation of non-state actors in weak-state zones with the divergence-machine in the world-machines frame. The fit is loose: the editors are policy analysts writing about one thirty-year neoliberal episode, not about a thousand-year infrastructure cycle, and they do not use machine language.
