Nigerian Drug Networks, the Bulk Cocaine Trade and Shadow States in West Africa

Commons · Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Ellis describes the adhocratic, Igbo-dominated structure of the Nigerian drug trade (barons, strikers, couriers), the post-2005 shift to bulk cocaine shipments that has drawn in Latin American traffickers and state actors across West Africa, and argues the trade rests on weak-state political conditions and the 'shadow states' produced by 1980s liberalization.

Chapter by Stephen Ellis (first published in African Affairs 108, April 2009), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).

This is the second half of Ellis's chapter on West Africa's international drug trade. He first lays out the classic structure of Nigerian trafficking: a flexible, project-based network of barons who need cheap supply at source, a contact in a diaspora in the destination country, and capital; strikers who act as self-employed logistics brokers and recruit couriers; and the couriers or 'mules' at the bottom. He then turns to the 'new bulk trade', in which multi-ton cocaine shipments from South America reach West Africa by sea, air and container, with Latin American traffickers (including alleged FARC operatives) resident in the region, and with the trade penetrating the armies, politicians, oil militias and Saharan smuggling routes of several countries. He closes by arguing that the relocation of the trade is explained less by price than by institutional weakness, and that its roots lie in the 'shadow states' of 1980s structural adjustment layered on older colonial and pre-colonial practices. Ellis relies heavily on police and UN officials (Gelbard, Mazzitelli, UNODC, Costa) and on the economist Francisco Thoumi; where he is reporting their claims, and where press accounts are puzzling, he says so.

Themes

Adhocracy rather than mafia. Ellis argues, with a management-theory borrowing, that Nigerian traders constantly form and re-form business relationships from a wide pool of acquaintances, in contrast to the corporate-style American 'mafia' model of popular imagination. He quotes US official Robert Gelbard calling Nigerian networks among the most sophisticated transshipment, money-moving and document-forging organizations in the world, sought out by Asian and Latin American producers.

Layered cut-outs as an anti-detection design. Ellis (drawing on Nigerian law-enforcement informants) shows how independent strikers insulate barons from couriers, who often do not know the baron exists. Oaths at religious oracles, recruitment of strangers or holders of prestigious foreign passports, and use of 'deep' African dialects on the phone all raise the cost of policing. The human cost falls on couriers: Ellis cites a Nigerian police report that 69 percent of 316 people arrested at Lagos airport with cocaine or heroin (January 2006 to 5 September 2007) were swallowers, and the case of a young Nigerian hanged in Singapore. The human-wave tactic means West Africans in prison are probably disproportionate to the volume seized (Ellis's inference).

Ethnic and social embeddedness. Ellis reports a consensus that the trade is dominated by Igbo people, and notes the profiles used by authorities may omit Nigerians arrested in Saudi Arabia. He places this alongside other ethnically concentrated trades (the Edo State prostitution trade to Europe, many legal businesses), explains it through home-village networks and oracle oaths, and treats Igbo prominence as stemming from the civil war's aftermath, older entrepreneurial traditions, land pressure and the aspiration to wealth and chieftaincy titles. Police officers quoted by Ellis say Igbo drug traders seek wealth and social esteem rather than a criminal career in the Western sense.

The shift to bulk and the Latin American connection. The first recorded bulk Nigerian heroin case dates to 1993; by the 2000s Ellis lists cocaine cargoes of 2.3 to 14.2 tons intercepted around West Africa. He cites Mazzitelli's view that 2005 marked a change of scale, notes that UN and Nigerian figures differ, and flags that the 14.2-ton Lagos seizure is puzzling as reported. Nigerians act as local partners paid in cocaine (lots of up to 200 kilograms), which they feed back into courier channels to Europe.

State penetration, with Guinea-Bissau as the limiting case. Ellis reports (via an official and a former judicial police chief) that the Bissau-Guinean army stockpiles cocaine on military premises, that suspects were bailed and vanished, and that the investigator was fired. Ghana, regarded by donors as 'virtuous', is said by its own law-enforcement officers to be penetrated by drug money, and a Ghanaian MP was convicted in the US. In the Niger Delta, militias are paid for smuggled crude partly in cocaine; Ellis speculates that part of it is bartered in an oil-arms-drugs trade along the Gulf of Guinea. A trans-Saharan route with Tuareg guides is said by intelligence officers (not verified by Ellis) to yield taxes for Al-Qaeda in the Islamic Maghreb. A Lebanese syndicate, Ellis notes, first used West Africa for heroin transit over fifty years ago.

Weak states as the real comparative advantage. Ellis agrees with UNODC and Thoumi that illegality gains competitive advantage where rule of law is weakest, so that institutions, governability and social values explain the location of the trade better than prices do. 'A pliable sovereign state is the ideal cover for a drug trafficker.' This links to the editors' wider claim that deviant globalization thrives where state capacity is hollow, though Ellis is describing West Africa, not generalizing.

Shadow states, liberalization and the long term. Ellis argues Nigerians were already active in the 1970s, before structural adjustment, but that 1980s economic reform and the dismantling of colonial-era bureaucracy contributed to 'shadow states' in which rulers draw authority from controlling markets. He roots these in initiation societies and indirect rule, which let unofficial governance networks operate out of European officials' sight. He says the drug trade is already, not prospectively, perverting politics (Liberia under Taylor as a criminalized state), notes that for Guinea-Bissau and Guinea-Conakry it makes a huge unofficial contribution to national income, and leaves open the historical-sociological debate on how new the turn to predation is. The arrival of China and Chinese crime gangs is flagged as a further element.

Notable passages

Adhocracy versus mafia (c1). A short, quotable statement of Ellis's organizational thesis, with the management-theory analogy and the contrast with The Godfather image of organized crime.

Strikers and couriers (c2). The most detailed account of how the middle layer works: self-employed, serving several barons, recruiting couriers, and acting as a 'cut-out' that defeats police detection. It also shows how oath-taking and dialect exploit social trust and linguistic opacity. Resonates loosely with Legibility: the system is built so that a state looking at couriers cannot see the people above them, though Ellis does not use that language.

Igbo enterprise and status (c3). Ellis's attempt to explain ethnic concentration in terms of history (civil war, colonial-era opportunity, land pressure) and social aspiration rather than criminality, attributing the 'avenue to wealth and social esteem' reading to Nigerian police officers.

Guinea-Bissau (c4). The clearest case of military-level involvement and the political effect of the trade, with the reporting sources (law-enforcement officials, the former police chief, UNODC) distinguished and the FARC identification hedged.

Institutions, not prices (c5). The analytical core: UNODC and Thoumi on why illegal business locates where state and social controls are weak.

Shadow states and indirect rule (c6). Ellis's long-term argument that West African criminal networks are tied to the 1980s liberalization and to older, partly hidden governance traditions. It resonates with the volume's wider claim that deviant globalization is inseparable from mainstream globalization and from the post-Cold War retreat of state-led development, but Ellis makes the point regionally and with historical-sociological hedging.
