Robb on Global Guerrillas, Systems Disruption and the Terrorist-Criminal Merger

Commons · Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Robb argues that open-source guerrilla networks, using systems disruption and merging with transnational crime, are hollowing out nation-states, drawing on Iraq, Afghan opium, Hamas and the Madrid bombings.

Chapter by John Robb (Brave New War, Wiley, 2007; excerpts from ch. 1 and pp. 142–151), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).

Robb, a security analyst writing in the mid-2000s, argues that globalization and technology are "morally neutral forces" that also empower stateless armed groups. He uses Iraq as a testing ground for open-source, networked insurgency; claims that these groups can fight states through "systems disruption" of critical infrastructure; and argues that the most viable such groups ("guerrilla entrepreneurs") are fusing with transnational crime. His evidence includes Naím's estimates of the illicit economy, the Taliban's opium ban and its aftermath, Hamas's social services, and the financing of the Madrid bombings. The tone is that of a policy polemic with forecasts, not a systematic study; its claims are Robb's own and carry his hedges and rhetorical flourishes.

Themes

Open-source insurgency as organizational innovation. Robb argues that the Iraqi insurgency is made of seventy-five to one hundred small autonomous groups of "zealots, patriots, and criminals alike". Its main asset is an open-source community network, comparable to the software industry, with decision cycles shorter than the US military's and no center of gravity for conventional force to hit. This is Robb's own analogy, not a finding he demonstrates.

Systems disruption. Robb claims that attackers can erode a state's legitimacy by attacking the networks (electricity, oil, gas, water, communications, transport) that let it deliver services. His illustration is an estimated $2,000 pipeline attack in southeast Iraq that he says cost more than $500 million in lost revenue. The argument resonates with weaponized-interdependence, where states exploit network chokepoints, though Robb sees the non-state attacker striking the infrastructure from outside rather than a state controlling it.

Terrorist-criminal symbiosis. Robb draws on Moisés Naím's Illicit, citing Naím's figures of a $2-3 trillion criminal economy growing seven times faster than legitimate trade (a figure Robb restates elsewhere as $1-3 trillion, and says some estimates put at up to 10 percent of the legal economy). He describes smuggling networks as living "in the spaces between states," profiting by arbitrage across legal systems and corrupting officials. In his account these are decentralized, Lego-like horizontal structures, not vertical cartels. He cites DEA statistics that nearly half of forty-one listed terrorist organizations are involved in drug trafficking, and Kaplan's reporting on hashish and ecstasy financing of the Madrid attacks. This lines up closely with the editors' notion of moral and regulatory arbitrage in the volume, though Robb frames it as a security threat rather than a development route.

Guerrilla entrepreneurs and the Afghan opium case. Robb argues that the Taliban's 2000 opium ban cost it the support of warlords and about two million people's livelihoods; that US payments and the promise of unfettered poppy growing kept the warlords from rallying to the Taliban; and that production then soared (nearly 90 percent of world opium by 2005, about $2.5 billion a year, nearly half of GDP). The moral for Robb is that states will increasingly recruit such actors as temporary, profit-driven proxies. Note that the figures and the causal story are Robb's account, and the Karim Rahimi quote is a UN official's observation.

Modern means, old loyalties. Against Juan Cole's picture of mobilized, literate populations and Ralph Peters's "erratic primitives" typology (underclass, disrupted young males, believers, former military), Robb says the truth is in between: globalization is "layering new skill sets on ancient mind-sets." Warriors are wired, educated and mobile, but bound by tribal, clan or religious cohesion that blocks full integration. Robb adds that survival pressures push them either to proto-states (Hezbollah, Hamas) or, increasingly, to transnational crime.

Proto-states and the hollowing of the state. Hamas, in Robb's telling, runs education, food distribution, health care, public safety and more, gaining popular shelter, recruits and funding; its 2006 electoral win is treated as likely to fail because it inherits the weaknesses that made states vulnerable. This echoes the volume's theme of state hollowing, and loosely the failing-state vacuum in which alternative orders form. It also rhymes, only loosely, with the late-modernity-machine picture of declining state capacity and the divergence-machine's proliferation of alternatives. Robb is not a machine theorist and makes no such claim.

Notable passages

The Iraq passage (c1) sets out the open-source network claim and the analogy to Microsoft facing a "loose, self-tuning network". The systems-disruption passage (c2) gives the $2,000 versus $500 million asymmetry. The passage on smuggling networks (c3) is the closest to deviant globalization proper: arbitrage across legal systems, corruption of states and horizontal structure. The Afghan opium episode (c4) shows an illicit crop as a political and military variable. The "ancient mind-sets" paragraph (c5) is Robb's thesis on who the new warriors are. The Hamas passage (c6) gives the proto-state model.
