Ibn Khaldun as Teacher and Stylist: Pedagogy, Poetry, Rhetoric and the Prose of the Muqaddima

Commons · Robert Irwin · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Irwin surveys Ibn Khaldun's career as a teacher and his views on pedagogy, memorization, poetry, rhetoric and adab, then assesses the style of the Muqaddima as that of a lecturer writing to instruct rather than to persuade or entertain.

Irwin opens by noting that Ibn Khaldun spent more of his life teaching than in politics or writing, and that the last chapter of the Muqaddima treats pedagogy. The unit reconstructs the education he received (one-to-one or small groups, ijazas, dictation plus commentary, no madrasa enrolment), then his views: oral transmission over books, a distrust of commentaries and abridgements, religious study first, logic only as an auxiliary, and rote memorization of the best early poetry as soul-shaping. It moves through poetry (its own rise-and-decline cycle, its use in statecraft, the chrestomathy in the Muqaddima), rhetoric and adab, and ends with a stylistic assessment of the Muqaddima: abstract, instructive, repetitive (perhaps from lectures), saturated with Qur'anic formulae, and judged very differently by modern scholars.

Themes

Education as transmission, not originality. Irwin stresses that knowledge was passed down through licensed teachers (sanad, ijazas) and that "originality was at a discount." This is the setting for a thinker whom modern readers often call original; Irwin places him inside a culture of continuity. See ibn-khaldun.

Books as a veil; too many commentaries. Ibn Khaldun treats books as something the student must strip away and sees the profusion of commentaries and abridgements as an obstacle to scholarship, already in the fourteenth century. This is an early complaint about textual over-accumulation, though Irwin notes he had himself written such handbooks as a young man.

Religion first, logic as a limited tool. Religious sciences and hikma take priority; logic, philology and arithmetic are auxiliaries. Ibn Khaldun warns against those who think logic is a natural means to truth, and holds that inspiration from God guides understanding better. Irwin does not read this as proto-secular pedagogy.

Rote learning as a source of creativity. Irwin quotes his own earlier formulation that Ibn Khaldun "believed that one was what one had committed to memory." Memorization shaped the soul, and the quality of what was memorized mattered. Poetry, meanwhile, was also an instrument of statecraft, and Ibn Khaldun wrote a long poem to win back an emir's favor while denouncing poetry written to solicit favors.

Poetry on a cycle of rise and decline. The Muqaddima presents poetry as subject to rise and decline, "just like political regimes": a pre-Islamic peak among the desert Arabs, a distraction into theology and law, an 'Abbasid golden age, then a new decline. Irwin also flags, in a parenthesis, that this is a romanticized view of the primitive Bedouin. This loosely rhymes with the dynastic-cycle logic and with the WMP interest in cyclical pessimism (compare decay-sequence, linear-vs-cyclical-paths), though here it is a literary-cultural cycle and Irwin does not develop it into a general theory. Language too is corrupted by admixture and by urban luxury: bayan is a luxury studied in large prosperous eastern cities, and good Arabic is best acquired by living among the Bedouin.

Style as the man's instrument. Irwin compares Ibn Khaldun with Gibbon and others: abstraction, little rhetorical persuasion, little description, no irony, and protagonists seen as beneficiaries or victims of broader social forces rather than psychological individuals. He suggests, with hedges ("may sometimes be based", "may be the result"), that the plain, repetitive presentation reflects oral lecturing. Personal opinions are often prefaced with "perhaps" (rubbama), and Qur'anic invocations act as "an extended form of punctuation." Modern scholars (Rosenthal, Gibb, Silvestre de Sacy, Monteil) disagree about the style and, Irwin says, about the meaning of what he wrote, which sets up the reception chapter.

Notable passages

  • Education and ijazas (c1). The account of Ibn Khaldun's own schooling and the culture of dictation, commentary and continuity.
  • Oral teaching and too many books (c2). The veil metaphor, the attack on commentaries and abridgements.
  • Rote learning (c3). Memorization as creativity and soul-formation, with the Kitab al-Aghani as ideal.
  • Poetry's cycle (c4). Poetry rises and declines like regimes; Irwin's parenthetical skepticism about the romanticized Bedouin.
  • Language and style (c5). The Gibbon comparison and the claim that Ibn Khaldun wrote just to instruct.
  • Qur'anic punctuation (c6). The invocations that the modern Western reader overlooks but that mark every section end, evidence of the religious frame Irwin insists on.
