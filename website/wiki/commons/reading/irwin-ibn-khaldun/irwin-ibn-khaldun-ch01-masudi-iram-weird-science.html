Al-Mas‘udi as the Historian to Be Surpassed: Marvels, Weird Science, and Ruin as Judgment

Commons · Robert Irwin · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Irwin presents al-Mas‘udi as the predecessor Ibn Khaldun admired, borrowed from and had to attack (the City of Brass, Iram, Alexander's diving box, giants), shows the limits of Ibn Khaldun's rationality (sun, plague, spontaneous generation), and argues that he explained ruin and dynastic fall through a double causation of social development and God's decree.

Irwin opens with the melancholy of the fourteenth- and fifteenth-century Arab historians (Ibn al-Khatib, Ibn Khaldun, al-Maqrizi), who had no belief in progress and waited for God to declare the End of Time. The Black Death, he says, called for a new kind of history embodying warnings, which made the entertaining chronicle of al-Mas‘udi's Muruj al-dhahab obsolete. He then profiles al-Mas‘udi (896-956): a travelled, widely read, adab-minded writer, steeped in Greek learning, curious about non-Islamic peoples, and the first Arab historian to reflect seriously on the principles and purposes of history. Ibn Khaldun called him "the imam of the historians" and quoted him often, but criticized him for writing to entertain as well as instruct. Much of the unit is a catalogue of the stories from al-Mas‘udi that Ibn Khaldun attacks in the opening of the Muqaddima: the City of Brass, Iram of the Columns (with a Lovecraft aside), Alexander and the sea monsters, the Barmecides and Harun al-Rashid's drinking, the long-lived perfect first men, and the giants who supposedly built the Pyramids. Irwin then turns to the limits of Ibn Khaldun's own rationality (the sun's heat, plague causation, spontaneous generation, talking donkeys), and closes on ruins: do they signal natural and social causes or God's judgment? Irwin's answer is both, and he links this to the ‘asabiyya cycle and to Ibn Khaldun's reluctance to inquire into causality at all.

Themes

Melancholy history without progress. Irwin contrasts the Arab historians with Whig historians such as Macaulay: no belief in the progress of humanity, abandoned houses and deserted villages, an expectation of the End of Time. This is a pessimistic, non-progressive frame for history, and it is Irwin's framing rather than a claim Ibn Khaldun makes in the passage. It rhymes with linear-vs-cyclical-paths and civilization-undone-by-its-own-success, though the Arab historians' pessimism is eschatological rather than structural.

Al-Mas‘udi as the predecessor. Ibn Khaldun admired al-Mas‘udi's universal scope, his interest in geography, climate and race, and his systematic cross-referencing, and, Irwin says, "it also seems likely" that Ibn Khaldun's cult of the nomad was inspired by him: the Muruj presents the Arabs as essentially nomads who chose desert life over the shame of urban settlement. Both men dispensed with isnads. Ibn Khaldun instead relied on "his sense of the inherent probability of an alleged fact", the idea that there are ways things happen in human society and ways they do not. Irwin's closing hint is that "perhaps the Muruj al-dhahab did have to be attacked in order to create space for the Muqaddima".

Critique of credulity, and Irwin's defence of mirabilia. Ibn Khaldun dismisses the Copper (Brass) City, Iram, and Alexander's diving box as storytellers' absurdities, using arguments from lack of traveller reports, mistranslation ('imad as tent poles, not columns), engineering plausibility and the nature of the jinn. Irwin counters that al-Mas‘udi almost certainly did not believe the City of Brass story and placed it in a chapter on the marvels of strange lands: he was producing literature, and mirabilia were an important part of medieval Arab literature. Al-Mas‘udi himself doubted the Iram story and said many learned men thought such tales lies invented to gain favour with kings, and he cites The Thousand and One Nights by name. Ibn Khaldun's rejection of the Harun al-Rashid and al-Ma‘mun stories rests on their piety, which is a moral probability argument.

Common sense, not sophisticated method. Irwin stresses that Ibn Khaldun attacked al-Mas‘udi's claim of perfect, long-lived first men, and the giant-builders theory, with "common sense and simple logic". His positive replacement, that large monuments "were the product of superior social organization and the skillful use of machinery", is the kind of point that makes him look modern.

Limits of rationality: weird science. Irwin immediately qualifies the modern impression. Ibn Khaldun's account of the sun ("neither hot nor cold", only luminous, and hotter near the earth by reflection), his acceptance of spontaneous generation of scorpions, bees and snakes, the report of Egyptians teaching donkeys to speak, and his contradictory explanations of plague (too much civilization corrupts the air; yet crowds keep the air moving) are called weird science, oddities even to his contemporaries. Irwin contrasts Ibn al-Khatib, who deduced contagion. He also notes the diving-box error (suffocation rather than overheating). Attribution matters here: the judgments of irrationality are Irwin's, and the concessions, such as the failed alchemy, come from Ibn Khaldun.

Ruins: natural cause and divine decree. Ruins signal mortality and bad government, and Irwin finds "an apparent ambivalence" throughout the Muqaddima: mostly natural or social causes (plague, bad government, townspeople's softness, nomad destructiveness), but at times God's decree, as in Qur’an 17:16 on the luxurious made to act wickedly. The Shaddad inscription from the Nights tale delivers the same warning about doomed communities. Irwin's resolution is that Ibn Khaldun "conceived of God working through natural causes", with al-Ghazali's occasionalism cited as a parallel.

Overdetermined dynastic fall. Irwin summarizes the ‘asabiyya thesis, with the detail to be developed in chapter 4: after a tribal conquest of a city, decay sets in over three or four generations as luxury and extravagance grow, solidarity weakens, mercenaries and slave soldiers are needed, and non-Islamic taxes are imposed. The fall is at once nearly inevitable social development and the just result of sin and greed, so a single effect has two sufficient causes, which Irwin likens to overdetermination in Freud. This connects to the dynastic cycle in secular-cycles and ibn-khaldun, and to decay-sequence in a loose way; the Khaldunian version is explicitly moralistic and theological, which the secular-cycle literature is not.

Avoiding causality. Irwin reads Ibn Khaldun's social science as "moralistic" and notes his refusal to theorize causality: philosophers who try will "return unsuccessful", and the Lawgiver forbade studying causes. Irwin offers this hedged, suggesting it "perhaps" felt too close to probing the mind of God.

Notable passages

The passage on Ibn Khaldun's admiration for al-Mas‘udi (c1) establishes the debt, including the possible origin of the nomad cult and a quotation from the Muruj on the ancient Arabs preferring the desert. The Copper City passage and Irwin's rebuttal (c2) is the clearest case of Irwin correcting Ibn Khaldun's literal-minded critique. The giants and monuments passage (c3) shows the seemingly modern explanation of ancient buildings by social organization and machinery. The weird science paragraph (c4) is the unit's main anti-anachronism move. The ruins passage (c5) states the natural-versus-divine ambivalence, and the passage from "So, as far as Ibn Khaldun is concerned" (c6) gives the double-causation resolution and a first compressed statement of the dynastic-decay mechanism, which sits close to civilization-undone-by-its-own-success.

The Lovecraft aside (Irem, the City of the Pillars, in the nameless city and in "The Call of Cthulhu") is a reception curiosity: the Qur’anic ruin becomes a horror trope. It is not curated.
