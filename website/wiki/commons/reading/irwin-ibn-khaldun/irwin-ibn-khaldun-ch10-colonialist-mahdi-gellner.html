Retrofitted Ibn Khaldun: Lacoste, Mahdi, Gellner, Crone and the Arab rediscovery

Commons · Robert Irwin · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Irwin surveys twentieth-century readings that remade Ibn Khaldun in their own image (Lacoste's Marxist anti-colonial materialist, Mahdi's Straussian esoteric philosopher, Gellner's secular value-free sociologist), criticizes each as anachronistic, sets Crone's contrast between Confucian and Khaldunian cycles beside them, and closes with the nineteenth-century Arab rediscovery of the Muqaddima via European editions and the Nahda.

This unit is reception history with a critical edge. Irwin first notes that the French colonialist Ibn Khaldun drew fire from Brunschvig and, more fiercely, from the Marxist geographer Yves Lacoste, whose own reading he judges just as anachronistic. He then turns to Muhsin Mahdi, who under Leo Strauss's influence presents Ibn Khaldun as a concealed rationalist philosopher, and to Ernest Gellner, who presents him as a secular, value-free sociologist of ideal types. Each reading is tested against what Ibn Khaldun actually wrote. Patricia Crone's comparison of Confucian and Khaldunian cycles is then used as a sharper foil. The unit ends with the Arab rediscovery of Ibn Khaldun (al-Tahtawi, al-Afghani, ‘Abduh, Rashid Rida), which Irwin frames as a Western cultural re-export made possible by European publications.

Themes

Every modern reader builds an Ibn Khaldun in their own image. Irwin says so of Gellner ("Like so many who have studied Ibn Khaldun, Gellner created an Ibn Khaldun in his own image"), and the pattern runs through Lacoste and Mahdi too. This is the book's central stance made explicit: Irwin, the skeptic of anachronism, treats each retrofit as evidence about the retrofitter. Lacoste's Marxist Ibn Khaldun, a "Marxist manqué" who supplies evidence for the Asiatic mode of production and for underdevelopment predating colonialism, is judged "as anachronistic and culture-bound as Gautier's". Calling the fourteenth-century Maghreb part of a "Third World" is called questionable. Lacoste himself thought Ibn Khaldun a reactionary religious thinker and criticized him for missing the role of declining international commerce.

Mahdi's esoteric philosopher. Mahdi (following Strauss's Persecution and the Art of Writing) argues that Ibn Khaldun was a philosopher in the Graeco-Islamic tradition of Averroes who had to write obliquely in a hostile Maghreb, so that the search for his "deliberate intentions" replaces attention to his surface meaning. Irwin, quoting Lacoste with approval, notes that nothing in the text supports this and that Ibn Khaldun proposes no reforms. Mahdi's readings of individual terms are also rejected: badawi as "primitive" (from bada‘a, "to begin") and ‘ibar as "looking for the unity of the plan underlying the multiplicity of events". Irwin grants that the latter describes what Ibn Khaldun was doing but finds no evidence he used ‘ibar in that sense. Irwin gives one qualified concession: Mahdi's suggestion that the Muqaddima is an attempt to understand Ibn Khaldun's own political failure "may be worth thinking about", though it is unclear how the book answers it, and he doubts Hodgson's philosopher-king idea.

Gellner secularizes and Europeanizes. Gellner praised Rosenthal's translation, compared Ibn Khaldun to Keynes, Weber and Machiavelli, and credited him with "dispassionate analysis, instead of pious moralising". Irwin replies that piety and the need to deliver religious warnings were the primary impetus behind what Ibn Khaldun wrote (compare ibn-khaldun and niccolo-machiavelli for the comparison Gellner drew). He also points out that Gellner fused the Muqaddima with Plato and Hume's pendulum between polytheism and monotheism, and made Ibn Khaldun a theorist of "society as such".

Gellner's schematic model against the evidence. Gellner's three rings (sheep, sheepdogs, wolves) and the "circulation of elites" are a cyclical-elite model in the vein of Toynbee and Pareto. Irwin objects that the Dawawida were sheepdogs when paid and wolves when not, and never became sheep. He adds that Gellner's contrast between puritan townsmen and superstitious rural saint-followers inverts Ibn Khaldun, who stressed urban comfort and decadence and praised the strict monotheism of nomads. Gellner also missed Ibn Khaldun's positive view of the mamluks and wrongly assumed that ‘asabiyya arises only from kinship: Ibn Khaldun allowed proximity and mamluk barracks life to generate it, citing al-Salih Ayyub's purchase and training of mamluks in the Ta‘rif. Irwin also faults Gellner for generalizing from his Berber saints (igumaren) fieldwork, and for relying on Ibn Khaldun's account of Mamluk Egypt and Syria, which Irwin says Ibn Khaldun himself misrepresented. This resonates with WMP's interest in group cohesion as the engine of state formation and decay, and with secular-cycles. It differs from WMP in one respect: Gellner's model is a fixed structural schema, where WMP frames cycles as long-run machine lifecycles (world-machines).

Crone: whose point of view does the cycle take? Following Crone's Slaves on Horses, Irwin reports that Confucian historians also had a cyclical theory, but there the settled state slowly loses its vitality and falls to barbarian conquerors who lack virtue and must be civilized. For Ibn Khaldun, "tribal conquest marks the high point of the cycle": he "saw the cycles from the barbarians’ point of view". Crone (as quoted, not Irwin) also reads this as a Muslim fixation on the tribal past and traces the Muqaddima to disappointment at an urban man's exclusion from politics. This is a useful contrast for linear-vs-cyclical-paths and the nomad/sedentary dynamic. Irwin reports it without endorsing every claim.

Re-export and the Nahda. Brunschvig's lament that Ibn Khaldun met "systematic lack of comprehension" among his own people is called only a slight exaggeration, and the Arab rediscovery came via European publications. Al-Tahtawi had the Muqaddima printed at Bulaq in 1857 and seems to have treated hubb al-watan (patriotism) as a kind of ‘asabiyya. Al-Afghani taught it, ‘Abduh lectured on it (his notes were seized and burned in 1879) and denounced luxury in Khaldunian terms. The Salafi Rashid Rida found ‘asabiyya over religion un-Islamic. Irwin's framing links to the Nahda as a reaction to colonialism that still drew on Western values and genres.

Notable passages

Lacoste's Marxist retrofit (c1). Lacoste presents Ibn Khaldun as a materialist and source on the Asiatic mode of production, and reads tribal divisions as having handed Algeria and Morocco to the French, with underdevelopment predating colonialism. The paragraph shows the political use of the Muqaddima as a counter-colonial weapon.

Mahdi's Straussian esotericism (c2). The passage sets out Mahdi's lineage (al-Farabi, Strauss) and his claim that Ibn Khaldun hid his philosophy, with Averroes as the type. It is the clearest statement of the move Irwin resists, which is reading for concealed intentions over the surface text.

Gellner as tourist (c3). Irwin's rebuke, using Hartley's line that the past is a foreign country, is his sharpest statement that the secularized Ibn Khaldun is a modern construction and that the religious warning was primary.

Sheep, sheepdogs and wolves (c4). Gellner's three-ring cycle of elite circulation and Irwin's empirical counterexample from the Dawawida, plus the inversion of urban puritans and rural saints.

Confucian cycle versus Khaldunian cycle (c5). Crone's argument, as reported by Irwin, that Ibn Khaldun's cycle peaks at tribal conquest and sees history from the conquerors' side. Compare, without equating, chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders for raiders as a political type.

A Western re-export (c6). The closing claim that the Arab rediscovery of Ibn Khaldun came through European publications, and that the Muqaddima joins the Thousand and One Nights and the Rubaiyat in this circuit.
