From Tunis to Cairo: patronage, Mamluk turbulence and a growing Muqaddima

Commons · Robert Irwin · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Irwin follows Ibn Khaldun from his forced departure from Tunis in 1382 to Mamluk Cairo, where patronage, judgeships and Sufi posts came to him amid Circassian factional wars in which he was compromised, and where he kept expanding the Muqaddima and the ‘Ibar, while showing that his theory of Egypt as tribe-free and his elite-focused history do not match the tribal, plague-ridden realities he lived through.

After finishing his draft at Qal‘at Banu Salama, Ibn Khaldun returned to Tunis in 1378, taught, and was driven out in 1382 by the hostility (allegedly) of the chief Maliki jurist Ibn ‘Arafa, using a hajj pretext to leave; he went to Cairo instead, with his family held back as probable hostages. Irwin describes Cairo as the cultural capital of the Islamic world and Ibn Khaldun's ecstatic eulogy of it, then sets out how the city's waqf-funded madrasas and khanqas made it a magnet for scholars. He follows Ibn Khaldun's rapid rise under the Circassian sultan Barquq (professorships, Maliki chief qadi, Sufi posts), the shipwreck that killed his wife and five daughters, the 1389 revolt that briefly deposed Barquq and in which Ibn Khaldun signed a fatwa against his patron, and his subsequent loss of office. The chapter closes on how, out of office, he revised and expanded the Muqaddima and the ‘Ibar with Egyptian and Persian/Turkish material and with occult additions.

Themes

The mamluk institution as engineered ‘asabiyya. Ibn Khaldun admired the mamluk system because it let a regime "artificially" reinforce its ‘asabiyya (‘isaba) by importing vigorous warriors from remote tribal regions, and he called it a providential gift of God for the salvation of Islam. Irwin reports Patricia Crone's judgment that the passage "brilliantly describes the mamluks as institutionalized tribal conquerors", and notes that Ibn Khaldun borrowed the providential framing from the earlier chronicler Baybars al-Mansuri because it suited his theory. The nomadic virtues are preserved by slavery, "undefiled" by luxury; this is the badawa/hadara logic of ibn-khaldun applied to a slave-soldier state, and it is theological as well as sociological. It rhymes loosely with the WMP interest in group cohesion as the engine of state formation (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders), though Ibn Khaldun's framing is providential where WMP's is structural.

Waqf, fear of confiscation and the patronage economy. Ibn Khaldun himself explained the profusion of waqfs by emirs' fear that the sultan would confiscate their wealth from their slave-descended heirs; the resulting endowments funded schools and Sufi houses and drew scholars from Iraq, Iran and the Maghreb. Ibn Khaldun was an example of that flow. Here the mechanism of learning is a by-product of predatory royal authority (mulk), an observation of Ibn Khaldun's own, reported by Irwin.

Where theory meets fact: Egypt without tribes. Irwin's sharpest criticism: the Muqaddima says Egypt and Syria are free of tribes and group feeling, so that dynasty there is easy, but the civil war of 1389 was heavily shaped by Arab, Turkoman and Kurdish tribes, the Banu Fadl, Bedouin from Kerak, and later the Hawara Berbers in Upper Egypt. Irwin calls the description "nonsense" and finds it "surprising" that Ibn Khaldun never revised it. This is Irwin's judgment, and it is in keeping with his broader stance that the Muqaddima is not a smoothly empirical social science.

Opportunism and blind spots. Ibn Khaldun signed the fatwa delegitimizing Barquq (only the Maliki qadi al-Rakraki, his enemy, refused and was beaten), lost his post when Barquq returned, and in his memoir (the Ta‘rif) glossed over both this and the real reason. Irwin also notes that his works ignore the plagues of 1388-89 and the famines of 1394-96 and 1403-4 (al-Maqrizi reports more than half the population dying), and suggests, with a hedge ("perhaps"), that his rosy portrait of the sultanate aimed at patronage. He was "always more interested in winners than in losers". The winner-imitation maxim from the Muqaddima is quoted as an illustration.

Revising the Muqaddima. The core was written at Qal‘at Banu Salama, but the Egyptian recension is half as long again: he filled gaps on Persian and Turkish dynasties, extended the ‘Ibar into a universal history, updated the Berbers to the 1390s, added material prompted by students' interest in hadith and fiqh, and added much on the occult and supernatural (deferred to chapter 7). Irwin notes revisions continued to 1404. Sources include insiders such as the emir Altunbugha and borrowings from Ibn al-Furat; the history is thin on dates and is not annalistic. A theory-text in perpetual revision, not a finished system, and one that also grew through occult material, cuts against the modernizing reading of Ibn Khaldun as secular social scientist.

Career as judge and Sufi. Ibn Khaldun held Maliki chief qadi five times, always briefly because of rigor and incorruptibility, lectured on hadith and Maliki fiqh at al-Azhar, and headed Sufi khanqas. These are the markers of a man embedded in the religious-legal establishment of his time.

Notable passages

The providential eulogy of the mamluks (c1) is the fullest statement of how the theory of ‘asabiyya and nomadic virtue is turned into a defense of an actual regime. The waqf explanation (c2) shows Ibn Khaldun's political-economic observation about confiscation and endowment. The pair of passages on Egypt and tribes (c3) and on the fatwa (c4) show Irwin holding the theory against the evidence and the man against his self-presentation. The passage on the elite focus and the ignored plagues (c5) sets out Irwin's charge of selective vision. The final passage (c6) documents the Egyptian expansion of the Muqaddima and the ‘Ibar. Loose WMP hooks: secular-cycles and civilization-undone-by-its-own-success for the cyclical-decay backdrop, though this unit itself argues about biography and practice more than about the cycle.
