The Maghribi Game of Thrones: Borrowed Geography and a Scribe's Early Career

Commons · Robert Irwin · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Irwin sets out the post-Almohad struggle among Hafsids, Merinids and ‘Abd al-Wadids, shows how much of Ibn Khaldun's geography is borrowed from al-Idrisi and encyclopedic rather than analytical, and narrates his early career (1350–1359) as a scribe-courtier who repeatedly changed sides amid tribal desertions, plague and royal murders.

Irwin opens with the political map Ibn Khaldun grew up in: after the Almohad collapse, three dynasties (Hafsids in Ifriqiyya, Merinids in Morocco, ‘Abd al-Wadids at Tlemcen, the gold-route chokepoint) fight an inconclusive war of reunification, with the Nasrids of Granada meddling, unreliable tribal levies and no rule of primogeniture. A section on topography then shows that Ibn Khaldun took his world-geography (seven climes, the temperate middle zones) largely from al-Idrisi, with material irrelevant to the Muqaddima's historical argument, and that he credited Gog and Magog while doubting the City of Brass. Irwin adds a physical sketch of the Maghreb (Braudel's arid zone, Tell, High Plateaux, Atlas, the Sanhaja/Masmuda/Zanata Berber distribution). The final long section follows Ibn Khaldun from birth in Hafsid Tunis in 1332 through his teacher al-Abili, the plague, and posts under Ibn Tafraghin, the Merinids at Bougie and Fez, a twenty-one-month imprisonment for plotting, and his switch to Abu Salim in 1359, where rivalry with Ibn Marzuq begins.

Themes

Irwin deflates the "theory" reading. Much of the geography is "irrelevant to the historical concerns of Muqaddima", and in these sections the work "reads more like a comprehensive encyclopedia than a closely focused thesis about the underlying forces in history". Ibn Khaldun's climatic-zone claim that the middle zones produce more temperate people is a medieval geographical commonplace, not a proto-scientific environmental determinism (that is Irwin's framing; the unit does not argue otherwise). Note the hedge in Irwin's explanation of the Gog and Magog belief: "Presumably he did so" because the Qur'an attests them. This fits Irwin's wider picture of a man who believed in a supernatural, apocalyptic frame.

Tribal levies as the fragile engine of dynastic power. Merinids relied on Berber tribesmen and "the unreliability of the tribal levies" was a running problem. In Irwin's narrative Abu’l-Hasan loses to the Banu Sulaym and deserting Zanata levies at Qairouan (1348), and Abu ‘Inan is deserted by the Dawawida Arabs in 1357, while Ibn Khaldun himself recruits Banu Merin tribesmen for Abu Salim. The Hafsid ruler needs Bedouin cavalry against other Arabs yet never lets them sleep in Tunis. This resonates with the nomad/sedentary dynamic and group cohesion behind state formation and decay in the Muqaddima (ibn-khaldun; compare chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders and secular-cycles), but Irwin here records concrete political practice and does not present it as a theory of ‘asabiyya. The murder of Abu ‘Inan is said to mark "the beginning of the decline of the Merinids", a decline driven by succession contests rather than any stated generational cycle.

Learned men as insignia of royalty. Maghribi and Andalusian rulers displayed scholars at court like the parasol, the Friday-prayer bidding and coinage; Mamluks patronized scholars but almost never gave them important posts. Ibn Khaldun's career as a "bureaucratic condottiere" who "operated in dangerous waters" is the lived form of this. Loosely relevant to royalty-administration-and-antimemetics, though Irwin's point is about ritual prestige and not information management.

Loyalty was situational and elites were porous. Ibn Khaldun deserts the Hafsids for the Merinids (1352), joins a plot against them, is jailed, shows "no gratitude" to al-Fududi who freed him, and backs Abu Salim. Irwin's tone is unsentimental, and he does not soften this into principled theory. Andalusi refugees and Almohad-descended aristocrats compete for office, and Ibn Khaldun, though admiring Ibn Tumart, is hostile to the Hafsid "nostalgic cult of the Almohads" (a small case of a dead legitimating machinery kept as ornament, cf. decay-sequence, though Irwin does not frame it so).

Education outside the madrasa. Al-Abili, master of the rational sciences, refused to teach in madrasas and opposed textbooks and abridgements, and Ibn Khaldun "does not seem to have ever studied at a madrasa". Abu ‘Inan valued madrasas as tools of state control of religion and scholarship.

Notable passages

The opening paragraph (c1) frames the three-cornered struggle, Tlemcen's gold route, tribal-levy unreliability and succession chaos. The al-Idrisi passage (c2) is Irwin's clearest statement that the geography is borrowed and encyclopedic. The Gog and Magog passage (c3) shows Ibn Khaldun's selective skepticism grounded in scripture. The scribal-career paragraph (c4) gives his description of the ideal secretary and Irwin's image of a bureaucratic condottiere. The insignia paragraph (c5) explains the peculiar Maghribi-Andalusian political use of scholars. The 1357–59 narrative (c6) compresses desertion by tribal levies, imprisonment, the murder of Abu ‘Inan and Merinid decline.
