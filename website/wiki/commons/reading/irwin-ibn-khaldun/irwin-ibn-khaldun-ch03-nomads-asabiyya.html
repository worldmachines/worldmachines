Nomads, Health, and ‘Asabiyya: The Tribal Engine of the Muqaddima

Commons · Robert Irwin · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Irwin sets Ibn Khaldun's retreat to write the Muqaddima among the Awlad ‘Arif, tests his claims about nomad health against later observers, and presents ‘asabiyya and the three-to-four-generation dynastic cycle as the book's central but geographically limited and over-stressed thesis.

Irwin opens with Weber's definition of the state to make the point that fourteenth-century North Africa had none in that sense, because Arab and Berber tribes both created and destroyed dynasties; that cycle is "at the heart of the Muqaddima". He then places the composition of the Muqaddima at Qal‘at Banu Salama (1375, under the protection of the Awlad ‘Arif), stresses Ibn Khaldun's hands-on experience of tribal politics (raising taxes and armies, bribing and sometimes leading Dawawida cavalry), and describes the semi-nomadic, transhumant reality behind his "nomads". A section tests the Muqaddima's claim that desert people are healthier than sedentary people against Lawrence, Dickson, Thesiger and Cole, and notes Ibn Khaldun's odd views on hunger. The rest of the unit explains ‘asabiyya (etymology, function, place in God's plan), the dynastic cycle and its decay through luxury, the contrast with Gibbon, and Irwin's caveats: the model fits some dynasties (Almoravids, Almohads) but not others (Merinids, Hafsids, ‘Abd al-Wadids, Mamluks, the eastern and Andalusian lands), and modern readings have overstressed it.

Themes

Theory grounded in practice, but through a religious frame. Ibn Khaldun was "the scholarly product of an urban culture" who had negotiated with, bribed and led tribesmen, so he was "not an armchair theoretician". Irwin compares his retreat to the Sufi practice of khalwa, but says his meditations were "focused specifically on how God worked in the world through social processes". This is consistent with Irwin's stance that the Muqaddima is not secular social science.

The nomads were not pure nomads. The Maghribi tribes were transhumants, tied to towns by trade, taxation and military service. Irwin says Ibn Khaldun thought the countryside was more dependent on the towns than the reverse. Tribal reluctance to leave grazing grounds sabotaged urban generals' campaigns. This is the empirical ground of the badawa/hadara contrast, and it is more entangled than the theory's tidy opposition suggests.

The nomad-health thesis is contested. Ibn Khaldun argued that scarcity makes desert people healthier and better in character than plentifully fed hill people. Chatwin later romanticized this. Irwin notes that later observers (Lawrence, Dickson, Thesiger, Cole) report disease and malnutrition, and that Ibn Khaldun's denial that hunger kills is "somewhat eccentric". Irwin is quietly sceptical of romantic primitivism, ancient and modern. Compare bruce-chatwin.

‘Asabiyya as dynamic, divinely used group feeling. The word appears over five hundred times. Irwin gives two possible etymologies (binding of persons, or the turban or headband) and prefers the first, hedging with "may". He argues that Ibn Khaldun was an ethnographer only in a restricted sense, since only ‘asabiyya interested him; that it has "a life of its own" and drives toward power ("royal authority is the goal of group feeling"); and that it is "one of God's tools". Religion reinforces it (prophecy or sainthood as a coloring for Arab royal authority), but the Merinids, Mongols and Chagatai Turks show that religion was not always needed.

The dynastic cycle and luxury. Conquerors lose ‘asabiyya within roughly three or four generations, succumb to luxury, tax extortionately to pay other soldiers, become vulnerable when seen as corrupt and impious, and fall to the next puritanical wave. Ibn Khaldun's Torah citation of the third and fourth generation is one Irwin doubts actually supports the thesis. This resonates with the decay-sequence and with civilization-undone-by-its-own-success and secular-cycles as a cyclical, pessimistic account of state formation and decay (ibn-khaldun). It differs from the world-machines cycle in scale: a few generations and a tribal-military mechanism, not a roughly thousand-year infrastructure of institutions. See also linear-vs-cyclical-paths. Gellner's remark that the tribe is "both an alternative to the state and also its image" is Gellner's summary, not Ibn Khaldun's own claim.

Limits of the model, and Gibbon. Irwin says the model "seems to work quite well" for the early Arab conquests, the Almoravids and the Almohads, but not universally; Ibn Khaldun himself exempted the eastern Islamic lands, Andalusia and the Mamluks, and the long-lived Merinid, Hafsid and ‘Abd al-Wadid dynasties do not fit. Against Gibbon's "barbarism and religion" as causes of decline, Ibn Khaldun made them sources of empire. Irwin concludes that ‘asabiyya's importance "has been overstressed in some modern readings".

Notable passages

The khalwa passage (c1) shows both the religious frame and Ibn Khaldun's confidence in his own originality. The health passages (c2, c3) show Ibn Khaldun's humoral reasoning against later ethnographic evidence, with Chatwin as a modern romantic inheritor. The ‘asabiyya passage (c4) and the cycle passage (c5) are the unit's core theory. The final caveats passage (c6) is where Irwin bounds the model.
