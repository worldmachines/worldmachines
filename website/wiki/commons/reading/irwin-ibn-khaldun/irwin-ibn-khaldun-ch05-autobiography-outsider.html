The Ta‘rif and an outsider in Mamluk Egypt

Commons · Robert Irwin · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Irwin argues that Ibn Khaldun's Ta‘rif is a reticent curriculum-vitae-cum-chronicle rather than a confessional autobiography, then shows him as an unpopular North African outsider in Cairo, praised by two disciples (al-Maqrizi, Ibn ‘Ammar) and slandered by rival scholars whose accusations Irwin weighs sceptically.

This unit does two things. First it treats Ibn Khaldun's late work Al-Ta‘rif ("Presenting Ibn Khaldun and His Journeys in the East and the West") and argues that it is only nominally an "autobiography": it is short on self-revelation, modelled on the fahrasa (a curriculum vitae of teachers) and on the chronicle, and shaped by a medieval Arabic biographical convention that places a person in a "genealogy of authority" instead of explaining how he "came to be who he was". Second, under the heading of the outsider in Egypt, Irwin surveys how Cairene contemporaries and successors received Ibn Khaldun: two admiring disciples, al-Maqrizi and Ibn ‘Ammar, who absorbed only part of his method, and a hostile chain of scholars (Ibn Hajar, al-Sakhawi, al-‘Ayni, with echoes of Ibn ‘Arafa and al-Rakraki) whose charges of arrogance, forgery and sexual immorality Irwin repeats but often doubts. It closes with his repeated judgeships, his death in office on March 16, 1406, and a burial in a Sufi cemetery that Irwin says only "may suggest" he was a Sufi.

Themes

A reticent "autobiography". Irwin lists what the Ta‘rif omits: wife or wives, children, the Black Death in Tunis, his early writings (known only through Ibn al-Khatib), and al-Maqrizi and other Egyptian friends. He credits Michael Cooperson for the point that pre-modern Arabic biography was not confessional. The genre lens is Irwin's own historicizing move, and it works against reading Ibn Khaldun as a modern self-analysing thinker.

Credentials and chains of transmission. The long lists of teachers in the first half of the Ta‘rif serve to establish scholarly authority in a culture where oral transmission from teacher to student outranked reading books. The book also condenses the ‘Ibar for public events, and is padded with Ibn al-Khatib's letters and courtier poetry, since poetry was "an instrument of politics".

Hedged motives. Irwin offers three possible purposes and hedges each: he "perhaps intended" to illustrate how history worked (though the lessons are "not obvious"), it is "also possible" he wanted to understand his own political reverses, and it is "probably" also emulation of Ibn al-Khatib. The Ta‘rif began as a tailpiece to the ‘Ibar and became independent after the Timur encounter, according to Irwin's reading ("it seems").

Selective inheritance: al-Maqrizi. Al-Maqrizi absorbed the conviction that history matters and a pessimistic view of its course, and echoed the religious purpose of history as "warnings and exhortations" (compare ‘ibra, lessons and warnings). But Irwin says he was far more credulous, ignored ‘asabiyya and tribesmen, and moralized economic analysis: famines and high prices he blamed on corruption, sale of offices and overtaxation rather than natural causes. Irwin's judgment is that he "failed to grasp Ibn Khaldun's underlying methodology". This is a small early case of the reception theme, a follower keeping the moral and dropping the mechanism; note that Irwin himself calls Ibn Khaldun's own economics moralistic too, only less so.

Hostile Cairo. Ibn Hajar concedes fluent lecturing but calls him an al-Jahiz-like maker of plausible lies, a rager ("the spearhead") who kept Moroccan dress as a judge, forger of a fatwa, and a libertine. Al-Sakhawi recycles this, al-‘Ayni adds implausible smears (probably as a proxy attack on his rival al-Maqrizi), Ibn Taghribirdi praises his austerity and incorruptibility. Irwin sorts attackers by motive (rivalry, disappointed careers, resentment of a strict, bribe-refusing judge) and even questions the moral logic of one charge. Al-Sakhawi's narrower view of history as ancillary to hadith criticism contrasts with Ibn Khaldun's wider one.

Context of decline. Egypt under the weak sultan Faraj is a scene of mamluk infighting, locusts, famine and plague (1402-4), with Ibn Taghribirdi blaming failed inundation, lack of harmony in government and rapid turnover of officials. This is background Irwin gives, not a claim that Ibn Khaldun theorized it here. It rhymes loosely with decay-sequence and with ibn-khaldun's dynastic decline, though the passage is chronicle rather than theory.

Notable passages

The curated passages cover the reticence of the Ta‘rif, the genre point about authority-genealogies, Irwin's hedged account of why he wrote it, al-Maqrizi's partial inheritance of the method, Ibn Hajar's mixed verdict, and the wider rancour of Cairene scholarship. For World Machines the interest is mostly in reception and in what an outsider's pre-modern science of history looked like to its first readers: as literary brilliance, suspect, and only partly understood. It is not evidence for a proto-sociology, and Irwin's stance is that the later modern readings need this medieval frame. The text is thin on cyclical theory or ‘asabiyya, mentioning them only as what al-Maqrizi ignored.
