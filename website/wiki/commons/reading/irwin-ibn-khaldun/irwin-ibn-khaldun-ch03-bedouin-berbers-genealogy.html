The Old Times Were Best: Bedouin Virtue, Arab and Berber Categories, and the Uses of Genealogy

Commons · Robert Irwin · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Irwin argues that Ibn Khaldun's pessimistic cyclical theory of desert virtue and sedentary decay rests on a shared moralizing mythology of ascetic early Arab warriors rather than a newly invented sociology, then complicates his categories (Bedouin, Arab, Berber) and shows how he treated genealogy as a socially useful, partly fictional instrument of ‘asabiyya.

Irwin opens by locating the moral engine of Ibn Khaldun's cyclical theory in an inherited historical mythology: the austere, fearless Arab conquerors of the first Islamic century, whose poverty guarded their virtue until booty and luxury corrupted them. He then shows that Ibn Khaldun's key terms (badawi, ‘Arab) are slippery and context-dependent, revisits the Banu Hilal invasion as a legend later historians have undermined, surveys the Berber peoples and Ibn Khaldun's partisanship for the Zanata, and closes on genealogy (nasab), which Ibn Khaldun both used and doubted, treating lineages as fictions that serve social needs and reinforce ‘asabiyya.

Themes

Moralizing trope before sociology. Irwin's central judgment is that Ibn Khaldun's pessimistic cyclical theory owes more to a widely shared chroniclers' trope (austere golden-age Arabs contrasted with decadent present) than to a newly invented sociology: "It was, I think, this kind of moralizing, more than a newly invented sociology, which inspired Ibn Khaldun’s engine of history." This is Irwin's interpretation, offered with a hedge, and it is the opposite of the proto-sociologist reading. Poverty as guardian of virtue is placed in a wider medieval Islamic pattern via Patricia Crone, and the Qur'anic (17:16) and prophetic (buildings competing in height) background is supplied.

Badawa versus hadara, and luxury as corrosive. Ibn Khaldun holds that Bedouins are closer to goodness and that sedentary life is the last stage of civilization and the point where decay begins. Irwin finds the contrast sweeping and implausible for fourteenth-century North African townspeople (water-sellers, tanners, bathhouse stokers), notes Ibn Khaldun's ignorance of prosperous rural communities, and observes that the rural/urban line was blurred in practice. This is Irwin's critique, not Ibn Khaldun's claim. It rhymes with WMP interest in the nomad/sedentary dynamic and with civilization-undone-by-its-own-success and the decay-sequence, though Ibn Khaldun's frame is religious and moral where those are structural.

Modern echo: Penman. Irwin cites Jim Penman's Biohistory (2015) as a present-day version of the luxury-ruins-empires view, quoting Vico's sequence (crude, severe, benign, delicate, dissolute). Penman differs by stressing child-rearing, declining anxiety and loosened controls over women. Irwin reports rather than endorses this. See giambattista-vico and linear-vs-cyclical-paths.

Slippery categories. Following Rosenthal, Irwin argues that badawi need not mean nomadic; it can mean anyone outside the city, including peasants and beekeepers, and ‘umran badawi means countryside culture. Ibn Khaldun himself says agriculture is a Bedouin craft prior to sedentary life, and that Bedouins depend economically on towns. ‘Arab is ethnic (he was proud of his Hadramawt lineage) but also pejorative for nomadic invaders ("Places that succumb to the Arabs are quickly ruined"), so he is ambivalent: he deplores their destructiveness while praising their courage, austerity and mutual loyalty.

The Hilali legend. Ibn Khaldun's picture of Banu Hilal and Banu Sulaym as a "swarm of locusts" ruining Ifriqiyya, which Irwin notes has Qur'anic resonance, has been challenged: the Zirids were already declining, the Arabs first came by invitation as mercenaries, and the migration was probably a slow process of some fifty years. Irwin flags that the pejorative ‘Arab later fed French colonialist discourse (chapter 10). The point for reception history is that a partly legendary account became the template for a whole narrative of decline.

Berbers. The Berbers are the indigenous North Africans (Sanhaja, Zanata, Masmuda confederacies), whose identity rested on tribal affiliation, real or fictitious, and who were politically ascendant by the fourteenth century. The ‘Ibar seems to have been planned first as a history of the Berbers and then widened to Arabs and other peoples, working backward from current dynasties. Ibn Khaldun praised Berber virtues in the established mafakhir al-Barbar genre, blamed wealth and decadence for their decline, and was a partisan of the Zanata (the Merinids' tribe). Irwin suggests, with hedges ("possibly", "maybe"), that patronage from a Berber ruler was one motive, as was filling a gap in al-Mas‘udi.

Genealogy as socially functional fiction. Nasab was a nomadic-Arab specialty; urban solidarity rested on marriage ties. Ibn Khaldun knew lineages could be invented for political ends and reinforced ‘asabiyya, and he used a crude test (fewer than three ancestors per hundred years suggests fabrication). He mocked the Yemeni Ifriqish and Sand River story yet was inconsistent on Berber origins, and he was relaxed about claims such as Ibn Tumart's Prophetic descent and defended the Fatimid pedigree, which brought accusations of crypto-Shi‘ism (Irwin calls these unjust). Irwin adds that Ibn Khaldun's own genealogy may have been fabricated, citing the Andalusian un ending and Serjeant's failure to find the Banu Khaldun in South Arabia; this is Irwin's suggestion, hedged with "may".

Notable passages

The passage on the ascetic cameos (c1) carries Irwin's thesis that moralizing preceded sociology. The Bedouin/sedentary passage (c2) is the core statement of badawa-versus-hadara and luxury as corrosive, with its Qur'anic and hadith backing. The Banu Hilal section (c3) shows Ibn Khaldun's narrative undone by later scholarship. The slippery-terms passage (c4) shows how his concepts blur. The two genealogy passages (c5, c6) show nasab as a tool of ‘asabiyya that Ibn Khaldun both deployed and criticized. For WMP this is a pre-modern, morally framed precursor of cohesion-and-decay cycles (ibn-khaldun, secular-cycles), and of the raider/ruler dynamic in chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders, though Irwin warns against reading the theory as modern social science.
