Popular Fiction and the Regression to Polarities

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Victorian popular fiction—Karl May's adventure tales, Eugène Sue's Les Mystères de Paris, Dumas's musketeers—gratified readers by reviving the polarized, wish-fulfilling logic of the fairy tale and of infancy, a regression that Karl May's own fraudulent self-mythologizing life embodies and finally exposes.

Where the century's serious fiction increasingly worked through ambivalence and mixed motive, its popular counterpart—Karl May's adventure novels, Eugène Sue's Les Mystères de Paris, Dumas's Les Trois Mousquetaires, the love stories of the women's magazines—sold readers an escape into a world without ambiguity: heroes wholly good, villains wholly evil, and every wrong righted before the last page. Gay reads this machinery psychoanalytically, as a regression to the polarized, all-or-nothing perceptual world of infancy, and anchors the argument in the extraordinary case of Karl May, Germany's most popular storyteller, whose own biography turned out to be exactly the kind of self-serving fairy tale he sold his readers.

Themes

Fiction as compensatory daydream. Gay opens with Karl May's own 1909 diagnosis of his trade: popular fiction is a "psychological salve for those 'shoved aside' by society," a promise, delivered by the "book peddler," that happiness still exists somewhere even though "life offers them only labor, toil, and trouble." Gay generalizes May's self-serving insight into a method: the century's best-sellers are a reliable index of "the mind-set of their readers" precisely because the fantasies they gratify—identification with heroes, revenge on villains—are "partly unconscious."

The reader as collaborator, not consumer. Reading, in this account, is never passive reception but a "collusion profitable to both sides." Otto Ludwig's dictum that a book is completed only by its reader, George Grosz's confession that fiction let him float free of his "sooty" proletarian Berlin, and the case of Dickens rewriting the ending of Great Expectations at Bulwer-Lytton's urging all illustrate readers actively remaking texts to fit their own needs—sometimes literally changing the plot in mid-serialization.

The fairy tale as the genre's true architecture. Gay argues that popular Victorian fiction is fairy tale in modern dress: foreordained heroes and villains, miraculous coincidence, sadistic punishment dressed as poetic justice (the Grimms' boiling oil and pecked-out eyes; Sue's blinded murderer; Karl May's tortured and executed villain). This is not incidental borrowing but a return to the “severely polarized” perceptual world of earliest infancy—love and hate, presence and absence, male and female—before nuance or mixed motive were possible. Popular fiction, Gay writes, "restored lost omnipotence": it reawakened "primitive fantasies, long buried but never extirpated," rather than depicting the real, anxious, constrained facts of childhood.

The commercial infrastructure of regression. The chapter grounds this psychology in the material history of the feuilleton: Girardin's price-cutting La Presse (1836), the roman-feuilleton format, and Sue's serialized Les Mystères de Paris (1842–43), whose Rodolphe—omnipotent avenger, "the dreamt-of father, more divine than human"—became the template for a generation of supermen (Dumas's Monte Cristo among them). The mass newspaper industry did not simply carry these fantasies; it manufactured and monetized the readership for them, turning "the old battle of day against night" into a business proposition (Thackeray's "quack, but one of the cleverest quacks now quacking").

Karl May: the author who became his own fiction. May's biography—blindness and poverty in childhood, a violent father, petty fraud, four years in a penitentiary, then a second life as "Old Shatterhand" and "Kara Ben Nemsi"—is Gay's proof text. May did not merely write escapist fantasies; he impersonated their hero in life, buying costumes, forged titles, and a fabricated command of languages, until journalists exposed the fraud and destroyed him. May's own words—"I myself, my own ego, had to become a fairy tale"—collapse the distance between the psychological analysis of readers and of the author himself: the same regressive, omnipotence-restoring logic that gratified his audience also organized his self-presentation, with genuinely poetic-justice consequences he did not escape.

A gendered qualification. Gay notes that the love stories aimed at women readers were, for all their heroines' pluck, ultimately conservative: rebellion against prescribed gender roles was reserved for characters “who come to a bad end,” so that the genre's regressive comforts doubled as instruction in bourgeois domestic hierarchy.

Notable passages

The unit opens on May's own theory of his trade as psychic relief for the socially marginal (c1), then generalizes it via Otto Ludwig and the Dickens/Bulwer-Lytton anecdote into a theory of reading as collusion between author and reader (c2). Its psychoanalytic center is the passage tracing popular fiction's polarities back to the infant's undifferentiated, combat-structured perceptual world—fiction as a technology for "restoring lost omnipotence" rather than truthfully depicting childhood (c3). Sue's Rodolphe is offered as the purest specimen of the type: judge, rescuer, and "dreamt-of father" rolled into one, closing on Gay's thesis-line that this is finally just "the polarized vision of the world" (c4). The Karl May biography supplies the unit's most striking turn, where May's own confession that he had to become his own fairy tale (c5) is followed by Gay's verdict that reality, unlike fiction, rarely obliges with poetic justice—May's exposure and ruin were disproportionate to his offenses. The closing paragraph ties the whole argument explicitly to Freud: adult readers return to Winnetou because, in Freud's terms, "no one likes to give up a pleasure once enjoyed" (c6).

Resonances

May's promise of "a way back to the enchantment of which a cruel world had robbed them"—delivered on demand, for a subscription price, by the mass-market "book peddler"—rhymes with enchantment-as-managed-fiction: enchantment here is not a private delusion but a manufactured, purchased product, though where that concept traces enchantment changing hands between individuals, Gay's case is industrial-scale and commercial from the start (the feuilleton press, not a household).

Karl May's declaration that "I myself, my own ego, had to become a fairy tale" is a dark mirror of self-construction-through-writing: the self here is not iteratively discovered through sincere diary-keeping but iteratively fabricated through public fiction-making, a construction that collapses once print culture's fact-checkers catch up with it.

The unit's account of Sue's Les Mystères de Paris as “one of the first Victorian novels reshaped by audience responses,” and of Dickens rewriting Great Expectations at a friend’s request, sits in productive tension with print-culture-and-the-individual-author: that concept holds print culture responsible for fixing texts around a solitary, accountable author, but the serialized popular novel shows the opposite pressure at work—texts kept fluid and reader-negotiable precisely because they were commercial products aimed at satisfying a mass audience's fantasies in real time.
