Heart Religion: The Romantics' Aesthetic and Erotic Reconsecration of Faith

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
European romantics recast religion as a matter of aesthetic and often erotic feeling rather than doctrine—Chateaubriand arguing Christianity true because beautiful, Schleiermacher preaching a self-styled “Herzreligion,” and Novalis fusing eros, death, and Christ around his dead fiancée—while rehabilitating Spinoza's pantheism and waging, chiefly over the meaning of Nature, a running battle against the Enlightenment's disenchanted, law-bound cosmos.

Gay traces how the romantic generation—English, French, Dutch, Italian, and above all German—rebuilt religion as an affair of feeling and beauty rather than doctrine: Keats's “egotistical sublime” and Byron's self-absorption set the mental key; Manzoni, Bilderdijk, and Chateaubriand each mount a national defense of faith grounded in its sheer loveliness rather than its truth; the Germans (Hölderlin, Novalis, Friedrich Schlegel, Schleiermacher) push furthest, fusing religious ecstasy with erotic longing and rehabilitating Spinoza's pantheism against the Enlightenment's law-bound, disenchanted Nature; and the chapter closes on Wordsworth and Goethe as the two poles—poetic and scientific—of a Nature-worship that Mill, reading Wordsworth decades later, still felt as a “medicine” for a wounded mind.

Themes

Aesthetic subjectivity is the seedbed of romantic religion. Gay opens with Keats's diagnosis of the “Wordsworthian egotistical sublime” and Macaulay's Byron (“himself the beginning, the middle, and the end of all his own poetry”) to establish that the romantic self's absorption in itself and in beauty were “dual, intertwined” rather than separate impulses. Religion becomes simply the domain where this fusion runs deepest: “the re-enchantment of the world often began with self-enchantment before the mirror.”

The romantics framed their piety as a rescue mission against Enlightenment disenchantment. Convinced the philosophes had starved a transcendence-hungry humanity, “the commanding majority” of romantics (Shelley's and Fichte's atheism notwithstanding) set out to “undo the damage the philosophes had left behind,” even enlisting the creative imagination itself in religion's defense (Schleiermacher: faith “depends on the direction of the imagination”). This is the modernity-machine's disenchantment/legibility project read from the losing side: where the Enlightenment sought to render nature and self orderly and law-governed, romantic religion is an organized counter-current insisting the world (and the self) remain mysterious, sacred, and irreducible to law—a direct instance of enchantment-as-managed-fiction, since the re-enchantment on offer is knowingly constructed rather than naively inherited.

National variety, one underlying style: religion as sentiment and beauty. Manzoni's Catholicism is sustained less by doctrine than by the faith's “literary attraction”; Bilderdijk wages a lone Calvinist crusade in Dutch verse; Chateaubriand's Génie du Christianisme becomes an instant, women-led sensation (“not a woman in Paris slept”) precisely because it turns Christianity into “a sentimental and appealing personal matter.” Across confessions and nations, Gay finds the same move: an argument from beauty rather than proof.

Self-testimony becomes sufficient evidence of religious truth—and it is manufactured as much as felt. Chateaubriand's famous account of his conversion (his dying mother's plea, his sister's death “from the tomb”) is demonstrably retouched—the sister actually died over a year after the mother, and Chateaubriand had candidly called the book an “occasional work” intended to catch a fashionable revival of piety even before the supposed deathbed appeal. Gay's point is not hypocrisy but the romantic innovation itself: “romantic believers, unlike most of their devout ancestors, thought their personal testimony sufficient” evidence for faith's validity, not merely its impetus. This is self-construction-through-writing pressed into theological service: the self narrates itself into belief, and the narrating is inseparable from the believing.

German “heart religion” pushes subjectivity into the frankly erotic. Schleiermacher names his own creed, in a pun to Henriette Herz, “Herzreligion”—“so through and through that I have room for no other”—and describes religious ecstasy in explicitly sexual terms (“modest and tender like a virginal kiss, sacred and fruitful like a bridal embrace”; “I lie on the bosom of the infinite world”). Novalis goes further still, welding his mourning for the dead Sophie von Kühn to his Christian faith until eros, death-wish, and devotion become, in Gay's phrase, “virtual Siamese twins”—his private journal moving in the same weeks between “Love can merge into religion through absolute will” and confessions of “lasciviousness.” Friedrich Schlegel's pantheistic praise of Dorothea Veit (“her whole essence is religion, although she knows nothing of it”) belongs to the same current, made scandalous in his Lucinde. Novalis and Friedrich Schlegel are both direct actors in this thread.

Pantheism is rehabilitated from heresy into secular sainthood. Spinoza—long “the most dangerous of all seducers: the plausible atheist”—is reread by Lessing and then johann-wolfgang-von-goethe as “the thinker drunk with God”: “Existence is God,” Goethe insists, praising him as though he were “christianissimum.” baruch-spinoza's formula “God or Nature” becomes, for Schelling's Naturphilosophie and for Schleiermacher (who invokes “the spirit of holy, rejected Spinosa”), the theological ground for treating the study of Nature itself as worship.

The real battlefield is Nature—legible law versus living revelation. The Enlightenment's science, in Gay's reading, both implicated humans in Nature (as determined phenomena, like planets or trees) and separated them from it (as detached, neutral observers)—a rendering of the world as knowable, measurable, and ethically indifferent that resonates with legibility-as-machine-core. The romantics reversed the valence: Coleridge's Nature is “revelation of God”; Wordsworth's “Let Nature be your teacher” culminates in the attack on “our meddling intellect” that “murder[s] to dissect.” Goethe enacted the same reversal as a working scientist: he amassed specimens and ran his own optics experiments against Newton, yet “detested the contrivances of modern science: the telescope, the microscope, the mathematical formula,” precisely because they “interfere with the unmediated, pious experience of God-Nature.” Where the Modernity Machine's instruments are built to extend legibility, Goethe rejects the instrument for breaking the very communion it claims to serve.

Wordsworth stands as the culmination Gay is building toward. Despite becoming, late in life, one of “the most clerical of citizens,” it is his earlier, churchless nature poetry—quoted at length from Tintern Abbey—that Gay credits with setting “a standard for the adoration of Nature that no other poet… was able to surpass.” Mill's testimony, decades on in his Autobiography, that this poetry was “a medicine for my state of mind,” closes the unit on the note that will carry into the rest of the book: the naked heart seeking, and finding, its own cure in feeling.

Notable passages

The opening paragraph on the “egotistical sublime” supplies the unit's master key: romantic self-absorption and romantic re-enchantment are two faces of one move, and Gay states this almost as an aphorism (“the re-enchantment of the world often began with self-enchantment before the mirror”).

Chateaubriand's aesthetic apologetics (“not that Christianity is beautiful because it is true; it is true because it is beautiful”) is the unit's clearest statement of the argument-from-beauty that organizes the whole chapter, and Gay's surrounding irony about the book's calculated marketing sharpens rather than undercuts the claim.

Schleiermacher's description of religious ecstasy as “a seductive coupling of self-abasement and self-glorification that is literally amorous” is the unit's most explicit fusion of eroticism and piety, and gives the section its title concept (Herzreligion) its full charge.

Novalis's entanglement of Sophie von Kühn's death with Christ (“a closet drama of love and death, with a single actor on stage”) shows the same fusion at its most extreme and most autobiographically documented, drawn straight from his private journal.

The Spinoza rehabilitation passage (ending “It made Spinoza respectable for those inclined to romantic religiosity”) is load-bearing for the chapter's pantheism thread and for the direct line it draws from Lessing and Goethe to Schelling's Naturphilosophie.

Goethe's rejection of “the telescope, the microscope, the mathematical formula” as “repellent instruments of torture” crystallizes the unit's central quarrel between Enlightenment legibility and romantic re-enchantment in a single, vivid image.
