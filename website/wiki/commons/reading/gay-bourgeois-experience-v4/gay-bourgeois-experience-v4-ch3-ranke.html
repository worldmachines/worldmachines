Ranke and the Historicist Cult of Objectivity

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Leopold von Ranke's cult of archival 'objectivity' and self-effacing empathy for the past made him the century's most revered historian even as his conservatism, state-worship, and undogmatic Lutheran faith shaped what he claimed was a value-free science of history.

This unit is Peter Gay's portrait of Leopold von Ranke as the nineteenth century's paradigmatic professional historian: the most decorated, most imitated, most publicly celebrated practitioner of a craft he did more than anyone to invent. Gay traces the paradox at Ranke's core — a devout, politically conservative Prussian Bürger who built his immense authority on the claim to have banished himself, his politics, and his feelings from his books, while his letters and diaries reveal a man whose historical vocation was inseparable from an undogmatic but intense Lutheran faith, an emotionally charged love affair with the archive, and a theory of the state that flattered German nationalism.

Themes

"How it really was" as a disclaimer, not a claim. Ranke's historicist creed — that every epoch is "immediate to God" and that the historian's task is only to say "how it had really been" — sounds like a claim to scientific neutrality but functions, in Gay's reading, as a disclaimer: Ranke proposed to enter the past "humbly, hat in hand," stripping away present interests to let vanished ages speak for themselves. This is the founding slogan of the historicist school, and it depends on a specific act of empathetic self-erasure that Ranke himself named Selbstauslöschung — self-obliteration. The method closely rhymes with einfuhlung, Herder's concept of imaginative empathy entering "the centre of gravity" of another age rather than judging it by present standards; Ranke absorbs "the feelings, the values, the beliefs of distant ages" much as Einfühlung prescribes, though Ranke frames the discipline as a religious calling (a "secular priest['s]" duty) rather than Herder's more secular hermeneutics. Möser's earlier historicist pluralism — the claim that each age and locality has its own irreducible Zeitstil that must be understood on its own terms — is the same principle in miniature; Ranke universalizes it into a professional method (justus-moser).

Objectivity as an achieved public image, not a private fact. Lord Acton's tribute that Ranke taught history "to be critical, to be colourless, and to be new," praising him for repressing "the poet, the patriot, the religious or political partisan," captures how contemporaries received the Rankean ideal. But Gay is careful to show the gap between this public image and the man: Ranke was "no ordinary bourgeois" at home with kings, a German conservative anxious about "the socialist menace," and — by Acton's own later charge — "ignorant of the power of religious sentiments" outside his own tradition, unable to enter the religious mind of the Middle Ages. Objectivity, in other words, was a disciplined performance with its own blind spots, not the absence of a standpoint.

The archive as the instrument of "pure perception." Ranke's confidence that documents, not "secondary writers," yield "the most pristine communications" and "pure perception" of the past turns the archive into a technology for rendering history legible — a project that resonates, with real qualification, with legibility-as-machine-core: where the Modernity Machine renders the present world readable and governable, Ranke's archival programme applies the same faith in exhaustive documentation to render the past transparent and certain. His letters show this was no dry epistemology but a "consuming gluttony of the mind": he wrote of an untouched archive as a virgin he longed to court, calling himself "the Columbus of the relazioni." Gay reads this vocabulary of discovery and conquest, alongside his poems, letters, and diary entries ("I still hope for a life that is childlike... secure, active, without doubt"; his 1877 diary note on why "a historian must grow old"), as exactly the kind of self-revealing document that constructs rather than merely records a self — an instance of self-construction-through-writing.

Re-enchantment folded into a science. Ranke's historicism was, Gay argues, "Lutheranism by other means": he found God not in special providences but in "the rise of empires, the clashes of nations, the careers of great men," seeing history itself as "a holy hieroglyphic." This theological undertow sits oddly beside his reputation as founder of a value-free discipline, and it also freights his political theory: he modeled great states as individual persons, "thoughts of God," locked in productive conflict — a doctrine patriots readily used, in Gay's account, "as an agent for German self-satisfaction." The arc from romantic inward faith to state-worshipping nationalism half-rhymes with romanticism-and-political-danger's account of Romantic self-assertion migrating from the individual to the collective, though Ranke's route runs through theology and the state rather than aesthetics.

A social psychology without a discipline of psychology. The unit closes by placing Ranke within the century's uneven, largely improvised historical psychology: unlike the positivists (Buckle, Taine, the young Henry Adams) who sought historical "laws" on the model of natural science, Ranke treated individuals as "in large measure social products," trees drawing strength "from air and light, wind and weather" rather than their own soil — great men could only make history when "in tune with the drift of his age." It was, Gay notes, a serious advance on Carlyle's great-man history even though Ranke never systematized it, and it was nearly all historians had until the twentieth century.

Notable passages

The chapter's hinge is Ranke's own formulation of historicism — "Every epoch is immediate to God" paired with the pledge to say "only how it had really been" — which Gay glosses as the supreme slogan of the school Ranke founded. Around it cluster the passage on Selbstauslöschung (empathetic self-obliteration as the price of entering the past), Acton's "colourless" eulogy (objectivity as public reputation), the 1820 letter finding God "manifest in all history" (the theological substrate of his "science"), the 1836 letter to his brother turning a newly opened archive into an unseen beloved ("I am longing for the moment when I gain admittance to her"), and the closing metaphor of persons as trees fed by their historical surroundings (his unsystematic but genuine social psychology).
