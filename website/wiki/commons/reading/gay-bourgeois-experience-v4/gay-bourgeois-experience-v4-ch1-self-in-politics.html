The Self in Politics

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Across France, Germany, and England, the romantic generation processed revolution, censorship, and reform through the filter of private feeling, converting public events into autobiography and, in Friedrich Schlegel's and William Wordsworth's cases, into an explicit poetics of the self.

This section of Gay's chapter on romantic re-enchantment traces how the first romantic generation, radicalized by the French Revolution, mostly abandoned or aestheticized political engagement, and argues that even where romantics stayed politically active — above all in France — they metabolized public events into private feeling and autobiography rather than analysis. Gay works comparatively across France (the most political romantics), the German states (the most withdrawn), and England (a spectrum from Coleridge's retreat to Byron's and Shelley's activism), using Hazlitt's 1818 diagnosis of romantic "egotism" as a hinge and Friedrich Schlegel's career as the fullest case study of an explicitly antipolitical politics.

Themes

Feeling substituted for analysis, even amid real engagement. Gay's opening claim is that romantic politics was "drowned in self-regarding passions": "their response to events proved of greater moment to them than the events themselves," so that the Revolution, Napoleon, and repressive ordinances "meant less to them than how they felt—felt, rather than thought—about them." This is not simple withdrawal — Gay is careful that "all politics... harbors an expressive dimension" — but a claim about degree and emphasis distinctive to the romantics.

National divergence: France politically saturated, Germany politically barren, England split. The French romantics (Hugo, Chateaubriand, Musset, Constant, Stendhal, Lamartine) had parties, a press, and a revolution to react to, and were, in Gay's words, "the most political romantics of all," writing "their autobiographies on the barricades." Yet even their politics "never lost a certain literary flavor." German romantics had no comparable openings — three hundred small absolutist states with no legal opposition — and largely made a virtue of retreat, per Friedrich Schiller's and Friedrich Schlegel's turns to "the holy quiet domains of the heart." English romantics ranged between these poles: Coleridge ended in Anglican and Tory quietism, Wordsworth subordinated a genuine early radicalism to autobiography and later reaction, while Byron and (per Gay) Shelley are offered as counterexamples to the egotism thesis.

Friedrich Schlegel's antipolitical politics. Gay's central case study is Friedrich Schlegel, who moved from youthful sympathy with the Revolution to a career as a publicist for Metternich while insisting he stood "far removed from... all present-day parties." His 1800 fragment — "Do not squander faith and love in the political world, but sacrifice your innermost to the divine world of science and art" — and his mature theory of the state as guardian of "eternal and essential" institutions (church, family, guild) amount, Gay argues, to a politics that is really religion in disguise: "Unalterably opposed to making a religion of politics, Schlegel made a politics of religion." friedrich-schiller supplies the same move in verse: his 1801 poem answers the question of where freedom can be found in a warring world by fleeing to the "holy quiet domains of the heart."

Autobiography as the vehicle: Wordsworth's Prelude. Gay treats Wordsworth's political evolution — youthful radicalism during the 1790 and 1792 visits to France, disillusionment after the Terror and the invasion of Switzerland, eventual reactionary hostility to the Great Reform Act — as inseparable from his choice to narrate it as an "autobiographical poem" tracing "the growth of a poet's mind." The famous line "Bliss was it in that dawn to be alive, / But to be young was very Heaven" is glossed by Gay as exemplary of "the romantic self in politics": political memory reprocessed as a register of feeling. This is a clean instance of self-construction-through-writing — Wordsworth does not merely report a self that had opinions about the Revolution; the self is built, retrospectively and revisably, in the act of narrating those opinions.

The egotistical sublime. Hazlitt's 1818 lecture, quoted at length, credits the French Revolution with begetting a whole poetic style: Wordsworth's school "had its origin in the French revolution," and produced poets who see "nothing but himself and the universe," whose "egotism is in some respects a madness." Gay uses this both as period diagnosis and as a hinge to complicate it — noting that Hazlitt's sketch fits Wordsworth and Coleridge far better than Byron or Shelley, and that Keats's rejection of the "wordsworthian or egotistical sublime" (seeking instead a poetical impersonality "that has no self") shows romantics themselves contesting the type.

WMP resonance. The chapter's throughline — public history absorbed and reissued as private, self-narrating feeling — rhymes with the modernity-machine's association (per the WMP context pack) with the rise of the individuated, self-narrating subject, though the wiki page itself foregrounds legibility and institutional class relations rather than literary self-fashioning, so the fit is at the level of a shared historical moment rather than a shared mechanism. Schlegel's case gives a partial, inverted echo of romanticism-and-political-danger: where Berlin's account (per that page) traces Romantic self-expression migrating outward into collective nationalist self-assertion, Schlegel instead licenses political withdrawal and quietism that nonetheless serves an authoritarian, Metternichian order — self-cultivation as an alibi for reaction rather than as its engine. Both jean-jacques-rousseau (credited by Gay as the source of the French romantics' conviction that "everything is at bottom linked to politics") and johann-wolfgang-von-goethe (a faithful reader of the liberal Parisian daily Le Globe) appear as reference points rather than sustained subjects of this unit.

Notable passages

The opening paragraph sets the unit's thesis: the romantics "played passionately at radical politics" but "most of them sooner or later—the Germans sooner—defected," preferring "a higher inwardness" to Victor Hugo's assertive claim that "every literary work... is an act." A tighter formulation follows a few paragraphs later, naming the mechanism directly: the "romantic cult of the self" led romantics to transform "revolutions, wars, censorship, economic misery, social legislation into so much autobiography."

Hazlitt's lecture supplies the unit's sharpest character sketch of the type: a poet who "tolerates only what he himself creates" and whose egotism verges on madness — a passage Gay treats as illuminating but only partially generalizable across the romantics he surveys.

The Schiller/Schlegel passage is the unit's fullest statement of the German pattern: political despair resolved not through action but through flight to the "holy quiet domains of the heart," and Schlegel's fragment urging sacrifice of "faith and love in the political world" to art and science instead.

The Wordsworth passage built around "Bliss was it in that dawn to be alive" is the unit's most explicit naming of its own theme, closing with Gay's own gloss: an "instructive comment on the romantic self in politics."

The closing paragraph ties the unit together by way of Keats's rejection of the "wordsworthian or egotistical sublime" and Gay's final claim that what distinguished the romantics from earlier politically engaged thinkers, "from Plato to Voltaire," was not idealism or indignation as such but "the sustained cultivation of the responsive self in the public as much as in the private domain."
