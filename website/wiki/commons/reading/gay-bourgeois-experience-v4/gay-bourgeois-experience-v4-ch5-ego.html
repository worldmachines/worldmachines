Images of the Mind: Self-Portraiture and Landscape as Confession

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Opening Chapter Five, Gay argues that the nineteenth-century's explosion of self-portraiture — epitomized by Lovis Corinth's 1913 "Ego" — and its parallel elevation of landscape painting into religious autobiography (above all Caspar David Friedrich's) made the visual arts a second confessional medium alongside the memoir and the novel, disclosing the bourgeois self even as its cheerful public surfaces worked to conceal it.

Gay opens Chapter Five of The Naked Heart by turning from writing to painting: if the Victorian bourgeoisie's "Autobiographical times" (Carlyle's phrase) produced diaries, memoirs, and confessional novels, they also produced an unprecedented boom in self-portraiture and a new, quasi-religious ambition for landscape painting. Section 1, "Ego," is anchored by Lovis Corinth's 1913 self-portrait from St. Ulrich — a jaunty tourist snapshot undercut by a glowering, fearful face and the single inscribed word "Ego" — and then widens into a five-hundred-year history of the self-portrait genre (Dürer, Rembrandt, Reynolds, Graff) before showing nineteenth-century painters (Courbet, Friedrich, van Gogh, Menn) turning the whole of their oeuvre, not just formal self-portraits, into claimed fragments of autobiography. The section closes by extending this confessional ambition to landscape painting itself, where Constable, Corot, Thomas Cole, the Barbizon painters, and especially Friedrich sought, through fidelity to or communion with Nature, a second vehicle for the same self-revelation — a project the respectable art-buying public was slow, and sometimes decades late, to accept.

Themes

Self-portraiture as bourgeois confession. Gay treats the nineteenth century's acceleration of an old genre (self-portraits had accumulated in the Uffizi since Cosimo de' Medici III) as qualitatively new: painters no longer merely recorded their likeness but claimed, as Courbet told his patron, to have "written my autobiography" in paint. Goya's biographer Carderera and Caspar David Friedrich both generalized the claim to all of a painter's output — "every picture," in Friedrich's words, is "more or less a character study of the one who painted it." This is the same impulse Gay traces elsewhere in diary-keeping and confessional autobiography, now transposed onto canvas.

A legible surface hiding an illegible depth. Corinth's 1913 self-portrait is Gay's paradigm case of concealment coexisting with disclosure: the "mechanical cheerfulness of his tourist's attire" and hunter's costume are a "protective garb" for "patent misery,” with only the eyes and the laconic "Ego" betraying the depression and post-stroke anguish beneath. Gay is explicit that Corinth spent much of his career feeding a public misreading of himself as a man who "easily, glibly affirms life," hiding "his suffering soul in his wrestler's body," and only occasionally abandoning disguise to expose "the pain in his naked heart" — the volume's title phrase, applied here to a painted rather than a written confession. This is a private analogue to what WMP calls Legibility: the self is made partially readable to its viewers even as the sitter manages what remains withheld, revelation and reserve operating in the same image.

Confession within a shared, rule-bound vocabulary, not raw transparency. Gay is careful that self-portraits are not unmediated windows into an interior: they are "acts of pictorial speech" addressed to an audience, "play with appearances... that obeys rules and employs a vocabulary... others have made." Even Gauguin's self-styled outsider individuality "was still working on the shadow side of the culture" he claimed to reject. This resonates with self-construction-through-writing's claim that the self is not a prior entity merely expressed but iteratively constructed through the act of making — here through paint and pose rather than prose, and constrained by an inherited genre-grammar in a way the writing case does not always foreground.

Landscape as a second, and religious, self-revelation. The section's final movement generalizes self-portraiture into landscape: Constable's "Painting is another word for feeling," Corot's decades-long "unremitting quest for fidelity to nature," and Thomas Cole's ambition for a "higher style of landscape" that could "impress a sentiment or enforce a truth." Friedrich is Gay's central and most extreme case — "metaphysician with the brush,” who held that "every picture is essentially a self-portrait" and who felt his "self melting into his work," reading "hieroglyphics of omnipotent Nature" in his own canvases and calling art an "intercessor between Nature and humans." This is close to Naturphilosophie's vision of Nature as a living, self-organizing whole rather than inert mechanism, redeployed here as a painter's private theology; it is also a concrete instance of the Modernity-era modernity-machine motif of the individuated, self-narrating subject, since Friedrich's landscapes function as autobiography precisely by displacing confession onto scenery. Gay underscores the cost of this ambition: Friedrich was dismissed and warehoused by museums for six decades after his 1840 death, rediscovered only after 1900, and the Barbizon painters spent "decades in penury" before the bourgeois public caught up to what they were offering.

Notable passages

The Corinth passages (a full account of the 1913 "Ego" portrait, then the retrospective on his post-stroke self-portraits) carry the section's central image of concealment-within-disclosure. The Carderera/Friedrich passage supplies the era's own theoretical claim that a painting is a self-portrait regardless of genre. The passage on self-portraiture's "cultural dimension" is Gay's methodological caution against reading these images as unmediated confession. The final Friedrich passage, on Nature's "hieroglyphics" and art as "intercessor,” gives the fullest statement of landscape-as-religious-autobiography that closes the section.
