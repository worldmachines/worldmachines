Re-enchantment and the Unleashed Imagination

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Introducing Peter Gay's romantics as a fractious, undefinable ‘mood’ united mainly by an inward turn, the unit traces their shared claim that Enlightenment rationalism and associationist psychology had disenchanted the world, and their answering claim for a self-authorizing, quasi-divine creative imagination advanced by the Schlegels, Coleridge, and Shelley as the instrument of re-enchantment.

Peter Gay opens The Naked Heart by introducing the romantics as the century's most influential shapers of the bourgeois self, despite their inability to agree on almost anything: a scattered, mutually contemptuous “mood rather than a movement” bound together mainly by a shared turn to the interior and a shared “historic mission to re-enchant the world” against Enlightenment “bloodless scientism.” The unit's substantive first section, “1. The Imagination Unleashed,” locates the fight in a single faculty: the romantics built their case for a self-authorizing, quasi-divine creative imagination against the Enlightenment's own mechanistic psychology of sense-impression and association, drawing (often silently) on Kant, Schelling, and each other even as they denounced the philosophes who had, in fact, anticipated much of what they claimed to discover.

Themes

Romanticism is a mood, not a movement, defined mainly by a turn inward. Gay catalogs the romantics' near-total disagreement with one another — kierkegaard's 1836 protest that romanticism “implies overflowing all boundaries,” Delécluze's inability to “extract a basic idea from all this chaos,” the mutual contempt among Keats, Byron, Wordsworth, and Coleridge — before locating the one thread that holds despite the chaos: an emphatic turn to the self. Novalis's charge to “romanticize” the world by identifying “the lower self… with a better self,” and Hegel's clinical gloss of “the romantic” as “absolute inwardness,” stand in as the closest thing to a definition the unit offers. Gay is careful to note this inward self was not the romantics' invention: “that had been in the making at least since michel-de-montaigne.”

Re-enchantment is explicitly posed as an answer to Enlightenment disenchantment. The romantics cast the philosophes — Voltaire, Hume, Holbach — as vandals whose “bloodless scientism” had “damaged the inner life almost beyond repair,” and set themselves the “historic mission to re-enchant the world.” Gay flags the romantics' polemical strawman of the Enlightenment (unbounded faith in reason, blind to passion, indifferent to history) as itself unjust — “no less unjust to the philosophes than the philosophes had been to the Christians” — even as it named something real about mid-century secularization.

The imagination is the battleground because the Enlightenment had built its own, mechanistic account of mind. David Hartley's associationism (1749) explained thought as sense-impressions mechanically linked to one another — a “Newton of the mind” project that let the philosophes dispense with “supernatural agencies” in the psyche and treat imagination as a derivative, combinatorial faculty (a centaur is just borrowed images recombined). Enlightenment progress-narratives (Trublet 1754, Gérando 1800) predicted the imagination would recede as reason matured, a scheme continued into the romantic era itself by anti-romantic writers like Macaulay and Peacock. Romantics from Wordsworth and Kant (an unlikely ally) to Blake and Coleridge insisted instead that imagination is irreducible, “godlike,” even “promethean” — the self, on this view, does not passively register the world but makes it, a claim that “trembled on the edge of a religious view of humanity.”

Romanticism resists both periodization and a clean opposition to classicism. The unit catalogs failed attempts to systematize the romantics — by nationality, by chronology (1798 in England and Germany versus the 1820s–30s in France), and by the romantic/classical antithesis popularized by johann-wolfgang-von-goethe and August Wilhelm Schlegel, though Goethe himself, who once defined “the romantic as sick and the classical as healthy,” nonetheless “wrote like a romantic.” Wordsworth's denunciation of the “paradoxical reveries of” jean-jacques-rousseau and Shelley's later praise of the “exertions of Locke, Hume, Gibbon, Voltaire, Rousseau” show the romantics could not even agree about the Enlightenment they claimed to be rejecting.

The Schlegels theorized the imagination more than they practiced it, and imported theory into life. Friedrich Schlegel and his brother August Wilhelm, editors of the Athenäum, gave the German romantic imagination its rationale — restless and creative, but (“pensive”) disciplined by reflection and craft rather than raw spontaneity. Friedrich's one novel, Lucinde (1799), converted his real affair with Dorothea Veit into “a manifesto in the shape of a novel,” a vivid instance of self-construction-through-writing: the self here is not reported by fiction but built through it.

The stakes were quietly political and secularizing. Coleridge, borrowing heavily and often silently from Schelling and A. W. Schlegel, made the “primary IMAGINATION… a repetition in the finite mind of the eternal act of creation in the infinite I Am” and cast himself among the “rare explorers” who “propose their own nature as a problem.” Shelley's Defence of Poetry pushed the logic furthest: poets are the true “institutors of laws” and “founders of civil society,” a claim that quietly replaces the priest (or saint) with the poet as custodian of value, and — because genius, not birth, is what counts — reads as a “democratic move” akin to Napoleon's careers-open-to-talent, even while remaining aristocratic in its vision of the gifted few. This resonates, loosely, with the modernity-machine's drive toward legibility: Hartley's associationism is essentially an attempt to render the mind lawful and legible, and the romantic imagination is mounted as a deliberate refusal of that legibility, a jurisdiction the mind reserves for itself. The fit is partial — Gay's romantics are resisting a psychological-scientific legibility, not a bureaucratic or state one, and their “re-enchantment” is pursued through poetry and criticism rather than institution-building.

A resonance and its limit. Shelley's move to ground authority in the poet's inner genius, rather than birth or office, is one version of the “creative self generating its own ends” that romanticism-and-political-danger traces migrating toward collective nationalist self-assertion. Nothing in this unit points that direction — Gay's Shelley moves toward individualist, democratic secularization, not collective identity — but the underlying move (the self as the final court of value, displacing inherited religious and social authority) is the same generative step that account is tracking toward its later, darker uses.

Notable passages

“The prophets, poets, and propagandists…” opens the unit by naming re-enchantment as the romantics' “historic mission,” framed explicitly as an answer to Enlightenment “bloodless scientism” — the clearest single-sentence statement of the chapter's argument.

Novalis's charge to “romanticize” the world, and Hegel's gloss of the romantic as “absolute inwardness,” are the two formulas Gay substitutes for a definition, given that romanticism resists any single formula.

The Hartley associationism passage shows the Enlightenment building its own “Newton of the mind” — a lawful, sense-impression-based psychology that made supernatural explanation, and with it any special status for imagination, dispensable. This is precisely what the romantics are arguing against.

Schlegel's Lucinde literalizes the self-through-writing theme: a real love affair, with Dorothea Veit, reworked as “a manifesto in the shape of a novel.”

Coleridge's account of “the mind's self-experience in the act of thinking” and his self-identification with the “rare explorers” who “propose their own nature as a problem” is Gay's most direct bridge from romanticism to the Freudian self-analysis invoked at the chapter's opening.

Shelley's Defence of Poetry conclusion closes the unit with the political payload: poetic genius, not birth, becomes the ground of authority — “a plea for the restitution of Innerlichkeit to a world from which magic had fled.”
