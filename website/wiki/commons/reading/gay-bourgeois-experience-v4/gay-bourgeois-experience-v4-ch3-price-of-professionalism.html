The Price of Professionalism

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Nineteenth-century historians professionalized their craft into an academic science — new chairs, journals, and congresses — and gained expertise and self-confidence precisely as they lost the broad bourgeois readership that literary, biography-entwined history had once commanded, a trade traced through the shift from confiding first-person narration to clinical, statistics-led prose and through the double-edged psychological tool of "identification" that bound historians to their subjects.

Gay narrates how nineteenth-century history-writing turned itself into an academic profession — university chairs, learned journals (Historische Zeitschrift, Revue historique, English Historical Review, American Historical Review), historical associations and congresses — and argues that the trade was real: rising scholarly rigor and self-confidence bought with a shrinking bourgeois readership and waning cultural authority. He dramatizes the cost in the 1903 quarrel between J. B. Bury, who declared history "herself simply a science, no less and no more," and G. M. Trevelyan, who defended Clio as a muse; in the century's drift in authorial voice from Macaulay's confiding "I propose to write" to Henry Adams's opening census statistic; and, most originally, in "identification" — the historian's affective merger with the dead — which he reads as the ambivalent psychological engine that had made history readable and alive in the first place, for better (insight) and worse (partisan aggression dressed as objectivity).

Themes

Professionalization was a trade, not a pure gain. Sales figures anchor the paradox: Macaulay's History of England sold some 300,000 copies by the mid-1870s and J. R. Green's Short History found "hundreds of thousands of readers," but Henry Adams, writing scrupulous multi-volume history a generation later, sold barely three thousand sets in a decade. As historians organized into a discipline with "exacting standards," critics — some from within the guild — accused them of "willfully whittling down their audiences with tedious monographs and unreadable syntheses." Gay's summary line is the unit's thesis: "precisely when the historians' professional self-awareness and self-confidence rose, their influence seemed to be waning."

Biography and history, long fused, pulled apart under specialization. Carlyle ("History is the essence of innumerable Biographies") and Emerson spoke for an early-century consensus that treated history as biography writ large; Ranke, Motley, and Michelet all wrote history through outsized personalities. Tocqueville and Marx, and later professionalizing historians, shifted weight onto impersonal forces — institutions, classes, economic structure — a shift that, in Gay's account, left biography in possession of the popular market history had ceded.

The science-versus-literature quarrel became open, personal combat. Bury's 1903 Cambridge inaugural insisted history was a science whose literary qualities were merely "pleasing dress"; Trevelyan, related by blood to two historians famous for literary touch, took the rebuke personally and spent a decade sharpening a public reply defending history as an art whose readers deserved "felicitousness" rather than "dry-as-dust footnotes." Even Ranke and Burckhardt, who insisted science and style were reconcilable, produced a masterpiece (Burckhardt's Kultur der Renaissance in Italien) that sold 200 copies in its first year — evidence, Gay suggests, that the reconciliation was harder to live than to proclaim.

Authorial voice itself enacted the century's reveal/conceal shift. Macaulay could still open his History of England in 1848 with a confiding first-person promise; forty years later Henry Adams opened his history of the Jefferson and Madison administrations with a bare population count, no "I" anywhere. Gay frames this as a move "from emotional self-display to clinical self-concealment" — the same defended, managed boundary between revealing and hiding the self that runs through the volume's account of Victorian diaries and confession, now enacted at the level of a discipline's prose style rather than a private diarist's page.

Identification was the profession's central, ambivalent technique. Gay defines it as a mental act ranging from ordinary empathy to "total merger with the other," citing Fustel de Coulanges's disclaimer that "it is history that is speaking through me," Michelet's self-dissolving dialogues with "the Spirit of the Revolution," and Macaulay's and Motley's readiness to feel themselves walking seventeenth-century streets. He treats it as genuinely double-edged: the same intimacy that delivered "prized insights" into past minds also let Motley indulge, and disguise as scholarship, his moralized hatred of Philip II — identification serving "aggression no less than insight."

World Machines resonances (partial, worth naming). The unit's roll call of new professorships, journals founded in quick succession (1859, 1876, 1886, 1895), and international congresses reads like a history-machine assembling in real time — independently developing institutional components locking together into a new "scientific history" paradigm without any single revolutionary act, displacing the older literary-biographical mode by accretion rather than argument. The shift from Macaulay's confiding narration to Adams's census-opening, and Bury's insistence that history is "simply a science," is a local, textual instance of Legibility as the modernity-machine understands it: prose remaking itself to be verifiable, footnoted, and impersonal rather than persuasive and self-revealing — though what is being made legible here is a discipline's own authority claim, not a population or territory, so the fit is partial. Identification, finally, rhymes with self-construction-through-writing: the historian does not simply express a prior self in prose but is built, and at the limit unmade, by the act of writing toward "merger with the other" (Michelet becoming "the Spirit of the Revolution" is the extreme case) — though the self under construction here is a professional, disciplinary persona rather than the purely personal self that page's diarists construct.

Notable passages

  • The opening frame names the unit's stakes directly: unlike biographers, historians "faced far greater tensions, with others and in themselves" as they wrote, in Gay's phrase, "the autobiography of their culture."
  • The professionalization paradox is stated as flatly as Gay ever states a thesis: rising scientific rigor accompanied by waning influence, "the paradoxical position that historians had created for themselves in bourgeois culture."
  • The institutional roll call — Sorbonne chairs, Oxford and Cambridge Regius professorships, the founding of four national historical journals between 1859 and 1895, and the congresses that followed — shows the discipline's infrastructure assembling piece by piece, with only "a few Cassandras" warning it meant "the victory not of science but of pedantry."
  • The Macaulay-to-Adams contrast ("I propose to write the history of England..." versus "According to the census of 1800, the United States of America contained 5,308,483 persons") is Gay's most compact illustration of the shift from self-display to self-concealment in historical prose.
  • Bury's inaugural lecture — "History...is not a branch of literature" and, of Mommsen, that his true greatness lay not in his prose but in the Corpus and the Staatsrecht — stages the science side of the quarrel in its most combative form.
  • The definition of identification, moving from ordinary empathy to "total merger with the other," is the passage that supplies the unit's psychological core and sets up the Fustel, Michelet, and Motley examples that follow.
