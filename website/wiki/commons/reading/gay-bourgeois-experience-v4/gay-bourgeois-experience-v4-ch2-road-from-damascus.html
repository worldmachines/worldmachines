The Road from Damascus: autobiography as the genre of religious crisis

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 26 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Peter Gay reads nineteenth-century autobiographies of religious crisis — Darwin, Jouffroy, Renan, Edmund Gosse's Father and Son — as the era's central genre for working out, in public and in private, the bourgeois struggle between inherited faith and modern unbelief.

This unit (Gay's section "3. The Road from Damascus") argues that nineteenth-century bourgeois autobiography became the privileged vehicle for processing an unresolved civilizational contest between faith and unbelief. Gay surveys a string of exemplary autobiographers — Charles Darwin, the obscure Jewish memoirists Jacob Epstein and Clara Geissmar, the French philosopher Théodore Jouffroy, Ernest Renan, and above all Edmund Gosse in Father and Son — to show that the "road from Damascus" (losing faith) was, for the Victorian bourgeois, exactly as crowded and as narratable as the road to it. Confession of doubt or belief becomes a structural feature of the genre itself, not an incidental theme.

Themes

Secularization was a contested, unfinished war, not a settled Enlightenment victory. Gay opens by insisting that the "warfare of science with theology" (Andrew Dickson White's popular phrase) was real as felt experience but false as a narrative of clean triumph: Darwin's age was also Cardinal Manning's and papal infallibility's age, and "renascent mysticism" (Durkheim's phrase) coexisted with materialist assault. This sets up autobiography as the terrain where the outcome of that war was fought out person by person, never resolved at the civilizational scale.

Publication imposed discretion; the manuscript intended for the drawer told the truth. Gay makes an explicit publicity/privacy distinction: religious convictions were "virtually public property" for the Victorian bourgeois embedded in family and neighborhood, which meant frank statements of doubt were rarer in autobiographies written for an audience than in those meant to stay private. Darwin's reticence about religion is explained this way — not a lack of conviction but a calculated concealment to protect his devout wife and avoid controversy. The genre's frankness is thus gated by an audience the writer must manage.

Loss of faith takes two temperaments: the slow drift and the traumatic rupture. Darwin exemplifies the first — disbelief that "crept over me at a very slow rate" and caused him "no distress" — arriving at a tentative, unsettled theism late in life without ever staging a crisis. Jouffroy is the opposite case: a single terrifying night of self-analysis during which he "dredged his unconscious to bring his conflict to the surface," experiencing the loss of faith as a violent, involuntary "melancholy revolution" that happened beneath the "daylight of his consciousness." Renan supplies a third pattern — a decades-long, scholarly, philologically driven disillusionment (his mastery of Hebrew and German higher criticism convincing him that a single textual error would unmask the Bible as a merely human document) that never resolved into either orthodoxy or full scientific naturalism, leaving him, in his own words, "a tissue of contradictions."

Losing the old faith did not kill the need for one. Gay stresses that Jouffroy, Renan, and the era's other honest seekers rejected both supernatural religion and cold materialism, turning instead toward "moral romanticism," spiritualism, or aestheticized natural religion — Renan's ecstatic prayer to the "abyss" atop the Acropolis is offered as the paradigm case. This is presented as a continuation of the romantics' project of re-enchantment, carried, in Renan's case, decades past romanticism's official death.

Father and Son turns religious crisis into a family drama that anticipates Freud. Gay's extended treatment of Edmund Gosse's memoir reframes the son's rebellion against his father's Plymouth Brethren fundamentalism as inseparable from an unresolved triangle with his dead mother, whose will "guided" the father even after her death. The pivotal moment — young Gosse's discovery that his father was fallibly ignorant of a trivial fact — is described as the birth of an inner "companion and a confidant": himself. Gay closes the unit by naming this pattern explicitly Freudian, tying the whole century's crop of religious-crisis autobiographies to the emerging theory of the Oedipus complex: autobiographers, he concludes, are always writing about the family triangle that made them, "sons—and daughters—longing to discover who they were."

Notable passages

The opening paragraph frames the whole unit: an age in which no autobiographer could sidestep "the issue of religion—his religion," because secularism, far from secure, coexisted uneasily with resurgent orthodoxy. The passage on publication versus manuscript candor makes explicit the pressure that shaped what could be said and to whom — a distinction that resonates with legibility-as-machine-core read in reverse: here the bourgeois self manages its own illegibility to a public that has a stake in its beliefs, reserving full disclosure for the private page, which is itself a mode of self-construction-through-writing. Jouffroy's account of his "December evening" — the felt sensation of an "involuntary labor" tearing away the veil over his own unbelief, followed by dread of "the unknown void" — reads as a lay instance of the structure kierkegaard gives to anxiety: freedom's vertigo experienced as a night that remakes the self, though Jouffroy's crisis resolves into a substitute creed rather than Kierkegaard's sustained existential category. Renan's passage on the Bible's internal contradictions ties religious crisis to the disenchanting machinery of historical-philological method itself. Gosse's discovery of an inner "companion and a confidant" is the unit's clearest instance of the nineteenth century's self-narrating, individuated subject coming into being through the act of noticing and narrating itself — a small-scale instance of the turn the modernity-machine is built on, though Gay keeps the frame psychological and familial rather than institutional. The closing paragraph's invocation of the Oedipus complex is the unit's own bridge from these individual crises to Freud, whom Gay treats, across this volume, as the presiding intelligence of the bourgeois turn inward — the same turn that makes world-machines's account of re-enchantment as a response to disenchantment recognizable in this material, even though Gay's own frame stays psychological rather than civilizational.
