Lords of Dalmatia

Commons · City of Fortune: How Venice Ruled the Seas · updated 24 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Roger Crowley traces Venice's ascent from a marginal, self-governing lagoon settlement into the acknowledged master of the Adriatic, anchored by Doge Pietro Orseolo II's year-1000 campaign against Narentine pirates and by Venice's slow climb within the Byzantine trading system from subject to indispensable-but-resented monopolist, sealed by the Golden Bull of 1082.

This opening chapter of City of Fortune establishes Venice's founding geography and political character before narrating its first great strategic act: Doge Pietro Orseolo II's year-1000 expedition down the Dalmatian coast to break the Narentine pirates, an act that inaugurated four centuries of undisputed Venetian command of the Adriatic. The chapter then widens its lens to Venice's long, asymmetric relationship with Byzantium — client, then partner, then, after the Golden Bull of 1082 granted duty-free trade throughout the empire, a jealously resented commercial power — ending on the eve of the Fourth Crusade in 1198, with Venetian–Byzantine trust exhausted.

Themes

Geography as constitution. Crowley opens not with politics but with hydrology: the Adriatic's asymmetric coasts, the Po's silt advancing the delta, the extinction of Adria and Aquileia and the survival of Venice precisely because it was unreachable. The lagoon is not backdrop but the mechanism that produces everything else about Venice — its lack of feudalism, its mercantile nobility, its improvised, hydroponic city grown "conjured out of marsh." This reads close to a world-machines logic: the lagoon functions as terroir-defended interior space, a proto-The Thicket whose portal (the lidi, the shifting channels) both admits trade and repels conquest, while its opacity to outsiders ("its social structure enigmatic and its strategies distrusted") is itself a defense.

A polity built for capture-resistance. Without land, Venice had no feudal hierarchy and no fixed loyalty to pope or emperor; it built a republic that shackled its own doge and made political survival depend on refusing capture by either Rome or Constantinople. The famous churchman's jibe that Venice is "a quintessence" belonging "neither to the Church nor to the emperor, nor to the sea nor to the land" names this directly — a self-conscious ideology of capture-resistance organized entirely around commercial Conatus ("Alone in all the world, Venice was organised for economic ends"). The city's readiness to trade with Muslim Egypt against papal and imperial wishes, and to smuggle St Mark's relics past customs, shows this ethos operating from the very start.

Orseolo's campaign as template and founding myth. The year-1000 expedition against the Narentine pirates — diplomacy backed by force, town by town down the coast, culminating in the storming of Curzola and Lagosta — becomes, in Crowley's telling, the pattern for Venetian statecraft across the whole life of the Republic: "a mixture of shrewd diplomacy and the precise application of force." The doge's assumption of the title Dux Dalmatiae and the resulting Ascension Day ceremony (the Senza, with the Bucintoro) ritualize the moment into founding myth, turning a one-off military success into the axiom that "the Adriatic must be a Venetian sea."

Vassal to monopolist: the Byzantine relationship. The chapter's second half traces Venice's climb within, and ultimately against, Byzantine authority — from ritual subject (prayers named the emperor before the doge) to indispensable naval defender to, via the 1082 Golden Bull, a tax-exempt trading power with its own quarter on the Golden Horn. Crowley is careful to show this as gradual and structural rather than heroic: Byzantine naval decline and aristocratic disdain for "vulgar commerce" created the vacuum Venice filled. This has the texture of a Lohse-style history-machine — a shift in the underlying economic infrastructure of the eastern Mediterranean that assembles silently over a century before becoming visible as the Golden Bull.

Prosperity curdling into xenophobia. The chapter closes by tracking Byzantine resentment as Venetian wealth and numbers grow — "swarms and tribes," "marsh frogs," the 1171 mass arrest of Venetians by Manuel I — setting up the mutual mistrust on which the Fourth Crusade (the subject of later chapters) will detonate.

Notable passages

Cassiodorus's sixth-century description of the Venetians living "like seabirds" on osier and wattle, sustained by salt rather than gold, is the chapter's founding ethnographic image — an outsider's astonished sketch of a classless, waterborne society organized around a single scarce commodity, prefiguring everything Crowley will later say about Venetian exceptionalism.

The churchman's "quintessence" gibe crystallizes Venice's structural refusal of capture by either spiritual or temporal overlords — read against the World Machines vocabulary, this is a polity explicitly organized to resist absorption into either of the era's dominant frameworks.

The passage on the Narentine campaign's aftermath ("nostra chaxa," 'our house') marks the moment Crowley treats as the true origin point of Venetian maritime empire — not a battle for its own sake but the establishment of an axiom that will govern policy for the life of the Republic.

The Golden Bull of 1082 sequence, ending on the word "monopoly," shows Crowley building to a single point of maximum leverage: the emperor's own decree becomes the instrument of Venice's future domination of Byzantine trade, at the direct expense of Genoa, Pisa, and Amalfi.

The Byzantine backlash passage ("swarms and tribes"; "marsh frogs") documents the social cost of that ascent in the language of contemporary xenophobia, giving the chapter's ending its charge of impending rupture.
