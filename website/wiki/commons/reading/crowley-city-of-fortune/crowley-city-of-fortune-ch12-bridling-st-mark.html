Bridling St Mark

Commons · City of Fortune: How Venice Ruled the Seas · updated 24 August 2026 · source on GitHub
A dispute over precedence at a Cypriot coronation escalates through contests over Black Sea access and the island of Tenedos into the War of Chioggia, in which Venice's fleet is nearly destroyed at Pola, Genoese and allied land forces seize Chioggia at the edge of the lagoon, and the city survives only through total civilian mobilization and a popular revolt that frees the disgraced admiral Vettor Pisani from prison to lead its last-ditch defense.

The unit narrates the opening acts of the War of Chioggia (1378–79), Venice's existential conflict with Genoa: a petty diplomatic insult in Cyprus and a scramble over the Black Sea chokepoint of Tenedos draw in Padua and Hungary against Venice, the admirals Vettor Pisani and Carlo Zeno are sent to sea, a disastrous forced engagement destroys the Venetian fleet at Pola, and the Genoese-led coalition seizes Chioggia at the mouth of the lagoon, bringing the enemy within sight of St Mark's campanile — at which point Venice, stripped of its fleet and half its territory, mobilizes universally and a popular uprising forces the aristocratic senate to release Pisani from prison to command the city's last stand.

Themes

Escalation from precedence to total war. Crowley frames the conflict's origin as strikingly disproportionate to its outcome: a quarrel over who holds a king's horse and who sits at his right hand at a coronation banquet in Famagusta spirals, through the Genoese sack of Cyprus, contested kingmaking in Constantinople, and the strangehold both republics sought over the Black Sea trade route via Tenedos, into "the last quarter of the fourteenth century both sides went for the jugular." Earlier Venetian-Genoese wars had ended in truce; this one, the text insists, was "fought to the finish."

Land entanglement changes the character of the war. For the first time Venice possessed a modest stato da terra as well as its stato da mar, and the unit shows how this reshaped the conflict: river traffic, mercenary condottieri, gunpowder bombards, and alliances with the duke of Milan all become instruments of a war that was, in the end, decided not on the high seas but within sight of the lagoon itself. The aside on mercenary unreliability ("disunited, ambitious and without discipline... in peace one is robbed by them, and in war by the enemy") signals how precarious this new land dimension of Venetian power was.

Institutional caution versus charismatic command — and its catastrophic failure. Pisani and Zeno are portrayed as near-legendary figures — Pisani "Father," Zeno "the Unconquered" — operating under a bureaucratic war committee whose "jumpy and contradictory orders" repeatedly override sound naval judgment. The disaster at Pola is the clearest case: Pisani's sober tactical caution is overridden by moral pressure and a personal insult to his honor, with fatal consequences. The Republic's subsequent handling of the defeat — condemning Pisani to prison rather than execution, then being forced by a genuine popular revolt to reinstate him — exposes a deep fault line between the aristocracy's institutional prerogatives and the ordinary sailors' loyalty to a proven commander, nearly tipping into insurrection ("give us our Captain Pisani! We want Pisani out of prison!").

Existential siege and the logic of humiliation. Once Chioggia falls, the conflict is explicitly reframed by the Genoese commander Pietro Doria as one of total domination rather than negotiated peace — Venice's blank sheet offering any terms is rejected in favor of a demand to "put a bridle on those horses of yours on the portico of St Mark's," a threat to Venice's central civic and religious symbol. This precipitates a remarkable mobilization: universal conscription, suspended salaries, women donating jewelry, collapsing property values, and the deliberate erasure of the lagoon's navigational markers to make the city's home waters unreadable to the invader — an inversion of the legibility that normally organizes Venetian commercial and administrative power. Where the rest of City of Fortune shows Venice building systems of control and measurement (galley schedules, colonial administration, standardized contracts), here the Republic weaponizes deliberate illegibility as its final line of defense, a move worth flagging against legibility-as-machine-core as a case where legibility is inverted rather than pursued.

Notable passages

The removal of the briccole — the lagoon's channel markers — turning the city's home waters into "a primeval labyrinth in which nothing snagged the eye," is the sharpest single image of the unit: a maritime republic built on charted, legible space deliberately un-mapping itself as a survival strategy.

The battle of Pola shows institutional and psychological failure in miniature: Pisani's considered caution collapses under "moral pressure," a public accusation of cowardice, and the crew's baying impatience, launching the fleet into an ambush that costs Venice its navy in ninety minutes.

The fall of the Chioggia bridge — a volunteer soldier burning it under fire for a bounty, the Venetians failing to raise the drawbridge in their panic, the piazza "stained red with Christian blood" — marks the campaign's true turning point, the moment the war stops being fought at a maritime remove and becomes a fight for the lagoon itself.

Doria's reply to Venice's blank peace offer, promising to "bridle" the bronze horses of St Mark, crystallizes the stakes as symbolic rather than merely material — this is a war over which city gets to remain a subject of history rather than an object of another's domination.

The popular uprising demanding Pisani's release shows the Republic's aristocratic self-image genuinely strained by class resentment ("the unbearable haughtiness of the noble class stuck in the popular gullet"), forcing the senate to bend twice to a crowd that hammers on the council-chamber doors chanting his name.
