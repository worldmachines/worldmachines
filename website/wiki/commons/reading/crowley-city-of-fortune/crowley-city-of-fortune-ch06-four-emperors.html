Four Emperors: Fire, Betrayal, and the Collapse of Byzantine Legitimacy

Commons · City of Fortune: How Venice Ruled the Seas · updated 24 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Constantinople is devastated by a catastrophic fire set by Venetian, Pisan, and crusader looters, and in the ensuing five months Byzantine authority disintegrates through failed payments, mob violence, and a palace coup, leaving four rival emperors in the city before the crusaders manufacture a theological pretext to storm it outright.

Covering August 1203 to April 1204, this unit traces the unraveling of the fragile bargain struck between the Fourth Crusade and the restored Byzantine emperor Alexius IV. A catastrophic fire — set off by looting Venetians, Pisans, and Flemish crusaders and fanned by a dry summer wind — guts four hundred acres of Constantinople and turns the city's population irrevocably against the westerners camped across the Golden Horn. As Alexius IV fails to make good on his promised payments, popular fury, court intrigue, and outright treachery combine to destroy him: the mob crowns a puppet emperor in Hagia Sophia, the courtier Murtzuphlus stages a coup and strangles Alexius IV in his cell, and the crusaders, now unpaid, unfed, and unable to retreat, talk themselves into a theological justification for attacking the city they came to save.

Themes

Fire as the hinge of history. Crowley stages the accidental August fire as the true point of no return — more decisive than any treaty violation. Once the flames "cut a dark gash through the heart of urban Constantinople," reconciliation between Greeks and Latins becomes structurally impossible; the text calls it "a firebreak that could henceforth not be crossed," a literal disaster that forecloses political possibility.

Contract versus honor, and the collapse of both idioms. The crusader ultimatum to Alexius IV is framed in two incompatible registers at once — feudal honor for the barons, broken commercial contract for the bourgeois Venetians ("to the bourgeois Venetians it was a broken contract"). When Alexius refuses either framing, both idioms of legitimacy fail simultaneously, and Dandolo's furious personal confrontation on the shore ("Contemptible boy… we hauled you out of the dung heap") marks the final breakdown of the personal relationship that had underwritten the whole crusader-Byzantine alliance since Corfu.

Multiplying, rival sovereignties. As Alexius IV's authority dissolves, no single successor is able to claim the vacated legitimacy cleanly. Within days the city holds a blind co-emperor (Isaac), an imprisoned emperor (Alexius IV), a usurper in the palace (Murtzuphlus), and a mob-crowned nonentity in Hagia Sophia (Kannavos) — "the elaborate dignity of the great empire had completely collapsed." This is a vivid instance of an interregnum where an old order's legitimacy has died before any new one can consolidate — resonant with the gramsci-gap concept: multiple claimants proliferate precisely because none can yet command the field alone.

Realpolitik dressed as piety and vice versa. Murtzuphlus's murder of Alexius IV is bluntly instrumental — Alexius alive is a standing pretext for the crusaders' presence — but the sources narrate it in moralized and gruesome terms (poison refused, strangulation, a hook through the ribs). Symmetrically, the crusaders' own decision to storm Constantinople is naked self-preservation (no money, no food, no way home) dressed in manufactured doctrine: the clergy's ruling that "Constantinople, by sleight of hand, had become Jerusalem" shows legitimating narrative built to order after the material decision was already made.

Notable passages

The fire's description (paragraph beginning "Fires were a common hazard") renders the conflagration almost as a character in its own right — "a river of fire" that moves with intention, dividing and recombining, evading Hagia Sophia by what Choniates calls a miracle. It sets the tone of catastrophe that governs everything after.

Dandolo's shoreline confrontation with Alexius IV is the emotional pivot of the chapter: a ninety-year-old doge, blind, rowed out under armed guard, delivering a curse ("we hauled you out of the dung heap and we'll drop you back in it") that ends any pretense of alliance and opens formal war.

The coup passage — Murtzuphlus's night arrest of Alexius IV and the resulting tally of "four emperors in the city" — is the structural crux the chapter's title names: legitimate authority has fragmented into simultaneous, mutually exclusive claimants.

The strangling of Alexius IV closes his arc with Crowley juxtaposing lurid Latin gore-reporting against Choniates's more theologically freighted (and equally damning) Greek account, underscoring how thoroughly each side's sources are propaganda for their own audience.

Finally, the clergy's post-hoc justification for attacking Constantinople — turning the city into a surrogate Jerusalem — is the chapter's clearest instance of legitimating doctrine manufactured after the fact to license a decision already forced by hunger and stranded logistics.
