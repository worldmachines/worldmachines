The Canon's Quarrel with Chivalry and the Tale of Leandra

Commons · Don Quixote · updated 19 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Sancho tries to reason Don Quixote out of believing himself enchanted, the freed knight and a skeptical canon stage a full debate over whether chivalric romances are lies or history, Don Quixote rapturously narrates a stock romance adventure as proof of their delight, and the unit closes with a goatherd's pastoral tale of Leandra, whose elopement with a swaggering soldier has turned a valley full of spurned suitors into a shepherds' Arcadia of lament.

This unit spans the close of Chapter XLIX, the whole of Chapter L, and the opening of Chapter LI. Sancho corners Don Quixote with a homespun syllogism about enchantment, the knight is released from his cage on his word as a knight, and the freed Don Quixote enters an extended debate with the canon over whether books of chivalry are wicked lies or transmitted truth — a debate that culminates in Don Quixote's rhapsodic recitation of a stock romance adventure. The chapter break brings a goatherd who addresses his runaway goat like a philosopher and then tells the assembled party the story of Leandra, the local beauty whose elopement with a soldier-poet has scattered her disappointed suitors into the hills as a fellowship of grieving shepherds.

Themes

Enchantment as a self-preserving fiction. Sancho's proof is disarmingly empirical: enchanted people, by common report, don't eat, drink, sleep, or answer questions — but Don Quixote does all of these, so he cannot be enchanted. Don Quixote's rebuttal abandons evidence altogether and retreats to necessity: he needs to be enchanted, because the alternative — that he is simply a coward hiding in a cage while people who need him go unhelped — is unbearable. "I know and feel that I am enchanted, and that is enough to ease my conscience." This is not self-deception in the naive sense; it is a deliberate choice of the frame that lets him keep being a knight-errant, made in full awareness that the frame is contestable. The move has a conatus-like shape — the self-narrative exists to let a striving persist against evidence that would otherwise dissolve it — though Cervantes plays it for pathos and comedy rather than doctrine. [[Conatus]]

The canon's case: romance as counterfeit history. The canon's indictment is not merely that chivalric books are false, but that their fluency in false particulars — genealogies, itineraries, day-by-day chronicles — makes them dangerous counterfeits of legible history, "founders of new sects and modes of life" that "unsettle the wits of gentlemen of birth and intelligence." He proposes real history (the Book of Judges, Viriatus, Caesar, the Cid) as the corrective: true because verifiable, useful because instructive. It is a legibility argument in miniature — a demand that the record of the past be answerable to evidence rather than to appetite.

Don Quixote's case: relics, genealogy, and the reality-effect. Don Quixote does not defend the romances as fiction worth reading; he insists they are true, and marshals the same evidentiary apparatus the canon prizes — physical relics (Roland's horn, Pierres' pin, Babieca's saddle), genealogical continuity (he traces his own lineage to Gutierre Quixada), and eyewitness memory (his grandmother's offhand comparison to Dame Quintañona) — to blur the line between attested history (the Cid, the Twelve Peers) and pure invention (Amadis, Fierabras). The canon partially yields ground on the Spanish knights-errant while holding firm against the romances proper, producing a debate that is really about what counts as evidence at all, not just whether particular stories are true.

The pleasure argument. Having failed to win on evidentiary grounds, Don Quixote pivots in Chapter L to an argument from delight: he narrates, at length and with evident relish, the stock furniture of a chivalric adventure — the lake of bubbling pitch, the plaintive voice, the enchanted castle of gold and jacinth, the bathing and feasting of the knight — as self-evident proof that such books "banish any melancholy." The passage is Cervantes performing the very seductiveness he has the canon condemn, letting the reader feel exactly why Don Quixote is captured by the genre even while the frame (a captive madman being carted home in a cage) undercuts every word of it.

Sancho's theory of lordship. Don Quixote's fantasy of becoming emperor and making Sancho a count draws out Sancho's own naive political economy: a lord need only farm out the actual administration and collect the rents, "enjoy my rents like a duke, and let things go their own way." The canon gently corrects him — that the lord of a seigniory is answerable for justice and for "a firm determination to find out the truth" — a brief, serious counterweight to both men's fantasies, planted just before Sancho's own comic syllogism of contentment ("when one has nothing more to desire there is an end of it").

The pastoral Arcadia of the spurned. The goatherd's tale of Leandra — courted by the narrator Eugenio and his rival Anselmo, then swept away by the glittering, self-mythologizing soldier-poet Vicente de la Roca, found despoiled in a mountain cave, and shut up in a convent — ends not in her story but in the aftermath: a valley that her rejected suitors have converted into a shared habitat of ritualized grief, singing, cursing, and adoring her name until "the mountains ring with 'Leandra.'" It is a small, self-organizing community built entirely around a shared wound and a shared practice (song, complaint, pastoral retreat), sustained by its own local conditions rather than by any resolution of the underlying loss — a minor echo of a thicket: a bordered habitat for a way of being, defended less by walls than by the shared, non-transferable grief that makes the place what it is. [[The Thicket]]

Notable passages

Sancho's enchantment-proof and Don Quixote's admission that belief in his own enchantment is a conscience-saving necessity form the emotional core of the unit's opening — comic logic meeting a genuinely poignant act of self-authorship. The canon-Don Quixote debate that follows is Cervantes at his most dialectically generous: neither side is a strawman, and the argument turns on what kind of evidence (verified chronicle vs. surviving relic and unbroken genealogy) is allowed to certify the past. Don Quixote's lake-of-pitch rhapsody in Chapter L is the unit's set piece, showing rather than telling why the genre is dangerous — it is genuinely enchanting prose in the mouth of a man en route to a cage. The goatherd's tale, breaking off mid-story at the chapter's end, plants Leandra's elopement and its fallout — a valley turned collective mourning-ground — as the unit's closing image.
