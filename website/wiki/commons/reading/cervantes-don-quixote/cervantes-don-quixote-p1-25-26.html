Don Quixote's Penance in the Sierra Morena

Commons · Don Quixote · updated 19 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Don Quixote withdraws into the Sierra Morena to stage a deliberately unmotivated, literary-modeled madness in imitation of Amadis of Gaul and Roland, dispatches Sancho with a love letter to Dulcinea, and the curate and barber, having intercepted Sancho, plot a disguise scheme to lure the knight home.

Having left the goatherds and the raving Cardenio behind, Don Quixote and Sancho press deeper into the Sierra Morena, debating along the way whether Don Quixote was right to defend the honor of the (fictional, romance-derived) Queen Madasima against Cardenio's ravings. Don Quixote then reveals his true purpose: not to find the madman, but to perform a self-imposed penance modeled explicitly on Amadis of Gaul's withdrawal to the Peña Pobre and, as a fallback, on Orlando's berserker madness in the Orlando Furioso. He composes a love letter and a signed order for three ass-colts (both written in Cardenio's abandoned notebook), strips half-naked, performs somersaults for Sancho's benefit as proof of his madness, and sends his squire off toward El Toboso. Alone, Don Quixote settles on the gentler Amadis model over the destructive Roland model, fashions a rosary from his shirt-tail, and carves love poetry into trees. Meanwhile Sancho, riding out, runs into the village curate and barber (already searching for Don Quixote), garbles the letter from memory when he discovers he has lost the notebook, and the two clergymen hatch a plan: the curate will disguise himself as a damsel in distress to lure the knight out of the mountains and back home.

Themes

Chivalric imitation as conscious craft, not delusion. Don Quixote's penance is framed not as a symptom of madness but as a deliberate act of artistic imitation, argued through an extended analogy to painters copying "the originals of the rarest painters" and to Homer and Virgil shaping Ulysses and Aeneas as exemplars "not representing or describing them as they were, but as they ought to be." Don Quixote positions himself in this same tradition of exemplarity, selecting Amadis as his polestar. This complicates any simple diagnosis of insanity: he is executing a literary program with full awareness of its models and stakes.

Madness engineered for its own sake. The chapter's structural joke is that Don Quixote insists true chivalric distinction requires going mad without cause — "no thanks to a knight-errant for going mad when he has cause; the thing is to turn crazy without any provocation." He then manufactures a double-bind letter to Dulcinea that guarantees resolution either way: a favorable answer ends the penance, an unfavorable one tips him into real madness that, being madness, will no longer be felt as suffering. The penance is a self-authored performance calibrated to be win-win by its own logic.

Epistemic relativism as chivalric doctrine. Don Quixote's explanation of the barber's-basin-as-Mambrino's-helmet — that "a swarm of enchanters" reshape appearances differently for different observers, so that objects go "always by contraries" — generalizes into a working theory of how his whole quest can be simultaneously delusional to onlookers and coherent to him. It is less a symptom than an interpretive framework he applies consistently.

Textual fragility and comic transmission. The letter to Dulcinea, composed with care and read aloud to Sancho for memorization, survives only in a notebook that Sancho loses almost immediately — and his subsequent attempt to reconstruct it from memory ("Exalted and scrubbing Lady" for "Sovereign and exalted Lady") turns the sentimental original into slapstick. The unit stages its own document's unreliable transmission, undercutting the romance register even as it invokes it.

The frame narrative reasserts control. The curate and barber's scheme — disguising as a damsel-in-distress to exploit chivalric obligation itself as the lever to extract Don Quixote from the mountains — shows the surrounding social world beginning to actively manage, rather than merely observe, Don Quixote's fiction, using his own genre conventions as the mechanism of recapture.

Notable passages

The Amadis-as-exemplar speech (c1) is the unit's clearest statement of Don Quixote's self-understanding: chivalric imitation is presented as continuous with the highest literary and artistic traditions, not opposed to sanity. The "there is the point" passage (c2) supplies the logical architecture of the penance — madness engineered so that any outcome from Dulcinea resolves it. The enchanters/contraries speech (c3) generalizes this into an epistemology that explains away all contradictory evidence. The letter itself (c4) is the sincere emotional core Cervantes then immediately deflates through Sancho's garbled recitation (c5). Finally, the curate's disguise scheme (c6) marks the pivot from the knight's self-directed fiction to the village's counter-fiction designed to reel him back in.
