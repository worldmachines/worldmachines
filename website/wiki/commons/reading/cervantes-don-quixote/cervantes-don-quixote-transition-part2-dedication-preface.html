The Dedication to the Count of Lemos and the Author's Preface to Part II

Commons · Don Quixote · updated 19 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Cervantes dedicates the finished Second Part to his patron the Count of Lemos with a wry anecdote about a Chinese emperor's invitation, then in the preface answers the spurious Avellaneda "Second Part" with wounded pride, two comic dog parables about the ease of criticizing books, and a promise to kill Don Quixote off for good so no counterfeit continuation can follow.

This unit opens Part II proper with two short front-matter pieces: a dedication letter to Cervantes's patron Pedro Fernández de Castro, Count of Lemos, and the author's preface, in which Cervantes confronts the notorious unauthorized "Second Part" of Don Quixote published in 1614 under the name Alonso Fernández de Avellaneda. The dedication turns patronage into comedy — a fictitious Chinese emperor begs Cervantes to come found a Spanish college, and Cervantes politely refuses in favor of the real support he gets from Lemos — while the preface mixes genuine hurt (over jabs at his age and his maimed hand from Lepanto), theological hair-splitting about envy, two folk parables about madmen and dogs, and a closing vow: this authentic Part II will end with Don Quixote "dead and buried" precisely so no third, spurious Quixote can ever again "masquerade through the whole world."

Themes

Patronage as the real economy of authorship. The dedication stages an absurd choice between an emperor who offers prestige (a college chair, the honor of authoring the textbook of a whole national curriculum) and offers no travel money, versus the Count of Lemos, who offers neither title nor throne but concrete, ongoing support. Cervantes's joke — "Emperor for Emperor and Monarch for Monarch, I have at Naples the great Count of Lemos" — is also a serious statement about the material conditions of writing under absolutism: literary survival runs through personal protection, not institutions or markets. The same point recurs in the preface, where Lemos and the Archbishop of Toledo are named as the two patrons whose "Christian charity" shields Cervantes against every hostile press.

Wounded authorship and the defense of a body of work. Avellaneda's preface had mocked Cervantes as old and one-handed and called him envious. Cervantes's response reframes his Lepanto wound not as a liability but as the highest honor he could have received — he says he would rather have his share in that battle again than be whole and absent from it. This is Cervantes doing something the novel itself constantly does with Don Quixote: refusing to let a body's damage be read as diminishment, insisting instead that scars are legible as honor to those who know how to read them.

Two parables against easy criticism. The reed-and-dog story (a madman who inflates stray dogs for a laugh, then dares the crowd, "Do your worships think, now, that it is an easy thing to blow up a dog?") and the stone-and-lurcher story (a madman who drops slabs on dogs until one owner beats him senseless, after which he only ever calls dogs "lurchers" and never drops another stone) are both parables about the asymmetry between destructive criticism and constructive making. Cervantes converts the first directly — "Does your worship think now, that it is an easy thing to write a book?" — and lets the second imply that Avellaneda, having taken his beating, will not dare "discharge the weight of his wit" again. Both stories let Cervantes attack without lowering himself to the name-calling he explicitly disavows ("Thou wouldst have me call him ass, fool, and malapert, but I have no such intention").

Killing the hero to close the canon. The preface's final move is the most consequential: Cervantes announces that this authentic continuation will show Don Quixote "continued, and at length dead and buried, so that no one may dare to bring forward any further evidence against him." This is an author using narrative finality as an act of textual property enforcement — the only way to permanently foreclose future counterfeit sequels is to make the character definitively, irreversibly dead. It is a strikingly literal instance of the tension WMP names between legibility-as-machine-core (an author imposing a single, closed, authoritative version) and the uncontrolled proliferation of unauthorized variants that print culture enabled — an early and vivid case of a canonical text fighting to stay legible against its own runaway, divergent copies.

Notable passages

The Chinese-emperor anecdote in the dedication is Cervantes at his most performatively modest and most quietly proud: an emperor of the known world wants him, and he turns the offer down for a patron who actually pays. The Lepanto passage is the collection's clearest statement of Cervantes's relationship to his own body and his own legend — wounds as "stars that direct others to the heaven of honour." The two dog parables are the preface's comic engine, translating literary combat into street-level slapstick with a real sting in the tail. And the closing vow to bury Don Quixote for good is the hinge on which the rest of Part II turns: everything that follows is written under the shadow of a promised, deliberate ending, made necessary by a rival's theft of the character.
