The Making of Don Quixote: Cervantes's Later Life and Death

Commons · Don Quixote · updated 19 August 2026 · source on GitHub
A biographical sketch follows Cervantes from his penniless return to Spain through failed careers as playwright and tax collector, the accidental gathering of material that became Don Quixote, the novel's contested reception, Avellaneda's spurious sequel that forced him to finish Part Two, and his death in 1616, closing with a defense of his life against the charge that Spain neglected him.

This unit resumes Cervantes's biography from his return to Spain after the Portuguese campaign through his death in 1616. It tracks a string of failures — a stalled military career after his crippled hand, an unprofitable first novel (the "Galatea"), three unsuccessful years as a dramatist, a stint as a tax collector for Granada that ended in a debtor's prison term — before turning to the accidental genesis of "Don Quixote," its surprisingly strong reception, the shock of a forged continuation by "Avellaneda" that finally drove Cervantes to finish Part Two, and his death within a year of that. The essay closes with an argument, against the consensus of Cervantes's biographers, that his life should not be read as simply unhappy, and a rebuttal of the charge that Spain neglected him.

Themes

Don Quixote's material was gathered unintentionally, not designed. The essay is explicit that the texture of the novel — inn life, wayside characters, hostelry art, the reapers listening to a chivalric romance read aloud — was noted down not as literary research but as incidental observation during Cervantes's years as an itinerant tax collector for the crown. The biographer even speculates that Cervantes may have encountered a real "pauper gentleman, with his lean hack and his greyhound and his books of chivalry" on these journeys. The novel's most celebrated material, in other words, accumulated as a byproduct of an unrelated, humiliating job before it was ever organized into a book.

Cervantes treated Don Quixote as a stepchild, not his true ambition. Repeatedly the essay stresses that Cervantes's real hope was to be recognized as a dramatist — "the Aeschylus of Spain" — and that he wrote and rewrote "Don Quixote" carelessly, "by fits and starts," never even bothering to correct the printer's errors across editions. He is quoted (via the biographer's paraphrase) calling himself "more a stepfather than a father" to the book, and regarding it as a mere "libro de entretenimiento," an amusement rather than a magnum opus. The book he actually prized, and would have traded all of Quixote's success for, was a single hit comparable to Lope de Vega's.

A forged sequel is what forced the real book into existence. The unit's central turn is the appearance in 1614 of Avellaneda's spurious "Second Volume" — abusive toward Cervantes personally, mocking his age, his maimed hand, his poverty, his imprisonment. The biographer argues, with evident irony, that this act of malice is precisely what we owe the finished "Don Quixote" to: without it, Cervantes's habitual dawdling (interrupted by pastoral romances, comedies, and vague promises of "en breve") would likely have left the book an unfinished torso, or extended indefinitely into a projected Third Part with Don Quixote and Sancho as shepherds. Robbed of the leisure to meander, Cervantes "set himself to finish off his task and protect Don Quixote in the only way he could, by killing him." A genuinely contingent, uncoordinated act — a rival's plagiarism — becomes the efficient cause of the canonical text's completion, a small case study in how a finished cultural artifact can be the product of accident rather than design.

The essay reframes "unhappy life" against the biographical consensus. Against biographers who call Cervantes's life unhappy, the essay insists his "vital energy and mental activity, his restless invention and his sanguine temperament" made him proof against despondency by "fortitude of a high spirit" rather than by naive buoyancy. The same "indomitable spirit that kept him from despair in the bagnios of Algiers, and prompted him to attempt the escape of himself and his comrades again and again" is what let him persevere through failed plays, debtor's prison, and poverty at sixty-eight still "full of life and hope and plans for the future as a boy of eighteen." This is a portrait of striving-to-persist as temperament and survival strategy — a mode of conatus-like endurance that treats adversity as material to be outlasted rather than a verdict on a life's worth. Conatus

The final defense reframes neglect as ordinary controversy, not indifference. The closing pages argue that Spain's mixed reception — hostility from the chivalry-romance readers, the Lope circle, and the culto poets, alongside piracy and rapid foreign editions — is simply what any book that "turned their favourite reading into ridicule" should expect, not evidence of a nation's ingratitude. The essay's final gesture, invoking "Si monumentum quoeris, circumspice" over the inadequate bronze statue in Madrid, argues that the book's continuing circulation in every bookseller's shop is monument enough, dismissing the demand for a physical memorial as beside the point.

Notable passages

The tax-collecting passage ("It was as he journeyed from town to town...") is the unit's clearest statement that "Don Quixote"'s famous texture was harvested incidentally, not composed to order — the germ of the book found in a humiliating day job rather than in literary intention.

The Avellaneda passage ("Nemesis was coming, however...") stages the accident that forced the work to close: an anonymous rival's plagiarism and personal abuse, arriving specifically because Cervantes had let eight and a half years pass on a "vaguest" promise of a sequel.

The "stepfather" passage ("He shows plainly enough, too, that 'Don Quixote'...") is Cervantes's own reported self-assessment of the book's place in his ambitions — evidence, in the biographer's reading, for why the novel was written and revised so negligently.

The "unhappy life" passage ("Was it an unhappy life, that of Cervantes?...") is the essay's thesis statement on temperament: hardship endured not by naive cheerfulness but by a deliberate fortitude that made poverty itself "a thing to be laughed over."

The closing monument passage ("It has been also made a reproach to Spain...") delivers the essay's final rebuttal to the neglect narrative, ending on the epitaph-like Latin tag repurposed to argue the book's ubiquity is Cervantes's true monument.
