Pastoral Dreams, a Pig Stampede, and Altisidora's Staged Resurrection

Commons · Don Quixote · updated 19 August 2026 · source on GitHub
On the road home after his defeat, Don Quixote proposes a pastoral retirement with Sancho, the pair are trampled by a drove of pigs and later abducted to the duke's castle, where they are forced to endure a staged catafalque ceremony in which Sancho's whipped face and pinched arms supposedly resurrect the feigned-dead Altisidora, whose plot is then revealed as Samson Carrasco's and the duke's joint contrivance.

This unit spans Chapters LXVII through LXX: Don Quixote, homeward bound after his defeat by the Knight of the White Moon, proposes to Sancho that they become shepherds during his year of enforced retirement; the two are knocked down and scattered by a passing drove of pigs; they are then seized at lance-point by the duke's men and marched to the ducal castle, where they are forced to sit through an elaborate mock funeral-and-resurrection rite for Altisidora, whose "revival" is staged as depending on Sancho enduring smacks, pinches, and pin-pricks; the narrative then steps back to explain that the whole charade, like the earlier disenchantment scheme, was engineered by the duke in league with the bachelor Samson Carrasco (the erstwhile Knight of the Mirrors/White Moon); and the unit closes with Altisidora's mock-recovery visit to Don Quixote's bedroom, including her invented vision of hell where devils use a spurious sequel to the History of Don Quixote as a tennis ball.

Themes

  • Retirement as invention, not defeat. Don Quixote's pastoral scheme is not idleness but a redirection of the same romance-making faculty that produced knight-errantry: he renames himself and his companions (Quixotize, Panzino, Samsonino/Carrascon, Niculoso, Curiambro), plans costume and music, and folds Dulcinea seamlessly into the new fiction. Sancho, characteristically, worries about his daughter's virtue and his wife's nickname (Teresona) rather than the literary conceit, grounding the fantasy in domestic realism.
  • Proverbs as a battlefield of authority. The recurring argument over who uses proverbs well (Don Quixote claims his are apt, Sancho's are dragged in "by the head and shoulders") is a running meta-commentary on rhetorical propriety that mirrors the novel's larger concern with fitting language to reality.
  • Degradation after defeat. The pig stampede is presented by Don Quixote as providential chastisement fitting a vanquished knight, extending the novel's pattern of physical humiliation following spiritual overreach; Sancho counters by refusing the logic of inherited punishment ("what have the Panzas to do with the Quixotes?").
  • The disenchantment economy literalized as spectacle. The duke and duchess convert the fiction that Sancho's self-flagellation will disenchant Dulcinea into a fully staged theatrical liturgy (catafalque, judges Minos and Rhadamanthus, torches, a sung elegy) in which Altisidora's mock-death is cured by inflicting pain on Sancho's body instead. This exposes, comically, how thoroughly the aristocratic hosts have industrialized Don Quixote's private delusion into public entertainment.
  • Authorial self-consciousness and the rival Quixote. Altisidora's invented vision of hell, where devils play tennis with books and single out the spurious "Second Part" by the Aragonese of Tordesillas for special contempt, is Cervantes' most direct metafictional jab at Avellaneda's counterfeit continuation, delivered through a character who is herself an elaborate fiction within the fiction.
  • The narrator's own verdict. Cide Hamete Benengeli interrupts the frame to judge the duke and duchess as scarcely less foolish than their victims for the effort they expend mocking two fools — a rare moment of the chronicler passing explicit judgment on the aristocratic tormentors rather than merely reporting events.

Notable passages

The pastoral proposal (Ch. LXVII) shows Don Quixote's imagination reconstituting itself immediately after defeat, folding classical eclogue conventions and etymological digression (the disquisition on Moorish al- words) into the new scheme. The praise-of-sleep speech Sancho delivers when roused for a whipping is one of the novel's set-piece meditations, echoing Sancho Panza's earlier praise of sleep in Part II and drawing unexpected praise from his master ("Never have I heard thee speak so elegantly as now, Sancho"). The pig stampede is recounted factually: a drove of over six hundred pigs being driven to market passes directly over the two men in the dark, scattering their gear and unhorsing Rocinante, after which Don Quixote reads the incident as heaven's chastisement of a vanquished knight rather than mere accident. The catafalque scene formalizes the duke's earlier disenchantment trick into full pageantry, with Rhadamanthus's sentence dictating the precise number of smacks, pinches, and pin-thrusts Sancho must suffer. Cide Hamete's aside naming the duke and duchess as fools for their pains, and the devils' tennis match with the spurious Avellaneda continuation, are the unit's clearest instances of the text turning its irony back on its own aristocratic manipulators and its literary rivals.

c1 — The pastoral plan

Don Quixote's proposal to turn shepherd, complete with invented pastoral names and an inventory of woodland comforts, recasts his retirement as a new fiction rather than a retreat from fiction.

c2 — Sancho's praise of sleep

Sancho's meditation on sleep as the leveler of shepherd and king is one of the chapter's set-piece speeches and draws explicit admiration from Don Quixote.

c3 — The pig drove

A factual account of the drove of pigs passing over the sleeping pair, which Don Quixote interprets as heaven's chastisement of a defeated knight.

c4 — Rhadamanthus's sentence

The mock tribunal's decree specifies the exact pains to be inflicted on Sancho, converting the private disenchantment fiction into staged public liturgy.

c5 — Cide Hamete's judgment

The chronicler steps outside the narrative to judge the duke and duchess as nearly as foolish as the two men they are mocking.

c6 — The devils and the spurious sequel

Altisidora's invented vision of hell singles out the counterfeit continuation of Don Quixote's history for special scorn, Cervantes' clearest metafictional strike at the rival Avellaneda text.
