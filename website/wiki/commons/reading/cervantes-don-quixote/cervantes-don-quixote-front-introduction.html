Prefatory: The History and Ethics of Translating Don Quixote

Commons · Don Quixote · updated 19 August 2026 · source on GitHub
John Ormsby's prefatory essay surveys a century-and-a-half of English translations of Don Quixote—Shelton, Phillips, Ward, Motteux, Jervas, Smollett, and lesser imitators—to argue that fidelity to Cervantes's spirit and plain, unaffected style, not archaism or comic embellishment, is the translator's real duty, and to state the principles guiding his own version.

Ormsby opens his 1885 translation of Don Quixote not with the novel itself but with a critical history of its English afterlives. He traces the line of translators from Thomas Shelton (1612, 1620) through John Phillips (1687), Ned Ward's verse burlesque (1700), Peter Motteux (1712), Charles Jervas/"Jarvis" (posthumous), Smollett (1755), and a scatter of minor and derivative later versions, judging each against a single standard: does it preserve the "unsmiling gravity" and "sententious terseness" that make Cervantes's humour Cervantes's, or does it substitute the translator's own comic register for his? The essay is simultaneously a scholarly bibliography and a manifesto for how Ormsby will render the book that follows—plainly, without antiquarian affectation, keeping the names and phrases already naturalized in English.

Themes

Contemporaneity as an untranslatable advantage. Ormsby's opening move is to admit that no later translator, however skilled, can recover what Shelton had for free: having lived in Cervantes's own generation. Shelton's version is riddled with faults—hasty, inconsistent, "barbarously literal" in places and "very loose" in others—yet Ormsby ranks its vitality above the technical correctness of his successors, because Shelton did not have to imaginatively cross two centuries to see what Cervantes saw. This sets up the essay's real subject: translation is not a solvable technical problem but a series of historically situated compromises, each generation's version legible chiefly as a record of what that generation thought "Don Quixote" was for.

The impossibility of a wholly satisfactory translation. Ormsby insists this is not English's failure alone but structural: the "sententious terseness" that gives the book's humour its flavor is a property of Spanish itself, only ever "distantly imitated" elsewhere. This is a rare moment where the essay's logic brushes against the World Machines idea of a decay-sequence—not applied to institutions here but to a text's successive re-embodiments, each rendering losing some register of the original as it moves outward from its native tongue and century, the way canonical infrastructure drifts from Functional toward Ornamental the further it travels from its founding conditions.

Moral judgment of translators by their treatment of tone. The essay's real spine is a taxonomy of failure modes. Phillips's version is "a travesty" of "coarseness, vulgarity, and buffoonery." Motteux's team produces a "Franco-cockney" pastiche assembled from Shelton, French intermediary sources, and borrowings from Phillips—decent in language but hollow of Spanish flavor, treating the book "as a comic book that cannot be made too comic." Ormsby's fiercest language is reserved for this move: infusing Cervantes with cockney flippancy is not a stylistic misstep but an "absolute falsification of the spirit of the book." Jervas, by contrast, is defended at length against the era's low opinion of him (compounded by Pope's jab that he "translated 'Don Quixote' without understanding Spanish"): Ormsby credits him as the first translator to grasp that Quixotic humour depends on not smirking at its own jokes, even though this gravity reads to others as mere "stiff, dry" woodenness.

Fidelity to spirit over letter, and the case against archaism. Ormsby closes by stating his own rule: avoid "everything that savours of affectation," including deliberately antiquated language, since Spanish has changed so little since the seventeenth century that plain modern English is usually the truest equivalent of Cervantes's own plain register. He also commits to preserving the "old familiar names and phrases" already naturalized in English usage rather than re-translating them for novelty's sake—an explicit acknowledgment that two and a half centuries of English readership have themselves become part of what "Don Quixote" now is in English.

Notable passages

The Shelton/Shakespeare passage does double duty: it flatters Shelton's proximity to Cervantes while also staging Ormsby's central claim that translation quality is inseparable from historical position. The passage on the structural impossibility of perfect translation generalizes this into a theory: no version, however careful, escapes the gap between Spanish terseness and any other tongue's expressive habits. The Motteux passage is the essay's moral center of gravity, condemning comic inflation as a category error rather than a stylistic quirk. The Jervas passage recovers a maligned translator by re-reading his supposed defect (wooden gravity) as his rare virtue (fidelity to Cervantes's deadpan). The final passage states Ormsby's own working method plainly, closing the historical survey by turning it into a program.
