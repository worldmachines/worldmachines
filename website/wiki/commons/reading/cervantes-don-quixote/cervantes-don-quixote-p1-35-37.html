The Wineskin Battle, the Lovers' Reunion, and the Arrival of the Captive

Commons · Don Quixote · updated 19 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Don Quixote's dream-battle with wineskins closes the tale of "The Ill-Advised Curiosity," after which chance reunites all four lovers (Dorothea, Don Fernando, Luscinda, and Cardenio) at the inn, and the arrival of a Christian captive and his veiled Moorish companion Zoraida opens a new thread just as Don Quixote launches into a discourse on arms versus letters.

This unit spans the close of Chapter XXXV, all of Chapter XXXVI, and the opening of Chapter XXXVII. It begins with Don Quixote's sleep-fought "battle" against the innkeeper's wineskins (mistaken by Sancho for a slain giant), which interrupts and then allows the completion of the embedded novella "The Ill-Advised Curiosity," ending in Anselmo's, Camilla's, and Lothario's deaths. The narrative then pivots to the inn's central set-piece: a chance arrival brings Don Fernando and the grieving, veiled Luscinda face to face with Cardenio and Dorothea, precipitating a cascade of recognitions, confessions, and reconciliations that resolves both love triangles at once. The unit closes as a new pair of travelers — a Christian former captive and his veiled Moorish companion, who insists she is "Maria" not "Zoraida" — arrives at the inn, and Don Quixote, restored to his role as guest of honor, begins an extended supper oration on whether arms or letters is the nobler calling.

Themes

Illusion collapsing into farce, then illusion sustained by consent. The wineskin battle literalizes the novel's central joke: Don Quixote's chivalric perception overlays a mundane reality (skins of red wine) with heroic content (a decapitated giant), and the collision is resolved not by Don Quixote recognizing his error but by everyone else managing the gap — the landlord demanding payment, the curate promising restitution, Dorothea sustaining the Micomicona fiction a while longer. Sancho, characteristically, straddles both worlds: he abandons belief in this particular enchantment ("there is no enchantment about it at all, but a great deal of bruising and bad luck") only after his own body — blanketed, unpaid, disappointed of a county — has taught him skepticism, even as he continues to trade on the larger fiction for his own ends.

The Curious Impertinent's moral collapses on cue. The embedded novella ends in total ruin — Anselmo's unfinished dying confession, Camilla's convent death, Lothario's death in battle — and the curate's flat critical verdict ("I cannot persuade myself of its truth... it is impossible to imagine any husband so foolish") deflates the tragedy immediately, characteristic of Cervantes' layering of genres: romance, tragedy, and literary criticism juxtaposed without transition.

Providence and coincidence engineer the reunions. The narrative insists, through the curate's own words, that the meeting of all four lovers at one inn was "not, as it might seem, by accident, but by a special disposition of Providence." The recognition scene is structured as a series of unveilings — literal veils falling from faces — that force each character's private truth into public view: Luscinda's fidelity to Cardenio, Dorothea's prior claim on Don Fernando, and finally Don Fernando's capitulation, engineered as much by the assembled company's moral pressure (the curate's appeal to honor, Christian duty, and Dorothea's beauty and constancy) as by his own conscience.

Rhetoric as the instrument of resolution. Both Dorothea's and Luscinda's set-piece speeches operate as formal persuasive orations — full of anaphora, appeals to honor, religion, and reciprocal obligation — that do the narrative's actual work of resolution; Don Fernando's change of heart is presented explicitly as being "conquered" by argument ("Thou hast conquered, fair Dorothea, thou hast conquered, for it is impossible to have the heart to deny the united force of so many truths").

A new axis opens: captivity, conversion, and cross-cultural encounter. The unit's final movement introduces the captive and the Moorish Zoraida, whose insistence on the name "Maria" foreshadows a coming captivity narrative and adds a register — religious conversion, Christian-Moorish relations — distinct from the courtly love plot just resolved. Don Quixote's arms-versus-letters discourse, delivered with uncharacteristic coherence ("for the time being he made it impossible for any of his hearers to consider him a madman"), reasserts his voice as a serious rhetorician even as the surrounding company knows him for a madman — the novel repeatedly grants Don Quixote passages of real eloquence that complicate the simple portrait of folly established by scenes like the wineskin battle just pages earlier.

Notable passages

The landlord's discovery of Don Quixote mid-battle (curated as c1) is the unit's comic set-piece: the deflation from Sancho's breathless report of a slain giant to the sodden, half-dressed reality is total, and the landlord's fury previews the material reckoning (payment for the ruined wineskins) that chivalric fantasy always defers. Anselmo's unfinished last letter and the novella's terse closing judgment (c2) supply the unit's tragic register before the tone pivots entirely. Dorothea's and Luscinda's rival appeals (c3, c4) are the rhetorical engines of the recognition scene — Luscinda's image of herself as ivy clinging to "the wall" of her true husband is especially dense with the novel's marriage-as-fidelity imagery. Don Fernando's capitulation and Cardenio's answering embrace (c5) resolve both plots in a single compressed exchange. Finally, Don Quixote's aside at supper — asking who, seeing this company, would guess at their true identities (c6) — turns the inn's whole evening of unveilings back into a question about appearance and reality that the chapter has just spent itself dramatizing.
