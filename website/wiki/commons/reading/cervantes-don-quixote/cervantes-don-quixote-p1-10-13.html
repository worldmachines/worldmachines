Sancho's Balsam, the Golden Age, and the Death of Chrysostom

Commons · Don Quixote · updated 19 August 2026 · source on GitHub
After the joust with the friars' escort, Don Quixote and Sancho banter over the fictional balsam of Fierabras and a chivalric vow before lodging with goatherds, where Don Quixote delivers his Golden Age discourse on lost innocence and property, and the goatherd Pedro (followed by Vivaldo and Ambrosio) tells the tale of the scholar-turned-shepherd Chrysostom, who died of love for the independent shepherdess Marcela, whose funeral the company then attends.

This unit runs from the tail of Chapter X through the start of Chapter XIII. It opens with the immediate aftermath of Don Quixote's skirmish with the Biscayan and the friars' muleteers: Sancho kneels for his promised island, gets a lecture on the difference between cross-roads scuffles and island-winning adventures, and the pair fall into a long comic exchange about the fictitious balsam of Fierabras and a parody chivalric vow modeled on the Marquis of Mantua. They then take shelter with a company of goatherds, where Don Quixote is fed acorns and, moved by them, delivers his set-piece oration on the lost Golden Age. That evening the goatherds have the youth Antonio sing a ballad, and the unit closes with the news, delivery, and unfolding of the story of Chrysostom -- a wealthy student who abandoned scholarship for shepherd's dress out of love for Marcela, a beautiful, wealthy, and fiercely independent orphan who refuses all suitors -- culminating in the company's arrival at Chrysostom's graveside, where his friend Ambrosio delivers a eulogy just as Vivaldo opens the dead man's final poem, the "Lay of Despair," to read aloud.

Themes

Chivalric idealism deflated by domestic pragmatism. The recurring comic engine of the Quixote-Sancho relationship runs throughout: Don Quixote's grandiose remedies (the balsam), oaths (the Marquis of Mantua vow), and dietary asceticism ("it is the glory of knights-errant to go without eating for a month") collide with Sancho's hunger, fear of the Holy Brotherhood, and blunt material sense. Sancho also refuses Don Quixote's offer to "level" master and squire by seating him as an equal, preferring to eat alone, unceremoniously, over false honors he cannot use -- a small but telling assertion of his own terms of comfort against a courtesy imposed from above.

The Golden Age as a fantasy of a world before property and law. Prompted by nothing more than a handful of acorns, Don Quixote's discourse imagines an age when people "knew not the two words 'mine' and 'thine,'" when the earth gave freely and "arbitrary law had not yet established itself in the mind of the judge, for then there was no cause to judge and no one to be judged." This is a pastoral commonplace (ultimately classical, filtered through Renaissance convention), but it is also, structurally, a fantasy of a world with no legibility apparatus at all -- no boundary lines, no courts, no measured claims -- collapsing the moment the "crooked plough" and the coveting of gold arrive. The speech's total unintelligibility to its actual audience ("the goatherds... listened to him gaping in amazement without saying a word") underscores that this is a humanist commonplace performed for an audience that cannot receive it, not a real historical claim. legibility-as-machine-core

Marcela as a figure who will not be captured. The goatherd Pedro's tale, expanded by the traveler Vivaldo and confirmed by Ambrosio, builds Marcela as beautiful, wealthy, orphaned, and given real latitude by her uncle-guardian to choose her own path -- latitude she uses to become a self-sufficient shepherdess rather than a wife. The text is emphatic that her chosen liberty is not license: she keeps "such and so great vigilance... over her honour" that no suitor can boast even the smallest hope, and meets any declaration, however proper, by flinging the man off "like a catapult." The countryside reads her self-possession as cruelty and blames her for Chrysostom's death, but the narration keeps insisting on the distinction between her chosen solitude and any wrongdoing. This is a striking dramatization of a will that refuses to be absorbed into another's design -- striving to persist on its own terms against a whole social apparatus (suitors, gossip, a dead man's accusing testament) that wants to capture and moralize her. capture-resistance Conatus

Love as consuming, one-directional catastrophe. Chrysostom's arc -- wealthy, learned, admired, ultimately undone entirely by unrequited love -- and Ambrosio's eulogy ("he wooed a wild beast, he pleaded with marble, he pursued the wind, he cried to the wilderness, he served ingratitude") stage courtly love's self-annihilating logic at its starkest, setting up the debate (voiced by Vivaldo) over whether Marcela's papers should be burned per Chrysostom's dying wish or preserved as a warning -- a debate about whether a wronged party's testimony belongs to the dead man's grievance or to the public record.

Notable passages

The balsam-of-Fierabras exchange (c1) is pure chivalric-romance parody, with Don Quixote describing a cure so absolute it could reattach a man "cut in half through the middle of the body" -- the joke depends entirely on Sancho's credulous, practical follow-up questions about cost and recipe. The Golden Age speech supplies two of the unit's richest passages: the opening on the absence of "mine" and "thine" (c2) and the closer on the absence of "arbitrary law" and judges (c3), together forming a compact fantasy of a pre-legible world undone by property and the plough. Pedro's description of Marcela's guarded independence (c4) is the unit's clearest statement of her deliberate self-possession against the countryside's romantic claims on her. Ambrosio's eulogy over Chrysostom's body (c5) is the unit's emotional peak, cataloguing the asymmetry of a love that was never returned.
