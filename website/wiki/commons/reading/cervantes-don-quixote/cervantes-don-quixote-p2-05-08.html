Bargaining for the Third Sally: Teresa, the Niece, and the Road to El Toboso

Commons · Don Quixote · updated 19 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Sancho bargains with his social-climbing wife Teresa and then with Don Quixote over wages and titles, Don Quixote answers his niece's and housekeeper's objections with a discourse on lineage and the nature of knight-errantry, and after the trickster bachelor Samson Carrasco maneuvers Don Quixote into departing, master and squire ride out by night on their third sally, debating fame, saints, and immortality on the road to El Toboso.

This unit spans Chapters V through the opening of VIII of Part II. It opens with Sancho's domestic negotiation with his wife Teresa Panza over his return to Don Quixote's service and the fantasy of a governorship and a countess-daughter; moves to Don Quixote's household, where his niece and housekeeper try to talk him out of a third sally and provoke a set-piece discourse on knighthood and lineage; introduces the bachelor Samson Carrasco, who cynically encourages the very folly the women are trying to prevent; stages Sancho's wage negotiation with Don Quixote and his tearful recommitment; and ends with the pair's night departure and a long philosophical conversation on the road — on fame, immortality, and the comparative glory of saints versus knights-errant — as they approach El Toboso.

Themes

Domestic ambition versus household stability. Teresa Panza is not a comic foil so much as a rival value system to Sancho's. Her objections to Sancho's dreams of governorship and a titled daughter are not timidity but a coherent ethics of "equality" and staying within one's station — she fears exposure, mockery, and the loss of a known, sufficient life for the glittering uncertainty of rank. Sancho's counter-arguments (luck must be seized when it knocks, a daughter married up is a daughter secured) are the same acquisitive logic that drives his service to Don Quixote. The chapter frames their marriage as a running argument between security and advancement that neither side wins outright.

Lineage as a decaying or ascending structure. Don Quixote's taxonomy of the four kinds of lineage — humble origins that rise to greatness, great origins that hold steady, great origins that taper to nothing "like the point of a pyramid," and plebeian lines that never distinguish themselves — is offered to console his niece and housekeeper, but it is also a small, self-contained theory of historical rise and decline. The Ottomans exemplify ascent; Pharaohs, Ptolemies, and Caesars exemplify the collapse of once-great houses into nothing. It is a compact echo of the rise-dominance-decline pattern that structures much longer historical cycles, though Cervantes' interest is moral (virtue and generosity outlast blood) rather than institutional; readers attentive to that broader pattern may hear a distant kinship with the decay-sequence by which structures empty out from functional grandeur to nothing at all, though the text's own argument is about personal virtue, not infrastructure.

The knight-errant's claim to unmediated experience. Don Quixote's answer to the housekeeper's practical question — why not serve the king as a courtier rather than roam as a knight-errant — draws a sharp distinction between the courtier who "range[s] the world over by looking at a map" from the safety of the court, and the true knight-errant who "measure[s] the whole earth with our own feet," meeting enemies not "in pictures" but "in their own real shapes." It is Don Quixote's most explicit statement of why his madness insists on the road rather than the armchair: representation (the map, the picture, the history-book) is what courtiers and readers consume, while knight-errantry claims direct, embodied contact with the world, however delusional that contact turns out to be in practice (windmills, sheep, fulling-hammers).

Metafiction and the unreliable translator. The unit is framed twice over by narratorial doubt: the "translator" repeatedly interjects that he considers Sancho's eloquence in this chapter "apocryphal" because it exceeds his "limited intelligence," and later Hamete Benengeli opens Chapter VIII with a ritual invocation of Allah before promising the "true" adventures are about to begin. Cervantes keeps reminding the reader that this history has passed through multiple unreliable hands — translator, Moorish chronicler, and the counterfeit "history" (Avellaneda's spurious sequel) that Sancho and Don Quixote discuss anxiously on the road, worried their "honour goes dragged in the dirt" in a book they did not choose to appear in.

Fame, sainthood, and the economy of glory. The long conversation on the road to El Toboso — prompted by Don Quixote's meditation on why Horatius, Mutius, Curtius, and Caesar risked everything for a name that outlives them — turns into Sancho's deflating, characteristically material argument that friars and saints, whose relics draw lamps, kisses, and veneration, achieve "better fame in this life and in the other" than any knight-errant's, and that it would be more efficient to "set about becoming saints." The exchange stages two competing regimes of glory: chivalric fame won by risk and deed, and institutional sanctity conferred and consumed through relics, chapels, and the Church's apparatus of veneration — a comic but pointed contrast between individual heroic striving and a legitimizing institution that manufactures and markets glory on its own terms.

Notable passages

Sancho's opening confession to Teresa — that he would be glad, "if it were God's will," not to be so pleased with his decision to leave — captures the unit's central ambivalence: his conatus-like pull toward adventure and reward is inseparable from grief at leaving his family, and he cannot resolve the two, only hold them together.

Don Quixote's four-part taxonomy of lineage gives the housekeeper and niece (and the reader) his fullest statement of a historical-moral cosmology: houses rise, hold, or collapse to a point, and only virtue, not blood, secures lasting worth.

His defense of knight-errantry against the housekeeper's practical suggestion that he simply serve at court crystallizes the novel's recurring opposition between mediated, comfortable knowledge (the courtier's map) and direct, costly encounter (the errant's exposure to "sun... cold... air").

Sancho's tearful recommitment — "I do deign," delivered "with tears in his eyes" after fearing his master would leave without him — is the emotional hinge of the unit, converting a wage negotiation into a declaration of loyalty that owes nothing to profit alone.

The spare sentence describing their night departure — unseen by anyone but Samson, who walks with them half a league — marks the formal beginning of the third sally with a deliberate quietness that contrasts with the earlier village uproar.

Sancho's syllogism that sainthood outperforms knight-errantry as a path to lasting fame — "let us set about becoming saints, and we shall obtain more quickly the fair fame we are striving after" — is the unit's sharpest piece of folk-logical satire, puncturing Don Quixote's high rhetoric on glory with an appeal to the visible, transactional machinery of religious veneration.
