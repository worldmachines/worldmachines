The Meaning and Making of Don Quixote

Commons · Don Quixote · updated 19 August 2026 · source on GitHub
John Ormsby's critical essay traces Don Quixote's uniquely cosmopolitan-yet-national popularity, argues Cervantes attacked not chivalry itself (already a century dead) but its degraded theatrical mockery, reconstructs the book's unplanned, accretive composition (Sancho, Cid Hamete Benengeli), and locates its humor in the deadpan incongruity between Don Quixote's ideal and the mean, prosaic Manchegan world he moves through.

This unit is John Ormsby's critical essay (from his translator's introduction) on the character, reception, composition, and humor of Don Quixote. It moves through several linked arguments: an account of the book's unmatched and paradoxical popularity (at once the most cosmopolitan and the most intensely national of books); a polemic against both the trivializing "queer droll book" reading and the over-earnest allegorical reading that followed it; a historical claim about what Cervantes actually took aim at (a degenerate performance of chivalry, not chivalry itself, which was already dead); a reconstruction of how the novel grew without a master plan, acquiring Sancho Panza and the Cid Hamete Benengeli frame-device only as afterthoughts; a comparison of the flatter, borrowed-sentiment Don Quixote of Part I against the more discreto, individualized figure of Part II, alongside Sancho's parallel development from a plain liar into Dulcinea's indispensable inventor; and a theory of Cervantes's humor as resting on grave, unsmiling incongruity — nowhere better illustrated than in the mismatch between Don Quixote's chivalric imagination and the shabby, dignity-less plain of La Mancha.

Themes

Cosmopolitan yet intensely national. Ormsby opens with the paradox that has driven the book's centuries of diffusion: naturalized among readers who have "never seen or heard of a book of chivalry" and cannot feel the burlesque, yet as thoroughly Spanish "in character, in ideas, in sentiment, in local colour" as Tom Jones is English or Rob Roy Scotch. He credits this to the book's catholicity — "something in it for every sort of reader" — citing Cervantes's own pride that it is read by children, young people, grown men, and the old alike.

Farce first, over-solemnity after. Ormsby traces a reception arc: the book was first prized purely for its farce (the sheep, the wineskins, Mambrino's helmet, Sancho tossed in the blanket) and printed cheaply as a chap-book curiosity until England's 1738 Carteret edition dignified it; then, as admirers grew "ashamed of laughing over it," a countervailing critical fashion arose that denied the humor was primary at all and searched for hidden philosophy — culminating in the German allegorical reading of the book as the eternal war between the ideal and the real. Ormsby rejects this as anachronistic and un-Cervantic, though he grants the antagonism of Sancho and Don Quixote is a permanent human pattern, present since "the stone age."

What was actually attacked: not chivalry, but its degrading mockery. This is the essay's sharpest historical argument, and the one most resonant with world-machines thinking about institutional death and the decay-sequence. Ormsby insists Cervantes could not have "smiled Spain's chivalry away" because chivalry, being "essentially republican in its nature," had already died with the fall of Granada and could not survive Ferdinand's centralizing rule over "the free institutions of mediaeval Spain." What Cervantes swept away was a hollow, performative afterlife of chivalric costume and gesture — cavaliers "prancing and curvetting before the windows of their mistresses" — the kind of ornamental husk a dead institution leaves behind before ridicule finishes it off, per Don Felix Pacheco's testimony that after the book's appearance any man in that "celebrated drapery" was mocked as a Don Quixote himself.

Not a pessimistic book. Against readings of the ending as tragic, Ormsby draws a distinction between true enthusiasm (which the book does not punish) and spurious enthusiasm "born of vanity and self-conceit," made an end in itself and acting on mere impulse — that is what the book chastises, and rightly so in Ormsby's judgment.

Unplanned, accretive composition. Ormsby reconstructs the novel's genesis as opportunistic rather than architected: Sancho Panza was not in the original outfit and enters only via the landlord's offhand remark that knights travel with squires; the chapter divisions and the fictive source-manuscript device of Cid Hamete Benengeli were bolted on partway through, in imitation of the chivalric romances' own trick of claiming a recondite source. This is a case study in composition-by-distributed-accretion rather than singular authorial design — the kind of silent, lurching assembly the project associates with a history-machine rather than "great men" intentionality, though here it is a single author discovering his own book's shape rather than a paradigm assembling across many hands.

Part I versus Part II: the hero and squire mature. In Part I, Don Quixote is argued to have "no character or individuality whatever," merely repeating chivalric-romance sentiment; Ormsby's counter to Byron's line that "'tis his virtue makes him mad" is that the reverse is true — "it is his madness makes him virtuous." By Part II, with the characters now beloved "veritable entities to the popular imagination," Cervantes writes more confidently, makes Don Quixote reliably discreto outside chivalric matters (useful as a mouthpiece for authorial digression), and lets Sancho grow from a teller of "plain working lies" into the inventive fabricator of the three-wenches-as-Dulcinea episode, on whose "matchless mendacity" much of the second volume's plot turns.

Humor as incongruity, best read against La Mancha. Ormsby's theory of Cervantic humor rests on unsmiling gravity applied to mismatch — Don Quixote's ideal set against a deliberately unglamorous reality. He argues this is inseparable from the choice of La Mancha, "of all the regions of Spain... the last that would suggest the idea of romance," and faults illustrators (Gustave Doré by name) for missing this by prettifying the settings — turning a rude stone trough into an elaborate fountain — and thereby losing the exact point of the vigil scene. He contrasts this deadpan, self-effacing humor (shared with Swift) against the self-conscious performance of humorists like Sterne, where the audience is always aware of the author winking at them.

Notable passages

The essay's structure is essentially a sequence of set-piece arguments, each anchored in a distinct passage: the cosmopolitan/national paradox opening; the historical claim about chivalry's prior death and Pacheco's testimony about ridicule finishing off its costume-culture afterlife; the anti-pessimist reading of the book's moral; the reconstruction of Sancho's and Cid Hamete Benengeli's accidental origins; and the Doré illustration as a test case for the incongruity theory of Cervantic humor.
