The Fall of Barataria, Ricote's Exile, and the Sham Duel

Commons · Don Quixote · updated 19 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Sancho's governorship of Barataria collapses in a staged mock-siege that leaves him bruised and disabused of ambition, and on the road back to Don Quixote he meets the exiled Morisco Ricote, falls into an underground pit, and is rescued, before the unit closes with Don Quixote's near-duel against the duke's lacquey Tosilos.

This unit spans the close of Sancho Panza's mock governorship (end of Chapter LIII), his journey back toward Don Quixote and his encounter with the exiled Morisco Ricote (Chapter LIV), his misadventure falling into a pit and rescue (Chapter LV), and the opening of the farcical duel between Don Quixote and the duke's lacquey Tosilos over Doña Rodriguez's daughter (Chapter LVI). Across all four chapters, the narrative alternates between two registers: the comic dismantling of borrowed authority (Sancho's staged "siege" and his lucid renunciation of office) and a sudden vein of historical realism (Ricote's testimony on the 1609 expulsion of the Moriscos), before returning to the novel's ongoing machinery of aristocratic practical jokes and enchantment-as-alibi.

Themes

Governance as theater, dismantled by its own audience. Sancho's "government" ends not through any real crisis but through an elaborate prank — a fake night invasion staged by the duke's household, complete with shields bound so tightly he cannot move, torches, drums, and shouted battle orders from men standing on top of him. The chapter's opening meditation on time's wheel ("everything seems to go in a circle... time rolls with never-ceasing wheel") frames Sancho's fall from office as a cosmic inevitability dressed in Cide Hamete's philosophical garb, but the actual mechanism is pure spectacle: authority evaporates the instant the people who installed it decide to stage its undoing. This is governance exposed as costume rather than substance — legible titles (governor, island, siege) laid over a reality that was fictional from the start.

Sancho's renunciation as a claim to "old freedom." Rather than clinging to office, Sancho immediately and eloquently rejects it, embracing Dapple with more feeling than he ever shows toward the trappings of rule, and delivering a speech that inverts the usual logic of ambition: "I was not born to be a governor... A reaping-hook fits my hand better than a governor's sceptre." His insistence on leaving exactly as poor as he arrived ("naked I was born, naked I find myself") and his later refusal of Ricote's treasure for the same reason mark a consistent ethic: he will not be captured by wealth or office, preferring a life of plain subsistence he can actually control. This reads as a small but genuine instance of a figure refusing capture by an external apparatus of governance and prestige in favor of self-continuity — his own version of conatus, and a move legible as capture-resistance against the duke's whole administrative game.

Ricote's testimony: legibility as instrument of expulsion. The unit's most historically weighted passage is Ricote's first-person account of the 1609 royal edict expelling the Moriscos from Spain. Ricote frames the expulsion as a bureaucratic-legal act ("the proclamation or edict... positive enactments which would be enforced at the appointed time") that classified and removed an entire population regardless of individual loyalty, scattering families between France, Germany, and Barbary, and leaving Ricote himself in a permanent in-between state — unwelcome in Africa, sentimentally bound to a homeland that expelled him, disguised as a German pilgrim to move through it undetected. This is the underside of the era's drive toward classification and control: a population made legible to the state chiefly in order to be sorted and expelled. The resulting condition — belonging fully to neither the old order nor the new, sustained only by disguise and improvised community with fellow pilgrims — resonates with the interregnum logic of a gramsci-gap, though here it is imposed on a person rather than a civilizational transition.

The underworld interlude as anti-chivalric parody. Sancho's fall into the pit deliberately echoes Don Quixote's earlier descent into the Cave of Montesinos, but where his master reported marvels, Sancho expects only "toads and adders," and gets nothing but discomfort, fear, and mundane physical effort (feeling along smooth walls, digging with a stone, coaxing Dapple through a narrow gap). The joke is structural: chivalric romance supplies enchanted otherworlds to those who believe in them, and flat, frightening holes in the ground to those who don't. Don Quixote's arrival to rescue him, and his instinctive theological reasoning about whether Sancho might be a soul in purgatory, folds the rescue back into the novel's persistent confusion between literal and enchanted registers.

Enchantment as a ready-made excuse. The Tosilos duel, only begun within this unit, already shows the household using "enchanters" as an all-purpose explanation the moment reality becomes inconvenient — anticipating how Don Quixote himself will later explain away Tosilos's unmasking by blaming malicious magicians rather than admit the duke's practical joke. The device that earlier let Don Quixote rationalize his failures now becomes contagious, offered by the duke himself as a face-saving fiction.

Notable passages

The circle-of-time overture recasts Sancho's fall as fated rather than merely engineered — a bit of narratorial misdirection the chapter itself undercuts by showing exactly how contrived the "siege" was. Sancho's farewell to Dapple is the emotional center of the political material: an embrace of the actual, load-bearing partner of his "toils and sorrows" set against the abstraction of the office he is abandoning. His speech to the majordomo generalizes the renunciation into a creed (better a reaping-hook than a sceptre), and his final account to the duke and duchess turns the whole episode into a considered verdict on power ("he would not give anything to be governor... of the whole world"). Ricote's long testimony is the unit's most sustained piece of historical reportage inside the fiction, giving documentary weight to an expulsion the rest of the novel treats only in passing. And Sancho's underground soliloquy, measuring himself against Don Quixote's Montesinos vision, is the clearest statement of his own disenchanted, body-first relationship to the marvelous.
