Guanyin Assembles the Pilgrimage

Commons · Monkey King · updated 26 August 2026 · source on GitHub
The Buddha commissions Guanyin to find a scripture pilgrim in the east, and on her journey she converts three banished, monstrous exiles — the future Sandy and Pigsy, and a condemned dragon prince — into disciples-in-waiting, then visits Sun Wukong, still imprisoned beneath Five-Phases Mountain, and extracts his promise to join the pilgrimage.

Five hundred years after Monkey's rebellion, the Buddha at Thunderclap Monastery decides the sinful, ignorant Land of the East must be saved by scripture, and sends the Bodhisattva Guanyin to find a mortal pilgrim willing to fetch the sutras from the west. Guanyin, accompanied by her disciple Hui'an, travels east at low altitude, and along the way she converts three banished, monstrous exiles — a flesh-eating river demon, a lecherous pig-monster, and a condemned dragon prince — into future disciples who will atone for their heavenly crimes by protecting the pilgrim. She then finds Sun Wukong still trapped beneath Five-Phases Mountain, extracts his promise of reform, and finally reaches Chang'an to begin the search for the pilgrim himself.

Themes

Punishment as narrative machinery. Every monster Guanyin meets has an identical structure: a heavenly official commits a minor transgression (breaking a cup, drunken harassment, an accidental fire), is savagely overpunished by the Jade Emperor, and degenerates into a man-eating exile. The chapter uses this repeated pattern — crime, disproportionate celestial punishment, monstrous exile, eventual redemption through service — as the mechanism that populates the pilgrimage's cast, turning bureaucratic overreaction into monster-genesis.

Survival as its own argument. The pig-monster's retort to Guanyin's moralizing — "So what was I supposed to eat—the wind?" — and the sand-monster's frank accounting of eating passersby out of "cold and hunger" both frame predation not as innate wickedness but as a desperate, almost reasonable response to abandonment and hunger. This is the text's most vivid statement of raw persistence-drive overriding law or doctrine, a conatus-like insistence on continuing to exist by whatever means available before religion offers a better path — see Conatus.

Conversion as bureaucratic mercy, not moral transformation. Guanyin's method with each monster is transactional: join the mission, atone, and you regain your old post in Heaven. She does not argue that eating people is wrong in the abstract so much as that continuing to do so forecloses a path back to legitimacy. The pig-monster capitulates not because he is convinced of virtue but because Guanyin offers him an exit from a "dead end" — redemption is administrative reinstatement, not conscience.

Monkey's punishment is explicitly framed as pedagogical, not final. Guanyin's improvised poem asks "When will he be freed to make use of his talents?" — signaling from the outset that Monkey's five-hundred-year imprisonment is a holding pattern awaiting the right vehicle (the pilgrimage) rather than a permanent sentence, mirroring the other monsters' arcs of crime, banishment, and conditional restoration.

Notable passages

The Buddha's opening speech sets the whole mission in motion, characterizing the eastern lands as needing rescue via scripture (c1). Guanyin's five treasures, especially the tightening hoops meant for "a monster with extraordinary magic powers," establish the coercive backstop underlying the pilgrimage's discipline (c2). The river monster's confession — his identity as the disgraced General of Curtain-Drawing, his flying-sword torment, and his grim habit of stringing pilgrims' skulls as a necklace — gives Sandy's later, placid demeanor a startling prior history (c3). The pig-monster's parallel confession and his blunt survival logic against Guanyin's moralizing dramatize the chapter's clearest statement of hunger overriding doctrine, before he relents and asks how he might atone (c4). The dragon prince's plea and Guanyin's intercession with the Jade Emperor show the same crime-punishment-redemption pattern compressed to its simplest form (c5). Finally, Guanyin's poem addressed to the buried Monkey, and his indignant recognition of her, reopen the story's central unfinished business — his eventual release — while confirming his awareness of exactly how long and how bitterly he has been waiting (c6).
