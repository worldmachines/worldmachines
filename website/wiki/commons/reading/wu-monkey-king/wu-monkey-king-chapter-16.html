Monkey Unmasks the Pig Demon of Gao Village

Commons · Monkey King · updated 26 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Tripitaka and Monkey arrive at Gao Village, where Monkey bullies information out of a reluctant local, poses as an exorcist, disguises himself as the imprisoned bride to trap her monstrous husband, and exposes the demon (soon to be Zhu Bajie) by needling him into confessing his own backstory before the mere mention of the Great Sage Equal to Heaven sends him fleeing.

Tripitaka and Monkey arrive at dusk in the prosperous-looking Gao Village, where the elderly landowner Mr. Gao is desperate to be rid of a demon who married into his family three years earlier, at first as a hardworking live-in son-in-law, but who has grown steadily more porcine, ravenous, and menacing, and has locked the youngest daughter, Emerald Orchid, away from her family for half a year. Monkey extracts the whole story first from a fleeing kinsman, Gao Cai, then from Mr. Gao himself, before disguising himself as the missing daughter to ambush the returning "son-in-law." Through a mix of physical comedy, feigned illness, and a fabricated domestic complaint, Monkey coaxes the demon into confessing his own name, origin, and grievances — and then casually drops the name of the Great Sage Equal to Heaven, at which the unmasked demon's composure collapses and he flees, setting up his eventual identity and recruitment as Zhu Bajie (Pigsy).

Themes

Trickster interrogation as method. Nothing in this chapter is learned by simple asking. Monkey physically restrains Gao Cai until he talks, badgers Mr. Gao's own account out of him mid-hospitality, and then most elaborately impersonates the captive daughter to interrogate the demon from inside his own bed. Information here is always pried loose through coercion, mockery, or disguise rather than volunteered — the recurring engine of how these monster-hunting episodes get their exposition.

The demon's own defense reframes monstrosity as labor. When "Stiff-Bristles" believes he is talking to his wife, he does not deny his ugliness or appetite; he defends the marriage bargain on its economic merits — hard, uncomplaining farm labor in exchange for food and comfort. His account (chunk c3) is the funniest and most sympathetic moment in the chapter precisely because it is delivered in total innocence of who is actually listening.

Reputation as a weapon that precedes the blow. The demon is routed not by force but by a name. The instant Monkey has his disguised self mention that the family might hire "the Great Sage Equal to Heaven," the color drains from the demon's face and he abandons the marriage outright — legend doing the work combat has not yet needed to do (chunk c4).

Comic domestic bureaucracy and the marriage economy. Mr. Gao's real anguish is social standing as much as safety: a demon son-in-law is shameful because it forecloses "hobnobbing" with respectable in-laws, not primarily because it is dangerous. The whole affair is framed in the vocabulary of prenuptial agreements, dowries, and family name — a household negotiation gone monstrously wrong rather than a straightforward menace.

Notable passages

The chapter opens on pastoral calm — the village Tripitaka spots "as dusk fell one evening" (c1) — before the household's hidden crisis surfaces. Gao Cai's furious recap of "three years ago, a monster mov[ing] in" (c2) supplies the inciting complaint: a coerced marriage, an imprisoned bride, and a string of failed exorcists. Once inside the trap, the demon's unguarded self-justification of his farm labor (c3) briefly humanizes him before Monkey's counterfeit complaint about "the Great Sage Equal to Heaven" (c4) shatters his nerve and sends him bolting. Framing all of it is Monkey's opening tactical move — transforming into "the exact likeness of the girl" (c5) — the disguise that makes the whole interrogation and unmasking possible.
