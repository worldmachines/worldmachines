The River Turtle and the Land of Women

Commons · Monkey King · updated 26 August 2026 · source on GitHub
The pilgrims cross the River to Heaven on the back of a self-taught, centuries-old turtle who begs Tripitaka to ask the Buddha how he might finally acquire a human body, then travel on into the all-female kingdom of Western Liang, where Tripitaka and Pigsy accidentally fall pregnant by drinking from the Mother-and-Child River and Monkey must trick a vengeful immortal out of Abortion Spring water to cure them.

This unit spans the back half of Chapter Twenty-Eight and all of Chapter Twenty-Nine. The pilgrims are ferried across the four-hundred-mile River to Heaven by an elderly turtle who has spent thirteen centuries cultivating himself toward a human body, then continue into Western Liang, the Land of Women, where Tripitaka and Pigsy drink from the wrong river, fall magically pregnant, and must be cured with water that Monkey steals from a hostile immortal holding a grudge from an earlier adventure.

Themes

Self-cultivation has a ceiling, and the text is honest about it. The turtle has spent thirteen hundred years lengthening his life, lightening his body, and teaching himself human speech — extraordinary discipline — and still cannot shed his shell. His request to Tripitaka is modest and poignant: not scripture or salvation, just a question relayed to the Buddha about how to finish becoming human. The episode treats striving-to-transcend-one's-given-form as a real, decades-spanning labor rather than a plot device, which is the same register in which the poem later shows Monkey and company's own bodies (also non-human, also striving toward something) — a resonance with the Conatus idea of a being's effort to persist and expand beyond its present constraints, though the text never states this explicitly and the payoff (the Buddha's answer) is deferred past this unit.

The Land of Women inverts the pilgrims' expectations of danger and safety in one comic swoop. Western Liang has no men; the population's laughter at the pilgrims' arrival, initially read as menace, turns out to be delight. But the old woman's aside — that a house of young women would have forced the men to have sex and then skinned them for perfume bags — reveals that the register was never purely comic: gender inversion here still encodes real danger, just redirected. The chapter gets its laughs (Pigsy's relief that he is "too smelly" to be perfumed) without fully defusing the threat underneath it.

Bodily comedy carries the plot's actual stakes. The Mother-and-Child River sequence turns pregnancy — normally outside the male disciples' bodily vocabulary entirely — into a slapstick emergency, complete with Pigsy trying to spread nonexistent birth canals and Sandy needling him about umbilical cords. The humor doesn't cheapen the episode's structure, though: it is a textbook obstacle-and-remedy unit (poisoned water paired with a single named cure, Abortion Spring, guarded by a single antagonist) of the kind that recurs throughout the pilgrimage.

The demon world is a small, gossiping family, and old debts don't expire. The True Immortal of Wish Fulfillment turns out to be uncle to Red Boy (defeated and reassigned by Guanyin in an earlier unit) and brother to the Bull Demon King, one of Monkey's old sworn brothers. The confrontation only escalates because Wish Fulfillment already has a letter of grievance waiting; Monkey's breezy claim that Red Boy is better off as Guanyin's attendant does nothing to cool him down. The episodic quest structure keeps stitching itself together this way — every new obstacle turns out to be someone's relative.

Notable passages

The turtle's request (c1) is the emotional center of the river-crossing scene: thirteen hundred years of cultivation and he still "cannot escape my turtle shell." The old woman's explanation of the Mother-and-Child River (c2) is the unit's expository hinge, converting a simple drink of water into the chapter's entire crisis with deadpan procedural detail (the Embryo-Reflecting Spring, the twentieth-birthday rule). The pregnancy-panic exchange between Tripitaka, Pigsy, and Sandy (c3) is the broadest comic set piece, playing the male disciples' total unfamiliarity with childbirth for maximum absurdity. The account of what young women in Western Liang would have done to the pilgrims (c4) is the passage that keeps the chapter's humor from becoming toothless — it is the one moment the danger is stated plainly rather than played as farce. Finally, the diversion-and-theft sequence at Dissolving-Maleness Mountain (c5) shows the pilgrims' now-familiar teamwork solution to a guarded resource: Monkey draws the fight away while Sandy breaks in and fills the bucket.
