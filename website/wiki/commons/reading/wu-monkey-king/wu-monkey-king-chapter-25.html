Cart-Slow Kingdom: The Slaves of the Three Pure Ones

Commons · Monkey King · updated 26 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Monkey infiltrates Cart-Slow Kingdom, discovers that three Taoist "immortals" who ended a twenty-year drought have enslaved and are killing the local Buddhist monks, kills two of the sect's young enforcers after they refuse to free the slaves, and that night leads Pigsy and Sandy in a raid on the Taoists' temple, impersonating the Three Pure Ones and dumping their statues in a latrine before the trio is nearly caught.

The pilgrims arrive outside Cart-Slow, a city where Buddhist monks are worked as slave labor by order of a king who, twenty years earlier, credited three Taoist priests with ending a catastrophic drought. Monkey disguises himself as a wandering Taoist to learn the city's history, executes two of the sect's arrogant young enforcers when they refuse to free the monks, and reveals his true identity and mission. That night, unable to sleep, he spies on a lavish Taoist ritual, recruits Pigsy and Sandy, and the three infiltrate the temple: they eat the ritual offerings, replace the statues of the Taoist Trinity with their own transformed bodies, and dispose of the real idols in a latrine — a scheme that nearly succeeds until a returning acolyte overhears them breathing.

Themes

Religious authority as transactional patronage. The king's devotion to Taoism is explicitly a payment for services rendered — rain summoned during a drought that Buddhist prayer failed to break — and the same transactional logic runs backward: when the Buddhists lost the contest, the king "destroyed all the monasteries and images of the Buddha and gave all the monks to us as slaves." Faith in this world is not belief but a supply contract, renewable or revocable by demonstrated utility, and its losers are stripped of both institutions and bodies.

Endurance and hope under total capture. The enslaved monks describe a surveillance regime ("distributed portraits of us all across the kingdom") that forecloses escape, alongside repeated, failed suicide attempts — "our knives were too blunt and our nooses broke." Their only recourse is a nightly, half-superstitious hope pinned on prophecy: a monkey disciple who will restore their order. The passage reads as a study in bare persistence — striving to endure without any material means of resistance — close to what WMP calls Conatus, the drive to persist that capture regimes work to grind down.

Desecration as narrative justice. Monkey's solution to the Taoists' religious authority is not argument but literal defilement: he and his companions displace the statues of the Jade Pure One, the Supreme Pure One, and Laozi, install themselves as the idols, and hurl the originals into a latrine Monkey rebrands the "Bureau of Rice Reincarnation." The gag stages, almost too literally, an object's fall from cultic centrality to discarded refuse — the same collapse WMP's decay-sequence traces from Functional through Ornamental to Absent, compressed here into a single night's prank.

Farce escalating toward confrontation. Pigsy's gluttony ("Make way for Pigsy!"), his relish at fouling the statues, and Monkey's deadpan euphemisms keep the episode comic even as it sets up real stakes: the three Great Immortals still rule the city, and the unit ends on the raid's exposure, with alarmed Taoists reconvening "with lamps and torches" — a cliffhanger pointing toward the magical contest to come.

Notable passages

The Taoists' own origin story for their rule — drought, failed Buddhist prayer, miraculous rain — establishes the transactional basis of the king's devotion and the casual cruelty ("They now rule over us") with which converts describe their own subjugation.

The monks' testimony of enslavement lays out the totalizing character of their captivity: bounty portraits, botched suicides, and a prophecy that is their only surviving form of agency, ending on the hope that Monkey will "destroy the Taoists and restore Buddhism in Cart-Slow."

Monkey's killing of the two young Taoists and his subsequent reveal — undercut by his own joke ("Is that a flying iguana?") — marks the pivot from reconnaissance to open war against the sect, and sets his promise to confront the king directly.

The temple raid's centerpiece, in which the disciples displace and then dispatch the statues of the Three Pure Ones to a latrine, is the unit's most sustained comic set piece and its clearest thematic image: sacred authority reduced to household waste.

The closing mishap — a returning acolyte's dropped handbell and the alarm it raises — closes the unit on unresolved tension, the trio's disguise blown just as the night's mischief seemed complete.
