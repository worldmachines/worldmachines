The Freeing of Monkey and the Golden Fillet

Commons · Monkey King · updated 26 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Tripitaka frees Sun Wukong — the “Great Sage Equal to Heaven,” trapped for five centuries beneath the Mountain of Two Frontiers — who joins him as disciple and bodyguard, but Monkey's casual slaughter of a tiger and then six robbers provokes the pilgrimage's first moral rupture, after which Guanyin (disguised as an old woman) equips Tripitaka with a golden fillet and binding spell that gives him permanent, pain-based control over his volatile disciple.

Guided by the hunter Boqin, Tripitaka discovers the monkey sealed for five hundred years beneath the Mountain of Two Frontiers, lifts the Buddha's golden talisman to release him, and gains Sun Wukong — the self-titled "Great Sage Equal to Heaven" — as a disciple already promised to him by Guanyin. Almost immediately Monkey's raw, undomesticated power surfaces: he slaughters a tiger for its skin, then kills six robbers who confront the travelers, and when Tripitaka rebukes him for taking life so freely, Monkey flies off in a rage. Guanyin, disguised as an old woman, intercepts Tripitaka and gives him a shirt, a cap, and a secret spell; when a repentant Monkey returns and tries the garments on, the cap's hidden golden fillet fuses to his skull, and Tripitaka discovers he can inflict unbearable pain on his disciple at will, permanently ending Monkey's capacity for open rebellion.

Themes

Liberation is a covenant, not an escape. Monkey's release from the stone casket is never framed as freedom in itself — Guanyin has already brokered the terms ("if I swear off violence, worship the Law of the Buddha, and escort the scripture pilgrim to the west, the Buddha will reward me"), so the lifting of the seal simply converts one form of captivity (five hundred years of physical imprisonment) into another (bonded service). The chapter's real business is establishing what kind of leash will replace the mountain.

Untamed power against doctrinal restraint. Monkey's fight scenes — smashing the tiger's skull in one blow, butchering six armed men who spend ten fruitless minutes hacking at his head — establish him as a figure of near-limitless, self-organizing capability ("I can grow as big as the universe or shrink to smaller than a hair"), a striving, autonomous force very close to what the World Machines vocabulary calls Conatus. Tripitaka's horror at the killings ("How can you hope to become a Buddhist monk if you take lives so recklessly?") sets up the book's central axis: compassionate legibility versus capture-resistant appetite. Monkey's own retort — "I killed more people than I can remember. I'd never have gotten to be the Great Sage Equal to Heaven otherwise" — insists that his past power and his past violence are inseparable, which is precisely what the pilgrimage now needs to suppress without extinguishing.

The six senses as highwaymen. The robbers' names — Eyes-That-See-and-Delight, Ears-That-Hear-and-Rage, Nose-That-Smells-and-Loves, Tongue-That-Tastes-and-Desires, Mind-That-Sees-and-Lusts, Body-That-Sustains-and-Suffers — are the Buddhist "six thieves" of sense-desire made literal and armed. That Monkey butchers them outright, rather than merely resisting temptation, is the joke and the problem at once: he defeats sensory desire by main force, a solution Tripitaka finds as troubling as the vice it eliminates.

A gift that is actually a leash. The shirt and cap arrive framed as generosity — heirlooms of a dead monk, offered by a kindly stranger — and Tripitaka compounds the deception by lying about their provenance ("they're just a couple of things I've had since I was a boy"). The device only becomes visible as an instrument of capture-resistance-defeat once it is already irreversible: the golden wire "had taken root around his scalp," and Monkey's own attempts to physically pry it off fail. This is control administered through consent and affection rather than force — Monkey chooses to put the cap on — which makes it a sharper illustration of capture than the mountain ever was: the mountain merely contained him, the fillet re-engineers what containment can do to him at any future moment of defiance.

External correction outside the religious frame. The Dragon King's telling of Zhang Liang and Huang Shigong — patience and humility rewarded with a text of strategy and eventual dynastic founding — gives Monkey a secular, Confucian-flavored reason to return to Tripitaka ("don't jeopardize your long-term future in the pursuit of instant gratification") that operates independently of Buddhist doctrine, showing the pilgrimage's disciplinary pressure coming from multiple directions at once, not just from Tripitaka's scolding.

Notable passages

Monkey's self-introduction ("I am the Great Sage Equal to Heaven...") supplies his entire backstory and the terms of his bondage in a single speech, anchoring everything that follows. The tiger-killing passage stages the disciple's power against the master's cowardice in miniature. The robbers' self-naming makes the six-senses allegory explicit before Monkey guts it of subtlety by killing them for real. The extended argument that follows — "What horror have you committed?" through Tripitaka's warning that Monkey will "never get to the west" if he keeps killing — is the chapter's moral center, the first open airing of the tension that the golden fillet exists to resolve by force rather than persuasion. The binding scene itself, from Monkey trying on the shirt to the wire that "had taken root around his scalp," is the chapter's structural payoff: the instrument that will govern the rest of the pilgrimage.
