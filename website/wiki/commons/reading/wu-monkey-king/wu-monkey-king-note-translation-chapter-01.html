A Note on the Translation, and the Birth of Monkey

Commons · Monkey King · updated 26 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Julia Lovell explains the principles behind her abridged translation of Journey to the West before the novel proper opens with the stone monkey's birth from an egg on Flower-Fruit Mountain, his winning of kingship by daring the waterfall, and his ten-year voyage to the Patriarch Subodhi, who names him Sun Wukong after mortality-fear drives him from his four-hundred-year paradise.

This unit joins two very different texts: Julia Lovell's short methodological preface explaining how and why she abridged and translated the hundred-chapter Journey to the West, and then the novel's actual opening — Chapter One, in which a stone egg on Flower-Fruit Mountain hatches into a monkey who wins kingship of his troop, grows afraid of death, and sets out on a decade-long quest that ends with the immortal Subodhi naming him Sun Wukong.

Themes

Abridgement as thematic triage, not mere cutting. Lovell frames her quarter-length version not as a summary but as a substitution strategy: she omits whole episodes (the Buddha's scheme to seduce Pigsy, a monastery of thieving monks, an evil doppelgänger, spider fiends playing soccer) but insists the themes those episodes carry — "the temptations of the senses; Monkey's struggles with multiple selves; the novel's extraordinary, inventive fantasy" — survive elsewhere in what she kept. This is a translator's version of the WMP's own decay-sequence logic: content can vanish while function is preserved through redistribution, so long as someone is deliberately tracking which functions matter.

Translation as negotiated fidelity. Lovell names the translator's split allegiance — "to the original text and to readers of the target language" — and is candid that she sometimes sacrifices "technical, linguistic fidelity to be true to the overall tone," cutting untranslatable wordplay rather than burying jokes under footnotes. She also strips the oral-storytelling scaffolding (recapitulations, descriptive poems) that a sixteenth-century marketplace audience needed but a modern reader does not, again trading local texture for readability at scale.

Origin without lineage. The stone monkey has no parents: a rock "nourished by heaven and earth, the sun and the moon" gestates an egg that hatches into a fully formed creature. This matters later — when Subodhi asks his name and parentage, Monkey can only say "I have no parents" and describe the rock splitting open. His entire selfhood is self-authored, cosmological rather than genealogical.

Merit through ordeal, not birth. Kingship of the mountain is not inherited or assumed; it is a wager the monkeys set themselves ("Whoever dares pass through the waterfall... can be our king") and the stone monkey wins it by literally leaping through the unknown twice — once to scout, once to lead. Sovereignty here is performative and immediately deliverable, with no debate once the deed is done.

Mortality as the engine of the quest. After three or four centuries of undisturbed pleasure, it is the thought of eventual death — falling "into the clutches of Yama, King of the Underworld" — that breaks the monkey king's contentment and sends him voyaging for a decade in search of "Buddhas, immortals, and holy sages" who alone escape the wheel of life and death. The striving to persist against dissolution — a very literal Conatus — is what turns a comfortable king into a pilgrim; his subjects, by contrast, are content precisely because they have not yet let the fact of death become a thought.

Naming as awakening, not identity fixed at birth. Subodhi assigns Monkey both a surname (Sun, stripped of its "animal radical") and a generational given name (Wukong, "awoken to emptiness") drawn from a rotating twelve-character cycle shared by all his disciples — identity here is relationally conferred, not innate. The narrator glosses the naming directly: "at the beginning of everything, there were no names — only emptiness," making Monkey's baptism a small enactment of the philosophy the rest of the novel will test him against.

Notable passages

The translator's note passages (c1, c2) matter less for plot than for establishing how much of the "real" Journey to the West this abridgement necessarily leaves off-page — a caveat worth remembering whenever later units seem to skip narrative ground. The origin passage (c3) is the mythic hinge of the entire saga: everything Monkey later becomes traces back to being born of stone rather than of woman. The waterfall passage (c4) establishes the book's recurring pattern of trial-by-daring as the route to status. The mortality/quest passage (c5) is the true narrative engine of Chapter One — paradise is not enough once its ending is imagined — and directly sets up the pilgrimage structure the rest of the novel will run on. The naming passage (c6) closes the unit by giving Monkey the name he will carry for the remaining ninety-nine chapters, and frames that name as a philosophical claim about emptiness underlying all identity.
