Chapter Nineteen: Three Strikes Against the White-Bone Demon

Commons · Monkey King · updated 26 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Sun Wukong (Monkey) kills the shapeshifting White-Bone Demon in three successive disguises to protect Tripitaka, but Pigsy's jealous slander convinces their master that each killing is murder, and after the third reveals her true skeletal form Tripitaka banishes Monkey from the pilgrimage anyway.

Having escaped the tree-spirit's honey-trap and made peace with the wronged horticulturalist, the pilgrims cross a beast-ridden mountain where a lone fiend — eventually unmasked as the White-Bone Demon — makes three attempts to isolate and devour Tripitaka for the life-prolonging virtue of his flesh. She appears first as a lovely girl bearing (poisoned) alms food, then as the girl's grieving mother, then as the mother's grieving husband; each time Monkey recognizes her instantly and kills her illusory body with his staff, and each time her spirit escapes, leaving a false corpse behind. Pigsy, resentful and self-interested, twice persuades Tripitaka that Monkey has murdered an innocent person, and even when the third killing finally exposes a skeleton inscribed THE WHITE-BONE DEMON, Pigsy's needling wins out again. Tripitaka writes Monkey a formal letter of banishment; Monkey bows farewell through four self-multiplied doubles and departs in tears, leaving Pigsy and Sandy to continue toward India without him.

Themes

Sight versus credulity. Monkey is the only member of the party who perceives the demon truthfully in all three of her guises, and he grounds his authority to see through illusion in his own criminal past — he explains that he used to shapeshift into gold, silver, or a beautiful woman to lure victims when he was "a full-time demon" himself. Tripitaka's holiness and Pigsy's lust both function as forms of blindness in the chapter: piety and appetite alike disarm scrutiny, while only the reformed trickster retains the trickster's eye.

Truth defeated by narrative. The chapter does not end in ambiguity — Monkey produces literal, legible proof each time (writhing maggots instead of rice, a skeleton labeled with the demon's name), yet proof is repeatedly overridden by Pigsy's self-interested retelling. The third unmasking is the clearest case: the evidence is inscribed on the body itself, and it still loses to slander.

Instrumental loyalty and the logic of use. Facing expulsion, Monkey names his own situation with a proverb about discarding a hunting dog once the game is caught, and pointedly reminds Tripitaka that the headache spell (the instrument of his continued submission) will still be needed the moment real danger returns. The passage frames loyalty here as transactional and coerced rather than freely given — a striving to remain useful and present (Conatus) that persists even under active suppression.

The golden hoop as a technology of capture. Monkey twice asks, unsuccessfully, to have the hoop removed, and it is precisely his inability to shed it — imposed by Guanyin, tightened by Tripitaka's recitation — that keeps him bound to a master who is actively casting him out. The hoop's one-way design (a Tight Hoop Spell with no matching release) makes explicit that Monkey's obedience is enforced rather than chosen, a small but sharp instance of the tension between autonomy and imposed control that recurs across the pilgrimage (capture-resistance).

Violence as tragic necessity, not wantonness. Before the third strike, Monkey works through his dilemma in an internal monologue parodying Hamlet's soliloquy: sparing the demon risks Tripitaka's capture, killing her risks the headache spell. The episode refuses to let either choice be costless, staging Monkey's violence as the only available action inside a trap rather than as unprovoked cruelty.

Notable passages

Monkey's confession of his own shapeshifting past is the chapter's clearest statement of why he, and only he, can see through the demon's disguises — he has literally used the same tricks himself. His parody of Hamlet's soliloquy over whether "to smash or not to smash" turns the third killing into a deliberated, cornered choice rather than an impulsive one, weighing present violence against future catastrophe. The unmasking scene is the chapter's hinge: a skeleton inscribed with the demon's own name is produced as proof, and Pigsy's slander defeats it anyway, converting an epistemic victory into a social defeat. Monkey's proverb about the dog fried after the rabbits are caught gives voice, in his own words, to the transactional loyalty he suspects he has been shown. And the final image — four self-multiplied Monkeys bowing from every side so that Tripitaka cannot avoid the gesture — closes the chapter on an act of ritual devotion offered by a servant who has just been formally, unjustly, discarded.
