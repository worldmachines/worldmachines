Six Dethroned Kings at the Venice Carnival

Commons · Candide · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
At a Venetian inn during Carnival, Candide is reunited with the enslaved Cacambo and dines with six dethroned kings, whose serial confessions of lost power, set against Theodore's destitution and Candide's diamond, turn fortune's reversals into a comic procession.

Candide and Martin are about to sup at their inn in Venice when a soot-faced man seizes Candide's arm: it is Cacambo, now a slave, who reports that Cunégonde is at Constantinople and tells Candide to be ready to leave after supper. At table with six foreign guests, Candide watches servants (one of them Cacambo) whisper to their masters, "your majesty," that ships and post-chaises are ready; the sixth servant warns that credit has run out and he must look to himself. Candide, taking it for Carnival masquerade, asks how they all came to be kings. Each stranger then declares himself in turn: Achmet III, Ivan, Charles-Edward, two kings of Poland, and Theodore of Corsica. All are deposed and in Venice "to spend the Carnival". Candide gives the penniless Theodore a diamond worth a hundred times the kings' twenty sequins each, which puzzles them. Four more deposed highnesses arrive, and Candide ignores them, thinking only of Constantinople and Cunégonde.

Themes

Fortune's reversals as a refrain. Every king repeats the same closing formula, "I am here to spend the Carnival at Venice", which makes ruin into a recurring joke and a social routine. The text states these losses (brothers and nephews dethroning each other, a childhood spent in prison, near a thousand friends executed) in flat first-person narration, so the horror of the content sits against the comic regularity of the delivery. This is satirical rhythm, not an argument that the narrator makes outright.

Providence as consolation, and its limits. The fourth king says "I resign myself to the will of Providence", and the fifth says Providence has given him other dominions where he has done more good. This is the vocabulary of Pangloss's doctrine (panglossian-optimism, the-best-of-all-possible-worlds) in the mouths of the deposed. It is an interpretation, but the passage reads as showing resignation used as a way to absorb loss rather than answer it. Theodore, who offers a plain inventory of loss rather than a providential gloss, is the one who gets help.

Power as precarious credit. The sixth servant's remark that "they will give no more credit to your majesty or to me" connects royal standing to debt and reputation. Majesty is something the household must keep up, and lapses when money fails. Candide's gift shows the reverse: a private person with a diamond outspends the kings. The five princes' question "Who can this private person be?" marks wealth as the thing that now confers rank.

Martin's role and Candide's distraction. Martin only watches coolly, and the unit gives him no speech. Candide's attention is fixed on Cunégonde at Constantinople; the four extra highnesses get "no manner of notice". The pattern of Candide's search overriding what he sees recurs across the book.

Notable passages

Cacambo's reappearance (c1). Cacambo's whisper, "I am a slave, and my master waits for me", gives the reunion its comic-pathos shape: joy at recovering a friend and the instruction to say nothing, plus the news that Cunégonde is at Constantinople. This launches the movement toward the final act.

The servants' cues (c2). The six servants dismiss their masters one by one, with the last frankly saying he must save himself. The scene works as a staged reveal, and Candide's misreading as a Carnival masquerade sets up the confessions.

Achmet, Ivan, Charles-Edward (c3). Three accounts of dynastic violence: sibling and nephew coups, a deposed infant raised in prison, and a claimant whose followers were executed. The text does not name the political cause beyond the characters' own words.

The Polish kings (c4). Both invoke Providence; the second claims to have done more good elsewhere than all the Sarmatian kings on the Vistula, a boast within the humility.

Theodore and the diamond (c5). Theodore's antitheses (money coined, not a ducat; two secretaries, no valet; throne, then London jail straw) are the most concrete statement of descent in the unit. The kings' twenty sequins against Candide's diamond closes the scene on a joke about wealth and rank.
