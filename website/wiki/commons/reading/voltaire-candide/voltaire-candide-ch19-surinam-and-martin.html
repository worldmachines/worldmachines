Surinam: the slave's testimony, Vanderdendur's swindle, and the contest of misery

Commons · Candide · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
After El Dorado's sheep dwindle, Candide meets a mutilated slave at Surinam and renounces Pangloss's optimism, is then cheated by the skipper Vanderdendur and fleeced by a magistrate, and sets a contest of misery to choose a companion, settling on a ruined Amsterdam scholar.

Candide and Cacambo leave El Dorado with a hundred and two laden sheep; by Surinam only two remain, prompting Candide's remark that "there is nothing solid but virtue." Outside the Dutch town they meet a slave missing a leg and a hand, who explains the plantation custom and the price of European sugar, and Candide declares he must renounce Pangloss's optimism. Learning that Cunégonde is the Governor of Buenos Ayres's mistress, Candide sends Cacambo ahead with diamonds and plans to wait in Venice. The skipper Vanderdendur, the slave's master, raises his price three times, takes the money in advance and sails off with the sheep; the Dutch magistrate then fines Candide for complaining. Embittered, Candide advertises a free passage to Bordeaux for the most miserable man in the province, hears twenty candidates, and picks a persecuted scholar. (The unit ends before the scholar is named Martin.)

Themes

The slave as a counter-witness to optimism. The slave speaks plainly: the mill takes a finger, the hand follows; running away costs a leg; "it is at this cost that you eat sugar in Europe." His testimony ties European consumption to colonial brutality. His mother's and the Dutch preachers' religious consolations are turned against themselves in his own ironic remark about being "second cousins." This is a character's speech, and the satire's target (slavery, and the religion that sanctions it) is an interpretation, not a stated thesis. It sits close to the Atlantic-plantation concerns of mann-1493 and grove-green-imperialism, though the novel gives no systemic analysis, only a single exemplary body.

Candide's break with Pangloss, in his own words. Candide defines optimism as "the obstinacy of maintaining that everything is best when it is worst". This is the novel's clearest on-the-page definition of the doctrine, and it is a character's (here a sympathetic but naive one's) verdict, not a settled authorial argument. Compare panglossian-optimism and the-best-of-all-possible-worlds. Note that the renunciation is prompted by witnessing suffering, and Candide still swings back to optimism-adjacent hopes afterwards in the book.

Greed, trust and venality. Vanderdendur reasons aloud, with comic repetition, that a man who pays without haggling must be richer; Candide's immediate agreement each time is read by the skipper as evidence of hidden treasure. The magistrate fines Candide for noise and then for fees. The narrator's dry tone ("cool insolence of the judge") makes institutions complicit in private fraud. Candide's reaction, "a trick worthy of our old world," shows he still half-imagines the El Dorado standard as the norm.

Misery as a measurable contest. Candide's advertisement turns suffering into a competition: pay for the "most unhappy" companion. The comedy is that all twenty claim to outdo one another; Candide recalls the old woman's bet and thinks of Pangloss's system. He reasons that "if everything is for the best, it is in El Dorado", a conditional, ironic, and character-voiced concession. The scholar chosen is robbed, beaten, abandoned, fired and persecuted as a Socinian: a catalogue of domestic, economic and clerical misfortune.

Notable passages

The slave's account (c1). The most direct depiction of plantation slavery in the novel, delivered by the victim and structured as practical rules (garment twice a year, hand for a finger, leg for flight) followed by the bitter genealogical argument.

Candide renounces optimism (c2). A short exchange in which Cacambo asks what optimism is and Candide answers with the definition quoted above, then weeps.

Vanderdendur's escalating price (c3). A mechanical comic sequence of ten, twenty, thirty thousand piastres, ending in the swindle. The skipper's soliloquies make greed legible as inference from the victim's behaviour.

The magistrate and the advertisement (c4). The state's response to theft is further extraction; Candide's despair then yields the odd criterion for a companion.

The contest and the scholar (c5). The old woman's wager is recalled; Candide's conditional about El Dorado; the selection of the scholar and a faint comic note that "no employment could be more detestable" than working for booksellers.
