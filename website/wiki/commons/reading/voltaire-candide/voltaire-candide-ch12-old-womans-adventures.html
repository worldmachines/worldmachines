The old woman's tale concluded: plague, Azov, and the case that everyone is miserable

Commons · Candide · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The old woman, narrating to Cunégonde, finishes her tale of sale after sale through the Mediterranean, the plague at Algiers, the siege of Azov where the janissaries eat the women's buttocks, and Russian and Northern European servitude, then argues that life is clung to absurdly and that every passenger will turn out to have cursed his existence.

The old woman (the pope's daughter, speaking to Cunégonde) resumes her narrative. A castrato from her mother's chapel in Palestrina recognises her in Morocco, tends her, and then sells her in Algiers instead of taking her to Italy. She survives the plague there, is resold from market to market across the Mediterranean to Constantinople, and ends up in the seraglio of an aga posted to the defence of Azov. During the Russian siege the starving janissaries eat the two eunuchs and then, on an imam's advice, cut a buttock from each woman. A French surgeon cures the survivors, a boyard works and whips her, and after his execution she wanders as an innkeeper's servant through northern Europe. She closes by turning her story into an argument and a wager about the other passengers.

Themes

Misery as a competitive ledger. The old woman opens by telling the castrato that "there were far greater misfortunes in the world" than his, and later says an earthquake is "a trifle compared to" the plague. Suffering is ranked and compared, which answers the young baroness's earlier claim to be uniquely wretched. This is the old woman's own rhetoric, in character, not a stated thesis of the book. Interpretively, it is an anti-optimist move, since each harm is shown as one among a great many, not as a part that justifies the whole.

Institutions and procedures that dress up atrocity. The imam delivers "an excellent sermon" recommending that only one buttock per woman be cut, with Heaven approving "so charitable an action". The surgeon assures the women the proposals made to them are "agreeable to the laws of war". The narrator's flat tone and the pious vocabulary of charity, humane sermon and laws of war are the satire. Read modestly, the target is religious and military justification (a rationalising system for harm), which rhymes with panglossian-optimism, though here the rationalisers are a pious imam and a surgeon, not Pangloss.

Commodification and circulation. She is sold from Algiers to Tunis, Tripoli, Alexandria, Smyrna and Constantinople, and the castrato's mission to Morocco gains cannon and ships against other Christian governments' commerce. People and weapons move through the same trade networks. The Naples castration statistic ("two or three thousand children every year", some of whom "are sent to govern states and empires") is an ironic aside that places bodily mutilation inside a career system.

The absurdity of the will to live. After a hundred near-suicides she calls her clinging to life a "ridiculous weakness", "perhaps one of our worst instincts", an image of caressing the serpent that devours us. Her empirical count is that of the many who abhorred existence, only twelve actually killed themselves. This is the old woman's argument, and the book does not simply endorse it. It does set up the question that returns later in the book (is existence worth its pains), and it undercuts the best-of-all-possible-worlds stance of the-best-of-all-possible-worlds by treating survival as instinct rather than as evidence of goodness.

Wager as method. She proposes that Cunégonde ask each passenger for his story, and offers to be thrown headfirst into the sea if any has not "cursed his existence many times". The scheme turns the novel's episodic structure into a test of hypothesis, in an empirical rather than systematic spirit.

Notable passages

The castrato's recognition scene (c1) mixes tenderness with grotesque reference to the Naples trade, and reports the mother's death in a single plain clause. The plague passage (c2) compares disasters by magnitude. The imam's sermon (c3) is the chapter's best-known black joke and the sharpest example of pious justification. The surgeon's line (c4) repeats the pattern with the laws of war. The old woman's reflection on carrying one buttock and loving life (c5) is the philosophical centre. Her closing wager (c6) states her thesis and sets up the frame of the next episodes.
