Portsmouth and Venice: the shot admiral, Pacquette and Friar Giroflée

Commons · Candide · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Candide watches an English admiral shot at Portsmouth for not killing enough, flees to Venice, and there, still hoping for Cunégonde, is shown by Martin through the wretched Pacquette and Friar Giroflée that apparently happy people are miserable.

Candide and Martin sail past the English coast. At Portsmouth they see an admiral shot on the deck of a man-of-war before a satisfied crowd, and are told it is done "to inspire the others to fight" (c1). Candide refuses to land and goes on to Venice, where he says "All is well—all very well; all as well as possible" while expecting Cacambo and Cunégonde. Cacambo does not appear; Candide sinks into melancholy and concedes to Martin that "all is misery and deceit", and Martin deflates his hopes about the valet (c2). Candide then wagers that a plump Theatin friar and his girl are happy, and Martin bets they are not. The girl is Pacquette, the maid who gave Pangloss his disease; she tells her story of seduction, dismissal and prostitution (c3). Friar Giroflée admits he hates his forced monastic life (c4). Candide gives them money, Martin predicts it will only make them wretched, and the gondoliers exchange closes the chapter on the cusp of the Pococuranté episode (c5).

Themes

War as ritual absurdity. The admiral's execution is narrated deadpan: four soldiers fire "with full composure" and the crowds go away "perfectly well satisfied". Candide's objection (the French admiral was exactly as far from the English one as the reverse) is logically sound, and the answer is simply that it is "necessary" now and then. Martin, earlier in the chapter, frames the Anglo-French war as a struggle over "a few acres of barren land near Canada" costing more than it is worth. This is satire of institutional logic that punishes a symbolic scapegoat to manage others' behavior; read modestly, it targets military and state practice rather than stating a thesis. It faintly rhymes with the-best-of-all-possible-worlds reasoning, in which whatever is done is made to sound necessary, though nobody here invokes optimism.

Candide's hope versus Martin's pessimism, tested by wager. Candide stakes a claim on observation: these two are happy. Martin's pessimism wins the bet twice, and the text lets him gloat ("I have won the wager already"). Note it is Candide who frames happiness as an empirical question, and Martin whose method is also anecdotal. Candide's slipping line "All is well" at the Venice arrival, and later "all is misery and deceit", show him oscillating rather than holding a doctrine; the shadow of panglossian-optimism persists as a reflex phrase. Even after losing the bet he reaches for hope through coincidence ("I have found my red sheep and Pacquette"), and Martin answers "I have seen the world". The narrator does not adjudicate: Martin also says the gondolier's life is preferable to the doge's, but only trivially so.

Hidden misery under visible cheer. Pacquette says her cheerfulness is "one of the miseries of the trade": she must smile for a friar the day after being beaten by an officer. Giroflée's vigor hides hatred of the convent he was forced into by parents wishing to enrich an elder brother. Both speak in reported first-person testimony, so the claims are theirs, not the narrator's. Interpretively, the episode is about the gap between legible surface and lived condition; this loosely echoes WMP's Legibility theme, though Voltaire's point is moral, not about measurement.

Institutions that exploit the poor and the young. Pacquette's chain (a Cordelier confessor, a surgeon's jealous wife, a judge who releases her for sexual favors, magistrates' extortion) shows religious, medical and legal authorities as sources of harm. Giroflée's monastic coercion and the prior who robs him add the Church. These are characters' accounts delivered with satiric economy; treat the targets (clergy, magistrates) as the satire's, offered modestly.

Money does not repair misery. Candide's gift of piastres is met by Martin's "perhaps this money will only make them wretched". The text leaves the outcome open here; the unit ends before we learn it.

Notable passages

The Portsmouth scene (c1) is the unit's sharpest compression of satirical logic: a ceremonial killing justified by deterrence. The Venice chapters then run as a structured wager: Candide's melancholy and Martin's cold advice about Cacambo (c2), Pacquette's self-account (c3), Giroflée's confession (c4), and the money and gondoliers exchange where Martin's relativism (doge and gondolier differ trivially) closes the argument (c5).
