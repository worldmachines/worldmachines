El Dorado: the old man, the king, and the choice to leave

Commons · Candide · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In El Dorado, Candide and Cacambo meet a learned old man and a gracious king in a closed, wealthy, priestless and lawless land, then choose to leave with gold-laden sheep, hoisted over the mountains by the king's engineers, to buy back Cunégonde.

Cacambo's host sends the travellers to a retired courtier, 172 years old, who explains that El Dorado is the old Inca country, preserved because its remaining princes forbade anyone to leave and because rocks and precipices wall it off from Europeans greedy for its "pebbles and dirt". He describes a religion with one God, thanked but never petitioned, no monks and no quarrelling over doctrine. The travellers are driven by sheep to the king's palace, greeted with a kiss on each cheek, and shown a city with no law courts and no prisons but a gallery of mathematical and natural-philosophical instruments. After a month Candide argues to Cacambo that, happy as they are, Cunégonde is absent and that they would be "just like everyone else" if they stayed, whereas a few sheep of pebbles would make them richer than the kings of Europe. The king calls the plan "rash and silly" but, since all men are free, has his engineers build a hoisting machine, and the pair leave loaded with wealth, planning to ransom Cunégonde and then buy a kingdom.

Themes

El Dorado as a counter-image to Europe. The utopia is built almost entirely out of absences of European institutions: no lawsuits, no prisons, no monks "to dispute, to govern, to intrigue, and to burn people who are not of the same opinion" (Cacambo's question, which the old man answers with "Do you take us for fools?"). This is the narrator's and characters' satire of dogma, the Inquisition and litigiousness; it can be read as an Enlightenment utopia in the tradition of more-utopia and thomas-more, with a faint echo of the noble-savage motif, though here the people are learned and technically capable rather than primitive. It is worth noting that the tone is ironic throughout (gold treated as pebbles, sheep that fly, a single opinion shared by all), so the text is not a straightforward blueprint.

Isolation as the price of innocence. The old man (a character, not Voltaire's mouthpiece) credits the Inca survivors' decree that no one leave the kingdom for "the preservation of our innocence and happiness", and says Europeans would "murder us all to the very last man" for the gold. The same passage reads the Spanish conquest of Peru as a result of Inca imprudence in conquering abroad. The colonial frame resonates loosely with columbian-exchange-mann and grove-green-imperialism, though this scene is about extractive greed rather than ecological exchange.

Science and engineering serve the state's courtesy. The Palace of Sciences, the three thousand mathematicians, and the machine built in about fifteen days put technical capacity in the service of hospitality. Interpretively, this is the Enlightenment's confidence in knowledge (enlightenment-monism, encyclopedie) shown without irony here, though the machine's purpose is to help the visitors carry away the thing the hosts despise.

Leaving paradise. The decisive turn is the visitors' own motive. Candide says that "If we stay here we will be just like everyone else", and the narrator says their fondness for roving, for making a name at home and for boasting of travels was "so strong in our two wanderers, that they resolved to be no longer happy". The king's reply ("when people are tolerably at ease in a place, I'd think it would be to their interest to remain there") sits against the book's wider test of optimism: a possibly best place is left because of desire, status and love. Candide's earlier aside to Pangloss ("if you could have seen El Dorado you would no longer have maintained that the castle of Thunder-ten-tronckh was the finest of all possible edifices") shows the doctrine of panglossian-optimism being undermined by comparison, within the character's own speech, not by argument.

Wealth as a plan. The stated plan is purely instrumental: gold pays the Governor of Buenos Ayres, ends fear of inquisitors and recovers Cunégonde. This sets up the later losses of the fortune; nothing in this unit yet shows that outcome.

Notable passages

  • The old man's history of the Incas and the closed kingdom (c1) gives the utopia's origin and its defensive logic.
  • The religion exchange (c2) is the satire's centre: one God, no petitions, no priests, no persecution, and Candide's remark about Pangloss.
  • The tour of the city (c3) lists the absence of courts and prisons and the presence of science.
  • The king's farewell speech (c4) joins freedom, tyranny and the "yellow clay".
  • The hoisting machine and the departure (c5) show the engineered exit and Candide's plan to buy back Cunégonde and then a kingdom.
