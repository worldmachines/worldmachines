The old woman's care and Cunégonde's history

Commons · Candide · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The old woman nurses Candide and secretly delivers him to a reunited Cunégonde, who recounts her own survival through rape, resale and a shared-custody arrangement between a Jewish banker and the Grand Inquisitor, ending with her verdict that Pangloss deceived her.

The old woman, silent about who she is, feeds Candide and treats his wounds with ointment for several days, then leads him at night to a moated country house where he finds Cunégonde alive. The reunion corrects the news Pangloss had given (she was ravished and cut open, but "these two accidents do not always prove mortal"). Chapter VIII is Cunégonde's own narrative: the Bulgarian raid on the castle, a soldier's attempted rape and her wound, a captain who bought and then sold her, Don Issachar the court banker, and the Grand Inquisitor, who forces a time-share of her and the house. She watched the Lisbon auto-da-fé, saw Pangloss hanged and Candide whipped, and concludes that Pangloss was wrong. The unit closes with Don Issachar walking in on the lovers.

Themes

Optimism tested by a victim's testimony. Cunégonde's closing complaint, "Pangloss deceived me most cruelly in saying that everything is fittest and best," is the plainest rejection of the doctrine so far. It is a character's reported speech, not an authorial thesis. The satire reads as aimed at panglossian-optimism, and the doctrine's explanatory power is checked against an account of rape, servitude and execution, though Candide himself has not yet abandoned it.

Atrocity narrated in an even, comic register. Cunégonde relays the murders at Thunder-ten-tronckh, her wound, and her sale from owner to owner with ironic understatement: she was "not knowing that what had happened at my father's castle was a customary thing". That detail, spoken in her voice, reads as a satirical comment on war treated as routine. Her remark that the captain "knew nothing of philosophy" and had not been "educated under Dr. Pangloss" turns the doctrine into a social vanity. Candide's "all imaginable simplicity" in asking to see the scar plays the same deadpan.

Religious and financial institutions as buyers and bargainers. The Grand Inquisitor threatens Don Issachar with an auto-da-fé to win a share of her. The weekly schedule (Monday, Wednesday and the Sabbath for the Jew, the other four days for the Inquisitor) and the quarrel over "whether the space from Saturday night to Sunday morning belonged to the old or the new law" present religious law as a property arrangement. This is an interpretation of the satire's target (the Inquisition), delivered through Cunégonde's reported account. The portrayal of Don Issachar should be read as a character in the narrator's and Cunégonde's voice, and the text keeps him as a self-interested banker whose Sabbath rights the plot immediately uses for a bedroom farce.

Ritual violence as policy and spectacle. The auto-da-fé is staged "to avert further earthquakes" and "to intimidate Don Issachar"; ladies receive refreshments between mass and execution. A causal theory of disaster is acted out as entertainment. The old woman, who carries messages and care without explaining herself, is a go-between whose own story is withheld here.

Resilience and self-regard. Cunégonde's quips ("A modest woman may be once violated, but her virtue is greatly strengthened as a result"; the Inquisitor and the Jew are fond of her because she withstood both) show a survivor's irony, and also a vanity about her rank ("beneath a person of my birth"). Her comparison of Candide's skin with the captain's keeps the tone ambivalent. Modest hedge: this is a satire of narration and character, not a theory of machines, so any mapping onto world-machines is only a loose rhyme (institutions that legitimize extraction by ritual), not an argument the text makes.

Notable passages

The reunion dialogue corrects Pangloss's report and sets up the exchange of stories. Cunégonde's opening, "I was in bed and fast asleep when heaven chose to send the Bulgarians," fixes her narrating voice: ironic piety ("heaven chose") over plain horror. The Bulgarian captain passage is the first of her sales and contains the Pangloss jab. The Inquisitor-and-banker passage is the book's sharpest joke about institutions sharing a woman. The auto-da-fé passage ties the earlier Lisbon chapters to her view, from a good seat, ending in her judgment on Pangloss.
