Arrival in El Dorado: from the Oreillons to a country that scorns gold

Commons · Candide · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Candide and Cacambo, fleeing the Oreillons and despairing of both hemispheres, drift by canoe through a mountain tunnel into a hidden land where gold and gems are children's playthings and inns are state-funded, and Candide begins to wonder whether this is the place where 'everything is right'.

Chapter XVII opens with Cacambo concluding that 'this hemisphere is not better than the other' and urging a return to Europe; Candide has no destination, since Westphalia is being ravaged by the Bulgarians and Abares, Portugal means burning, and Cunégonde is somewhere in the world they would leave. They head for Cayenne, starve for a month on wild fruit, then take an empty canoe down a river that plunges under a mountain for twenty-four hours and wrecks them on the far side. They emerge into a mountain-ringed plain, cultivated 'for pleasure as well as to produce the necessaries of life', with glittering carriages drawn by large red sheep. Children play quoits with gold, emeralds, rubies and diamonds; a schoolmaster smiles and throws back the stones Candide respectfully returns. In a village inn they are served a lavish dinner and learn from the landlord that gold is 'common pebbles of our highways', that inns are maintained by the government, and that the meal costs nothing. The chapter ends with Candide's tentative thought that this may be the place 'where everything is right', set against his remembered doubts about Pangloss.

Themes

Escape from a world where every option is dangerous. The opening exchange is Candide listing every place he could go and what it would cost him (slit throats, burning, being spitted). The narrator offers no consolation; Cacambo's remedy is practical (find Frenchmen, take the river), and Candide's 'let's let Providence guide us' is the optimist's fatalism used for travel, not for theodicy. Candide's earlier teaching is only lightly present here.

Value inversion: gold as gravel. The children's quoits are the satire's central visual joke. Candide and Cacambo read the scene through European assumptions: the children must be 'the king's sons', the schoolmaster 'the tutor of the royal family'. The schoolmaster's smile and the landlord's laughter show that their currency is worthless here, and the pair nonetheless 'took care, however, to gather up the gold'. The scene exposes how value is a local convention; it is gently ironic at the travellers' expense as well as at European greed. Read modestly, it resonates with the book's wider contrast between European and New World arrangements, as in more-utopia, where gold is likewise despised, though Voltaire's scene is comic where More's is argued.

A utopia built on public provision. The landlord's speech presents a state that funds inns for traders, has no need for money in them, and treats foreigners as rare. 'The useful was joined to the agreeable' (narrator) describes the landscape; the details are partly fairy-tale (red sheep, two-hundred-pound dishes). It is a literary utopia in the tradition of more-utopia and thomas-more, and bears on the Enlightenment's habit of imagining alternatives to Europe (noble-savage, jean-jacques-rousseau), though nothing in this unit yet explains how El Dorado works or why it is hidden, and the unit gives no verdict.

Candide's measured doubt about the doctrine. Candide's closing thought is a conditional: 'Possibly this is that part of the globe where everything is right, for there must certainly be some such place.' He then adds, in his own voice, that 'for all that Master Pangloss said on the matter, I often perceived that things went very badly in Westphalia.' The remark is Candide's, not the narrator's. It is a small pivot: his observation of Westphalia is set against Pangloss's system, a hint of the gap between panglossian-optimism and experience, while his own reasoning ('there must certainly be some such place') is itself a lightly optimistic move. Compare the-best-of-all-possible-worlds.

Notable passages

The despairing opening (c1). Candide's inventory of dangers shows Europe and the Americas as equivalent hazards and Cunégonde as the only thing that holds him; Cacambo's proposal moves the plot.

The hidden plain (c2). The mountains, the tunnel, the plain 'cultivated for pleasure as well as to produce the necessaries of life', and the sheep establish a place set apart from the world, 'inaccessible' by ordinary means.

The quoits (c3). Candide misreads the children as royalty and the schoolmaster as a royal tutor; the schoolmaster's gesture of throwing the gems down is the chapter's emblem. Candide infers from it that the children are taught 'to show such a contempt for gold and precious stones', a conclusion that fits the evidence better than his earlier guess.

The inn and the dinner (c4). The meal is a catalogue of abundance (parakeets, monkeys, humming-birds, rock-crystal dishes); the merchants and waggoners are 'extremely polite'. The excess is comic.

The landlord's speech and Candide's reaction (c5). The landlord states the rules: no money, government-maintained inns. Candide's reaction contains the chapter's only explicit reference to Pangloss and to the notion of a place where 'everything is right'.
