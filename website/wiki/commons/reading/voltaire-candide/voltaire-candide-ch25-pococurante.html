Chapter XXV: Pococuranté, the man who is bored by everything he owns

Commons · Candide · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Candide and Martin visit the rich Venetian senator Pococuranté, who dismisses his paintings, music, and every canonical author in his library, and Martin reads his universal disdain as a pleasure in having no pleasure rather than happiness.

Candide and Martin visit the palace of the noble Venetian senator Pococuranté on the Brenta. He is sixty, very rich, and receives them politely but without eagerness. Over the course of the visit he disparages his two girl companions, his Raphaels, the concerto and opera, and then, in the library, Homer, Virgil, Horace, Cicero, the Academy of Sciences' memoirs, plays, divinity, English books, and Milton. He even calls his garden badly laid out and promises another "tomorrow on a nobler plan". Candide, trained never to judge for himself, takes this for greatness; Martin, who largely agrees with the senator's opinions, sees the flaw. The chapter closes with Candide still pinning happiness on regaining Cunégonde, and a note that Cacambo has not returned and that Pacquette and Friar Giroflée have not come back to thank him.

Themes

Satiety passing for superiority. Pococuranté has every good the earlier chapters' sufferers lacked (wealth, art, companions, books, leisure) and enjoys none of them. His speech is a catalogue of fault-finding delivered coolly. The narrator's irony lets Candide's misreading do the work: "what a great genius this Pococuranté must be! Nothing can please him." This is Candide's own hypothesis about a happy life being tested, and it fails against what Martin sees. Read modestly, the episode sets a pessimist's diagnosis against Candide's still-naive search for the happy man; it is a satire of jaded connoisseurship, not a stated thesis.

Judging for oneself versus mere contrarianism. Pococuranté says "I read only to please myself. I like only what suits me," and the narrator notes Candide "had been raised never to judge for himself." The text offers an independence of taste as a quasi-Enlightenment virtue (he mocks the scholars who keep Homer as an antique, like rusty medals), but undercuts it by making the independent judge joyless and capricious. His verdicts also have a self-serving edge: he spent a great deal on the Raphaels only "out of curiosity" and admits he takes no delight in them. Where he is right and where he is merely sour is left for the reader.

Utility as a standard of value. He dismisses the eighty volumes of the Academy of Sciences as "empty systems, without one single useful thing" unless an author had "invented the art of pin-making". This is a pragmatic note that rhymes with the book's ending on cultivating a garden, though Pococuranté himself cultivates nothing and the line should be read as his remark, not the book's conclusion. It also touches on suspicion of system-building, which in this book is Pangloss's vice (panglossian-optimism); Pococuranté's complaint, however, is aesthetic and impatient, not a considered critique.

Censorship and liberty. On English books he says writing as one thinks is "the privilege of humanity", but that in Italy "we write only what we do not think" and no one dares acquire an idea without a Dominican's permission, while English liberty is soured by "passion and partisan spirit". The ironies are double: a free-thought sentiment spoken by a man who exercises no thought in using it, and a swipe at both Church control and party politics. It echoes the book's wider anti-clerical satire (enlightenment-monism and counter-enlightenment for the larger frame, which this chapter only gestures at).

Martin versus Candide on happiness. Martin: "he dislikes everything he possesses"; "there is a pleasure in having no pleasure." Candide, undeterred, says he will be happy at last with Cunégonde, and Martin answers only "It is good to hope." The exchange keeps optimism alive as a postponed fantasy rather than refuting it, and the mundane closing paragraph about Cacambo, Pacquette and Giroflée sets lingering unhappiness (and a loose end) against Candide's hopes.

Notable passages

The Raphaels (c1). Pococuranté bought two Raphaels at great cost "purely out of curiosity" and now finds the colouring dark, the figures flat, the drapery bad, and declares he approves only of what resembles "Nature herself". The passage is the cleanest statement of acquisition without enjoyment.

Homer as an antique (c2). He claims scholars who speak sincerely admit Homer sends them to sleep, yet keep him in their libraries like "rusty medals". This is a joke at canon-keeping as social performance, and the occasion for Candide to invoke Pangloss, who admired Homer.

Horace and judging for himself (c3). The senator's maxim, "I read only to please myself. I like only what suits me," meets Candide's astonishment and Martin's approval, framing the moral contrast of the two companions.

Cicero and the Academy (c4). He refuses Cicero because a thinker who doubted everything offered nothing he did not already know, and he mocks the Academy's volumes as useless systems against the unwritten art of pin-making.

English books and Italian censorship (c5). The senator's turn to politics: free writing is noble but Italy writes what it does not think, and England's liberty is corrupted by partisanship.

The verdict (c6). Candide calls Pococuranté happiest because "he is above everything he possesses"; Martin cites Plato's remark about stomachs and puts the diagnosis in the formula "a pleasure in having no pleasure". Candide's "I will be the only happy man" and Martin's "It is good to hope" close the scene.
