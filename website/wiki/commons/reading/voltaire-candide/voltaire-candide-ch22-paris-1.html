Candide in Paris: the swarm, the theater, the gaming house

Commons · Candide · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Candide arrives in Paris with his El Dorado wealth and Martin, and is promptly swarmed by parasitic doctors, friends and priests, led by a fawning Abbé through the theater's backbiting critics and the Queen-on-a-dunghill remark to a basset gaming house where he loses fifty thousand francs without complaint.

Candide leaves Bordeaux with a carriage and Martin, having left his red sheep with the Academy of Sciences there, and enters Paris. His diamond and heavy luggage immediately draw two physicians, false friends and pious ladies to his sickbed; a parish priest demands a note payable in the other world, and Martin throws him out. Convalescent, Candide is taken up by a little Abbé of Perigord who escorts him to a tragedy, where a nearby wit demolishes the play, then to talk of how Paris treats actresses, then to the gambling house of the Marchioness of Parolignac, where Candide loses fifty thousand francs and is taken for an English lord. The unit is a run of satirical set pieces about a city that feeds on visible wealth.

Themes

Wealth attracts a ring of professional predators. The narrator's ironic voice lists the physicians "he had not sent for" and friends "whom he had never seen"; the purging and bleeding make him worse. Martin's remark that he did well when poor, with no friends, nurses or physicians, is the pessimist's counterpoint. The priest's request for "a note payable to the bearer in the other world" turns salvation into a financial instrument; the satire's target looks like clerical rent-seeking, though that is interpretation. Candide's refusal is polite (he is not one to follow fashion) and Martin's is physical.

Criticism as a parasitic trade. At the play Candide weeps while a neighbouring wit insists the actress, actor and play are all awful, objecting that the author does not know Arabic and does not believe in innate ideas, and offers a score of pamphlets against him. The Abbé then supplies his own definition of the "pamphlet-monger": a writer of pamphlets, a fool, who slanders new work out of envy. The exchange on "five or six thousand" plays with "fifteen or sixteen" good ones is a numerical joke Martin caps. Note that the Abbé, himself a fawning go-between, condemns parasitism in others; the passage does not resolve who is right about the play.

Paris as a place of contradictions. The Abbé says Queens are honoured while attractive and thrown on a dunghill when dead; Martin confirms with Miss Monimia, refused burial in a churchyard. The Abbé's account of Parisians who laugh with anger in their hearts and "commit the most detestable crimes with a smile" is a character's description, not the narrator's verdict. Martin generalizes it into contradiction in government, courts, churches and spectacles. The scene echoes the priest's threat of denying Candide Christian burial, so burial is used as a lever twice.

Gambling as a sombre ritual. The basset table is described with deadpan precision: twelve melancholy gamblers with turned-down card corners as summaries of their bad luck, silence, a lynx-eyed hostess policing folded corners, her fifteen-year-old daughter signalling cheats. Candide earlier cannot turn a trick at cards, "and Martin was not at all surprised." His loss of fifty thousand francs, borne without distress, makes the servants conclude he is an English lord: wealth read as a social signal. His comparison with the Baroness of Thunder-ten-tronckh is the one note of home.

Optimism is quiet here. Pangloss is absent. The sheep episode at the top mocks academic over-explanation: a prize is given to a northern sage who proves by "A plus B minus C, divided by Z" why the sheep must be red and die of the mange. This rhymes with panglossian-optimism in its form (a necessity-proof reached after the fact), but the target is pseudo-demonstration generally, and the passage does not name Leibniz. Compare the-best-of-all-possible-worlds only loosely.

Notable passages

The sheep prize (c1) opens the unit with a parody of explanatory systems. The sickbed swarm and the priest (c2) set the pattern of Paris extracting from the rich. The theater (c3) and the Queen-on-a-dunghill talk (c4) present literary Paris and its cruelty to its own idols, with Martin's grim assent. The basset table (c5, c6) closes the unit on Candide's losses, which the narrator treats as a fashionable display. For World Machines, the unit is at most a distant rhyme with how a metropolitan culture of judgment and legibility (Legibility) reads strangers by their visible signs; this is a stretch, as the text is a farce, not a theory.
