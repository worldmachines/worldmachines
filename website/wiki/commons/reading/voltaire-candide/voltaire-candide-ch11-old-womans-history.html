The old woman's history, part one: from palace to heap of corpses

Commons · Candide · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
The old woman begins her life story, narrating her fall from a pope's daughter's splendour through a poisoned fiancé, pirate capture, rape and the mass slaughter of Morocco's civil wars in a deadpan voice that treats atrocity as trifles, ending as she wakes half-dead among corpses beside a man lamenting his castration.

In Chapter XI the old woman, who has until now been a silent servant, tells Candide and Cunégonde that she was once the daughter of Pope Urban X and the Princess of Palestrina, raised in dazzling luxury and engaged to a sovereign prince of Massa Carara. In quick succession her fiancé is poisoned by a jealous former mistress, her galley is raided by Salé pirates, she and her mother are stripped, searched and enslaved, she is raped by the Moorish captain, and on landing in Morocco she witnesses factions of the emperor's fifty warring sons tear the captives (her mother included) apart. She wakes alone on a heap of dead bodies and is found by a man who sighs over his own castration. The unit ends mid-story, in the middle of her account.

Themes

Atrocity narrated as trifle. The old woman's refrain is bathetic dismissal: the prince's convulsive death "is a mere trifle"; the rape and "hardships and violences" are "common things; they are hardly worth mentioning." The comic engine is the gap between what happens and the tone in which she reports it. This is the narrator-character's irony, not an authorial thesis; a modest reading is that the passage mocks any frame in which suffering is quickly absorbed and moved past, a rhythm that echoes panglossian-optimism, though here the speaker has lived the suffering rather than explained it away.

Vanity and pride undercut by catastrophe. She opens with absurd boasting of her beauty (the breast "formed like that of Venus of Medicis", eyes that "eclipsed the lustre of the stars"), the castle where a robe could buy half of Westphalia, and the castles of German barons that would not serve as her stable. The pride is aristocratic, and the pitiless chain of events that follows reduces it. The boasting continues even during the rape ("I was beauty itself, and then I had my innocence"), so she keeps her old self-regard while narrating her ruin.

Piracy, slavery and the hypocrisy of the civilised. The old woman reports that the Moors search captives' bodies for hidden diamonds, then adds that this is a long-standing practice among "civilised nations that scour the seas" and that the religious Knights of Malta never fail to do likewise to captured Moors: "one of those international laws from which they never deviate." The target is a double standard in which Christian and Muslim captors are the same; the Pope's soldiers meanwhile beg the corsair for absolution rather than fight. Read as satire of institutional religion and of the law-of-nations pretensions of Europe, though this is interpretation.

War as senseless slaughter, with devotion carried on regardless. Fifty civil wars of "blacks against blacks, of browns against browns, and of mulattoes against mulattoes" produce "one continued scene of car-cases", and the old woman closes the picture by noting the combatants "never missed the five stated times of prayer decreed by their prophet Mahomet." She also generalises about hot African blood versus the milk-veined "natives of Europe", which is the character's own ethnic and climatic stereotyping, written in the period's idiom and delivered with a heavy irony; the text does not endorse it, and it should be read with the same caution as the chapter's racialised language. The same piece of irony (devotion beside slaughter) recurs elsewhere in the book, but only the Moroccan instance is shown here.

Notable passages

The opening boast (c1). Her catalogue of rank, beauty and prospects sets up the fall; its absurd hyperbole (the beauty-struck maids, the sonnets) frames what follows as comic-pathetic.

The poisoned fiancé and the pirates (c2). "But this is a mere trifle" is the key beat: a murder mentioned in a subordinate clause, followed by a galley "gilded like the high altar of St Peter's" and pope's soldiers who beg the pirates for absolution. The story's tone is set by these bathetic juxtapositions.

The search for diamonds (c3). The passage's satire turns on the old woman's naive first reaction ("a very strange kind of ceremony") followed by her learned explanation, and the Knights of Malta's identical practice. Interpretation: the target is the pretended difference between civilised and barbaric captors.

Rape as an aside (c4). The captain who "thought he did me infinite honour" and the closing "I will not detain you any longer with such common things" give the book's characteristic combination of horror and flippancy. The violence is narrated by a survivor; the irony works at the expense of those who rank or minimise it.

Morocco in blood (c5). The civil-war massacre, the women pulled apart by four soldiers each, and the closing observation about prayer times. It is the unit's most concentrated image of senseless war; it is the old woman's account, and the narrator-character's exaggeration of numbers and ethnic detail is part of the satire.

Waking among the dead (c6). The unit's end: alone on a heap of corpses she is found by a man who laments being "senza coglioni", a grotesque juxtaposition that leaves her story unfinished and deflates any pathos with bawdy comedy. Pairs with the-best-of-all-possible-worlds only as contrast: in this scene the world's ordering is evidently not shown as providential.
