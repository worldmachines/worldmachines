Paris II: the supper, the Marchioness, the false Cunégonde and the arrest

Commons · Candide · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
At a Parisian supper Candide meets a fashionable chatter of literary opinion, then is seduced of his diamonds by a Marchioness and lured by the Abbé of Perigord into a staged false-Cunégonde scam and a fake arrest, escaping only by bribing the officer and sailing for England.

The second half of Candide's Paris chapter runs in four movements. At a supper the company trades "insipid jokes, false reports, false reasonings, a little politics, and a great deal of scandal" and passes verdicts on books and tragedies; Candide mistakes a connoisseur of taste for "a second Pangloss", is corrected by him, and argues with Martin. The Marchioness of Parolignac then takes him to her dressing-room, extracts his two diamonds, and the Abbé of Perigord, who shares in the proceeds, pumps him for the news that Cunégonde is expected in Venice. A letter and a bedside scene with a veiled "Cunégonde" follow, and a squad of musketeers arrests the "suspected foreigners". Martin concludes that woman, Abbé and officer are all cheats; Candide buys the officer off with three diamonds and is shipped to Dieppe and a Dutch vessel bound for Portsmouth, believing he has "just escaped from hell".

Themes

Talk as a performance of judgment. The supper is a satire on salon life, narrated in the ironic voice: opinions are delivered with total confidence on novels, archdeacons and tragedies, and the guests' own standards (the man of taste's rules for a good tragedy) are demanding enough that nothing meets them. The man of letters is introduced by the Marchioness as the author of a tragedy that "was panned" and a book that sold one copy, dedicated to her. The reader is invited to notice that the fluent judge of others' failures is himself a failure by his own measure. Read modestly, this is a joke at the expense of literary Paris rather than a stated thesis.

Optimism tested at the table, again. Candide's reflex is to look for Pangloss in anyone eloquent. The man of letters flatly rejects that: "I find that everything goes wrong in our world", a catalogue of quarrels (Jansenists against Molinists, Parliament against Church, husband against wife) summed as "one continuous scene of civil war". Candide then repeats the Panglossian line, that the evils are "shadows in a beautiful picture", and Martin answers that his hanged sage "laughed at you". The exchange turns on Candide's own move: "It is men who make these blemishes, and they cannot do otherwise," to which Martin replies "Then it is not their fault". That shows the optimist's determinism dissolving responsibility, which resonates with the explaining-away logic of panglossian-optimism and the-best-of-all-possible-worlds, though the text only dramatizes it through two characters' words. Note too that the man of letters is a pessimist who still enjoys his evenings, so his complaint costs him nothing.

Naivety as the con artist's raw material. The scam is built from what Candide says. He announces that he must wait at Venice for Cunégonde, tells the Abbé of the messenger sent "nearly two thousand leagues", and the Abbé "did not let a word of all this escape him". The next morning a letter in the beloved's name arrives, with details (Bordeaux, Cacambo, the old woman, the Governor of Buenos Ayres) that Candide himself supplied. The sickroom is kept dark and the "lady" cannot speak: Candide's love fills the gaps, and he leaves diamonds in a hand he has not seen. The Marchioness, for her part, offers a courtesy in which Candide's fidelity is a stock national type ("a young man born in Westphalia") against what "a Frenchman would have said".

Institutional corruption and xenophobic panic. The officer, supposedly the state, accepts a bribe and drops a legal fiction in a breath: "All foreigners get arrested here, but let me manage things." The Abbé explains the practice by pointing to royal assassination attempts in 1594 and 1610 and a man who "heard foolish stories". Candide's reaction, "Is it possible that such scenes could happen among a people who are perpetually singing and dancing?", sets France against El Dorado: "Travellers are not treated in this manner in the country of El Dorado". Interpretively, the satire's target is a society where suspicion, law and graft are the same machine, but that is a modest reading of a few lines.

Martin's Manichaeism. At the arrest Martin says "I am more of a Manichaean now than ever." He is the one who works out the plot (three cheats in a row) and advises the bribe, while Candide, who still thinks in terms of Cunégonde, leans on diamonds. Pessimism here is practical competence, not a mood, which matters for the book's later tension between optimism, pessimism and work.

Notable passages

  • (c1) The opening supper and the man of taste's catalogue of what a good tragedy must be, in which every rule is a way to fail. A comic self-portrait of an Enlightenment-era literary public sure of its taste. It resonates loosely with the polemical, critical culture around enlightenment-monism and the encyclopedie, but nothing in the passage names these.
  • (c2) The man of letters' rejection of optimism, Candide's Panglossian reply, and Martin's "Then it is not their fault". The most compact statement in the unit of how optimism evades blame.
  • (c3) The Marchioness's dressing-room: seduction as extraction, the handkerchief/garter exchange, and the diamonds that "were in an instant transferred from his finger to hers".
  • (c4) The Abbé's questioning of Candide, in which Candide's fondness for talking about Cunégonde supplies the scammer's script. Candide admits he never received a letter from her, which is the very gap the forged letter then fills.
  • (c5) The arrest scene and Martin's diagnosis of three cheats; Candide's comparison with El Dorado.
  • (c6) The bribe, the explanation of why foreigners are arrested, Candide's outburst against the country, and the escape to Dieppe and a Dutch ship for England: the cheat becomes a "most helpful of men" the moment he is paid, and Candide treats the escape as being out of hell, not as a lesson.
