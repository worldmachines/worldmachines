Schelling's living nature and the Jena circle, winter 1798-99

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
In autumn 1798 to spring 1799, Wulf sets Schelling's arrival in Jena and his lectures on nature as a living, self-identical organism (which she casts as the heartbeat of Romanticism) against the Jena circle's social life: Wallenstein's Weimar premiere, Goethe and Schiller's routines, and Caroline's growing attachment to Schelling.

The unit alternates between social narrative and one interpretive argument. The narrative follows Goethe and Schiller (renovations, Goethe's empirical curiosity, the Weimar premiere of Wallenstein, Schiller's compulsive rewriting), the overworked August Wilhelm Schlegel, and Caroline's growing fascination with the new arrival Schelling, including the carriage ride home from the premiere and her hints that no one suspected anything. The argument, which is Wulf's own, is that Schelling's lectures gave Romanticism its philosophical core: self and nature are identical, nature is one living organism, and the mechanistic, distanced picture of the scientific revolution is replaced by a felt connection. The unit ends with the circle at its warmest, with Tieck's arrival, Auguste, and Novalis calling Caroline the heart of the Jena Set.

Themes

Schelling against Fichte and Descartes. Wulf says Schelling, inspired by Fichte at Tübingen, went where Fichte did not: Fichte's Ich was shaped by opposition to the non-Ich, while Schelling held that self and nature were identical and that everything was one. This is Wulf's summary of the position, with Schelling's own words reported from his lectures ('secret bond connecting our mind with nature'). It is the core of Naturphilosophie.

Nature as a living organism, knowable from within. Per Wulf, Schelling's universe was 'one universal organism'. Because the system of nature is the system of mind ('Mind is invisible nature, while nature is visible mind'), nature is intuitively knowable, unlike Kant's thing-in-itself. Being in nature therefore doubles as self-discovery.

Romanticism versus the Enlightenment, in Wulf's telling. Wulf credits the scientific revolution but argues it distanced people from nature and excluded feeling and beauty. She cites Novalis on light reduced to refraction and 'mathematical obedience'. Schelling, she says, reunited nature and humankind. This is Wulf's interpretive framing, not a neutral verdict, and it is a somewhat one-sided portrait of the Enlightenment. It resonates with counter-enlightenment themes, though Wulf frames it as a change of sensibility, not a political reaction. The revolt against the 'mechanistic world' also loosely rhymes with WMP's critique of the modernity-machine and its drive for Legibility, though the resemblance is thematic, not something Wulf argues.

A shift in how people relate to the world. Wulf's argument from travel accounts is that eighteenth-century travellers observed as detached spectators, whereas early nineteenth-century Romantics climbed mountains and slept in forests to feel rather than observe. This is her own inference; she says the shift came 'as Schelling's ideas spread' and cites Hölderlin's Hyperion for the phrase 'to be one with everything living'. It connects to the wider theme of the historical construction of the self (cf. infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness) and to the nature-as-organism line in wulf-invention-of-nature and alexander-von-humboldt.

Jena as a collective mind. Wulf describes the Jena residents' combined knowledge as a 'great living encyclopaedia', contrasted with Weimar's courtiers, and shows Goethe finding the 'busyness contagious'. Goethe and Schiller even discussed turning Schelling's philosophy into a poem. This supports the network-scene reading of The Jena Circle; Wulf presents it as narrative colour more than as a theory of collective cognition.

Gossip and personal life. Caroline's infatuation with Schelling, her staying home from the second Wallenstein, and August Wilhelm's apparent indifference are reported through her letters and Wulf's inference ('No one suspected a thing'). They are hedged interpretations of private feeling, not established facts.

Notable passages

The curated passages track the argument: Schelling's break from Fichte (c1), the organism thesis and its epistemology (c2), Wulf's diagnosis of the Enlightenment's distancing and Schelling's remedy (c3), the change in how people experienced nature (c4), the students' near-religious reception and the rejection of the clockwork universe (c5), and the picture of Jena as a living encyclopaedia (c6). Wulf's claim that Schelling's philosophy 'became the heartbeat of Romanticism' is a strong interpretive statement, and it is hers, not one made by the historical actors.
