Goethe rides to Jena: two exhausted celebrities before the partnership (July 1794)

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Wulf opens the book on 20 July 1794 with Goethe riding from Weimar to Jena and, through his and Schiller's backstories, sets up two fading Sturm und Drang celebrities, one privileged and blocked, the other poor, ill and doubting his imagination, poised on the edge of their partnership, while Fichte's Ich-philosophy is already the talk of Jena's students.

The unit is scene-setting narrative rather than argument. Beginning with Goethe's ride from Weimar to Jena on 20 July 1794 for a Natural History Society botanical meeting, Wulf describes the landscape, the small town of Jena (bakers, butchers, taverns, students, no street lanterns), and then gives long backstories of the two men who are about to meet there. Goethe is the 'Zeus of Germany's literary circles': a wealthy Frankfurt-born author of The Sorrows of Young Werther, now a fat, powdered privy councillor in tiny Saxe-Weimar whose creative output has stalled. Schiller, ten years younger, is the author of The Robbers, formed under the despotic Duke Karl Eugen, now poorly paid, ill and afraid that Kant's philosophy has killed his poetry. The two have avoided each other for years. Fichte's Ich-philosophy is mentioned in passing as the thing that summer's students are talking about. The unit ends before the two actually speak.

Themes

Sturm und Drang as the prehistory of the self. Wulf presents Werther as the emblem of an age that 'had elevated feelings above the rationalism of the Enlightenment', quoting Werther's line 'I withdraw into myself and find a world there'. Interiority is already a mass cultural product (Werther costume, porcelain, alleged suicides). Wulf notes that Goethe has by 1794 'turned his back' on that phase. Compare sturm-und-drang and, for the Enlightenment contrast, counter-enlightenment. This is Wulf's framing of the movement, and the note does not claim she yet develops a thesis here.

Celebrity, patronage and money as conditions of creativity. The chapter is unusually specific about material conditions: Saxe-Weimar's 100,000 inhabitants and 2,000-strong court, Goethe's stipends and offices, Schiller's 200 thalers salary (800 in total) against Goethe's wealth. Schiller's remark that with Goethe's money 'the university in Jena could kiss my ass' is his own reported words. Readers interested in the social infrastructure of a creative scene can use this, though Wulf does not theorize it.

Freedom versus tyranny, at the personal and political level. The Robbers is described as speaking 'to the revolutionary mood of the time' (frontispiece 'In Tirannos'), and Karl Eugen's court is a foil of absolutism. Goethe, by contrast, dislikes the play's revolutionary leanings. This sets up the political backdrop (a revolutionary decade) without yet naming the French Revolution.

The Ich arrives as background noise. Fichte, who came to Jena in May 1794, has 'declared the self to be the supreme ruler of the world', and quotes 'The source of all reality is the Ich', 'empowering them with the idea of self-determination and a free will'. Goethe had recommended him for the post. This is Wulf's first flag of the book's central theme; it is only a paragraph here.

Personality and rivalry. Goethe is 'cold, monosyllabic god' to the young and 'greatest egoist I've ever met' to Wieland (gossip and opinion of others, not Wulf's verdict); Schiller feels admiration and dislike, comparing Goethe's acknowledgement to 'seducing a prude'. Wulf gives motives (Goethe's distaste for revolution, reminder of his Sturm und Drang, possible jealousy) with the hedge 'may have played a part'. Egotism is thus seeded early, in line with the book's later dark side.

Notable passages

Werther and the mass-marketing of feeling (c1, c2). Wulf reports the novel as a bestseller that men imitated in dress, and Byron's joke that Werther 'has put more individuals out of this world than Napoleon himself'. She frames it as Goethe's most vivid contribution to Sturm und Drang.

Fichte in the taverns (c3). The students' talk about the Ich, with the note that Goethe himself had suggested Fichte. Passing but thematically the most important sentence in the unit for the book's argument.

The Robbers and its reception (c4). Schiller's play as a revolutionary text, the frontispiece 'against tyrants', the riotous premiere, and Schiller's flight from Württemberg to the Jena chair.

Goethe's avoidance of Schiller (c5). The reason two famous neighbours never talked, including Goethe's later admission and Wieland's judgement.

Schiller's stalled imagination (c6). Schiller's fear that philosophy has eradicated poetry, in his own later words to Goethe: 'Imagination disturbs my abstract thinking' and 'cold reason my poetry'. It hints at a tension between reason and imagination that the Jena Set will later try to bridge. This is an interpretive gloss for WMP, not Wulf's claim in this unit.

See also johann-wolfgang-von-goethe and friedrich-schiller. The unit is background for The Jena Circle (the town and its taverns are the stage), though the Circle itself has not yet formed. Little here maps onto machine-level WMP concepts; this is human-scale group biography and Wulf is a narrative historian, not a machine theorist.
