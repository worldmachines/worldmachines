The Jena Set scatters: exodus, Staël's arrival and Schiller's death (1804–1805)

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Wulf narrates the dispersal of the Jena Set in 1804–1805 (professors and students leaving for Würzburg, Schlegel, Fichte and Humboldt scattering across Europe, Madame de Staël arriving to learn the new German philosophy) and closes with Schiller's death and Goethe's failed attempt to finish Demetrius.

Wulf tells how Jena's intellectual centre dissolved once Schelling left for Würzburg in 1803 and students followed, leaving Hegel behind and Goethe and Schiller nostalgic for the old friction. She then follows the scattered friends: Fichte lecturing in Berlin, Schelling and Caroline in Würzburg, August Wilhelm Schlegel departing with Madame de Staël for Coppet, Friedrich Schlegel and Dorothea disillusioned in Napoleonic Paris and moving to Cologne, and Alexander von Humboldt returning from South America. She ends the unit with Schiller's death on 9 May 1805 and Goethe's unsuccessful effort to complete Demetrius. The chapter is scene-driven narrative; Wulf's own interpretive claims are few and are marked below.

Themes

The collapse of a scene. Wulf presents Jena's decline as a vicious circle: professors were paid directly by students, so departures cut income and prompted more departures. The actors' voices (Goethe's 'current exodus', Schiller's 'the exodus of philosophy is complete', Crabb Robinson's 'brink of Ruin') supply the diagnosis; Wulf's own image is that the town's 'beating heart' 'simply stopped'. For the project this is a small case of a The Jena Circle as a dense network whose productivity depended on physical proximity and on a specific funding structure. Schiller's remark about being 'electrified by our intellectual friction' names the mechanism as he remembers it, in nostalgia, and not as a settled analysis.

Success without the thrill. Schiller has fame, nobility and money yet writes that his life is 'monotonous and empty'. Wulf reports this as his experience: recognition and comfort did not replace the collective intellectual life of early Jena. See friedrich-schiller and johann-wolfgang-von-goethe.

Germany seen from outside: Staël. Madame de Staël, exiled by Napoleon, comes to study the Ich and finds that Goethe and Schiller do not read political newspapers. Wulf reports her hypothesis (her words, hedged with 'maybe, she mused') that Germans live in the ideal because their real world is restricted, and that the resulting book Germany carried the Jena Set's ideas to European and American parlours. This half-rhymes with romanticism-and-political-danger and the theme of inwardness as compensation for political powerlessness, though here it is Staël's observation and not Wulf's own thesis, and the political danger is not yet in view. Staël also frames the French Revolution leading to Napoleon as 'something gone wrong', which sets up the French-versus-German contrast (too rational versus a new way of understanding the world).

Revolution's disillusion and the Orient turn. Friedrich Schlegel, once a supporter of the Revolution, watches Napoleon centralise power toward an empire; the ideals 'seemed to disappear'. Wulf reports his retreat into being 'stubbornly and idiotically German' and into Sanskrit and Persian study, using manuscripts plundered by the French. See Friedrich Schlegel. Money troubles, Dorothea's conversion and their marriage are the practical background.

Humboldt as the continuation of Jena's spirit. Wulf claims that Alexander von Humboldt, travelling in South America, 'stayed true to the spirit of Jena': feelings and imagination as tools for knowing nature alongside forty-two instruments. Humboldt credits Goethe's writings; Schiller's jealousy shows up in his advice to Cotta. See alexander-von-humboldt and, for the nature-as-organism strand, Naturphilosophie (not named in this unit).

Cotta's catalogue as diffuse infrastructure. Wulf's own summing-up: the friends, though moved or estranged, are reunited in Cotta's publishing list, which 'carried their ideas into the world'; the revolution, like the French one, was no linear path but pointed 'towards a self-determined life'. This is an interpretive claim from Wulf, and a nice case of a distributed publication channel doing the work the town no longer does.

Death of Schiller. Illness, autopsy and Goethe's grief are told scene by scene. Wulf's own reading is that Schiller's complaints were not hypochondria but the cost of producing his best work by pushing a weakened body. Goethe's inability to finish Demetrius, 'to spite Death', makes the partnership's end concrete.

Notable passages

The passage on the vanishing students and professors (c1) is the most explicit account in the unit of why the Jena scene ended. The Staël sequence (c2) gives an outsider's theory of German interiority and her purpose in learning about the Ich. Friedrich Schlegel's Paris disillusion (c3) ties the Jena Set to Napoleon's consolidation of power. The Humboldt passage (c4) argues for continuity of Jena's spirit in empirical exploration. The Cotta passage (c5) is Wulf's short synthesis of the movement's spread. The Schiller-death-and-Demetrius sequence (c6) is the emotional close of the partnership and shows Goethe's mourning.
