Caroline's Jena: open marriages, gossip, and Friedrich Schlegel's arrival

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Wulf shows Caroline Schlegel settling into small, gossipy, sexually relaxed Jena in 1796, where an open-marriage culture, her salon and the Shakespeare translations give her a freedom denied by the legal and pedagogical norms Wulf lays out, while Friedrich Schlegel arrives as a brilliant, abrasive critic who sets himself against Schiller.

This unit opens with Caroline Schlegel gaining confidence from the Humboldts' open marriage, then steps back to describe the legal and ideological subordination of women that she and Caroline von Humboldt defied. Wulf then paints Jena as a tiny, gossip-saturated town (a walking tour of under half an hour), notes the political calm of late 1796, and follows the Schlegels' new life: Caroline's salon, a visit to Goethe in Weimar, Novalis's visits during Sophie von Kuhn's operations, and the arrival of Friedrich Schlegel, whose review of Schiller's Musen-Almanach starts a feud. It closes on the Schlegels' collaborative Shakespeare translation and the fact that Caroline's work appeared under her husband's name.

Themes

Women's legal and intellectual constraint versus Jena's licence. Wulf's own framing: fathers and husbands "determined every aspect of a woman's life", the Prussian General State Laws made the man "the head of the family", and even Rousseau (jean-jacques-rousseau), progressive on childhood, wanted women "completely submissive". Against this, Wulf argues Jena was more relaxed (evidence: many illegitimate children) and that both Carolines took control of their destinies. The Rousseau point is a Romantic-era complication of Enlightenment egalitarianism: equal faculties, unequal roles.

Gossip as the network's medium. The reported gossip (Sophie Mereau, Madame Schutz, Karoline Paulus; Charlotte Schiller's indignation that the Humboldts had "trampled all propriety") is rumour, attributed to Friedrich, Caroline, Goethe or Charlotte, not Wulf's verdict. The walking tour makes the density literal: Fichte, Schiller, Goethe, the Humboldts and the editors of the Allgemeine Literatur-Zeitung all lie within minutes of one another. This is the physical substrate of what WMP would call a scene, cf. The Jena Circle, though Wulf offers it as narrative texture rather than a model.

Friedrich Schlegel as polarizing critic. His self-image as a "Dictator-Critic", his verdict that only Goethe, Shakespeare and Dante are worth reading, and his link between German poetry's fragmentation and the absence of a unified nation are his own claims, reported by Wulf. Friends and rivals disagree sharply about him (Novalis, Humboldt, Schiller, Goethe). His enthusiasm for Fichte and the Ich ties him to the project of the self. See Friedrich Schlegel, Novalis, friedrich-schiller, johann-wolfgang-von-goethe.

Collaboration and its attribution. Caroline and August Wilhelm translate Shakespeare side by side, with pages in both hands; she edits and reviews under his name. Wulf hedges the reasons (fees, wariness of attention) and calls August Wilhelm's later 'lacked ambition' remark 'a little disingenuous'. Collaborative writing in the household prefigures Symphilosophie, though Wulf does not make that link in this unit. The political backdrop is minimal: the French Storm has passed and Karoline Paulus wears revolutionary colours openly.

Notable passages

The passage on women's legal status and Rousseau (c1) is the unit's clearest piece of Wulf's own social-historical argument. The gossip paragraph (c2) shows Friedrich reporting and joining the scene's sexual freedom. The Dictator-Critic passage (c3) and the Schiller feud (c4) introduce Friedrich's temperament and the first fracture line in the Jena Set. The Shakespeare and anonymity passage (c5) shows collaboration and its unequal credit, and the salon passage (c6) shows Caroline as the town's social centre.
