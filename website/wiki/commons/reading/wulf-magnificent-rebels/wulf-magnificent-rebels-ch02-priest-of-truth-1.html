Fichte arrives in Jena: the Ich as a revolution of the mind

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Wulf introduces Fichte as the charismatic new Jena professor of 1794, tracing his rise from poor tutor to Kant's anointed successor and presenting his Ich-philosophy, which makes the self the source of knowledge and freedom, as the intellectual counterpart of the French Revolution in an authoritarian Germany.

Wulf opens with a set-piece, a packed Jena lecture hall in summer 1794 and a thirty-two-year-old Fichte in riding boots, then doubles back to explain how he got there. She gives his poor Saxon origins, the baron who paid for his schooling, his years as a badly behaved and perpetually broke tutor, and his conversion by Kant's Critiques. She then narrates the comic episode by which he became famous: his anonymous Attempt at a Critique of All Revelation was taken for Kant's own work until Kant publicly named him, and that led to the Jena professorship. The chapter then turns from biography to doctrine. Fichte drops Kant's thing-in-itself and makes the self-positing Ich the starting point of everything. Wulf closes by setting that philosophy against the tightly controlled German states and against the French Revolution, which she presents as the spark for it.

Themes

The self as lawgiver, then as first principle. Wulf's own explanatory gloss on Kant is that time, space and causality are categories of the mind, so that the self becomes something like a lawgiver of nature and is no longer a cog in a mechanistic universe. Fichte, in her account, radicalises this. He discards the thing-in-itself and says the Ich posits itself and, through that act, the non-Ich. She is careful to add that this does not mean the Ich creates the world, only our knowledge of it. This is the book's founding move for the invention of the self, and it is offered as intellectual history in Wulf's voice, not as a claim about machinery. It sits alongside Gay's treatment of inwardness (infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness, self-construction-through-writing-gay). Gay's inwardness is built from social practices, whereas Wulf here gives a philosophical genealogy of the self.

Biography as proof of philosophy. Wulf reads Fichte's own life as an enactment of the idea. The boy who abandoned a predestined path through his will, the man who dropped his Saxon accent and married up, and the pilgrim who abandoned his fiancée because marriage would ‘clip his wings’ all appear as proof of Kant's philosophy. Her wording is interpretive (‘In a way, his own life was the proof’). The book's later theme of egotism is foreshadowed but not stated in this unit, and the abrupt abandonment of Johanne is reported without comment.

Freedom under petty despotism. Wulf argues that the students were electrified because Fichte promised freedom when the German rulers regulated marriage, private theatricals and taxes. She cites Schiller's imprisonment and Schelling's obsequious petition as evidence. Her claim is that the Ich as first principle was ‘as revolutionary as any of the political changes witnessed in France’. This is her framing, and it says something about the Romantic turn inward: philosophical freedom substitutes for, or runs in parallel with, political freedom. That fits the counter-enlightenment and romanticism-and-political-danger questions only loosely at this point, since the unit is entirely optimistic about freedom and presents Fichte's system as building on the Enlightenment ideals of the Declaration of the Rights of Man.

Fame as a media event. The pseudonymity episode shows a literary public sphere at work: the Allgemeine Literatur-Zeitung, Jena's taverns and the Professors' Club all misattribute the book, and Kant's correction then makes an unknown famous. Goethe, per Wulf's report, quips that Kantian theory is like fashionable factory products, in which the first buyers pay the most. That remark is Goethe's, reported by Wulf, and is not her thesis. Read cautiously, the episode is a small case of how a network of readers, editors and ministers produces reputations. It rhymes with the scene-as-network theme of The Jena Circle, though the unit does not yet show collaboration.

The French Revolution as backdrop and spark. Wulf recounts the Revolution's abolitions, the Declaration's first article and the ‘general will’ (she notes its basis in jean-jacques-rousseau). She reports Fichte's own description of it as the ‘breaking dawn’ and his line that his system analyses the concept of freedom. Her framing is that the Revolution was ‘vivid proof of the power of philosophy’.

Notable passages

The passage on Kant's Copernican turn (c1) is Wulf's compact explanation of how the self gained agency. The Fichte passages (c2, c3) give the doctrine of the Ich and non-Ich and its promise of self-determination. The pseudonym episode (c4) is the narrative hinge that makes Fichte famous and gets him the Jena post. The political passage (c5) states Wulf's argument about why his ideas were revolutionary, and the closing passage (c6) ties the Ich-philosophy to the Revolution through Fichte's own words.
