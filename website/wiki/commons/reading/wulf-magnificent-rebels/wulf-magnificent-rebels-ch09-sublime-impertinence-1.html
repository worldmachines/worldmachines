Friedrich Schlegel in Berlin: the Athenaeum plan and the fragment

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Wulf narrates Friedrich Schlegel's Berlin winter of 1797–98, in which his friendships with Schleiermacher and Dorothea Veit and his plan for a journal of 'sublime impertinence' produce the Athenaeum project, the fragment as a deliberate genre and a collective 'symphonising' in which Caroline becomes editor.

Wulf follows Friedrich Schlegel through Berlin from summer 1797. He is a provocative twenty-five-year-old who calls for creative chaos, shares lodgings with the chaplain Friedrich Schleiermacher and falls in love with Dorothea Veit, the unhappily married daughter of Moses Mendelssohn. From this mix of sociability and passion comes his 'great plan': a journal, soon named Athenaeum, in which the Schlegel brothers would escape hack work, fight the literary establishment and be of 'sublime impertinence'. Wulf spends most of the unit on the method of the project: the deliberate fragment as a literary genre, written together, and Friedrich's badgering of Caroline and August Wilhelm in Jena for contributions. Caroline ends up as the de facto editor.

Themes

Revolution by words, and aesthetic anarchy. In Wulf's account Friedrich hopes an 'aesthetic anarchy' will bring a 'happy catastrophe' like the French Revolution, and that the old system will be torn down, philosophical systems and bourgeois conventions with it. The revolution is meant to be fought 'with words rather than swords'. This is Friedrich's own programme as Wulf reports it. Wulf adds that his sister Charlotte thought vanity was also a motive. It sits with Friedrich Schlegel's place in the early Romantic break with Enlightenment system-building (see counter-enlightenment), though this unit is scene and biography and does not itself argue that framing.

Salons as a social infrastructure that dissolves rank. Wulf presents the Jewish literary salons, hosted by women, as the only places in Prussia where religious, class and gender boundaries were absent, in contrast to bourgeois gatherings where the sexes kept apart. Henriette Herz is quoted: 'The mind is a powerful equaliser'. This is a concrete scene of the network that produced the group's ideas (compare The Jena Circle). It also complicates a monolithic picture of bourgeois sociability (compare the-bourgeois-experience), since the salons are set against it in Wulf's telling.

Love, law and the refusal of the middle way. Dorothea's marriage was arranged when she was fourteen, and Wulf notes that the 1794 Prussian General State Laws allowed adultery to be punished with up to a year in prison. Her risk-taking is voiced in her own words: 'I can no longer drift around on the shabby middle way.' The private and the public are entangled here, and freedom is bought at legal and financial risk. Friedrich's erotic boasts to Novalis and Dorothea's near-devotion are reported as their own testimony.

The fragment as a chosen genre. Wulf says the friends were the first to elevate a deliberate fragment, as opposed to an accidental survival, to a literary genre. Friedrich calls himself 'a system of fragments' and Novalis says his 'nature consists of moments'. Wulf lists the practical advantages: fragments are quick, need little research, can be composed together over a meal, and pack variety into few pages. She also reports Friedrich's hope that radical sentiments buried among hundreds of milder ones would escape Prussian censors. The fragment is a form fitted to distributed, many-hand production, and it half-rhymes with the WMP idea that paradigms assemble from independent components (history-machine). The fit is loose, since the Athenaeum project is deliberate and authored, whereas a history machine assembles silently.

Collective authorship: 'giant symphonising'. Friedrich's phrase for the shared endeavour is quoted directly, and the journal is to be 'their communal work of art'. This is the practice behind Symphilosophie. Wulf shows its costs as well: Friedrich's relentless demands strain Caroline and August Wilhelm's marriage, and Caroline sends guests out just to have an evening alone.

Caroline as editor and connective tissue. Wulf, in her own voice, characterises Caroline as the person whose thoughts were 'at the heart of the friends’ work and ambitions' and describes her by a river image, irrigating rather than staking a claim. Wulf notes that Caroline did not publish under her own name. This is Wulf's interpretive framing of women's uncredited intellectual labour and gives the collective its editorial center. Friedrich's belief in the equality of men and women is likewise reported as his own view.

Notable passages

Friedrich the guest (c1). Wulf's portrait of Friedrich as outspoken and provocative, wanting a new 'aesthetic anarchy' to incite a 'happy catastrophe' similar to the French Revolution. She also relays a friend's remark that he saw the world through 'an analogy of his own character'.

The salons (c2). The best statement in the unit of the salon as a rank-dissolving social form, which gives Dorothea and the Humboldts a shared world.

The plan for the journal (c3). The programme in four lines: fight the split between reason and feeling, celebrate subjectivity and imagination, wage open war on the literary establishment, and be of 'sublime impertinence'. Wulf balances it with Charlotte Ernst's report of the brothers' vanity, which she attributes to Charlotte.

The fragment (c4, c5). Wulf's definition of the deliberate fragment, her sampling of examples (Friedrich on women in poetry, Novalis on religion as an opiate, where Wulf says Novalis anticipated Karl Marx by half a century), and the pragmatic case for the form, including evading censors, and for collective symphonising.

Caroline as editor (c6). Caroline shortens, deletes, adds and rearranges. The passage is Wulf's own verdict on her role in the Athenaeum project.

For WMP the unit is mainly material on the scene-level mechanics of the early Romantic network, not a theory. Wulf is a narrative historian and does not frame it in machine terms.
