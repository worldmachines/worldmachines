Spring 1797: the 'little academy' at full pitch, then the Horen quarrel

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
In spring and early summer 1797 the Jena Set's daily interdisciplinary sociability, and Goethe's intense exchange with Alexander von Humboldt, gives way to the Horen quarrel in which Friedrich Schlegel's review ends Schiller's friendship with August Wilhelm Schlegel, leaving Goethe exhausted, neutral and turning back to Faust.

Wulf narrates the spring and early summer of 1797 in Jena. The first half shows the Jena Set at its most productive and convivial: daily meetings, Wilhelm von Humboldt reading Fichte aloud in Schiller's parlour, Goethe and Alexander von Humboldt running optical and anatomical experiments together. The second half turns to rupture. Friedrich Schlegel's caustic review of Schiller's journal Horen leads Schiller to dismiss August Wilhelm Schlegel, gossip carried by Caroline von Humboldt hardens factions, the Humboldts leave Jena, Friedrich is advised to withdraw to Berlin, and an exhausted Goethe, the only figure who stays neutral, mediates, then retreats to Weimar and reopens Faust. Wulf's own interpretive claims are modest and localised (the Goethe-Humboldt influence ran both ways; Schiller was blinded by jealousy). Most of the rest is scene and reported letters, gossip and opinion.

Themes

A scene that produced ideas across disciplines. Wulf describes the group as 'Interested in everything – art, science and literature' and says 'their interdisciplinary approach would become a major theme as their thinking evolved.' Philology, verse translation, Fichte's Wissenschaftslehre, animal electricity and colour theory circulate in the same rooms. This is concrete evidence for the group as a network or scene rather than a set of isolated authors (cf. The Jena Circle, Symphilosophie), though this unit shows co-presence and cross-fertilisation, not yet the formal collaborative writing of later chapters.

Poetry and science as one project (Goethe and Humboldt). Wulf argues the influence 'worked both ways': Goethe drew energy and experimental habit from alexander-von-humboldt, while Humboldt moved from 'purely empirical research towards an interpretation of nature that combined scientific data with emotional responses', in tandem with the Ich-philosophy's growing emphasis on subjectivity. She traces Goethe's later weaving of science into Faust, the 'Metamorphosis of Plants' and Elective Affinities, and Humboldt's later Cosmos line that 'the external world only exists for us so far as we receive it within ourselves'. This is Wulf's interpretive link and it rhymes with Naturphilosophie and wulf-invention-of-nature (nature grasped through feeling as well as classification), though the unit does not itself use the term. Note the later dates are Wulf's forward glances, not events of 1797. See also johann-wolfgang-von-goethe.

Jealousy and factional gossip. Schiller, in letters to Körner that he never showed the men concerned, calls Humboldt a show-off run by 'naked, analytical reason'; Wulf's own verdict is that Schiller was 'Blinded by jealousy'. The Horen dispute escalates through reviews, a dismissal letter, and rumour: Körner, briefed by the Humboldts, blames Caroline Schlegel for corrupting the brothers, and Caroline von Humboldt and Charlotte Schiller trade insults about her. These are the historical actors' words and gossip Wulf reports, not her own assessment; she does frame Charlotte's hostility as a clash between a conventional woman and a free spirit who 'had defied her destiny and prescribed place in society'. See friedrich-schiller and Friedrich Schlegel.

Selfhood as a source of friction. Schiller's judgment that this whole generation 'was just too self-centred and highly strung' and had 'taken Fichte's Ich-philosophy too literally' is Schiller's view as reported by Wulf, and an early sign of the book's later dark-side theme of egotism; Friedrich's delight in public confrontation ('I like it when people say, publicly and unambiguously, what they think') and dream of being 'the last man standing' illustrate the temperament at issue. Wulf does not yet make this her thesis here.

Goethe as the neutral hub, and his exhaustion. Goethe's frantic diary, his oscillation between warring parties, his wish to be at home and his plea to the duke for a journey show a central node strained by both the intellectual pace and the social work of mediation. Wulf's framing that 'Something didn't feel right in Jena' is hers, but the underlying cause she gives is a mix of fatigue, weather, quarrel and the Humboldts' departure.

Political backdrop, lightly touched. The Treaty of Leoben (Napoleon having evicted the Habsburg army from Italy) appears as an aside; it shapes only the Humboldts' travel hopes. It is background to the French Revolution and Napoleon context, not developed here.

Notable passages

The little academy (c1). The opening paragraph gives the group's daily working portrait: Humboldt on Aeschylus, Goethe on Hermann and Dorothea and optics, the Schlegels on Shakespeare, Schiller on Wallenstein.

Schiller's parlour and Goethe's dizziness (c2). Evenings of reading Fichte, Friedrich Schlegel and Caesar, and Humboldt's animal-electricity results, with Goethe reporting time passing 'like a rock crashing down a mountain'. A vivid case of philosophy and experiment sharing one room.

Influence both ways (c3). Wulf's most explicit interpretive claim in the unit: Goethe changed how Humboldt understood nature, and Humboldt's later view of the external world as received 'within ourselves' is tied to Jena.

Schiller's jealousy (c4). Schiller's private worry that science was shrinking art's realm and his caustic letters about Humboldt.

The dismissal letter (c5). Schiller fires August Wilhelm from Horen because of his brother's review; the friendship that brought August Wilhelm to Jena 'ended abruptly'.

Goethe oscillates (c6). The shuttle diplomacy, including the roundabout passing of edits, and Schiller's verdict on the young generation and Fichte.
