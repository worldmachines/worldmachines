Winter–spring 1801: Goethe's fever, Schelling's grief, Hegel's arrival, Novalis's death

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
In early 1801, as the Jena Set disperses, Goethe's fever-dreams of Schelling's Naturphilosophie feed his return to Faust, Schelling and Caroline are strained by grief for Auguste, Hegel arrives in a Jena that has 'almost blown itself out', and Novalis dies of tuberculosis at twenty-eight, leaving Friedrich Schlegel and Tieck fighting over his unfinished novel.

This stretch of Wulf's narrative runs from January to April 1801 and tells the dissolution of the Jena circle through illness, bereavement and departures. Goethe falls gravely ill, rants in delirium about Schelling's Naturphilosophie, and on recovery returns to Faust, writing the pact scene laced with Idealist allusions. Schelling, hitherto used to success, meets trauma after Auguste's death and lashes out at Caroline. Hegel, slow and sociable, arrives in Jena just as the circle has scattered, and Napoleon's Treaty of Lunéville marks the wider political change. August Wilhelm and Caroline renegotiate their marriage. Novalis dies at Weißenfels on 25 March 1801, and his friends quarrel over who may finish Heinrich of Ofterdingen.

Themes

Ideas that pass into the unconscious, and the fear of losing the self. Wulf reports that Goethe's feverish nights were dominated by Schelling's Naturphilosophie, and that he later joked he 'almost lost himself'. Wulf then asks, without answering, whether his relief at being 'back in my own self' meant regained control of his thoughts or escape from 'the dizzying dangers of an unconstrained self'. That question is Wulf's own interpretive framing, and she leaves it open. It touches the book's larger concern with the self (Naturphilosophie, johann-wolfgang-von-goethe).

Faust as a vehicle for Jena philosophy. Wulf argues that the post-illness scenes are 'peppered with allusions' to Schelling and Idealism: the cursed illusion, the spirits telling Faust to rebuild the world within his mind, and the revised opening of John, ending on 'In the beginning was the Act', which Wulf reads as the original act of the Ich positing itself. She adds Schelling's later verdict on Faust and lists the themes Goethe wove in: art and science, the pitfalls of rationalism, unity of man and nature, and the self. This is Wulf's interpretation, though the Schelling remark is reported as his.

The fragility of a charmed life. Wulf's portrait of Schelling is psychological and explanatory: success had not prepared him for darkness, so 'he attacked' Caroline rather than opening his heart. Caroline's letters, quoted directly, answer accusation with reassurance ('being true to myself means being true to you'). Wulf's causal account of Schelling's inner state is her own inference from his letters and biography.

A scene dispersing. Hegel, Wulf's contrast figure (sociable, level-headed, unmoved by Alpine peaks, interested in Swiss cheese making), arrives to find Fichte and Tieck in Berlin, the Schlegels in Braunschweig, Schiller in Weimar, and Schelling and Friedrich Schlegel not speaking. Wulf's phrase is that 'the literary whirlwind had almost blown itself out.' Her narration links this to Napoleon's victories and the Treaty of Lunéville, which gives the personal breakup a political backdrop (the French Revolution and Napoleon as background, but only lightly connected here). The personal and political are juxtaposed, not causally tied. The dispersal fits The Jena Circle as a scene whose intellectual energy depended on co-presence, though Wulf does not put it in those terms.

Marriage renegotiated, kept as friendship and collaboration. August Wilhelm and Caroline agree that he has done his duty and that she is 'no longer his lover'. They keep writing and Caroline advises on his work. She urges poetry over reviews because 'when the world goes up in flames like a scrap of paper, works of art will be the last living sparks' (Caroline's words, not Wulf's thesis).

Novalis's death and the afterlife of the fragment. Wulf gives the death in detail and reports the group's reactions, in the friends' own words: Tieck's 'amputation' and 'the unity has been split apart', and Friedrich Schlegel's 'divine'. She stresses that the group had always seen Novalis as almost mythical. She then summarises his surviving work (Pollen, Hymns to the Night, the unfinished Heinrich of Ofterdingen with its Blue Flower, described as fragmentary by choice like Lucinde) and the dispute over completion, in which Friedrich rejects any addition as 'sacrilegious, heinous, godless, unholy'. The episode shows the Romantic cult of the fragment and the reverence given to a dead poet, but the fragment cult here is shown through the actors' reactions, not analysed by Wulf (Novalis, Friedrich Schlegel).

Notable passages

Goethe's delirium (c1). Schiller's report that Goethe's fever-dreams centred on Schelling's ideas, and Wulf's double question about what 'back in my own self' meant.

Faust and the Act (c2). The passage where Wulf connects Faust's search for the world's beginning to Fichte's Ich and Schelling's nature-philosophy.

Schelling's collapse (c3). The account of why Schelling could not cope with grief, an example of Wulf's mode of psychological narration.

Hegel into an empty Jena (c4). The line that fixes the moment: the whirlwind has blown itself out and the only two left are not speaking.

Novalis's last day (c5). The final hours, Friedrich Schlegel at the bedside, and the group's response, including Tieck's remark that the unity has been split.

The fragment and the quarrel (c6). Novalis's surviving work and Friedrich Schlegel's fury at the idea that Tieck might complete Heinrich of Ofterdingen.

For World Machines, this is closest to the group-as-network theme: a scene held together by presence and shared work loses a node and disperses. Wulf is a narrative historian here, not a theorist of that dynamic, and the parallel is the reader's.
