Leutragasse, November 1799: the alliance of the chosen ones and a new religion

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
In November 1799 the Jena friends gather for four intense days at the Schlegel house in Leutragasse, where Novalis's essay Europe and Schleiermacher's On Religion turn their circle toward a Romantic religion of feeling and imagination, while the group's combative mood, the Kotzebue satire, the rift with Schiller and Goethe's cautious veto of publication expose the limits of their collective self-confidence.

Wulf narrates the four days in mid-November 1799 when Novalis's arrival completed the Jena group (the Schlegel brothers, Caroline, Dorothea, Schelling, Novalis and Tieck) under one roof. Against the backdrop of Napoleon's coup, which the friends watch approvingly from the safe distance of neutral Saxe-Weimar, she shows the circle at its most creative (poems, translations, experiments, dialogue on poetry) and its most combative (the Kotzebue satire, the hostile Allgemeine Literatur-Zeitung, the deliberate rift with the Schillers while Charlotte Schiller lay gravely ill). The centre of the chapter is Novalis's reading of Europe, which calls for a new religion of feeling and love and indicts the Enlightenment for turning the world into a grinding mill. Wulf places this alongside Schleiermacher's On Religion and Friedrich Schlegel's messianic ambitions, and ends with Goethe, asked to referee, advising against publishing Europe.

Themes

Collective creation as lived practice. The house in Leutragasse is a working instance of Symphilosophie: the friends agree that they never have to agree, because nothing is duller than 'flat uniformity of opinion', and Novalis says 'I produce best in dialogue.' Wulf's Athenaeum dialogue on poetry (four men, two women) is itself a product of these afternoons. This is a vivid picture of The Jena Circle as a scene that generated ideas collectively, though it is Wulf's narrative reconstruction from letters, not a theory of networks.

Nature as one organism, demonstrated with frogs. Johann Wilhelm Ritter's Galvanic experiments (electricity through frog muscle and metal) seem to the friends to prove Schelling's idea of nature as a single organism; Novalis records that Ritter is searching for the 'real soul of the world'. Wulf notes the parallel with Alexander von Humboldt's obsession with Galvanism (alexander-von-humboldt). This is the experimental face of Naturphilosophie.

Combativeness and insularity. Criticised from many sides, the friends become more combative rather than more careful: Friedrich is pleased by Kotzebue's play about him, August Wilhelm answers with a Lord's Prayer parody, Schelling demands to review his own work. Wulf reports that they feel invincible, cleverer and wittier than anyone. The counterpoint is the human cost next door: no one visits the Schillers although Charlotte is delirious, and August Wilhelm says 'We don't make enemies, we already have them.' The chapter shows a scene hardening into a faction, a preview of the fracturing Wulf traces later; it should be read as her framing plus the characters' own bravado.

Novalis's Europe: religion against the mechanical modern age. Wulf reports Novalis's argument that the Reformation destroyed a Europe once unified by one faith, that the Enlightenment stripped the world of spirituality, and that the world had become 'a materialistic machine, a gigantic millwheel'. He idealises the Middle Ages and a community that 'paid no attention to national borders', calling for a 'new confederation of minds'. Wulf pointedly notes what he ignores (the Inquisition's violence) and that the friends themselves asked whether he was going backwards. This is a Romantic critique of disenchantment that resonates with counter-enlightenment currents and with WMP's modernity-machine (a machine of reason, productivity and utility), though Novalis's target is the Enlightenment's mechanised cosmos, not legibility as such, and his remedy is a nostalgic-futural religion rather than a new infrastructure. The link to romanticism-and-political-danger is only latent here: the vision of a borderless spiritual union is peaceful, but Wulf highlights its idealising blind spots. See also Novalis.

Religion as self-in-the-universe. Wulf's own interpretive claim is that the friends were not becoming churchgoers; their turn to religion was 'not a search for God but a search for themselves as part of the universe', injecting feeling and imagination into a materialistic world. Schleiermacher's On Religion (religion as 'contemplation of the universe', drawing on Schelling's unity and baruch-spinoza's God-is-nature, and on the significance of the Ich) supplies the model. Friedrich Schlegel (Friedrich Schlegel) goes further and casts himself as prophet of a new bible with poets as priests, which his brother finds preposterous.

Caution and the limits of publication. August Wilhelm, Caroline and Dorothea hesitate over publishing Europe; Schelling's bawdy 'religion' poem lampoons the enterprise; the group cannot agree. They turn to johann-wolfgang-von-goethe, who after the Fichte atheism affair advises against publication and distinguishes what may be thought privately from what may be published. Novalis, undisturbed, remains certain his religion of love will succeed.

Notable passages

The four days in Leutragasse (c1) capture the collaborative ideal in the friends' own words. The Kotzebue passage (c2) shows the group's response to attack: delight, satire, and a sense that 'His revolution was succeeding.' Novalis's Europe (c3) is the chapter's ideological core, with its millwheel image of the Enlightenment. Wulf's own gloss on why religion mattered to them (c4) and the summary of Schleiermacher's On Religion (c5) supply interpretation and the intellectual source. Finally, Goethe as referee (c6) shows the older generation's restraint over the group's excesses; note that friedrich-schiller appears in the chapter mainly through the Charlotte illness episode and the rift, not as a participant in the debate.
