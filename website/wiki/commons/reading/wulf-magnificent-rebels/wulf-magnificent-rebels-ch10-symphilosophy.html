Dresden summer, 1798: the group in motion and Schelling's arrival

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
In summer 1798 the Jena Set vacation in Dresden, turning galleries, Napoleon and Novalis's dreams of transcending the body into shared conversation, while Schelling, freshly appointed to Jena, arrives with a Tübingen-born philosophy of freedom and nature that sits between Fichte's Ich and Goethe's realism.

Wulf narrates the Jena Set's July to October 1798 holiday in Dresden, hosted at Charlotte Ernst's house. The friends live out the collaborative ideal of ‘symphilosophising’ in public: they storm the picture galleries, debate art, and listen to Novalis's increasingly strange ideas about mind and body. Napoleon's Egyptian campaign and the French plundering of European art form a backdrop in which the friends admire him as a romantic genius. The second half introduces Friedrich Schelling: his strict Tübinger Stift youth with Hölderlin and Hegel, the shared manifesto that fed Jena thinking, his Naturphilosophie as a departure from Fichte, and Goethe's reluctant path to inviting him to Jena. Dorothea is absent, barred by cost, propriety and the Saxon ‘Jew toll’.

Themes

Thinking as group sociability (Symphilosophie, The Jena Circle). Wulf shows the friends spending most waking hours together, walking, gesticulating and arguing, and quotes Friedrich Schlegel's phrase for such gatherings, an ‘ur-assembly of humanity’. The scene is Wulf's own framing of collective idea production as lived practice, not an abstract claim. Caroline's essay ‘Paintings. A Conversation’ is presented as the written trace of these debates, in dialogue form.

Transcending body and mind (Novalis). Novalis tries to make an elixir to ‘cure physicality’ and wants ‘to touch his own mind’. Wulf calls it ‘just another attempt to liberate the mind’ and notes that even Friedrich Schlegel admitted he did not understand it. The torchlit plaster-cast galleries are her image of imagination made three-dimensional. This extends the Ich-philosophy's elevation of mind toward the literal, and the note is Wulf's interpretation, not a claim by the actors.

Art as religion, and the self in a sinner's story. The Sistine Madonna is read by the young Romantics as a fusion of sensuality, religion and art; religious art has ‘left the church and become poetic’. Caroline's defence of the Penitent Magdalene is offered by Wulf, with a hedge (‘Could Caroline have been thinking about herself?’), as echoing Caroline's own life.

Napoleon as romantic genius; war feels distant. Wulf says Napoleon was, for them, ‘in a sense’ their ideal of a genius driven by his own talents, a ‘force of nature’. She sets this against the Louvre-filling plunder of art and his scholars in Egypt. The friends bought busts of him, and with battles far off ‘war didn’t quite feel real’. This is a marker of the Revolution and Napoleon as political backdrop, before later dark turns; it resonates only loosely with history-machine thinking, which this unit does not itself argue.

Schelling: freedom, nature, and a controlling upbringing. The Tübinger Stift is described as a will-breaking institution (‘a miserable cog in a slave machine’) from which Schelling, Hölderlin and Hegel rebelled, celebrating the Revolution. Their one-page resolution wants to unite what had been divided, make philosophy sensual and poeticise the sciences, and opens on the Ich as absolutely free. Schelling's diverging point from Fichte is that nature and the Ich are an interconnected whole, his Naturphilosophie. This is a case of self-and-freedom claims emerging against an institutional discipline, cf. romanticism-and-political-danger for later dangers, which are not in this unit.

Goethe between realism and idealism (johann-wolfgang-von-goethe). Goethe, ‘a rigid realist’ who had moved toward the middle, says knowledge uses the mind's categories yet animals are shaped by environment. Fichte's tree-or-nose argument is set against Goethe's comparative anatomy. Wulf reports these as the two men's positions; Goethe's joke about enjoying his ‘undivided existence’ frames the gap Schelling was expected to bridge.

Notable passages

The Novalis elixir passage (c1) shows his change after Sophie's death and Wulf's reading of the experiments as a liberation of the mind. The gallery passage (c2) is the clearest picture of symphilosophy as noisy public conversation. The Magdalene passage (c3) shows Caroline's own moral voice in the group. The Napoleon passage (c4) gives the friends' romantic-genius reading of him. The Tübingen manifesto (c5) sets out what drove the Jena Set's thinking before Schelling arrived. The Goethe and Fichte passage (c6) is the philosophical crux: idealism against observation of nature, with Schelling as prospective mediator.
