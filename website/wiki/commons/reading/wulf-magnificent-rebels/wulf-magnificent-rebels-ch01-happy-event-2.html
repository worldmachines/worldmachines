Goethe and Schiller: a friendship begun in disagreement

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Wulf narrates the July 1794 walk from a Jena lecture on which Goethe and Schiller's friendship began, framing it as a clash of empiricism and idealism that became a decade-long complementary collaboration, set against Goethe's revulsion at revolution and war and his unconventional household with Christiane Vulpius.

The unit opens with the scene Wulf treats as the origin of the most fruitful literary friendship of the age: after a Natural History Society lecture in Jena in July 1794, Goethe sketches his primordial-leaf idea of botany for Schiller, who objects that it is 'an idea', not an observation. Wulf then backfills why Goethe was ripe for a new partnership: two miserable, unproductive years marked by the Terror, the war on France, and the bombardment of Mainz that he witnessed in 1793, from which he retreated into science and the Jena botanical garden. The narrative moves to Schiller's fortnight at Goethe's Weimar house in September 1794, the Roman Elegies, Goethe's unmarried life with Christiane Vulpius (and the court's gossip, including Schiller's), and then to the daily rhythm of shared work between Jena and Weimar, the men's contrasting temperaments and methods, and Goethe's emergence as the 'demi-god' of a younger generation.

Themes

Empiricism versus idealism as generative friction. Wulf presents the first conversation as the summary of a fundamental disagreement: Goethe, in her account, describes himself as a hard-headed realist who derives knowledge from observing nature, while Schiller, steeped in Kant, calls himself an idealist and holds that we perceive reality through categories of the mind. Per Wulf, Goethe himself later said that the competition between object and subject was the foundation on which they sealed their connection. Their methods are complementary (Goethe moves from the particular to the general, Schiller from the general to the particular), and each corrects the other's characteristic failing. This is Wulf's interpretive framing, supported by the men's own remarks, which she quotes.

Nature as a living whole. Goethe's morphology (behind variety, unity; every plant a variation of a primordial form) and the shared dislike of botany as a 'fragmentary way of looking at nature' foreshadow the Naturphilosophie that Wulf develops later in the book. In this unit it is a conversational seed, not yet a doctrine.

Political temperament mirrors scientific temperament. Wulf argues that Goethe's political beliefs mirrored his scientific ideas: as a Neptunist (slow sedimentation) rather than a Vulcanist (catastrophe), he favoured slow reform over volatile uprisings. The French Revolution, drenched in blood by the Terror, left him disillusioned; science became a 'plank in a shipwreck'. This is a historian's analogy, offered with the geological debate as illustration, and it sits in tension with the counter-enlightenment and revolution-vs-reform frame only loosely: Goethe here is a reformist retreating into study, not a reactionary. The backdrop is the political rupture that later shapes the Jena circle; compare romanticism-and-political-danger for where that goes later.

The self and its private arrangements. Goethe's open household with Christiane Vulpius, a seamstress, scandalises Weimar not for the affair but for her low social standing, according to Wulf's reading; Schiller's own tangled courtship of the Lengefeld sisters is described as more acceptable only because of class. Christiane is presented as the domestic stability that let him work. Wulf reports gossip (Wieland's 'a pig with a pearl necklace') as gossip. This is early evidence for the book's thread of lives lived as experiments, though here the experiment is one man's and is class-inflected.

Friendship as a working infrastructure. The letters shuttling between Jena and Weimar several times a week, mutual editing of Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship, shared evenings, and the ordinary content (wallpaper, fresh fish) show a two-person creative scene. It is a small-scale precursor of the collective production Wulf later traces in The Jena Circle and Symphilosophie, though here it is an asymmetric pair rather than a network; see also johann-wolfgang-von-goethe and friedrich-schiller.

Generational authority. The closing paragraph says that Goethe would become the hero and 'demi-god' of a new generation of young men and women, setting up the later relation of the Romantics to him.

Notable passages

The first-day exchange in Schiller's house (Goethe's leaf sketch and Schiller's 'an idea') is the hinge of the unit and is followed by Wulf's explicit gloss on the philosophical difference. The Neptunist passage carries her political analogy. The Mainz passage shows Goethe's reaction to war and his need for 'objects of thought'. The Christiane paragraph gives the class-and-gossip dimension of the Weimar court. The last curated span sums up how each man's method complemented the other's.
