How the Jena Set's ideas travelled, and what 'selfish' was meant to mean

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
In the Epilogue's closing parts Wulf traces how the Jena Set's ideas spread through Madame de Staël, Coleridge, the Transcendentalists, Whitman, Freud and Joyce, then argues that their 'Art of being Selfish' meant self-awareness as the precondition for empathy, moral duty and community.

The unit covers two sub-parts of Wulf's Epilogue. Part II ('Influence') is a narrative survey of reception: Madame de Staël's Germany (pulped by Napoleon in 1810, published in London in 1813) carried the Jena Set's ideas and the word 'romantique' across Europe; Coleridge absorbed and partly plagiarised Schelling; Wordsworth, Mary and Percy Shelley, Byron, Carlyle, the Transcendentalists (Emerson, Thoreau), Whitman, Melville, Poe, Freud and Joyce are all shown drinking from the same well, along with Polish, Swedish, Scandinavian and Russian Romantics. Part III ('The Art of Being Selfish') is Wulf's own interpretive argument: the Jena friends put the self and free will centre stage, that legacy is ours, and 'selfish' in their original sense meant self-awareness, which grounds empathy and responsibility to others and to the planet.

Themes

Reception as a diffusion network. Wulf presents influence through named transmitters: Staël and August Wilhelm Schlegel, Coleridge's Biographia Literaria, Carlyle's essays and translations, Crabb Robinson and Steffens as students of Jena. Ideas move by translation, borrowing and outright lifting (De Quincey's charge against Coleridge, Poe's charge against Hawthorne and his own use of Schlegel). Wulf hedges the plagiarism claim as 'a bit of an exaggeration' with 'some truth in it'. This is a story of a scene (The Jena Circle, Symphilosophie) whose output is later redistributed across a continent and an ocean, though Wulf writes as a narrative historian, not a network theorist.

Germany against France, self against the general will. In Staël's own account (reported by Wulf), the French are witty but conformist and 'all-powerful only en masse', whereas Germans discovered individuality and a self-determined Ich. Napoleon's suppression of the book is the political frame. This is Staël's and Napoleon's framing, with Wulf as reporter; it echoes the Romantic side of counter-enlightenment debates, though Staël here praises German depth rather than rejecting reason as such.

Unity of self and nature as the portable core. Wulf identifies the unity of mankind and nature as 'at the core of Romanticism'. Schelling's Naturphilosophie passes into Coleridge's revised 'The Eolian Harp' ('Schelling's Naturphilosophie writ in verse'), Caspar David Friedrich, Wordsworth, Emerson's Nature, Thoreau, Whitman and Tyutchev. Alexander von Humboldt (alexander-von-humboldt) reshapes Thoreau's Walden: scientific accuracy joined to imagination. Compare wulf-invention-of-nature, Wulf's separate book on the same thread.

Interiority and the modern psyche. Wulf claims that the centrality of self-consciousness helped pave the way for psychology and psychoanalysis, listing parallels with Freud (unity of self and nature, the unconscious, imagination). This is Wulf's interpretive claim; she does not say Freud derived his theory from Jena, only that he counted Goethe, Schiller and Schelling among his few great influences. It rhymes with the historicised-self theme in infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness, though Wulf treats the self as an idea that was discovered rather than as a product of infrastructure.

Fragmentary form carried into modernism. Joyce's Finnegans Wake is read (via a critic Wulf quotes) as 'Schlegelian': Romantic poetry as 'forever becoming, never perfected', chaos as generative. Friedrich Schlegel's formal programme survives even in the most modern Modernist.

The 'Art of being Selfish'. Part III is the book's thesis statement. Wulf argues that the Jena Set 'changed our world', that we have internalised Fichte's Ich, and that freedom brings 'both responsibilities and dangers'. She defends Fichte against 'Ich-fetishism' charges by citing his 1794 line that only those are free who try to make everyone around them free, and redefines 'selfish' historically as being aware of and in control of one's own being, liberated from predestined place under monarchs. In Schelling's frame it also means recognising one's unity with nature, so not harming the planet means not harming oneself. Wulf's own moral conclusion, resting on Novalis: self-understanding is the prerequisite for understanding others, hence for empathy. Note the hedge: this is Wulf's sympathetic reading, and she acknowledges the modern sense of selfishness and the perils of the emboldened self; the historical dark side (egotism, nationalism) belongs to earlier chapters and is only gestured at here. Compare romanticism-and-political-danger for the darker reading that Wulf's Epilogue does not develop.

Notable passages

Staël's contrast of France and Germany (c1). Reported argument that French sociability breeds conformity while Germans found individuality; frames the whole influence story as a claim about the self versus the collective.

Coleridge and Schelling (c2). The mechanics of transmission: unity of inner and outer world, art as 'mediatress', and the plagiarism accusation, which Wulf qualifies.

Emerson's Nature (c3). The unity of self and nature enters American thought; Wulf notes Emerson had just read Coleridge's borrowings from Schelling.

Thoreau and Humboldt (c4). A concrete case where a specific book changes a specific manuscript, showing empirical observation joined to poetry.

Fichte's freedom and moral duty (c5). Wulf's defence of the Ich against the charge of narcissism; freedom and duty as twins.

Selfishness redefined (c6). The historical meaning of 'selfish' as self-awareness and self-determination, tied to Naturphilosophie.

Novalis on self-understanding (c7). The closing argument: turning inward is a moral obligation because it is the route to empathy and to the greater good.
