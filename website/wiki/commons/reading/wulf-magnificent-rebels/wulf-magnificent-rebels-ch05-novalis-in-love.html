Novalis in love, and Fichte revised (Summer 1796)

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Summer 1796: against the backdrop of fourteen-year-old Sophie von Kühn's brutal surgery in Jena, Wulf introduces Novalis, his study of Fichte, his revision of the Ich-philosophy through love and feeling, and Friedrich Schlegel's arrival, with flashbacks to Schlegel's bond with Caroline and the Revolution.

Wulf opens with a long, visceral scene: on 8 July 1796 Dr Stark operates, without anaesthetic or sterilisation, on Novalis's fiancée Sophie von Kühn, and Novalis rides forty miles to her bedside. She then turns to Novalis himself: his sickly childhood, his youth under Schiller's spell, his charisma, his father's Moravian rigidity set against Sophie's open, Revolution-friendly family, and his diaries of sexual struggle. The intellectual core is his five-hundred-page notebook dialogue with Fichte's Ich-philosophy, where he finds the Ich circular, adds love as a 'synthesising force', and declares that philosophy begins in feeling. The chapter closes with Friedrich Schlegel's visit to Weißenfels, their shared turn from Fichte's closed self-reflection to an endless, dizzying back-and-forth, Schlegel's memories of Caroline's influence on his politics, and his anxiety about a review of Schiller's Musen-Almanach.

Themes

Self-knowledge as the road into philosophy. Wulf presents Novalis as taking Fichte's 'coronation of the Ich' personally: philosophy is 'a path into himself', and self and world are 'integrated halves'. This is Wulf's framing of Novalis, supported by his own notebook lines, and it fits her book-level thesis about the invention of the self (compare infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness and self-construction-through-writing-gay, though Wulf is telling a biography, not arguing about infrastructure).

Love and feeling against Fichte's cold Ich. In Novalis's own words, as Wulf reports them, the Ich cannot have created the world, so the non-Ich has to be already there and can only be sensed; love is the synthesising force that turns the non-Ich into a 'You'. This is a Counter-Enlightenment-flavoured move toward feeling and unity (counter-enlightenment), but Wulf does not label it so here.

Infinite reflection and the vortex. Novalis and Friedrich Schlegel, on Wulf's account, reject Fichte's idea that self-reflection completes and instead make philosophy an endless 'thinking about thinking itself', a see-saw of contradiction. This is the seed of what the book later presents as shared Romantic practice (Symphilosophie, The Jena Circle), and it shows two friends, not yet a group, generating an idea in conversation. Wulf's imagery (spinning vortex, ping-pong) is her own narration.

Friendship, difference and the bourgeois yoke. Schlegel and Novalis are a pair of quarrelling, mutually adoring friends, a mentor and a prophet; Schlegel refuses to 'shackle myself to a bourgeois yoke' while Novalis wants 'wedding night, marriage and heirs'. It is a small, characterful instance of the bourgeois/Romantic split (bourgeoisophobia), presented by Wulf as personality contrast rather than theory.

Words as political action. Wulf reports Schlegel crediting Caroline with opening his mind to the Revolution, and his line that revolutions must be spoken as well as carried out; Novalis later says a word of command set armies moving, 'the word freedom'. Wulf's connecting claim is that this circle met the Revolution mostly through print. This is the political backdrop for romanticism-and-political-danger but here it is only a prelude.

Medicine and bodies (context). The surgery scene is scene-setting; Wulf draws attention to the state of eighteenth-century medicine and to the risk to Sophie, which frames Novalis's investment in love. No theoretical argument is made from it.

Notable passages

The notebook passage shows Novalis's method (a long dialogue with Fichte, with maxims such as 'practise slowness') and the line 'I am for myself the ground of all thoughts.' The next passage gives his circularity objection and the formula 'Philosophy is originally a feeling.' The love passage completes the revision: Fichte's non-Ich becomes a 'You'. The Weißenfels passages show the Novalis and Schlegel move from Fichte's closed reflection to an infinite one, with philosophy as 'a path into himself'. The Caroline passage carries the chapter's political thread, the claim that speech is a form of revolutionary action.

WM note: the resonance with world-machines is weak here; the nearest fit is the Jena scene as a small network producing ideas through friendship (The Jena Circle), and Novalis's feeling-first, unity-seeking stance sits on the Romantic side of isaiah-berlin's Enlightenment/Counter-Enlightenment divide, with the caveat that Wulf does not frame it that way. See also Novalis and Friedrich Schlegel.
