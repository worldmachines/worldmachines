Schlegel travels to Caroline while Schiller struggles with Horen (1795)

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Wulf opens Chapter 4 with August Wilhelm Schlegel's 1795 journey to Braunschweig to reunite with Caroline Böhmer, sketching both of them, Caroline's disgrace after imprisonment in Mainz and Königstein, and her decision to accept a marriage of friendship, while in Jena an exhausted Schiller struggles with Horen and sees Schlegel as its most reliable contributor and a man to be lured to Jena through Caroline.

Wulf's chapter opens with a stagecoach journey, which serves as scene-setting for the whole chapter: August Wilhelm Schlegel, resigned from his tutoring post in Amsterdam as French troops advance, travels to Braunschweig to see the widow Caroline Böhmer, who had rejected him several times. Wulf then gives a double portrait. August Wilhelm is the orderly, pedantic, well-groomed son of a Lutheran priest. Caroline is a superbly educated, freedom-loving woman whose life includes an arranged marriage, the deaths of three children, life in Mainz among Revolution sympathisers, imprisonment, an illegitimate birth and the death of that baby in foster care. Barred from several German cities as a 'revolutionary whore', she accepts marriage to August Wilhelm as the least bad option, and she frames it as friendship rather than passion. The chapter then cuts to Jena, where Schiller, lonely and ill, is beleaguered by the burdens of editing Horen and by hostile reviews. He turns to Schlegel as a reliable contributor and prospective reviewer, and asks how to bring him to Jena; Humboldt's answer is that Caroline is the key. The unit is narrative, and most interpretation is carried by quoted letters, so it is important to say whose voice is speaking.

Themes

Women's freedom against the authority of reputation. Wulf reports Caroline's stance in her own words and her sister's: she would not give up her freedom, and she held that one was allowed to do what one wanted if one accepted the consequences. Wulf then shows the cost. Authorities in several cities, acting in the name of 'respectable families', refused her entry, and Wulf points out that even though the French had 'declared all men equal', German authorities still decided who could live where. The double standard is Caroline's own complaint ('a mistake that would have meant nothing were she a man'). This is an early, concrete instance of the self-authorship theme (see self-construction-through-writing-gay for a parallel concern with self-fashioning, though Gay's is bourgeois inwardness and Caroline's is public defiance), and of Revolutionary ideals colliding with the German state.

Marriage as a pragmatic, negotiated arrangement. Caroline's mother urges marriage as the only rescue. Wulf's account of Caroline's reasoning ('Friendship, she decided, was more important than love') and of August Wilhelm's hesitations (social etiquette, family opposition, a secure income) presents the pair as unromantic partners at this stage. This is Wulf's narrative reconstruction from letters and should not be read as a thesis about Romantic marriage; the unconventional marriages of the Jena set become an explicit theme later in the book.

Resilience and interior life. In the description of Caroline's grief, only her daughter Auguste sees her cry, and Caroline tries to find something beautiful to hold tight: 'I can make happiness even from suffering.' Wulf presents this as Caroline's character rather than as a philosophical position.

The Jena network in formation. Schiller's letters show a scene held together by friendship, patronage and quarrelsome journalism: friedrich-schiller needs contributors, Goethe (see johann-wolfgang-von-goethe) counsels calm, Humboldt scouts information, and the group closes ranks against the establishment's 'heavy cavalry'. Reviewers complain that Schiller has assembled his friends and that ideas enjoyed in conversation have little interest for general readers. That is a reviewer's charge, reported by Wulf, but it half-rhymes with the project's interest in The Jena Circle as a scene that generated ideas collectively (not yet the symphilosophy of the later chapters, cf. Symphilosophie). Schlegel's recruitment through his love for Caroline shows how personal ties and literary institutions are entangled.

Print culture strain. The practical difficulties of a war-time literary journal (manuscripts crossing battle lines, missed deadlines, publisher pressure toward lighter material) are the material backdrop. The 'watery soups' against 'hearty fare' remark is Schiller's own, showing a conflict between high abstraction and a broader readership.

Notable passages

Caroline chooses freedom (c1). Suitors and friends push a secure marriage; she refuses, and her sister's line ('loves freedom and not having to justify her every move') captures the attitude on which the rest of her story hangs. The passage records the views of Caroline and her circle, and the friends' 'overenthusiastic ideas of freedom' is their judgment, not Wulf's.

No city will take her (c2). The clearest statement in the unit of the gap between Revolutionary rhetoric and German practice, with Caroline's protest about the double standard.

Marriage as the only rescue (c3). Her mother's insistence, her earlier declaration that she would prefer never to marry, and the governess alternative she declined.

'I can make happiness even from suffering' (c4). The death of her baby, the concealment of her mourning, and her determination not to be destroyed.

Schiller abandoned and overwhelmed (c5). The journal's difficulties, and the sole reliable contributor being Schlegel. Note that the assessment of contributors is Schiller's, as reported in his letters.

Luring Schlegel to Jena (c6). Schiller asks how to draw Schlegel to Jena, and Humboldt's reply points to Caroline. This joins the two halves of the unit and sets up Caroline's move into the Jena scene.
