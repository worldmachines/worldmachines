Jena's magnetic pull: freedom, a circle of friends, and Fichte under siege

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Wulf explains why Jena's odd multi-duchy governance made it Europe's freest university town, sketches the Schiller-Goethe-Humboldt circle of 'social thinking' in winter 1794-95, and then follows Fichte's rise into student fervour, harassment by fraternities, and a rupture with Schiller over the Horen essay.

Wulf opens by arguing (with Schiller's testimony) that Jena drew talent because its university was governed by four Saxon rulers who had to agree on everything, so rules were hard to enforce and teaching, censorship and subject matter were unusually free. She contrasts it with post-1793 France and with Oxford and Cambridge. She then narrows to Wilhelm and Caroline von Humboldt, who move next door to Schiller in late 1794, and to the small circle of Schiller, Goethe and Humboldt. Attention then shifts to Fichte: his enraptured students, the difficulty of his prose, the Sunday-lecture row, the fraternity campaign of broken windows in 1795, and finally his quarrel with Schiller over an essay for Horen, which ends with Schiller's remark that they are 'of two very different natures'.

Themes

Institutional accident as the condition of intellectual freedom. Wulf's own explanatory claim is structural: divided governance made Jena hard to police, so theologians, law professors and philosophers taught things banned or unthinkable elsewhere. The contrast with narrow, clergy-run Oxford and Cambridge (where 'studying science there meant reading Aristotle') and with France's state-run écoles spéciales frames Jena as a special-case environment. This reads as a small-scale example of a history-machine-style story, where a distributed, unintended arrangement produces a scene, though Wulf herself does not use such language and offers a local explanation, not a theory.

A dense scene of friends thinking together. The Humboldts move so close to Schiller that they can wave from window to window; Humboldt becomes Schiller's sparring partner and Horen adviser (Schiller's own words: his ideas 'develop faster and better in conversation with him'); Goethe joins, and the trio becomes 'a three-leaf clover of critical thought' (Schiller's phrase, as reported by Wulf). Humboldt calls it 'social thinking'. This is an early picture of the collective idea-production later associated with The Jena Circle and Symphilosophie, though here it is conversation among older figures rather than the Schlegels' formal joint writing. The group has an unwritten rule: everything is discussed but politics, including the French Revolution.

Self-determination as the shared concern, with the seed of a split. Schiller and Humboldt are both fascinated by Kant's call to shape one's destiny 'from within'. Fichte's Ich-philosophy pushes the same idea further, and Wulf notes that Schiller, Humboldt and Goethe admitted to not understanding it all, while younger listeners such as Hölderlin embraced it. Schiller privately fears the Ich/non-Ich 'vortex' and the notion that the external world does not exist without the Ich. This is Wulf narrating a historical actor's worry, not asserting her own verdict, but it prefigures the book's later concern with the dangers of the self-centred turn (cf. romanticism-and-political-danger).

Personality, private life and the texture of friendship. Wulf's biographical scene-setting (Humboldt's childhood loneliness, Schiller's 'hazelnut shell' and armchair voyages, Caroline's fondness for her reserved husband) gives psychological interiority to the group; it is narrative colour rather than argument, but it fits the wider theme of the self as something lived and written about.

Fichte's fame and the friction it produced. Fichte draws 500 students, is called the 'soul of Jena' by Hölderlin, and is accused by enemies of running a 'church service of Reason' when his only free slot is Sunday morning. Herder, who disliked Fichte, is named as the churchly official in Weimar; the ban is reversed with Goethe's and Voigt's help. Fraternity students, whose drinking-and-duelling culture Fichte attacked, smash his windows for five months. Goethe's quip about the non-Ich being proven to him is Goethe's, reported by Wulf.

A quarrel made of drafts. Wulf reconstructs Schiller's two days and four drafts of a rejection letter (from 'dry, cumbersome and...often confused presentation' to 'shapeless length') and Fichte's wounded reply. The philosophical difference (Schiller: the artist is humankind's teacher; Fichte: the scholar) sits beside personal pride. Wulf's framing is that despite shared beliefs, including free will for the Ich, this 'prevented' nothing about the falling out; the break lasts years.

Notable passages

Why Jena (c1) gives Wulf's structural explanation. Jena versus France and England (c2) supplies the comparative case. Schiller and Humboldt (c3) shows the working friendship and the shared Kantian interest in self-determination. Fichticising (c4) captures both Fichte's magnetism and the incomprehensibility of his written Ich/non-Ich argument, quoted in full. The night of the stones (c5) is the most vivid scene of Jena's rowdy student culture and of Fichte's pistol-and-champagne rush. Schiller's misgivings (c6) states the intellectual objection to Fichte's Ich philosophy in Schiller's own images (abyss, vortex, thrown ball).
