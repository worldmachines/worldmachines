Schiller's Horen and the Aesthetic Alternative to Revolution

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
In the winter of 1794–95, with French armies overrunning the Rhineland, Schiller responds to the Terror by founding the apolitical journal Horen and arguing in his Letters on the Aesthetic Education of Man that beauty, not reason alone, is the route to moral maturity and freedom, while Horen turns the Jena Set into a group for the first time.

Wulf opens with the political backdrop of winter 1794–95: French armies win one victory after another, the Rhine freezes into a highway for troops, the Dutch declare the Batavian Republic, and Prussia, its coffers empty after annexing parts of Poland, signs a neutrality treaty and cedes the left bank of the Rhine. Goethe abhors the war and the Revolution; Schiller, once an honorary citizen of France, is horrified by the execution of Louis XVI and the guillotines. Wulf presents his founding of the journal Horen as "a writer's response": a deliberately apolitical monthly meant to gather "the nation's finest minds", and the moment where the Jena Set first becomes a group. The chapter then covers the journal's first contributors, the scandal of Goethe's Roman Elegies, Schiller's Letters on the Aesthetic Education of Man, and the journal's wide circulation through reading societies and salons.

Themes

Schiller's political turn, as Wulf tells it. Wulf reports that Schiller decided humankind was "simply not advanced enough" for the Revolution's principles: the French were, in his words, like "wild animals" needing "salutary chains", and the Revolution "had happened too soon". This is Schiller's belief as Wulf reports it, not her own verdict. She also notes his changed social position (aristocratic wife, a Danish princely patron), which frames his reaction as partly biographical. Goethe's stance is contrasted: he refuses to choose sides.

Culture as a nation without a state. Horen is described as an organ of the German Kulturnation, "a cultural nation independent of state lines but bound by a shared language". Wulf places Herder behind the idea (language embodies a worldview; a people is whoever was raised in the language). This is an early, non-political form of the national idea; Wulf does not yet draw the later line to nationalism in this unit, but it is the seed that later discussion of nationalism and counter-enlightenment-and-nationalism can pick up, and johann-gottfried-herder is the named source. It sits in tension with the Enlightenment-universalist reading of the Revolution.

Beauty against reason-only Enlightenment. In Wulf's summary of the Letters, Schiller argues that reason, rationality and empiricism had delivered knowledge (gravity, steam engines, declared universal rights) but not moral refinement, and that the guillotine proved this was insufficient. "Utility is the great idol of our time" is his diagnosis; beauty is the instrument that unites the sensual and rational sides, following Kant's third Critique, so that "art is a daughter of freedom" and "it is through beauty that we reach freedom." Wulf states the Letters became a founding document for the Romantics, who would elevate imagination above reason, science and philosophy. This resonates with the WMP contrast between legibility-driven modernity and what it leaves out (modernity-machine, Legibility), and with the counter-enlightenment framing, though Schiller here is a critic from within the Enlightenment tradition (via Kant) rather than a rejecter of it, and Wulf's account is a narrative summary rather than a machine-theoretic reading. See also friedrich-schiller.

A journal as network infrastructure. Horen brings Fichte, Wilhelm and Alexander von Humboldt, August Wilhelm Schlegel and Goethe into one publication, and Wulf claims it made the Jena Set a group. She closes with the circulation mechanics: monthly instalments, no ornament, reading societies, libraries and salons, copies passed through dozens of hands, reaching "a far larger audience than the subscription figures alone". This is a concrete case of print infrastructure producing a scene (The Jena Circle), and an instance of the shared reading infrastructure discussed in infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness, though Wulf does not frame it that way.

Generational split over sensuality. The Roman Elegies shock Weimar's older establishment (Herder's pun on Huren, Wieland's "lavatory temple") while the younger generation, the Schlegel brothers, adores them. This is gossip and reported reaction, useful as an early marker of the generational line between Weimar classicists and the emerging Romantics.

Notable passages

The passage on Schiller's disillusion (c1) records his claims about freedom requiring moral maturity and the Revolution coming too soon, the seed of the later argument about freedom's dangers (romanticism-and-political-danger). The Horen manifesto passage (c2) supplies the Kulturnation idea, with Herder's linguistic definition of a people (c3) as its intellectual source. The account of assembling contributors (c4) states Wulf's claim that the Jena Set became a group here. The Letters passages (c5, c6) give the core of the aesthetic-education argument: utility as idol, beauty as bulwark, art as mediator between heart and head.
