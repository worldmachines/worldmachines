Prologue: Wulf's question about the self, Caroline's arrest, and the Jena Set

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Wulf opens with her own unconventional path and the question of when and how the free, sometimes selfish, self arose, locates the answer in 1790s Jena, and introduces Caroline through her 1793 arrest in Mainz and the cast of the 'Jena Set'.

The Prologue has three movements. Wulf first tells her own story (refusing university, single motherhood, moving to London, becoming a writer in an acquired language) and uses it to pose the book's question: when did we come to expect the right to determine our own lives, and to take what we want? She then says she found the answer in Jena, where a group around Fichte, the Schlegels, Goethe and others, intoxicated by the French Revolution, put the self at centre stage. Finally she opens the narrative with Caroline Michaelis-Böhmer-Schlegel-Schelling: her arrest by Prussian soldiers on 30 March 1793 after leaving revolutionary Mainz, her imprisonment at Königstein with her daughter Auguste, her secret pregnancy, her release, her later marriage to August Wilhelm Schlegel, and a roll call of the group Wulf names the 'Jena Set'.

Themes

Free will versus selfishness as the organising tension. This is Wulf's own framing, and the strongest interpretive claim in the unit. She presents modern life as a thin line between free will and selfishness, self-determination and narcissism, empathy and righteousness, and repeatedly complicates her own autobiography (her independence 'became egotistical'; her freedom rested on middle-class privilege). Her two guiding questions are 'Who am I as an individual? And who am I as a member of a group and society?' This is a thesis-setting move, not evidence; the historical case follows in later units. It echoes the project's interest in the self as a historical construction (cf. infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness and self-construction-through-writing-gay), though Wulf's route is narrative and moral rather than structural.

Jena as the origin point of the modern self, argued by Wulf. She claims that in the last decade of the eighteenth century the group, 'intoxicated by the French Revolution, placed the self at the centre stage of their thinking', and that 'we still think with the minds of these visionary thinkers'. This is a strong, sweeping claim of influence; it is Wulf's assertion at the head of the book and not something the Prologue demonstrates. Fichte is the only actor quoted on the idea (a person 'should be self-determined, never letting himself be defined by anything external'). Compare the The Jena Circle and Symphilosophie pages; the group is treated here as a scene whose ideas 'collided and coalesced', a network-like account, though Wulf does not use the word.

The French Revolution as the political backdrop and enabling shock. Caroline's welcome of the Revolution (reported through her own letters: 'Let the wealthy step aside, and the poor rule the world'; 'we are living in very interesting political times') and the short-lived Mainz Republic, 'the first one on German soil', place the Jena story inside revolutionary politics. Wulf reports Caroline's excitement rather than assessing the Revolution herself. The political dangers of freedom (cf. romanticism-and-political-danger) are not yet raised here.

Caroline as a woman refusing a prescribed role, and as the story's centre. Wulf states that Caroline 'refused to be restricted by the role that society had intended for women' and 'stands at the centre of this inspirational story'. The arrest narrative is scene-setting: Caroline's sarcasm to her interrogator, the prison conditions, and the scandal of a pregnancy by a young French officer. Rumours of affairs with General Custine and Georg Forster are gossip that Wulf reports (she says 'some alleged', 'others suspected'), not established fact; the alleged Custine affair is what August Wilhelm's brother relays. Sexual reputation and legal dependence (the risk of losing Auguste) show the limits on a woman's self-determination.

The group as a constellation, and Goethe as its centre. The closing roll call gives short, characterising thumbnails: Novalis 'played with death and darkness', Fichte 'put the self at the centre of his work', the Schlegel brothers, Dorothea Veit, Schelling, Schiller, Hegel and the Humboldt brothers on the periphery, and Goethe at the centre as 'stern and benevolent godfather'. The characterisations are Wulf's. 'Jena Set' is her coinage, and her stated justification is coincidence of time and place: they 'all came together at the same time in the same place'.

Notable passages

The autobiographical passage that names the book's dilemma (c1) and the paragraph that turns it into historical questions (c2) are the clearest statements of Wulf's own argument. The Jena claim and the Fichte quotation (c3) and the 'nexus of Western philosophy' paragraph (c4) state the thesis of influence and centrality of the self. The arrest narrative (c5) shows Caroline in action and her revolutionary context. The final roll call (c6) is the reference passage for who the Jena Set are and how Wulf positions each, useful for later attribution of roles.
