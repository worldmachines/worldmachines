Novalis's death wish, and the turn inward

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Wulf narrates Novalis's grief after Sophie von Kühn's death in 1797, his Fichte-inspired plan to die by willpower (Nachsterben), and his recovery into Freiberg mining studies, the encyclopaedia project and Magical Idealism, arguing that he answered an Enlightenment of measurement and classification by turning inward and poeticising the sciences.

The chapter opens with Novalis arranging Sophie von Kühn's dress on her bed after her death on 19 March 1797, and follows his grief through the summer and winter of that year. His brother Erasmus dies a month later, and Novalis resolves to follow Sophie into death by willpower alone, a plan he calls 'Nachsterben' and builds from Fichte's free Ich. He keeps a diary that counts days since her death, corresponds with Friedrich Schlegel, and sits among literary companions of self-slaughter (Goethe's Werther, Romeo and Juliet in the Schlegel translation, Hölderlin's Empedocles). By July the resolve dissolves, Shakespeare and the Schlegels draw him back, and a detour on August Wilhelm Schlegel's Lectures on Dramatic Art and Literature shows the romantic/classical contrast entering English Romanticism. Novalis then studies at the Freiberg mining academy under Werner, starts a one-thousand-section 'encyclopaedia' meant to unite knowledge, and formulates 'Magical Idealism'. Wulf sets this against Enlightenment classification (Linnaeus, the metre) and industrial 'mechanical administration', and reads the Hymns to the Night as turning darkness from terror into an inward path.

Themes

The Ich taken to its limit. Wulf shows Novalis taking Fichte's free Ich and 'moulding it into his own eccentric concept of Nachsterben'. If the mind can lift an arm or blink an eye, he reasons, it might 'kill oneself by sheer force of will'. Wulf presents this as Novalis's own logic (his notes and diary), not as her endorsement. The death wish is framed as a sign of strength: 'Death is self-conquest.' This is a concrete instance of the dark side of the self-centred philosophy that runs through the book, and it fits the Novalis portrait of a mind that treated selfhood as a creative force. It is a personal, not political, case, so it only loosely rhymes with romanticism-and-political-danger.

Self-examination as a written practice. The diary, dated by days since Sophie's death and written in 'almost clinical prose', records pain, lust, mood swings and resolve. Wulf calls it 'an uncompromising investigation and meditation on the self'. It resonates with the project's interest in interiority built through writing (self-construction-through-writing-gay), though Gay's subject is the bourgeois nineteenth century and Wulf's is one poet, so this is an analogy, not evidence for Gay's thesis.

A shared literary scene shapes the wish. Wulf notes that they 'had all read' The Sorrows of Young Werther, that Friedrich Schlegel had himself spoken of suicide, and that the Schlegels' Romeo and Juliet arrived weeks after Sophie's death. Friedrich assures him 'I am entirely on your side' and is 'almost jealous'. Read this as Wulf's account of a friendship network passing ideas and moods between members, a small-scale instance of the The Jena Circle, not a claim about causation.

Idealisation and a private religion. Sophie, once smoking, card-playing and strong-minded, is mythologised into an 'ethereal angel'; 'Christ and Sophie' and 'I have religion for dear Sophie, not love' show poetry and love replacing the Christian Church he lamented. Wulf reports this as Novalis's own move.

Poeticising the sciences against an Enlightenment of legibility. Wulf's strongest interpretive passage in this unit contrasts Novalis's 'Magical Idealism' and his encyclopaedia (deliberately named against Diderot and D'Alembert; aiming to unite rather than order alphabetically) with the eighteenth century as 'the age of classification': Linnaeus's two-word names, and the metre defined by the planet. Novalis says 'The sciences must all be poeticised' and 'The more poetical, the more true', though Wulf stresses he was not against reason but for synthesis. This resonates with the modernity-machine and its optimisation for Legibility (standardised measure, taxonomy, administration), with the Romantic reaction as a counter-current; it also fits the counter-enlightenment frame, but Wulf's Novalis wants a poetic synthesis of science rather than a rejection of it, which sits uneasily with Berlin's picture of isaiah-berlin's anti-rationalists. Friedrich Schlegel's 'make Euclid singable' is the companion joke.

Darkness inverted; the interior as wilderness. The mines give Novalis an imagery of darkness that reverses the tradition in which darkness meant 'terror, destruction, evil and death'. Wulf concludes that the Jena Set, not Freud, opened the dark regions of the mind, and contrasts Alexander von Humboldt on the Orinoco with Novalis 'plunging into the wilderness of his self' (alexander-von-humboldt). Freiberg and Werner's geology also connect him to the organic, historical view of nature (cf. Naturphilosophie), with Goethe and Humboldt as fellow followers.

Romantic reaction to a mechanised world. In Wulf's framing, Prussia is run like a factory by 'mechanical administration', steam engines, clocks and manufacturing rationalise daily life, and Novalis turns inwards and seeks to 'romanticise the world'. This reads like a Divergence-versus-Modernity-machine tension, but Wulf herself uses no such vocabulary; the mapping onto world-machines is ours, and she is a narrative historian offering an interpretation, not a machine theorist.

Notable passages

The Nachsterben passage (c1) gives the death wish, the Fichte link and the line 'Grasp, then, a handful of darkness.' The willpower reasoning (c2) spells out the Ich-over-body argument. The diary-and-idealisation passage (c3) captures 'Death is self-conquest'. The Enlightenment contrast (c4) is Wulf's clearest statement of what Novalis is reacting against. The darkness passage (c5) covers the mines, the Hymns to the Night and the Humboldt contrast. The mechanical administration passage (c6) closes the chapter on the Romantic response to industrial disenchantment.
