Goethe and Alexander von Humboldt in the Jena anatomy theatre, spring 1797

Commons · Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
In spring 1797 Alexander von Humboldt's galvanism experiments in Jena's anatomy theatre, and Goethe's parallel dissections and metamorphosis studies, are shown as a shared inquiry into living matter, with Wulf sketching Alexander's driven character and the Napoleonic backdrop.

Wulf opens Part II (its epigraph is Caroline Schlegel's 1799 image of the group as a 'magic cauldron') with a scene of experiment. Alexander von Humboldt, in Jena from 1 March 1797 to finish his book on 'animal electricity', and Goethe cut and electrocute frogs together. Wulf uses the scene to place them in a wider European question about whether living matter is governed by different laws from inert matter. She then gives a biographical portrait of Alexander (restless, self-experimenting, newly wealthy after his mother's death and planning a 'great voyage'), Goethe's parallel studies of caterpillars, frogs and comparative anatomy, and Napoleon's Italian campaign as the noise Goethe wanted to escape.

Themes

Living matter versus the mechanical model. Wulf's own framing: Newton's inert matter and Descartes's animals-as-machines were being questioned. She says Humboldt's galvanic work 'marked the beginning of his thinking about the forces within nature'. Instead of a 'corset of classification' or a divine clockwork, he 'would come to describe it as a web of life'. This anticipates wulf-invention-of-nature and connects to alexander-von-humboldt and Naturphilosophie. Note the hedge: this is Wulf's retrospective reading, and the unit itself shows experiments, not a finished doctrine. The 'corset of classification' also loosely rhymes with WMP's Legibility critique, though Wulf's target is taxonomy, not state administration.

Science as conversation and friendship. Goethe says exchange and dialogue were essential to discovery (Wulf reporting his view). Schiller only listened politely; Humboldt became his 'scientific sparring partner' (Wulf's phrase). Goethe credits Alexander with having 'forced us' into the natural sciences. This is a small-scale example of the collective, place-bound production of ideas seen in The Jena Circle, although here the pairing is scientific rather than philosophical. Goethe's link to friedrich-schiller appears as a limit: collaborator in poetry, not in science.

Alexander's temperament. Wulf builds a character study largely from other people's words and Alexander's own: a fretful energy as if chased by '10,000 pigs', 'overcharged instrument', Wilhelm's worry that 'the poor boy is not happy'. Self-experimentation (electrodes under his tongue, infected wounds) shows the body as a laboratory. These are characterisations by contemporaries, and Wulf should be read as narrator, not diagnostician.

Goethe's morphology. Goethe distinguishes an internal force (Urform, archetype) from the external force of environment, and Wulf compares him to Lamarck and Darwin as recognising adaptation without divine plan. This is Wulf's own comparison; the arm-swinging anecdote is reported as Goethe's explanation to his neighbours. See johann-wolfgang-von-goethe.

Political backdrop. Napoleon's victories in Italy ('we fly') and Goethe's complaint that the world changes before a reply arrives are the acceleration against which the Jena scientific retreat is set. Wulf presents Alexander as 'the perfect distraction'. The French Revolutionary Wars also restrict where Alexander can travel.

Notable passages

The frog-leg scene and the 'breathing life into it' remark (c1) give the unit its image. The paragraph on organic and inorganic matter (c2) states the intellectual stakes. The portrait of Alexander's drive (c3) and his 'great voyage' plan with its aim of showing how 'all forces of nature are interlaced and bound together' (c4) introduce the future explorer. Goethe's need for a sparring partner (c5) and the Urform passage (c6) show the meeting of minds.
