Zaytun and the First Pass at Globalization: Why Fujian

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mann answers why Fujian became the hub of the Ming silver trade by tracing Zaytun, the cosmopolitan medieval port of the first Eurasian 'pass at globalization', from its fourteenth-century apogee through Mongol-era Muslim merchant rule and sectarian militia war to its destruction, after which its trading families fed the Yuegang-based silver trade.

This appendix is Mann's own answer to a question left open in the silver-trade chapters: why Fujian, and not some other Chinese region, became the center of the silver trade. His answer is that Fujian was the part of China most experienced with overseas exchange. He reconstructs the rise and fall of Zaytun (Quanzhou), 'one bay north of Yuegang', as the eastern terminus of the maritime Silk Road: a huge, multi-ethnic emporium at the fourteenth-century height of a Eurasian exchange system. He then narrates how the Song-Mongol transition put the port in the hands of a Muslim merchant lineage (the Pu family), how Sunni, Shi'ite and Sufi factions with private militias tore the city apart in the 1350s-60s, and how the Ming let it silt up. The closing move is Mann's own: Yuegang's merchants were descendants of Zaytun families, so the sixteenth-century 'birth of globalization' inherited a personnel and know-how from the earlier attempt.

Themes

Globalization in two passes. Mann calls the medieval Eurasian system 'a first pass at globalization' that 'reached its apogee in the fourteenth century', with an overland route (dominant until the Mongol empire fell violently apart) and a sea route that became safer as the land route decayed. The later silver-for-silk trade is framed as a second pass on the same coast. This is Mann's own framing, not a scholar's. It rhymes with archaic-globalization-bayly and the long pre-1500 world-economy argument of braudel-world-economy, though Mann cites neither here and offers a narrative rather than a theory.

Bulk trade, not boutique. Mann corrects Marco Polo's luxury-focused account: Fujianese traders 'made most of their money from items that Polo would have found mundane', such as bulk copper and iron for Southeast Asian temples. Zaytun was 'a full-service emporium, not a boutique'. He also records Ibn Battuta calling it the biggest port in the world, and judges that this was not exaggeration.

Commercial cosmopolis, hybrid religion. The city held neighborhoods for Malays, Persians, Indians, Vietnamese and a few Europeans, seven mosques, churches and a cathedral, and countless Buddhist institutions. Mann details a localized, partly unintelligible Fujianese Islam (tomb circumambulation, ancestor veneration, phonetically memorized Arabic recited without understanding). It is a small case of the mixing and syncretism that recurs through the book. Chinese converted to Islam to register as foreigners with privileges, so 'foreign' became a fiscal and legal category as much as an ethnic one, which Mann flags with scare quotes.

Merchant power, private militias and the state. The Pu family controlled trade-ship supervision, militia and navy, and handed Zaytun and its ships to the Mongols in 1276. It was rewarded with control of the port. Each faction later had its own harbor section and militia. The Yuan authorized more private militias against the peasant rebellions of the 1350s, and the militias escaped control: one plundered Xinghua, one made Fuzhou a private satrapy, and a Pu confederate made Zaytun an independent fiefdom and 'exterminate[d]' the Shi'ites. The Yuan commanders finally destroyed the Sunni militias and switched to the Ming. A state that hands security to commercial actors loses control of them, and the outcome is catastrophic for the port.

Contingent destruction and path dependence. The Ming, seeing Zaytun as pro-Yuan, let its waterworks fail and the harbor silt up, and foreign trade did not openly resume for two centuries. Revival came at Yuegang, and Mann stresses continuity of people, not of place: the Zaytun families left the hills and joined the new trade. He closes on an unintended-consequences note: merchants were 'agents of humankind's unending quest to enlace its most far-flung members in a single skein, a journey whose endpoints the travelers have rarely been able to anticipate'. Colón's dream of reaching Zaytun via Polo is mentioned as an aside, which ties this earlier globalization to the Columbian story.

Notable passages

The first passage (c1) defines the 'first pass at globalization', gives the two-route geography and corrects Polo on what actually made money. The second (c2) is the portrait of Zaytun as a walled, irrigated, polyglot metropolis with Ibn Battuta as witness. The third (c3) covers the 1276 handover to the Mongols and the Pu family's rise, including the strategic conversion to Islam. The fourth (c4) describes the hybrid Islam of Fujian and the factional split. The fifth (c5) covers the 1350s-60s militia war, the collapse of the city and the survivors' return via Yuegang. The sixth (c6) is Mann's closing claim about continuity between the two passes.

On World Machines readings: the merchant-run cosmopolis whose fate is set by Mongol and Ming political choices offers a small case of commerce building infrastructure that a later state can starve or reuse. The Ming neglect of the waterworks fits loosely with the decay of canonical infrastructure in modernity-machine terms, though Mann makes no such claim and the case is far earlier and smaller than Rao's 1200-1600 construction window. The Yuan losing control of privatized security is also only a loose parallel to any machine-level story. Treat these as suggested resonances, not Mann's argument.
