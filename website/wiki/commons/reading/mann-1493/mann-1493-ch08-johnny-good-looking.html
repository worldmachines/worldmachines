Juan Garrido and the human wing of the Columbian Exchange

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mann opens the book's human-exchange section with Juan Garrido, an African-born conquistador who planted Mexico's first wheat, then argues that humans were themselves buffeted by the Columbian Exchange and that the African slave trade, not European settlement, dominated the peopling of the Americas.

Mann opens Part Four with a biographical vignette. Juan Garrido ("Johnny Good-looking"), born in West Africa probably in the 1480s, reached Iberia (as an agent of his family, on Ricardo Alegría's reading, or almost certainly as a slave, on Matthew Restall's), then Seville, Hispaniola, Puerto Rico and Florida with Ponce de León, and finally the conquest of Tenochtitlan with Cortés. Mann narrates the 1521 siege, the smallpox epidemic that tipped it, and the ambush at the western causeway where Garrido later built the Chapel of the Martyrs. Garrido then became a municipal factotum of the new Mexico City and, in Mann's telling, planted the first wheat from three kernels Cortés found in a sack of rice. Mann then steps back: the book so far has treated humans as directors of the Columbian Exchange, but Homo sapiens is also a species distributed and reshuffled by it. The slave trade dominated this human wing, and the chapter previews its two topics (plantation slavery and the cultural mix it produced) and the next chapter's Indian-African encounter. The unit ends with Garrido's petition of 1538 and his disappearance from the record.

Themes

Humans as objects of the exchange, not only its directors. Mann says that until now "I have acted as if humankind were in the director's chair," whereas biologists treat humans as one species with a range. This reframes the Columbian Exchange as a convulsion within our own species, with people "like dice flung on a gaming table." It fits Mann's broader theme of unintended consequences, and loosely resonates with the history-machine idea of processes no one steered, though Mann is not a machine theorist.

Africa, not Europe, as the demographic source of the New World. Relying on the Eltis and Halbert slave-voyages database (about 35,000 voyages; 11.7 million captives leaving for the Americas 1500-1840 against about 3.4 million European emigrants), Mann argues that three Africans crossed for each European. He quotes Eltis: America "was an extension of Africa rather than Europe until late in the nineteenth century." The claim is Mann's synthesis of Curtin and Eltis; note Mann's hedge that the numbers rest on scholarly estimates. He adds that the nineteenth-century European wave, being larger, hid from later immigrants that they followed trails set by Africans.

Slavery as foundational institution. Mann states that the "great transformation ... was wrought largely by African hands" and that slavery was "the foundational institution of the modern Americas." This is Mann's own rhetorical thesis.

Individual agency inside structural exchange. Garrido is presented as both agent of the exchange (planting wheat) and product of it ("an African turned European turned American"). Mann leans on two scholars who disagree on Garrido's arrival (Alegría vs Restall) and keeps the uncertainty. The claim that Garrido's wheat reached Texas and the Midwest is attributed to Mexican smallholders and hedged by Mann ("If this is accurate").

Mexico City as first fully transoceanic city. African slaves, Asian shopkeepers, Indian laborers and European clerics made the first urban complex "in which a majority of the inhabitants had their ancestry across an ocean": the setup for the chapter's "crazy soup" theme of mixed societies. This can be read as a proliferation of ontologies in the spirit of the divergence-machine, though Mann does not frame it that way.

Conquest contingency. The Spanish victory hinges on smallpox and on Cortés's Indian allies; Mann notes the "Aztec empire" is a nineteenth-century term for the Triple Alliance.

Notable passages

The opening (c1) sets Garrido's chapel as possibly the first Christian church on the American mainland and introduces him as a figure of which little is known. The wheat passage (c2) carries Mann's claim about Garrido's economic legacy and López de Gomara's 1552 line about the debt owed "to a black man and slave." The pivot passage (c3) states the human-exchange framing. The numbers passage (c4) and the Africa-not-Europe passage (c5) carry the section's core argument. The closing Mexico City passage (c6) presents Garrido as a prototypical citizen of the new mixed world.
