Columbus's Lighthouse and the Homogenocene Thesis

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mann opens 1493 at the Columbus Lighthouse in Santo Domingo, using the monument's troubled history and a deflating portrait of Colón (pious, ambitious, wrong about the earth's size, lucky) to argue that the Columbian Exchange began a new biological era, the Homogenocene, that Colón created almost by accident.

Mann frames the book with his own visit to the Faro a Colón in Santo Domingo, a concrete cross-shaped monument that took almost 150 years to build and was boycotted at its 1992 inauguration. He surveys the polarized verdicts on Colón (nineteenth-century adulation versus modern condemnation as brutal, incompetent, fanatical, greedy), then gives a compact biography: the shifting names, the concealed origins as a weaver's son, the dynastic ambition, and a millenarian faith nourished by Joachim di Fiore's Franciscan admirers. He debunks the schoolbook myth of the lone round-earth prophet: scholars had long known the earth was large and round, and Colón was wrong about its size, wagering on a circumference of about 20,400 miles against Eratosthenes's roughly 25,000. Spain's monarchs, eager to bypass Islamic and Venetian/Genoese middlemen in the Asian luxury trade, ignored their expert committee and backed him anyway. Colón died bitter and messianic. The chapter closes on Mann's own thesis: the Columbian Exchange homogenized formerly distinct ecosystems (the Homogenocene), so the lighthouse should be read as recognition of the world Colón almost accidentally created rather than as a celebration of the man.

Themes

Colón as a flawed, unintentional agent. Mann weighs the standard charges against Colón, qualifying each (brutal by today's standards; not racist in the modern sense because modern race concepts did not yet exist; incompetent as an administrator but not as a seaman). His central move is to separate the man from the consequences: the Homogenocene is something Colón "almost accidentally created." This is Mann's own framing, and the unintended-consequences logic rhymes with the history-machine idea of a paradigm assembled by distributed components rather than by great men, though Mann is not a machine theorist and here still centers one individual's voyages.

Motives: trade routes and millenarian faith. Mann reports that Colón's plan joined commercial and apocalyptic aims. Colón predicted Chinese trade would fund a reconquest of the Holy Land within three years, in a milieu shaped by Joachim di Fiore's prophecies. The Spanish and Portuguese crowns wanted Asian textiles, porcelain and spices while Venice and Genoa held a deal with Islamic powers and monopolized the trade; Portugal's route around Africa was long and risky. The middleman monopoly resonates loosely with the Mediterranean-centred picture in braudel-world-economy, though Mann gives no Braudelian analysis here.

Myth versus geography. The fourth-grade image of Columbus proving the earth round is called exactly backward by Mann. The real dispute was about size. Colón took a ninth-century caliph's claim of 560 miles per degree (Mann notes it is closer to sixty-nine), multiplied by 360, and got 20,400 miles, then paired this with an overlong Eurasia to argue for a crossing of as little as three thousand miles. Mann's analogy for the expert committee's reaction (hoisting an anvil with a thread) makes the point that the monarchs gambled against expertise and, thanks to an unknown continent, won.

Memory and commemoration. The monument's history (approved in 1923, competition eight years later, built six decades after that under Trujillo, boycotted in 1992, protesters calling Colón "the exterminator of a race") shows commemoration tracking political legitimacy. Mann reports rather than endorses the critics, then argues that their conclusion is "understandable" but "mistaken" because of the exchange's scale.

The Homogenocene. Mann introduces the term as one some biologists now use, meaning the blending of once-distinct ecologies into a uniform mix, and links it to the Columbian Exchange. The claim that "the world has become one" prefigures the book's globalization argument. It also stands as a homogenization process, a counterpoint to the variety-multiplying dynamics of the divergence-machine in the WMP frame, though Mann's concern is biological rather than institutional.

Notable passages

Colón's ambition and faith (c1). Mann's sketch of a man driven by dynastic ambition and Joachimite crusading faith, quoting Colón's own prediction about the conquest of the Holy Land. It supplies the motives that the rest of the book treats as historically consequential but not as the explanation of outcomes.

The round-earth myth inverted (c2). Mann's personal anecdote of the schoolroom etching and his flat statement that scholars had known for fifteen hundred years the world was large and round.

Why Spain wanted a western route (c3). A concise account of the Asian luxury trade, Islamic and Italian middlemen, and Portugal's costly African route: the geopolitical demand that made a wrong circumference attractive.

Colón versus Eratosthenes (c4). The arithmetic of the miscalculation and the monarchs overriding their experts, with Mann's hedged, humorous framing of the gamble.

The lighthouse as a failed monument (c5). The Trujillo-era construction and the 1992 boycott and protests, which show that the memory of Colón was contested from its first century to its last.

The thesis statement (c6). Mann's explicit thesis: the Columbian Exchange, the Homogenocene, and the reading of the lighthouse as recognition of an accidental world-making.
