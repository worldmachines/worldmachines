About-Face: why England turned from servants to slaves

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mann asks why England abruptly swapped indentured servants for African chattel slavery in the Chesapeake between 1680 and 1700, rejects purely economic and demographic explanations as incomplete, and uses the failed Scottish colony at Panama to argue that malaria and yellow fever made forced European labor unworkable in the tropics.

Mann opens by calling malaria "a historical force that deformed cultures" and poses a puzzle. Seventeenth-century English planters in Chesapeake Bay, who had no gold or silver and so needed an export crop (tobacco), needed a great deal of labor. Indentured servants from England were cheaper and, by Adam Smith's logic, better workers than enslaved Africans, and England was Europe's most antislavery culture. Yet between 1680 and 1700 Virginia's enslaved population jumped from about three thousand to more than sixteen thousand, servants dwindled, and England became the dominant slave trader. Mann surveys the standard explanations, which he says must each be partly true but are each insufficient: the profits of the slave trade itself (small in aggregate economic terms), the availability of cheap land (which fails to explain New England versus Barbados), and post-civil-war labor scarcity and rising servant prices (which fail to explain why planters did not turn to desperate Scots). He then turns to the Scottish colony at Panama, which was wrecked by disease. His own conclusion, stated only at the very end, is that Europeans died too fast in malarial zones to serve as mass forced labor, so businesspeople looked elsewhere for workers.

Themes

Malaria as a shaping force, not just a misery. Mann frames disease as "an insistent nudge that pushed societies to answer questions in ways that today seem cruel and reprehensible." The chapter is built as a whodunit in which the successive economic explanations are each accepted as partial and then found wanting, leaving the disease environment as the missing piece. The claim is Mann's own synthesis. The final step is asserted briefly rather than proven here, and it leans on the Panama case and on McNeill.

Slavery as economically puzzling. Citing Russell Menard's price study, Mann notes that a prime-age male African slave cost the equivalent of about 25 pounds against about 10 pounds for a servant's contract. He then reports adam-smith's argument in The Wealth of Nations that free labor "comes cheaper in the end" because slaves are culturally unfamiliar, have every reason to sabotage or flee, and lack incentives. Mann adds Smith's own caveat that masters historically offered slaves paths to liberty or satisfying tasks, and that American chattel slavery removed all such workarounds. Smith's view of slavery as the irrational product of a "love to domineer" is Smith's, reported by Mann.

England's antislavery culture, and why it makes the turn stranger. Mann argues, as his own reading of the evidence, that English hostility to bondage was less moral advancement than rage at Barbary corsairs, who enslaved tens of thousands of English subjects. He notes the hypocrisy of celebrating Drake for similar raiding. Slavery had become rare in England, and there was no domestic slave industry to protect. Early colonies relied on servants (between a third and a half of European arrivals) and Virginia had only about three hundred slaves in 1650.

Partial explanations, each qualified. (1) Profit: citing Eltis and Engerman, the slave trade was a moral stain but a small share of British shipping, and Caribbean sugar was under 2.5 percent of British GDP. (2) Cheap frontier land: Smith's observation that workers would leave to become landlords was later developed by other economists into the claim that employers wanted to restrict movement, but this fails to explain New England and New York versus Barbados and St. Kitts. (3) Labor scarcity after the civil war, which Mann links to Little Ice Age unrest and the uncertainties of the silver trade: English population fell almost 10 percent from 1650 to 1680, driving up wages and servant prices. Yet Scotland's crop failures supplied a desperate pool of potential workers, which the colonists ignored.

The Darien natural experiment. William Paterson's Panama scheme, which Paterson pitched as an "arbitrator of the commercial world" breaking Spain's grip on the silk and silver trades, collapsed within months. Mann lists the many fathers of the calamity (unsellable woolens, rain, royal orders not to help, Spanish attacks) but identifies disease as the main cause. He connects it to Scotland's Union of 1707 through J. R. McNeill's remark that Great Britain was born "with assistance from the fevers of Panama." The Darien episode ties this chapter to the global silver-and-silk circuits and to the archaic-globalization-bayly and braudel-world-economy themes of world-economy formation, though Mann is not a systems theorist.

Unintended consequences and world-system resonance. A world-scale ecological fact, a disease environment that nobody planned or could read, shapes institutional choices such as chattel slavery and the labor regime. This loosely rhymes with the history-machine idea of a paradigm assembling distributedly, and with illegible ecological processes escaping the intent of the modernity-machine's legibility projects. It is only a rhyme: Mann offers one causal thread in a journalistic argument, and does not model any machine.

Notable passages

The price comparison (c1) sets up the puzzle: at about four years of English pay, the slave was the more expensive option. The Smith passage (c2) supplies the standard economic reasons a rational planter should have preferred servants. The lifelong sentence passage (c3) states the puzzle at full strength, that American slavery had every disincentive Smith identified and none of the historical workarounds. The 1680-1700 pivot (c4) is the chapter's empirical hinge. The Panama disease passage (c5) is the core of Mann's evidence that disease, not Indians or supplies, destroyed New Edinburgh. The closing paragraph (c6) delivers Mann's causal claim, hedged only by the wry "Alas, they found them."
