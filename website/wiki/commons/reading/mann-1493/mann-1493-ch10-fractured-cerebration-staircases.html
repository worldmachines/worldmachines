Bahay Kubo, Ifugao terraces, and fractured cerebration

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Opening the Coda in Bulalacao and the Ifugao hills, Mann uses the 'Bahay Kubo' garden song, Manila's invasive-species campaigners, and the dying Luzon rice terraces to show that supposedly age-old Philippine landscapes are products of the Columbian Exchange and the galleon trade, and that globalization's harms and benefits cannot be cleanly separated.

The Coda opens with Mann's own reportage from the Philippines. Manila botanists tell him that every plant in the beloved folk song "Bahay Kubo" is an introduced species, while the local Conservation International office campaigns against invasive exotics that are making the country a "vest-pocket version of the Homogenocene". Mann then admits his own contradictory stance toward his tomato patch and his nursery complaint, which he labels "fractured cerebration". The second half follows him to the Ifugao rice terraces of northern Luzon, where giant earthworms are said to be making terraces porous. Mann traces the worms back (via his grandfather's Philippine mahogany breakfast table) not to introduced species but to logging that pushed native worms into paddies. He then reports archaeological work (Beyer, Keesing, Maher) that shows most terraces are only a few hundred years old, built by refugees from Spanish-ruled Manila, so that they are a product of the same exchange now destroying them. The unit ends with the fight over Eighth Wonder's heirloom-rice export scheme and the unanswered question of what would count as saving the terraces.

Themes

Authenticity of tradition is itself a product of exchange. Mann's own argument (delivered via the botanists' amusement) is that the garden in "Bahay Kubo" is "a polyglot, cosmopolitan, thoroughly contemporary artifact". What counts as native, home or heritage is a moving target set by when one starts counting.

Fractured cerebration. Mann implicates himself: he complains at a nursery cash register that nothing is local while buying Mesoamerican, South Asian and European seedlings, "simultaneously promoting and denouncing the Columbian Exchange". The conservationists' worry about homogenization and his own gardening love are both real, and he does not resolve them.

Causal attribution is slippery. The obvious story (invasive worms, an "object lesson in the evils of globalization") is undercut when Filipino native worms turn out to be responsible, driven by mahogany logging for foreign furniture markets. Mann reports two biologists' 2008 finding, hedging that the natives were "probably" always present in small numbers. The activists' framing, in which people like his grandfather are unwitting agents of globalization, is reported rather than endorsed wholesale. Elsewhere in the unit, the golden apple snail episode shows failed escargot schemes producing unintended ecological damage.

Old-seeming order as recent, globalization-made artifact. Beyer's unevidenced 2,000-3,000 year estimate, Keesing's Spanish-records argument (no mention until 1801), and Maher's excavation (with early-2000s follow-up) together suggest a core about two thousand years old and a bulk only a few hundred years old. The radiocarbon dates suggest, in Mann's cautious phrasing, that Ifugao were refugees from Spanish labor demands linked to the galleon trade's shipbuilding, making the terraces "a monument to the galleon trade". This is Mann's synthesis of scholars, with its inference flagged as what the dates "suggested".

The politics of preservation. Terrace decline is driven by poverty, subsidies, migration to Manila, tourism, and water competition. Eighth Wonder's heirloom-rice export brings income but also cooperatives, standardization and mechanized milling. Anti-trade and antipoverty activists each accuse the other of moralizing at the poor's expense. Mann frames the point that the terraces were linked to global networks almost from the beginning, so the question is who receives benefits versus harms.

World Machines resonance (partial). The unit is a ground-level view of what divergence-machine and globalization-as-machine discussions treat abstractly: a world system that produces landscapes nobody designed. The standardization of Ifugao rice (uniform drying, cooperatives, machine milling for export) loosely rhymes with the drive toward Legibility pulling a locally illegible practice into a market. Mann himself is not a machine theorist, and this is only a rhyme; his emphasis is on ecological and ethical ambivalence, not on machine cycles.

Notable passages

The "Bahay Kubo" passage is the unit's hook: the botanists' verdict that every traditional garden plant in the song is introduced. The "fractured cerebration" paragraph is Mann's personal statement of the ambivalence that drives the Coda. The worm reversal (native worms, mahogany demand) is the central causal twist. The Maher passage delivers the historical claim that the terraces are largely a galleon-trade creation. The Eighth Wonder passage lays out the global-market dilemma without settling it.
