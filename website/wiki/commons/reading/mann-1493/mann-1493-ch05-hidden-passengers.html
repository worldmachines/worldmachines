American crops, pirates, and shack people: how the Columbian Exchange remade Qing China

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mann argues that American plants (tobacco, sweet potato, maize, potato) rode the Manila and Macao trade into China as an unplanned ecological invasion that, together with Qing policy and the pirate-disrupted silver trade, pushed landless migrants into the highlands and helped drive China's population surge.

This is the second half of Mann's silk-for-silver chapter. It opens with tobacco, which Portuguese and Manila ships carried into China (pipes from Guangxi by 1549), where it became a fashionable addiction; Mann sets this beside English snuff mania to argue that the same plant had trivial consequences in England and large ones in China. He then explains China's structural problem (about a quarter of the world's people on about a twelfth of its arable land, with rice and wheat confined to two flood-prone river basins) and reports Crosby's judgment that no Old World people adopted American food plants faster. The sweet potato smuggled from Manila by the merchant Chen Zhenlong becomes the emblem of that readiness, and Mann places it against a precedent, the Song promotion of Champa rice. He then follows the Ming collapse, the Qing coastal evacuation (from 1652), and the pirate king Zheng Chenggong (Koxinga), whose control of the silver trade briefly threatened Manila. The evacuation and the ban are shown to freeze China's money supply. Displaced coastal people and Hakka 'shack people' pushed into the highlands, joined by a state-encouraged westward migration. They planted maize, sweet potatoes and potatoes on land unsuitable for rice or wheat, and Mann links this to a population jump from Sichuan to Shaanxi and, nationally, roughly a doubling to as much as 300 million by 1800. He qualifies the claim: Qing smallpox inoculation, granaries, anti-infanticide campaigns and a waning Little Ice Age also mattered, but most growth took place where American crops were planted.

Themes

Unplanned ecological invasion. Mann's own framing is that tobacco 'was part of an unplanned ecological invasion that shaped, for better and worse, modern China.' Nobody designed the outcome; traders, soldiers, smugglers and poor migrants moved plants for local reasons and the aggregate was a demographic transformation. This is Crosby's Columbian Exchange applied to East Asia. It rhymes with the WMP interest in processes no institution made legible or controlled (legibility-as-machine-core); Mann is not a machine theorist and does not use that vocabulary.

Same plant, different context. The tobacco and snuff comparison is Mann's device: English snuff ritual (Beau Brummell) cost 'interrupted party chatter, high laundry bills, and nasopharyngeal cancer', while China's context (land scarcity, flood-prone core, migration) gave the same commodity a very different impact. The point is that outcomes depend on the receiving system, not the imported item.

Land-poor China and a hedged rebuttal of hydraulic despotism. Mann reports Marx, Weber and Wittfogel ('hydraulic societies', Oriental Despotism), notes that most Sinologists now reject the thesis, but keeps the uncontested premise that China lacked land suited to rice and wheat. Scholar-versus-Mann attribution matters here: the despotism thesis is reported and dismissed; the land-scarcity claim is accepted. Compare braudel-china-precocity-puzzle for the wider question of why China's advantages did not translate into a different trajectory.

Foreign tubers and state-led adoption. Chen Zhenlong's smuggling story (from a 1768 family book by a descendant bragging about his ancestors, so a partisan source) and the Fujian governor's distribution of cuttings during Little Ice Age famines show adoption driven by hunger. Mann sets it against the Song's promotion of Champa rice, calling aggressive adaptation of new technology 'a key reason for the nation's subsequent prosperity, and its preeminence.'

Silver, piracy and the money supply. The Qing coastal evacuation, like the Ming trade bans, 'effectively turned over the silver trade to wokou'. Koxinga's 1662 ultimatum to Manila (silver as before, in return for not being killed) triggered another massacre of the Chinese in the Philippines; his death from probable malaria ended the episode. Treasury official Mu Tianyan's complaint that closing the trade froze the money supply and produced deflation is Mann's illustration of China's dependence on imported silver, part of the world-economy formation traced by braudel-world-economy; the ban was lifted in 1681.

Shack people and marginal land. Hakka and coastal refugees rented steep, dry uplands, slashed and burned the tree cover for indigo, and moved on when the soil was exhausted. Neither rice nor wheat would grow there; maize ('jade rice'), sweet potato and later potato would. Qing bureaucrats omitted them from census reports, and a 1773 Jiangxi count found 274,280 shack people in Ganxian County against 58,340 settled inhabitants, a case of state illegibility in the Legibility sense, though Mann does not frame it that way. The Qing also actively resettled the west, forcibly incorporating non-Chinese peoples.

Population and causal hedging. Mann says American crops were not 'entirely responsible': Qing smallpox inoculation, granaries, disaster relief, anti-infanticide campaigns, a fixed cropland tax and a waning Little Ice Age all helped, some begun under the Ming. His conclusion is deliberately measured: 'Part of the reason China is the world's most populous nation is the Columbian Exchange.' A short comparison notes sweet potato revolutions in Polynesia and New Guinea (the 'Ipomoean revolution'), with the pre-Columbian presence of the plant in Oceania still disputed.

Notable passages

  • Tobacco culture (c1). Yao Lu's amazement, soldiers spreading the habit, the 'lovesick grass' name and aristocratic etiquette manuals, drawn from Timothy Brook's research. It shows how fast a New World commodity became embedded in elite Chinese life.
  • Snuff versus China (c2). The hinge sentence of the section: same plant, different context, an 'unplanned ecological invasion'.
  • Chen Zhenlong's vines (c3). The smuggling anecdote and famine background that make the sweet potato's arrival concrete.
  • Mu Tianyan on the frozen money supply (c4). The clearest statement of why cutting off silver hurt the Qing, in the official's own words about blocking a source of water.
  • Marginal land and American crops (c5). Why rice and wheat could not serve the shack people, and why maize and sweet potato could.
  • Weighing causes (c6). Mann's balanced attribution of the population surge to Qing policy plus American crops.
