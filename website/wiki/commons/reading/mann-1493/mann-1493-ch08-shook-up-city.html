Shook-Up City: Catarina de San Juan and Asia in New Spain

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Through the life of Catarina de San Juan, a Mughal-born girl enslaved and shipped to Puebla, Mann shows how the Manila galleon trade brought tens of thousands of Asian sailors, slaves and artisans to New Spain and made colonial Mexico City the first truly global city, a human branch of the Columbian Exchange.

Mann opens with the 1688 funeral of Catarina de San Juan, a Puebla holy woman whose Jesuit chroniclers claimed she was a Mughal aristocrat's granddaughter, abducted by pirates, baptized in Kochi, sold through Manila, and bought by a Puebla ship captain. From her story he widens to the forgotten Asian presence in New Spain: by Edward Slack's estimate fifty to a hundred thousand sailors, servants and slaves arrived via the galleon trade. He follows them into Acapulco's militias, Puebla's talavera potters, and Mexico City's Parián market, where Chinese barbers and goldsmiths fought Spanish guilds. He closes by declaring colonial Mexico City the world's first global city, and by qualifying what 'modern' and 'globalized' can mean.

Themes

Asia arrives in the Americas through the galleon trade. Mann, drawing heavily on the historian Edward R. Slack, argues that the Manila-Acapulco route moved people as well as silk and silver. Slack estimates that 60 to 80 percent of galleon crews were Asian, and many jumped ship. Asian slaves from India, Southeast Asia and elsewhere came despite a colonial ban (1672) that was rarely effective. The migrants were collectively called chinos. This is the human counterpart of the silk-for-silver world economy (braudel-world-economy is the nearest WM frame, though Braudel is not Mann's source here).

Mixed societies and improvised institutions. Authorities repeatedly bent their own racial and weapons rules: samurai were allowed to carry swords to guard silver convoys, and mixed-race Afro-Indo-Asian militias defended the Pacific coast, helping defeat Anson in 1741. Mann presents these as pragmatic accommodations by colonial officials, not as ideals.

Economic competition and guild backlash. Chinese barbers and goldsmiths in the Plaza Mayor undercut Spaniards by paying higher rents and working longer hours. Spanish barbers petitioned for restrictions in 1635. Mann reads the artfully worded complaint as protectionism, and notes that Slack found no evidence that the later 'barbershop czar' worked. Puebla's talavera pottery, mandated by guilds to imitate Chinese ware, was probably made partly by real Chinese potters (Mann's inference from Slack, hedged with 'more than likely').

Mexico City as first global city. Mann's own argument: a polyglot, conflict-ridden, flood-prone, unequal city where East met West 'under an African and Indian gaze' was the Homogenocene 'for Homo sapiens' and 'the first twenty-first-century city'. The claim is rhetorical and explicitly hedged: modernity is 'a patchy thing'. It rhymes with WM interest in globalization as an emergent structure (archaic-globalization-bayly) and in Rao's Modernity Machine window (modernity-machine), but Mann uses 'modern' descriptively, not as machine infrastructure, and offers no theory of legibility or machine lifecycle.

Provenance caution. Catarina's Mughal origin comes from her hagiographers (Aguilera, Ramos), whom the Inquisition later condemned as improbable. Mann says the claim was 'probably accurate, or mostly so' and notes one biographer claimed to have heard it from her own lips.

Notable passages

Catarina's odyssey (c1). The chain of events from Mughal court to Puebla: Akbar's persecution after a piracy incident, abduction, baptism in Kochi, re-enslavement, sale in Manila. It concretizes the trans-Asian, trans-Pacific slave circuit.

Fifty to a hundred thousand Asians (c2). Slack's estimates and the Espiritu Sancto example (75 sailors landed, 5 aboard for the return) show how the galleon labor system seeded a permanent Asian population.

Samurai and mixed militias (c3). The most vivid case of colonial pragmatism reversing racial rules, with Japanese exiles stranded by the 1630s closure of Japan.

The barber wars (c4). A small case study in how economic competition from migrant communities produced regulatory backlash that regulators could not enforce.

The Homogenocene for Homo sapiens (c5). Mann's thesis sentence tying the chapter to his book-wide framework.

The first twenty-first-century city (c6). The flooded, unequal, cosmopolitan city and Mann's qualification on 'modernity' as patchy.
