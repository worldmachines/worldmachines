Calabar and the hidden history of maroon freedom

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Opening Chapter 9, Mann uses Christian Santana's Calabar, a quilombo swallowed by Salvador da Bahia, to introduce the hemispheric phenomenon of maroon communities and to argue that Africans and Indians were agents, not just victims, whose largely hidden alliance and frequent victories shaped the New World.

Mann opens Chapter 9 with travel reportage from Salvador da Bahia, where Christian de Jesus Santana grew up in Calabar, a former fugitive settlement (quilombo) now encircled by high-rises but never legally part of the city. Mann (with the UCLA geographer Susanna Hecht, whose linguistic and historical expertise he credits) walks through it and, with a hedge, calls it "a living community, or so it seemed to me, a city within a city." He then widens the frame: thousands of maroon communities (quilombos, mocambos, palenques, cumbes) existed from Brazil to the U.S. Southeast, and he argues that the encounter of the two halves of the world was as much a meeting of Africans and Indians as of Europe and America. The unit ends with a catalogue of maroon victories (Suriname, Florida, Haiti) and a note that these struggles remain live politically.

Themes

Fugitive settlements as hybrid, illegible polities. Mann's own description: quilombos were protected by terrain and booby traps, joined almost always by Indians, and endured for decades or centuries. Calabar reached as many as twenty thousand inhabitants; Liberdade today is said (a hedge, not Mann's firm claim) to be the biggest Afro-American community in the Western Hemisphere. Mann notes that good records do not exist, so dating is inferential (going concerns by 1650, perhaps decades older on the word of a local historian he interviewed). They were both hunted and traded with by the Portuguese, so they were neither isolated nor autonomous in any pure sense. Their later fate (no legal title, sewers routed around, stolen electricity, a state "too weak to do much") shows an enclave that stayed outside the state's ledger. This loosely rhymes with Legibility and legibility-as-machine-core, since these were places the state could not or would not make readable, though Mann never frames it that way and the fit is an inference only.

The human exchange, seen from below. This unit belongs to Mann's extension of the Columbian Exchange to people: more than 1.5 million captives passed through the Bay of All Saints alone, and Mann says a third to a half of Brazil's slaves died within four to five years. His main argumentative move is against a schoolbook picture in which Africans and Indians have "no agency, as social scientists say." He insists that people "always seek ways to exert their will," and that Africans and Indians "fought with each other, claimed to be each other, and allied together for common goals, sometimes all at the same time."

Red and black as the central encounter. Mann reframes American history: for centuries most newcomers were African and the land was full of indigenous people, so the encounter was largely between Africans and Indians, "largely conducted out of sight of Europeans" and only now being unravelled by researchers. This is Mann's synthesis, credited to no single scholar in this passage, apart from the phrase "inventing liberty" which he attributes to the Brazilian historian Joao Jose Reis.

Freedom was often won. Mann lists cases: mass disappearances in Brazil, Peru and the Caribbean; Spanish recognition of autonomous maroon communities in Ecuador, Colombia, Panama and Mexico as buffers against Spain's adversaries; the 1762 Suriname treaty; a maroon-Indian alliance in Florida that forced the U.S. to grant liberty after two wars; and Haiti in 1804, "an entire maroon nation." For World Machines, this is a counter-current to the dominant plantation-and-empire machinery: bottom-up, unplanned polities forming in the interstices of a world system that was itself being built. It has some affinity with divergence-machine (proliferating local forms nobody designed), but Mann is a historian of agency, not a machine theorist, and the connection is ours, not his. The plantation system that produced the fugitives is part of the globalizing world economy of braudel-world-economy and the trade that Mann tracks elsewhere.

Present-day stakes. Descendants of maroons appear in legal battles from Florida to California, and Brazilian law gives maroon communities a role in the future of Amazonia. Mann treats the history as unfinished.

Notable passages

The opening scene (c1). Calabar is introduced as a "secret city" invisible from the bay side of the ridge, and the size of the Salvador slave harbor (second-biggest in the world, over 1.5 million captives) sets the scale. Mann's main claim here is that "countless thousands escaped" and formed hybrid settlements with Indians.

Trade with the hunters (c2). The Portuguese hunted runaways while trading manioc, rice, palm oil and dried fish for knives, guns and cloth, which shows the interdependence between colony and fugitive enclave. Abolition in 1888 changed little because the state was too weak to act against squatter settlements.

A hemispheric phenomenon and its names (c3). Mann generalizes from Calabar to thousands of communities, including more than fifty in the United States, and glosses "maroon" as apparently from the Taino word for the flight of an arrow (his hedge, "apparently").

The hidden red-black history (c4). The reframing of the encounter as one between Africans and Indians is the thesis-bearing statement of the unit.

Against the victim-only narrative (c5). Mann concedes the scale of suffering, including the mortality figures, while asserting agency and alliance, a stance he holds firmly.

The victories (c6). The passage on Suriname, Florida and Haiti is the evidentiary payload, and it includes the vivid detail that the European negotiators in 1762 had to drink their own blood, following African custom.
