The potato: Andean gene sea to European monoculture

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mann argues that the potato, domesticated amid the genetic diversity of the Andes and carried to Europe as a narrow clonal sample, ended routine famine in northern Europe, fueled population growth and the rise of the West, and set the template for the monoculture-based agro-industrial complex.

This unit opens Chapter 6 (Part Three, Europe in the World) with two linked movements. "Potato Wars" sketches the potato's botany, its curious Eurasian history (the Prussia-Austria "potato war" of 1778-79, Irish dependence) and Mann's framing claim: following William McNeill, the potato fed the population growth that let a handful of European states dominate the world, and its adoption set the template for the agro-industrial complex of improved crops, intensive fertilizer (Peruvian guano) and factory pesticides (arsenic against the Colorado potato beetle), which converged into the Green Revolution. "Sea of Genes" then goes to the Andes: the hostile geography, the terraces, raised fields and ridged fields, clay-detoxified wild tubers, freeze-dried chuno, and above all the chaotic landrace diversity of Andean potatoes. It follows the potato to Europe via the Spaniards, through the folklore of Drake, the suspicion and promotion of the crop (Diderot, Frederick the Great, Parmentier), and the demographic consequences: Braudel's tally of famines, Arthur Young's yield figures, Adam Smith's remarks on the Irish, and a 2010 estimate that the potato explains about an eighth of Europe's population rise.

Themes

The potato as a driver of European ascent (scholars' claim, hedged by Mann). Mann reports that "many scholars" see the potato's arrival as a key moment and cites McNeill's claim that it "permitted a handful of European nations to assert dominion over most of the world between 1750 and 1950." Mann himself frames it as ending hunger and creating the stability that let Europe exploit American silver. He then qualifies the monocausal reading: the potato arrived amid an "agricultural revolution" (transport, enclosure, clover, manure) and the wider ecological and economic shifts, echoing his treatment of China's sweet potato and maize boom. The Harvard/Yale economists' "most conservative" one-eighth estimate is presented as comparable to the steam engine. This sits near agricultural-revolution in the wiki, and Mann's argument about escaping the Malthusian trap is a concrete ecological input to how the modernity-machine could scale.

Diversity versus monoculture. The core conceptual contrast is between the Andean potato, "a bubbling stew of many related genetic entities," grown as many named landraces in mixed patches, and the European potato, descended from a few tubers and propagated as clones. Mann (drawing on Zimmerer, Hawkes, and Dutch taxonomists) stresses that even scientists cannot cleanly classify Andean potatoes; wild potatoes form a "trackless genetic swamp." Parmentier, by promoting mass cultivation of a small sample, "unknowingly" created monoculture: "a crazy gumbo" versus "an orderly array of identical parts." This is Mann's own framing and also a suggestive rhyme with the WMP legibility-versus-illegible-variety axis (legibility-as-machine-core, divergence-machine); the fit is loose, since Mann is discussing genetics and vulnerability, not state legibility, and no one designed the clonal potato for legibility.

Andean agronomy as engineered adaptation. Against steep terrain, El Nino and thin soils, Andean peoples built over a million acres of terraces, some 500 square miles of raised fields (with a microclimate benefit of up to 4 degrees F), and ridged fields worked collectively with foot plows. They detoxified wild tubers with clay (mimicking guanacos and vicunas) and made chuno, storable for years and the food of Inka armies. Mann treats this as sophisticated indigenous knowledge, not merely a raw resource Europe took, and notes the uncertain genetic and chronological origin of the domestic potato.

Famine, Malthus and population. Mann uses fernand-braudel's figures (forty national famines in France 1500-1778, Florence with 111 hungry years against sixteen very good harvests, riots across Europe) to establish the pre-potato Malthusian trap, then Arthur Young's yield figure (roughly eighteen times the calories per acre of grain) and the fallow-land argument (calories effectively doubled). Benefit was reliability more than volume: summer hunger gap, resistance to unseasonable weather, protection from foraging armies. adam-smith anticipated that potatoes would raise population; Ireland went from perhaps 1.5 million to about 8.5 million. The mechanism Mann stresses is indirect: better nourishment lowered infectious-disease mortality. He calls the worldwide population boom "a sign and effect of the onset of the Homogenocene."

Adoption politics and the agro-industrial complex. European suspicion (aphrodisiac, leprosy, "No Potatoes, No Popery!", Orthodox priests) and Parmentier's publicity stunts during the Flour War show a crop pushed by a state and reformers. The opening pages also foreshadow the rest of the chapter: guano voyages that (many researchers believe) brought the blight behind the Irish famine, the Colorado potato beetle and arsenic, and the Green Revolution as a 1950s-60s convergence of these three pillars.

Notable passages

McNeill, the empire claim, and the three pillars (c1). Mann sets out the thesis of the chapter: the potato as cause of Western rise and as template for modern agribusiness, with the phrase "improved crops, high-intensity fertilizers, and factory-made pesticides." Attribution is layered: McNeill's claim, Mann's summary, with "many scholars believe" as a hedge.

Andean landrace diversity (c2). Clones in developed countries versus 10.6 traditional varieties per family in a Peruvian valley, twenty landraces in some villages, and 3,700 in the International Potato Center collection. It grounds the later contrast with European monoculture.

Parmentier and monoculture (c3). The pivotal conceptual passage: European stock "created by dipping a teaspoon into the sea of genes in Peru and Bolivia," then planted as clones. It is Mann's interpretation, and it foreshadows the blight catastrophe discussed later in the chapter (not in this unit).

Braudel's famines (c4). Establishes the baseline of recurrent hunger, food riots and the Malthusian trap, and the Little Ice Age setting.

Young and doubled food supply (c5). The quantitative and mechanistic core of the potato-population argument, including fallow-land substitution and the claim that Europe could at last "produce its own dinner." The 40 percent of the Irish eating no other solid food shows dependence, a vulnerability Mann develops later.

The 2010 economists' estimate (c6). Mann's calibration of the strong claim: about an eighth of the population increase, comparable in importance to the steam engine. It should be carried as a hedged, single-study estimate.
