Magic Mountain: Manila's Massacres and the Two Roles of Trade

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mann uses the 1593 and 1603 Manila crises and the recurring Parián massacres to argue that trade has two conflicting roles, commercial gain and statecraft, and that both Spain and Ming China were caught between them, with China's silver dependence contributing to the Ming collapse.

Mann narrates a cycle of violence between Spanish Manila and its Chinese Parián: Governor Pérez Dasmariñas's 1593 abduction and conscription of Chinese galley slaves (ending in his murder by a mutiny), the 1596 expulsions, and the 1603 arrival of three Ming officials sent to check rumors of a gold-and-silver "magic mountain" in Cavite. The Spanish panic that followed produced a massacre whose Spanish and Chinese accounts diverge sharply, yet the trade resumed within months and the cycle repeated through 1820. Mann then asks why the Chinese kept returning and answers with Findlay and O'Rourke's distinction between trade as a market mechanism and trade as a tool of statecraft. He applies it to Spain (trading post versus imperial outpost) and to the Ming (silver as wealth and power, but also inflation, smuggling, and a tax system fixed in silver weight). By about 1640 silver's price had fallen to the world level, the Ming revenue collapsed, and Mann reports Atwell's contested claim that silver dependence helped topple the dynasty. The unit closes with Flynn and Giráldez on the real production costs China bore to obtain silver, and a pivot to the Columbian Exchange.

Themes

Commerce and politics constantly collide. Mann's own framing sentence, "Commercial and political imperatives constantly collided," opens the unit. Manila's Spaniards were few, dependent and frightened, so they needed Chinese labor and silk while fearing the Chinese. The result was a repeating loop of restriction, rebellion, massacre and repopulation, not a resolution.

Two sources, two stories. Mann sets the Spanish official account of 1603 (unprovoked mob, treacherous killing of an emissary) against Zhang Xie's Chinese account (disarmament, a residency check, and slaughter in courtyards). He does not adjudicate but signals that the Spanish version omitted damaging details. Note the attribution: the death toll of up to twenty-five thousand Chinese is Zhang's figure, as Mann reports it.

Trade has two roles. Following Findlay and O'Rourke (Power and Plenty), Mann argues that textbook comparative advantage describes private win-win exchange, while trade also serves statecraft, where "both sides usually do not win." The biggest expansions of trade occur when the two roles align, often through force. This is Mann's synthesis of their argument, applied by him to Manila and the Ming.

Ming ambivalence and the silver trap. Silver funded the Great Wall and a commercial boom but also inflation and an uncontrollable Fujianese merchant city outside imperial supervision. The tax rolls were set in silver weight, so when silver's price fell the state lost real revenue and could not print money. Atwell's thesis that this helped end the Ming is described by Mann as "vigorously debated."

The real cost of commodity money. Flynn (quoted from an e-mail to Mann) stresses that silver is a physical product with large production costs; China gave up a significant share of its GDP to obtain it. Acquiring it was rational but expensive.

Resonances with World Machines. The unit shows an emerging world economy assembled by actors pursuing conflicting local aims, none of whom controlled the whole, which loosely fits history-machine and braudel-world-economy. The Ming state's failure to control the silver flow or Fujianese merchants fits the tension between state legibility and illegible commercial flows (legibility-as-machine-core), though Mann does not use that vocabulary. The trade-as-statecraft point touches weaponized-interdependence only loosely; Mann is discussing early modern coercion, not networked chokepoints. braudel-china-precocity-puzzle and ming-dynasty-grand-secretaries are adjacent context for the Ming court but are not discussed here.

Notable passages

The conscription and mutiny of 1593 (c1) establishes the pattern of coerced Chinese labor and Spanish fear. The magic-mountain embassy (c2) shows mutual misreading: Mann suggests a "daffy con job" on the Chinese side and a suspected invasion plot on the Spanish side. Zhang Xie's account (c3) is the key counter-narrative. The Findlay and O'Rourke passages (c4) and Mann's application to Manila (c5) carry the unit's central argument. The Ming tax-in-silver mechanism (c6) and Flynn's comment on production costs (c7) explain why a rational trade could still be ruinous.
