Conquest by Marriage, Classification by Fear: The Making of Mixed New Spain

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mann traces how conquistador–native unions (Cortés, the Pizarros) and rising African presence produced a mixed society in Spanish America, and how anxious colonial authorities answered with the casta system, sumptuary restrictions and casta paintings, categories that people in practice gamed and that mixing steadily outran.

Mann opens with the fall of Tenochtitlan in 1521 and follows the family of Motecuhzoma: Tecuichpotzin, widowed twice, is passed among Spaniards and bears Cortés's daughter Leonor. He generalizes from Cortés to Pizarro and to the founders of Santiago, Chile: the first conquistadors mostly took elite native women as wives or mistresses and acknowledged their mixed children. As plantation slavery grew, Africans and Afro-Indian and Afro-European people multiplied. Colonial elites who had tolerated mixing in the sixteenth century turned in the seventeenth to fear, restrictions, and the elaborate casta taxonomy. Mann argues that people used the categories as tools, that the population blended faster than authorities could sort it, and that casta paintings captured this contradiction. He credits María Elena Martínez for the account of blood purity and flags that scholars dispute the subject.

Themes

Mixing began at the top, and was policy as well as appetite. Mann's own reading is that Cortés set out to graft Spanish authority onto native roots and to rule through Indian institutions. He treats the impregnation of Tecuichpotzin as part of that accommodation. Elite Extremaduran families intermarried with elite native families, and Mann says the Mexica case was prototypical. Ferdinand and Isabella encouraged intercultural coupling (though not outside Christian marriage). Some low-ranking Spaniards preferred a Taino ceremony, because it gave access to goods and workers controlled by high-status Indians. Data point: 57 percent of the 392 descendants of ninety-seven Santiago founders were of Indian descent.

The two-republic scheme failed by its own logic. The Franciscan plan for separate Indian and Spanish republics (Quiroga's Utopia-inspired reservation towns; Puebla) collapsed because Puebla itself ran on encomienda labor, free Africans arbitraged between neighborhoods, and mixed people belonged to neither republic.

From tolerance to moral contamination. Mann, following Martínez, argues that Spaniards had no biological concept of race but feared moral and religious taint passed on through descent and household habits. Africans were suspect because of their ancestors' Islam. Indians began as innocents and became suspect as evangelization stalled. A growing population of "untrustworthy groups" made elites feel they were losing control, and tolerance gave way to restriction and fear in a feedback loop.

Classification as an attempt at control, and its evasion. The casta system was never formally codified empire-wide, yet it ranked people by descent-linked moral worth, with ever more absurd categories. This rhymes with the modernity-machine drive toward legibility-as-machine-core (making a population readable in order to rule it), though Mann does not frame it that way and the casta scheme was a patchwork of local rules, not a designed cadastre. People shopped for identities, officials sold them, and mestizo heirs were promoted to Spaniards. Authorities tried harder to separate people as separation became impossible.

Enforcement fell unequally. Mann lists petty regulations, including different penalties for the same fraud (a fine for the Spanish butcher, a hundred lashes for the Indian one), curfews, and an annual assessment on people with African blood.

Notable passages

Cortés's grafting strategy (c1) shows conquest as a project of institutional inheritance. The Pizarro palace anecdote and the Santiago statistics show the pattern was not exceptional. The passage on Puebla (c3) is Mann's neat irony: a city built to protect Indians from forced labor rested wholly on it. The blood-and-habit passage (c4) carries the Martínez argument, which Mann himself hedges. The casta-system paragraph (c5) and the gaming paragraph (c6) set official taxonomy against practice, and c7 states the paradox that authorities tried harder to separate people as separation became harder. The closing casta-paintings material shows the taxonomy displayed like natural history, with the ironic note that the paintings omit the colony's large Asian population.
