Maroon Amazonia: Dona Rosario's farm, Mazagão and the quilombo land question

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Through Dona Rosario's Amapá farm and the Mazagão and Mojú quilombos, Mann argues that Brazil's maroon communities blended African and Indian knowledge to tend an Amazon that outsiders misread as wilderness, and that this hidden, state-illegible population finally gained legal recognition in the 1988 constitution and Lula's Provisional Law 458 (2009).

Mann opens with Dona Rosario Costa Cabral, born in a maroon community and repeatedly pushed off farms she built, who in 1991 bought looted floodplain land on Igarapé Espinel and restored it as a managed, apparently wild landscape. He uses her to enter the history of Brazilian quilombos: the accidental freedom of Vila Nova Mazagão (a Portuguese colony transplanted from Morocco to the equator in 1770 and abandoned by its Europeans), the thousands of small, shifting maroon villages that followed the fall of Palmares, and the festivals (bumba-meu-boi, lambe-sujos, the Mazagão battle with Caldeira) through which these communities remember themselves. The second half turns to modern dispossession, from the generals' highway programme to mining and pipeline concessions at Mojú, and to the 1988 constitution and 2009 Provisional Law 458, which finally gave maroons title. Mann closes on his own interpretive claim that the Amazon 'forest' is in large part a garden made by these people.

Themes

The forest as a made landscape. Mann's claim, reported through Susanna Hecht's presence and Dona Rosario's practice, is that 'almost every species' in the restored land 'had been selected and tended', and that two centuries of tending by maroons 'structured the forest'. Mixing native and African techniques (West African-style shrimp traps, Indian manioc processing and fish poison), they produced landscapes 'lush enough to be mistaken for pristine wilderness'. This rhymes with the desiccation-theory-grove and tropical-island-eden-grove line of argument that colonial observers misread managed landscapes as untouched nature, though Mann does not cite Grove here.

African-Indian hybridity as ecological and social technology. Mann contrasts the Portuguese, 'ideologically opposed to "going native"', who found the forest dangerous, with Africans (many already tropical farmers) who learned from Indians. The ten thousand or more small villages in a 'flexible, shifting network' are presented as a lesson learned from Palmares's centralized failure. Bumba-meu-boi is read by Mann as 'the tale of the quilombo itself'.

Freedom through pretence and invisibility. The Mazagão maroons and the Portuguese administration each pretended the other's fiction (Portuguese subjects guarding the frontier), a mutually convenient arrangement. Invisibility was both protection and vulnerability: quilombos had 'no legal existence', so concessions could be granted over them without consultation.

Illegibility as protection, legibility as double-edged. The generals looked at maps, saw 60 percent blank, and set about 'filling the emptiness' with highways, while the space was in fact occupied by Indians, peasants and quilombos. This is a clear case of a state's map failing to make a territory legible, and of legibility-by-force as an instrument of dispossession; compare Legibility and legibility-as-machine-core. Yet the ending reverses the sign: legal recognition (Pereira: the state cannot legally invest in schools and clinics while existence is contested) is how the maroons gain protection. Mann is not a machine theorist and does not frame it so; the resonance is that legibility here is both threat and shield.

Memory and festival under pressure. The Mazagão reenactment drifted far from the historical 1569 battle (Sultan Abdallah becomes Caldeira, and so on) yet still binds a community spread over dozens of rivers. Mann reports Joseane Jacarandá's anger at newcomers remaking the festival for tourism: an internal contest over whether outside attention is a gift or an enclosure.

Small infrastructure, large leverage. The electric wire, the phone charger and the chest freezer let Dona Rosario stop selling açai at forced-sale prices and call inspectors to stop surveyors, a small-scale illustration (Mann's own reportage) of how access to communication and capital changes the outcome of an old land-grab pattern.

Notable passages

Free while pretending (c1). The Mazagão passage lays out the mutual fiction that kept the maroons undisturbed and the ecological result, Mann's synthesis of native and African technique.

Bumba-meu-boi (c2). Mann's reading of a Maranhão folk drama as an allegory of quilombo formation, with America (tobacco, priests, forest) and Africa (cows, slaves) fused.

The generals and the blank map (c3). After abolition in 1888 the quilombos hid; the military government read blank maps as emptiness and drove highways through the interior. Mann's own point, with the 'blank' claim reported ironically.

Pereira on unintended scale (c4). The 1988 constitution's drafters imagined a few remnant quilombos; researchers now think as many as five thousand survive across roughly 30 million hectares. Mann hedges ('many researchers believe') and quotes Pereira for the implications.

Phone, freezer, title (c5). The concrete account of how Dona Rosario defeated the 2009 surveyors.

The world's richest garden (c6). Mann's closing reframing that 'forest' is the wrong word, a claim resting on Hecht-style research and Dona Rosario's own belief, offered with narrative rather than quantitative support.
