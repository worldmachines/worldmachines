Malaria's Columbian Exchange: Çiçiones, Fens, and Seasoning in Virginia

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mann argues, through a hedged reading of Colón's word çiçiones and the case of English fen and Virginia vivax malaria, that malaria and yellow fever, as Columbian Exchange imports, shaped who could settle where, why colonists turned to enslaved African labor, and how 'extractive states' arose, while cautioning against over-claiming.

Mann opens with a piece of detective work: a newly published copy of Colón's second-voyage chronicle says his men at La Isabela fell ill with çiçiones, which Noble David Cook and the translator read as malaria. Mann checks with the Spanish-language scholar Scott Sessions, finds that early dictionaries tie çiçiones to fever with chills (probably tertian fever), and concludes the identification is plausible but speculative. He then widens to the argument of Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson that European mortality in disease zones produced 'extractive states', and to his own claim that tobacco and sugar carried malaria and yellow fever to the Americas and so helped drive the turn to captive African labor. The second half ('Seasoning') explains Plasmodium biology, the temperature limits of vivax and falciparum, the Elizabethan draining of English fens that created a vivax belt, the origin of many English emigrants in that belt, the probable arrival of vivax in Virginia, and the colonial institution of 'seasoning', illustrated by the diaries of Samuel Jeake and Landon Carter.

Themes

Historical diagnosis under uncertainty. Mann models a hedged method: one word 'is not enough to make a diagnosis', yet he insists the question is too important to drop. Words like paludismo and mal aria show that people did not know malaria as a specific disease for centuries. The chapter's evidence is repeatedly labelled speculative ('highly speculative, to say the least'; the precise date of arrival 'will always lie in the realm of speculation').

Endemic versus epidemic disease. Smallpox and influenza (the Columbian Exchange as sudden outbursts) contrast with malaria, which became a permanent feature of the landscape and emptied previously salubrious regions (the southeastern United States, Mesoamerican and Amazonian forests) of their native inhabitants and killed many incoming Europeans.

Disease ecology to institutions (scholars Mann reports). Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson's thesis, reported and not Mann's own invention, holds that where settlers could not survive they built 'extractive states' (Belgian Congo as the emblem) rather than stable populous colonies. Mann adds his own chain: ecological introductions shaped an economic exchange, which had political consequences that persist, and then immediately qualifies it as not the whole explanation ('would be an exaggeration').

Biology as constraint. Plasmodium's life cycle, the temperature thresholds (falciparum about 72 F, vivax about 59 F) and the Anopheles vectors explain why falciparum thrived in Africa and vivax in northern Europe; hence falciparum came to America from Africa and vivax from Europe, 'a difference with historic consequences'.

Unintended landscape consequences. Draining English marshes under Elizabeth I, done to create farmland, produced 'an inferno of vivax malaria' (Mary Dobson's parish data); Virginian tobacco clearing and drought similarly made the tidewater 'quad-friendly'. Human land-management choices created disease habitat no one intended, a case of an illegible ecological process nobody controlled (compare Legibility, though Mann is not a machine theorist and does not frame it so).

Seasoning as social institution. Colonists' expectation of a 'Seasoning' (Yeardley 1620, Hugh Jones 1724) treated prolonged illness as normal; deaths fell from 20 or 30 percent around 1650 to 10 percent or lower around 1670 as Virginians learned to avoid marshes and dusk and acquired immunity. Diaries of Jeake and Carter give the human texture.

Notable passages

The çiçiones inference (c1). Sessions supplies the dictionary evidence (1611, then 1726-1739) that çiçiones meant chills-and-fever, probably tertian fever; Mann's conclusion is that Colón 'may well have been describing malaria'. The hedge travels with the claim.

Endemic malaria and extractive states (c2). The key statement of malaria's distinct role and the Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson argument, with the Conrad image of the Congo.

From tobacco and sugar to the slave trade (c3). Mann's chain from ecological introduction to Africans as 'the human wing of the Columbian Exchange', with the explicit disclaimer that disease does not single-handedly explain slavery or Latin American poverty. This sits near the wiki's concern with world-system formation (braudel-world-economy) but Mann's mechanism is ecological rather than structural.

Two parasites, two continents (c4). The vivax/falciparum split by temperature.

The drained fens (c5). Dobson's baptism and burial figures for twenty-four parishes show a population reversal after draining; an example of well-meant improvement producing mass mortality.

Seasoning (c6). Yeardley and Jones show how disease was institutionalized in colonial expectation; the mortality figures are Mann's summary of Virginia experience.

Overall, the chapter reads as ecological history in the history-machine register (assembling from distributed, unplanned components) only loosely; the fit is partial since Mann offers no machine or cycle theory.
