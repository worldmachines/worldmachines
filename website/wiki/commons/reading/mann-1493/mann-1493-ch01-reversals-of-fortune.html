The Homogenocene Seen from 35,000 Feet: A Round-the-World Cruise in 1642

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mann stages a thought-experiment world tour of 1642 to show how Spanish silver, American crops, disease, and a climate downturn tied Europe, the Americas, and China into one turbulent system, and argues that Columbian-Exchange depopulation may even have helped cool the planet and tilted global wealth from the tropical south to the temperate north.

Mann opens by admitting that the Homogenocene, a new epoch brought on by a world-spanning economy, sounds grandiose, then offers a thought experiment: a plane circling the globe in 1642. The route follows Spanish silver from Potosí to Lima, north past depopulated and feralizing Andean slopes, to Panama and Seville, and across the Pacific from Acapulco to China. Along the way he links silver-fuelled Spanish wars, defaults and European inflation; American crops arriving in Europe amid the Little Ice Age; Ruddiman's argument that Indian depopulation and reforestation cooled the climate; malaria, the tobacco labor problem and the start of the slave trade; horses transforming the Plains; racial mixing in Mexico City; and the collapse of the Ming as silver, weather and American crops converge. He closes by contrasting the tropical-centered urban order of 1493 with the temperate-north order that followed, and by noting that our current ecological and economic tumult has precedents.

Themes

Silver as the wire of a single world system. Mann presents Potosí as the central node of a network in which the Spanish peso is already "on its way to becoming a de facto world currency". This is a world economy in the sense discussed in braudel-world-economy, though Mann's emphasis is on causal linkage between distant places rather than on core-periphery structure. His own argument is that turmoil in Europe was, for the first time, "intimately linked to human actions on opposite ends of the earth".

A live historiographical dispute: Europe-driven or China-driven? How much silver went to China is contested. Official statistics say no more than a quarter; older historians assumed smuggling was about 10 percent; a newer group argues China took as much as half. Mann frames the stakes: either European expansion is the prime mover or the earth is "a single economic unit largely driven by Chinese demand". He reports the dispute and does not settle it.

Windfall wealth as a curse (Mann's synthesis, with Flynn as a cited source). Spain borrowed against future silver, defaulted in 1557, 1576, 1596, 1607 and 1627, and could not pay its troops. Mann is explicit that silver was not the only cause of European turmoil, but calls it "an essential part". The "price revolution" then immiserated the poor and fed the "general crisis" of the seventeenth century.

Ecological knock-on effects, including climate. Mann sets out the Little Ice Age causal debate (Maunder Minimum, volcanoes, both criticized) and then Ruddiman's proposal, from 2003, that epidemics ended Indian burning and farming, letting forests regrow and drawing down carbon dioxide. He cites Dull's team (2010) for up to a quarter of the temperature drop from tropical reforestation alone. The hedging matters: this is a hypothesis "increasingly treated seriously", not a settled result.

Unintended coupling of disease, cold and labor. Cold-favored forests may have improved mosquito survival; malaria arriving with English immigrants made Chesapeake labor recruitment hard; and African slaves were bought, so that the slave market "will entwine itself over time with the silver market". The slave ships also carry crops and yellow fever. Mann says "partly driven", and the malaria chain is offered as suspected rather than documented.

Adaptation and mixture. Spanish horses turn sedentary Plains farmers into the mounted nomads of the "ancient tradition", a claim Mann frames as rapid adaptation to the Exchange. Mexico City's casta lexicon and ethnic neighborhoods make it, in his phrase, "the world’s first twenty-first-century city".

Reversal of the old geography of power. In 1493 the largest cities (Beijing, Vijayanagar, Cairo, Tenochtitlan and others) sat in the tropics, and almost no European city would have made the list. By 1900 the top tier would be all Europe and the United States except Tokyo. Mann describes it as the globe turned upside down.

Notable passages

Potosí as extraction machine (c1). A compact description of the whole chain: Indian labor, mercury amalgamation, stamped bars, llama trains, Arica, Lima, convoys. It anchors the claim that silver made a single system.

Silver's flood and the price revolution (c2). The bathtub and billionaire-in-a-tiny-bank analogies explain how Spain's small financial sector passed money on to Italy, the Netherlands and the Empire, and why inflation followed.

Ruddiman and the carbon budget (c3). The most surprising claim in the unit: disease in the Americas as a climate forcing, "today’s climate change in reverse". This is unintended, distributed, illegible causation on a planetary scale, which loosely rhymes with the history-machine idea of paradigms assembling from independent components, though Mann's mechanism is ecological rather than institutional and he makes no such claim.

Malaria, tobacco and slavery (c4). A short passage that ties the disease environment to labor institutions and to the slave trade's connection with silver.

The Ming as casualty (c5). Silver-dependent taxation, the climate crisis, and American crops converge on dynastic collapse in 1644; the line that the emperors "don’t know" American trees are bringing the rains is Mann's gloss, not a Ming source.

The tropics-to-temperate reversal (c6). The unit's title claim. The shift in the geography of large cities and power is a global reorientation, comparable in scale to the transitions that modernity-machine and divergence-machine theorize, though Mann is not a machine theorist and gives no cyclical model; his frame is ecological and economic, and the fit is loose.
