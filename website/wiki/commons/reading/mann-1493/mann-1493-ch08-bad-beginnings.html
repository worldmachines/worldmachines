Sugar, Plantations and the Two Atlantic Islands: Madeira and São Tomé

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mann traces how sugar, from the First Crusade's captured Levantine estates through Madeira and São Tomé, fused plantation agriculture to African slavery, and argues that the presence or absence of malaria and yellow fever made the two islands into templates for very different societies, with São Tomé the progenitor of the extractive state.

Mann opens with a deliberately anachronistic riff on a coalition victory with no plan for the aftermath, then reveals it is the First Crusade (which he dates to 1096) and the crusaders' need to live off seized Muslim property. That property included sugar estates, and sugar, with its plantation form of production, is the chapter's engine. Mann follows the crop from New Guinea and India to the Levant, then to Mediterranean islands and finally the Atlantic islands of Madeira, the Canaries and São Tomé and Príncipe. On Madeira, plantation agriculture was joined, shakily, to African slavery. Mann reads São Tomé as a quasi-experiment that added malaria and yellow fever, and the two islands diverge sharply after Brazilian sugar crashed their markets in the 1560s and 1570s. He closes with the line that this was the system that crossed the Atlantic to the Americas.

Themes

Sugar and the plantation form. Mann treats sugar as a culturally near-universal craving (he cites Pollan and the chronicler Albert of Aachen for the crusaders' delight) and, more importantly, as the plantation product par excellence: single crop, big land, processed on site, shipped to distant markets. The recurring weak point, in his account, is labor.

Labor regimes were a choice, not a given. Mann reports the historian Mohamed Ouerfelli's finding that Islamic sugar plantations retained workers with relatively high wages, and that European-owned ones initially did the same (in Sicily, people migrated to work them). Europe's producers later reconsidered. Iberian slavery had been mostly domestic and status-oriented, with escape hatches such as self-purchase; Mann cites Domínguez Ortiz's speculation that it might have evolved into a system of extracting money rather than labor. Adam Smith's identified problems with slave labor (adam-smith) are invoked as what those escape hatches avoided.

Ecological improvisation and accident. The Porto Santo rabbits (Mann calls it a parable and defends Zurara's credibility), the Fuerteventura donkeys and the fire that engulfed Madeira's forest show settlers meeting islands without predictors or plans. Mann's tone is wry, and he hedges about the fire's reported seven-year burn ("Supposedly").

Disease as the hinge. Madeira lacked the mosquito vectors for malaria and yellow fever; São Tomé swarmed with them. Mann frames this as "a bit like a natural scientific experiment: change one variable and see what happens." Europeans died in droves, so plantations ran on huge enslaved majorities under a tiny, fever-ridden white minority, which Mann calls the progenitor of the extractive state. This is his own synthesis of the differential-disease argument, and the comparison is illustrative rather than controlled.

Divergent paths after the crash. Madeira, with disease-free wealthy settlers, mixed farms, cathedrals, hospitals and schools, pivoted to wine, freed most slaves and survived. São Tomé's absentee owners left or watched from afar, its economy degraded into "zombie enterprises," then cocoa and coffee, then de facto slavery via colonial debt taxes and, after 1975, nationalized super-plantations run much as before. This resonates loosely with path-dependence and the way infrastructure persists after its function lapses (cf. the WMP decay-sequence idea of institutions outliving their purpose), though Mann makes no such claim and no machine theory is in play.

Resistance. Runaway bands on São Tomé, forts staffed by armed slaves, and a 1595 revolt of up to five thousand slaves who destroyed thirty mills; guerrilla conflict lasted nearly two hundred years. This foreshadows the maroon communities the book treats later.

Notable passages

The definition of a plantation (c1) is the chapter's conceptual base: it lists the features that make sugar the archetype and sets up the labor problem. The Iberian slavery passage (c2) shows the older, milder system with its escape hatches, and c3 shows how Madeira transformed it while still not the fully developed system. The São Tomé natural-experiment passage (c4) states the disease variable explicitly, and c5 gives the extractive-state claim in Mann's own words. The comparison of the two islands after the Brazilian crash (c6) carries the divergent-outcome argument.

Caveats: Mann relies on secondary scholars here (Vieira for Madeira, Ouerfelli for wages, Domínguez Ortiz for Iberian slavery) and on chroniclers (Zurara, Albert of Aachen), and the numbers about deaths on São Tomé and the Dutch expeditions are as reported by Mann, not verified in this unit.
