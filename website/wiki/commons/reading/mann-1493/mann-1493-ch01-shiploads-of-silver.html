Manila 1571: the Legazpi–Urdaneta monument and the birth of the galleon trade

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Through a neglected Manila monument, Mann narrates Legazpi and Urdaneta's founding of the Spanish Pacific route, the 1570-72 contact with Chinese junks, and argues the resulting silk-for-silver galleon trade first bound the world in one network, unsettled Ming China via American crops, and opened the Homogenocene in Asia.

Mann opens at a neglected Manila monument to Miguel López de Legazpi and Andrés de Urdaneta, which he calls the nearest thing to an official recognition of globalization's origins, and uses it to tell how Spain reached the China trade by sailing west. He recounts the 1564 expedition, Urdaneta's northern return route, the botched 1570 encounter with Chinese junks at Mindoro, and the 1572 arrival of junks willing to sell silk and porcelain for Spanish silver. He argues this linked Asia, Europe, the Americas and (indirectly) Africa in one exchange network, that both empires distrusted it, that it destabilized Ming China through American crops, and that it opened the Homogenocene in Asia. He closes with the monument's own history, the Rizal Park bust of Rajah Sulayman, and the sameness of present-day Manila.

Themes

Economics as the counterpart of ecology. Mann's own framing: Legazpi and Urdaneta were "to economics what Colón was to ecology", an inadvertent origin of a great unification. This ties the silver trade to the Columbian Exchange frame that runs through the book. He also argues continuity of aim, citing historian Antonio García-Abásolo: Colón, Cortés and Legazpi all sought China.

A world network, dated. Mann dates a "new, distinctly modern era" to Spain's arrival in the Philippines, when nearly every populous area and every habitable continent except Australia was bound into one exchange network. African slaves are placed inside it as the labor in Mexico's silver mines, a point he defers to later chapters. This resonates with braudel-world-economy, though Mann is narrating a single hinge-event rather than theorizing world-economies. It also sits in tension with archaic-globalization-bayly, which would place proto-global links earlier, and with the WMP dating of the modernity-machine as built 1200-1600. Mann's 1571-72 date is a journalist's marker, not a machine-theoretic claim.

Mutual suspicion and loss of control. China, then the richest and most powerful state (Mann's assessment), saw Europe as too poor to trade with; the Mexican viceroy in 1573 admitted Spain had nothing China lacked, until silver, which "literally became China's money supply". The Ming court feared the first large-scale uncontrolled international exchange in Chinese history. Madrid too repeatedly restricted the trade. Currency dependence on foreigners faintly rhymes with weaponized-interdependence, though nobody here is deliberately weaponizing anything.

Unintended ecological consequence. Mann argues the fears proved right, but through species rather than people: sweet potatoes and maize (Song Junling: "one of the most revolutionary events" in imperial China's history) opened the dry uplands to settlement, causing deforestation, erosion, floods and deaths, further destabilizing an already strained regime, "to Europe's benefit". This is Mann's claim, hedged only lightly; it exemplifies an illegible, uncontrolled process, but he does not use that vocabulary.

Winners and rhetoric. Both sides gained, as theory predicts, but Europe emerged stronger; Mann quotes Andre Gunder Frank on Europeans buying "a seat" on the Asian train, and says the rise of the West followed in the slipstream. The unit stays journalistic and cautious about causation.

Memory and violence. The 1570 attack at Maujao was a rash act by Spanish troops against Chinese traders, against Salcedo's orders; Mann reads Legazpi's later razing of Sulayman's village (killing him and about three hundred people) as the real meaning of "friendship". He calls Sulayman and others the first "antiglobalization martyrs" who "lost". The statue's history (proposed 1892, stored until 1930) shows globalization's origin commemorated by accident, and he imagines a fuller monument with Chinese merchants as equal partners.

Notable passages

The framing of the two men as economics' counterpart to Colón (c1); the 1572 junks and the enormous, effectively insatiable trade (c2); China's suspicion and the fear of uncontrolled change (c3); the upland crop shock and its ecological and political fallout (c4); Spain's own restrictions and Frank's train metaphor, ending on the Homogenocene arriving in Asia (c5); and the Sulayman bust, which corrects the triumphal narrative (c6).
