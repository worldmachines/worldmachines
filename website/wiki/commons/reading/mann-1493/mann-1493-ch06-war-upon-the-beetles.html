The Colorado potato beetle and the pesticide treadmill

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mann traces the Colorado potato beetle from a Mexican buffalo-bur feeder to the scourge of industrial potato monoculture, arguing that uniform farms and fast transport (the Homogenocene) breed pest explosions that arsenic, DDT and successive pesticides answer only with a resistance treadmill.

The unit opens with Thomas Murphy's Kansas potato patch overrun by beetles in August 1861 and follows the insect's march across North America and, via ship holds, into Europe, where Germany imposed what Mann says may have been the world's first agricultural quarantine (1870). Mann then reconstructs the beetle's origin: a Mexican specialist on buffalo bur, carried north with the horses and cattle of the Columbian Exchange, which jumped to the cultivated potato after a chance mutation. The rest of the unit is Mann's own argument about why this mattered: industrial monoculture and modern transport made pest outbreaks routine, farmers answered with Paris Green and successor chemicals, and the beetle (and the blight) kept adapting, leaving a "toxic treadmill". Mann closes with his own 2009 blighted garden and a neighbor's blame of the Columbian Exchange.

Themes

Ecological imperialism by accident. The beetle's route depends on a chain of unintended links: Spaniards bring livestock, Indians steal and trade them north, buffalo bur hitches along in manes and saddlebags, and the beetle follows. Mann hedges heavily here ("Biologists believe", "apparently", "could also have been carried", "perhaps, according to one suggestive study"). The decisive step is a single tiny mutation, which Mann says was enough "to generate a worldwide problem". Nobody designed or controlled any part of this.

Homogenocene as pest infrastructure. This is Mann's central claim in the unit, and it is his own synthesis (with L. O. Howard's 1898 count of thirty-seven of seventy worst US insect pests being recent imports as evidence). Uniform fields, few varieties of one species, plus railroads, steamships and refrigeration turn each farm into an identical food pool that a pest need only adapt to once. This resonates with the Modernity Machine's drive to standardize and make land uniform and readable (Legibility, modernity-machine), though Mann is not a machine theorist and Scott-style legibility is my gloss, not his. It is the flip side: the ecological world answers standardization with illegible, uncontrolled explosions. It also rhymes loosely with divergence-machine, since the beetle's genetic diversity (high heterozygosity) is what beats the farmers' uniform response, but that is a loose analogy.

The chemical solution that reproduces the problem. Paris Green (a copper-arsenic pigment) is a "godsend" that also launches the chemical industry's habit of extending one success to every pest, then Bordeaux mixture for mildew and blight. Resistance shows up as early as 1912, DDT lasts about seven years, dieldrin about three, and by the mid-1980s each new pesticide is good for about a season. Mann reports critics' term "toxic treadmill" and Michael Pollan's "clean fields", and cites researchers who think pesticides also kill natural enemies and create secondary pests. Each step is locally rational and the aggregate is a self-reinforcing loop, which fits the WMP interest in machines whose own optimization generates the pressure that erodes them, though Mann does not say this.

Two-way exchange and contingency. Phylloxera goes from the US to France and Italy; the fix is grafting European vines onto American roots. The second blight mating type reaches Europe in 1981. The return of blight in 2009 is told as Mann's own reportage, with the neighbor's attribution ("Those tomatoes come from China") clearly the neighbor's claim, reported "accurately or not".

Notable passages

The mutation passage (c1) shows Mann's hedged account of how a small chance change opened a new niche. The Neolithic-to-industrial passage (c2) and the Homogenocene passage (c3) carry the structural argument about monoculture and transport. The Paris Green passage (c4) shows the industrial logic of extension. The heterozygosity passage (c5) gives the resistance timeline. The secondary-pests passage (c6) gives the counterproductivity argument, attributed to "many researchers".
