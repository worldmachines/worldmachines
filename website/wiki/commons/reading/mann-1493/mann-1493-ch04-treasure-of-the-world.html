Potosí: Silver Mountain, Mercury Marriage, and a River of Money

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mann narrates the 1545 discovery of Potosí's silver, the mercury-amalgam process and Toledo's forced-labor system that fed it, the lawless Basque-Vicuña violence of the boomtown, and then, following Flynn and Giráldez, how Spanish silver became a universal currency whose glut and price fall shook Europe while most of the metal flowed to Yuegang in China.

This unit (the opening section of Chapter 4, "The Treasure of the World") moves from a single discovery to a planetary monetary shock. Mann tells the (legendary) 1545 find of the Cerro Rico silver ledge by Diego Gualpa or Hualpa, describes the boomtown that grew around it, and then explains the technology that scaled it: mercury amalgamation using the Huancavelica deposits, and Viceroy Toledo's forced-labor tribute that turned Andean people into "wholly a source of labor." A long middle stretch is a set of vignettes of Potosí's violence, drawn largely from the chronicler Arzáns and the historian Crespo: the Montejo-Gudínez duel, the Basque-Vicuña factional war, and the tax inspector Martínez Pastrana. Mann then zooms out with the economic historians Dennis Flynn and Arturo Giráldez: more than 150,000 tons of silver, a monetary explosion across Europe, a falling silver price, fiscal crisis in Spain, and unrest from the Netherlands to the Thirty Years' War, while "most of the silver actually went to Asia," especially to Yuegang in Fujian.

Themes

Technology decoupled extraction from indigenous knowledge, and so from indigenous people except as labor. Mann reports (as Toledo's own realization, in his telling) that once mercury amalgamation replaced Andean smelters, "Spaniards could treat natives wholly as a source of labor." The technique is a rediscovery of a process known in China for centuries. Toledo's mita quota, modeled on Inka communal labor, is Mann's account of institutional re-use. Mann rejects the "three to eight million" death figure as an exaggeration but calls conditions appalling, especially at Huancavelica, where mercury poisoning was known and inspectors' recommendations lost to "reasons of state."

A boomtown outside effective law, but not outside the flow of silver. Potosí is depicted as the template for later boomtowns: conspicuous luxury, a fish sold for five thousand pesos, murder, duels in council chambers, and no European birth for more than half a century. The Basque-Vicuña conflict is presented as ethnic and commercial: Basques (Mann calls the Basque region "the Fujian of Spain") controlled two-thirds of the mines and the council by 1602 and evaded taxes; the anti-Basque Vicuñas resisted. Yet, Mann stresses, "the Basque-Vicuña war had almost no effect on the flow of silver." He is careful about sources: Arzáns embellished, may have invented Montejo, but "the underlying scenario seems indisputable." Mann also draws a comparison to the Ming coast (the wokou, Zhu Wan), where an incorruptible official was ruined, as Martínez Pastrana was in career terms.

Silver as a world-scale monetary shock (Flynn and Giráldez's argument, reported by Mann). The peso, the "piece of eight," became a universal currency, like the euro today in Mann's analogy. Flynn says the money supply across Europe exploded; "rapid, unplanned shocks to the money supply are generally a bad idea." Prices of silver then fell (a million pesos in 1640 worth about a third of 1540's), and Spain's un-indexed silver taxes collapsed in real value. Mann hedges: American silver "was not the sole cause," but threads of silver link the Dutch and Portuguese revolts, the Fronde, and even the Thirty Years' War. The Europe-centered story is reframed by Flynn and Giráldez as "a kind of sideshow."

Resonances for WMP, with qualifications. The silver system is a candidate machine-like infrastructure: an interlocking assemblage of technology (amalgam), coercive institution (mita), currency and logistics (llama trains, stamped bars) that produced planet-wide effects nobody planned, echoing braudel-world-economy and the early-modern reading of archaic-globalization-bayly, though Mann is a narrative historian, not a machine theorist, and does not use such framing. The stamping and serial-numbering of every bar, the tax-quota system (one-fifth to the crown), and the assayer's certification are a small legibility apparatus in the sense of Legibility and legibility-as-machine-core; the systematic evasion by Basque officials shows its limits. The unindexed tax and the uncontrolled monetary jump are unintended consequences arriving at the global scale, loosely rhyming with modernity-machine world-system formation, though the unit itself makes no claim about a c. 1200-1600 window. The fiscal-crash-to-revolt chain is a hedged, multi-causal claim, not a deterministic one.

Notable passages

Potosí's discovery and rise (c1). The ledge was "as much as 50 percent" silver against typical ores of a few percent; Spaniards boiled the silver away, and Andean smelters using llama dung and dry grass did what foreigners could not. The population went from about fifty thousand in the early 1560s to 160,000 by 1611, as big as London or Amsterdam.

The mercury marriage (c2). The amalgamation process, trod by men, mules, and horses, and Toledo's line about "the most important marriage in the world" show technology and state power fusing into a single extractive system.

Toledo's labor system and Huancavelica (c3). The passage where technology dependence ends and the labor quota begins. Mann's judgment on casualty numbers is a model of hedged attribution.

Potosí's mines (c4). The rope-and-leather ladder chains, the vanishing rest periods, and the priest's complaint: "If twenty healthy Indians enter on Monday, half come out on Saturday as cripples."

A river of silver and the peso (c5). Flynn and Giráldez's 150,000 tons, the 80 percent share of world output, the doubling of Europe's silver, and the peso as universal currency.

The price collapse and the turn to Asia (c6). The mechanism from falling silver value, through fixed tax rates, to Spanish fiscal crisis and riot, closing with the pivot to Yuegang that sets up the rest of the chapter.
