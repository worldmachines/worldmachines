Rubber's Round Trip: Wickham, Fordlândia, and the Leaf Blight Waiting in Asia

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mann traces natural rubber from Henry Wickham's 1876 seed-smuggling through the collapse of Brazilian rubber, Ford's Fordlândia failure to South American leaf blight, and China's rubber expansion into Xishuangbanna and Laos, then warns that the fungus Microcyclus ulei, once it reaches the clonal plantations of Southeast Asia, would complete the Columbian Exchange's cycle and seal the Homogenocene.

Mann opens with a rubber agent working a Laotian village (Ban Namma) and then goes back to the person he says the whole "ecological tumult" could be laid to, Henry Wickham, a failed planter who smuggled seventy thousand Hevea seeds out of Santarém in 1876 at the behest of Clements Markham, who had already moved cinchona from the Andes to India. Kew's seedlings reached Asia only sideways: Sri Lanka's coffee blight, and then the same blight in Malaysia and Indonesia, pushed planters to try rubber, and by 1912 Asia out-produced the Amazon and Manaus collapsed. Mann then tells the mirror-image story of Henry Ford's Fordlândia, undone by erosion, sandy soil, poor timing and above all the fungus Microcyclus ulei, which spreads readily among closely packed plantation trees. The narrative moves to China's Maoist and post-Maoist rubber drive in Xishuangbanna, the Go Out strategy and subsidies pushing rubber across the border into Laos, and a lopsided village contract. The closing section, drawn from Mann's interviews at the Xishuangbanna Tropical Botanical Garden, sets out the hydrological damage from rubber and the biological vulnerability of the region's clonal trees, most descended from Wickham's few survivors, and ends with an imagined leaf-blight epidemic that would finish the Columbian Exchange and confirm the Homogenocene.

Themes

Wickham as a "conscious human agent of the Columbian Exchange". This is Mann's own framing. He treats Wickham as an unheroic figure (a serial failure who was later knighted), so the world-historical effect of his seeds is disproportionate to his competence or intent. Mann also deflates the "bio-piracy" charge: Brazil then had no such laws, the British were open about the plan, and Brazil's own major exports are non-native. The outcome came from Markham's state-backed strategic project, which Mann presents as a repeat of the cinchona operation. This sits close to colonial-botanical-garden-grove (Kew as an imperial plant-transfer hub), though Mann tells it as an adventure with unintended ripples and not as an institutional history of botanical gardens.

Benefits and harms of moving species. Mann, citing the environmental historian Warren Dean, argues that "the huge benefits of moving species outweigh the huge harms", while conceding "the balance can be closer than free-exchange advocates tend to admit". This is Mann's hedged judgment on the whole exchange, made with the potato and sweet potato in mind. Dean's phrasing is the more triumphalist one, and Mann only partly endorses it.

Contingency and the accidental route to Asian rubber. Kew's gardeners ignored Wickham's advice, Sri Lanka's planters did not want rubber, and the plant took hold only because coffee disease forced replanting elsewhere. Mann's reading is that a world-changing shift came from unplanned coincidence. He also notes the Manaus elite's disbelief that a boom could end.

Monoculture, clones and disease. In the wild, Hevea trees stand far apart and spores die quickly. On plantations, trees are packed together and the fungus spreads from crown to crown or on workers' clothes. At Fordlândia and Weir's second plantation, Sumatran high-yield clones had, through selection for latex alone, "inadvertently produced varieties with even less resistance to blight". Mann's core ecological point is that the plantation lets disease swamp a system that was resilient at low density, and that Southeast Asian rubber is now a genetically narrow slice of Wickham's slice. This half-echoes the WMP concern with Legibility: uniform, countable stands are easy to manage and easy to lose. Mann does not use that frame, and the failure here is ecological, not administrative.

Isolation as the hidden protection, and its erosion. For a century distance between Amazonia and Asia has held M. ulei off. The new Southeast Asian highway, future flights and Mann's own unsprayed-visa travel show how globalization erodes that buffer. The threat is presented as a boomerang: "The cycle of the Columbian Exchange will be complete, taking away what it once gave." These are Mann's projection and his interviewees' warnings (Tang Jianwei), not established fact, and Mann says the epidemic's scale is speculation.

Rubber as strategic material and geopolitics. Natural rubber is still over 40 percent of the market, essential for aircraft tires and medical goods, and the U.S. rubber blockade of China in the Korean War led China to grow its own. Beijing later used tax and tariff subsidies to turn Laos's opium fields to rubber, as an anti-drug measure. This has a loose resonance with weaponized-interdependence (control over a chokepoint commodity), though Mann treats it as a strategic-supply story and does not theorize it.

Uneven legibility at the village edge. The Huipeng contract, with eighteen paragraphs for the company and one for the villagers, thumbprints against signatures, and a village head who cannot answer basic questions about it, shows that "to be a modern economic agent requires a huge set of habits, assumptions, and expectations". Mann's claim is the incomplete transmission of a market's habits to people who want its goods, not a thesis about state legibility, but it resonates with WMP's interest in what a legible, contract-based economy demands of people entering it.

Homogenocene closing. Mann names the Homogenocene explicitly in the last sentence: an era in which Asia and the Americas are "increasingly alike", here biologically, through a shared pathogen and a shared crop. This is a forward scenario, not a finding.

Notable passages

Wickham's life (c1). Mann's character sketch of Wickham: failed plantations on three continents, a wife who was made to choose between him and the coconuts, and a late-life reputation. It supplies the phrase "a conscious human agent of the Columbian Exchange" and shows how weakly individual intent maps onto historical effect.

Bio-piracy versus benefit (c2). Mann's defence of species transfer, using Dean's quotation. It is one of the few places in the book where Mann states a net judgment, and he hedges it.

Why plantations invite blight (c3). The wild-versus-plantation spacing logic and the explicit statement that the fungus travelled on workers' clothes and fingernails at Fordlândia.

Weir, clones, and the end of Fordlândia (c4). The irony of hiring a rubber expert just as the blight hit, the second Asian cartel banning removal of any living plant part, the high-yield clones proving even more susceptible, and the numbers on Asia's share of world rubber.

The village head at Ban Songma (c5). Mann's reportage on a young village leader unable to answer questions about a contract he negotiated. Mann's judgment ("the questions were beyond his frame of reference") is his own interpretation, offered with visible sympathy.

The imagined epidemic (c6). The closing forecast: leaf blight sweeping from China to Indonesia, visible from space, and planters realizing they live in the Homogenocene.
