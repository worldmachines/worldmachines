The Amazon rubber boom: Craig's railroad, Manaus, and the Putumayo

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mann uses his ancestor Neville Craig's failed 1878 Madeira-Mamoré railroad expedition to open the story of the Amazon rubber boom, arguing that malaria, a labor shortage, extractive violence (caucho, Arana's Putumayo slavery) and inter-imperial scrambling for control of a vital raw material made Brazil's boom-era wealth in Belém and Manaus brutal, unstable and geopolitically contested.

Mann opens with a family anecdote: a portrait in his living room of Neville Burgoyne Craig (1847-1926), an engineer who sailed for the Amazon in 1878 to help build a railroad around the rapids of the Madeira River so that Bolivian rubber could reach the Atlantic. The expedition starved in one of the world's agricultural heartlands, split over a wage strike that the engineers put down by caging strikers at gunpoint, and was finally destroyed by malaria. Mann then widens to the boom itself: the demographic scramble of drought refugees upriver, the paradox of rubber prices tripling while output rose tenfold, the extravagant boomtowns of Belem and Manaus (with the mistress "bather in the bubbly"), the labor-control systems that made the boom possible (debt bondage, then outright slavery), the destructive caucho trade, Julio Cesar Arana's Putumayo atrocities exposed by Hardenburg and Casement, and finally the imperial jockeying (France, Britain, the US via Maury and a Bolivian proxy) that ended with Brazil holding the rubber trade after the Acre war of 1902. The unit sets up, but does not itself narrate, the later smuggling of Hevea seeds to Asia.

Themes

Ecological and disease context comes first. Mann ties the boom to the earlier Columbian Exchange: malaria, "Introduced to the coast by African slaves, probably in the seventeenth century", had depopulated the basin into "fever valleys" (he is picking up a story from Chapter 3), and vulcanization then pulled people back in. Newly arrived migrants "provided new targets for malaria". Craig's party also illustrates Mann's recurrent point that Europeans starved amid plenty for lack of local knowledge; they only survived by adopting Indigenous fishing (Strychnos poison) and manioc. Mann notes Amazonia's agricultural role as a domestication center (manioc, peanuts, chili peppers, tobacco, chocolate, peach palm), attributing the evidence to agricultural geneticists and recent accumulating research.

A boom that defied the usual price logic. Mann emphasizes as anomalous that rubber output rose by an order of magnitude while prices roughly tripled (1870-1910), contrasting Texas oil after 1900 where doubled output crashed prices. He quotes a resource economist (Michael C. Lynch) expressing surprise, and reports the New York Times's hyperbole ("nearly its weight in pure silver") while conceding that speculation and currency turbulence make precise figures hard. He offers no full explanation here, just the puzzle and its speculative frenzy.

Labor scarcity produces coercion, not high wages. Mann states that "Economic theory suggests" a labor shortage would raise wages; instead, promised wages were eaten by charges for transport and board, workers were held in dormitories with armed guards, and interior concessionaires were, in the words of Craig's chief engineer, "absolute masters of their peons". The structural reason is Mann's own inference: tapping needed constant care in remote malarial camps, Indigenous populations had been cut back by disease and raids, and Europeans had not replaced them. This is a materially grounded version of an extractive-institution argument, though Mann does not name the theory in this unit.

Hevea versus caucho: two extraction regimes. Hevea tapping is repeatable and sustainable in principle but was over-worked around Belem; caucho (Castilla elastica) was harvested by felling trees, which Mann says put "a premium on being the first into a new area" and produced a loot-and-leave frontier. He carries Indigenous labor recruitment into violence through Fitzcarrald (relayed through Euclides da Cunha) and Arana (Peruvian, the Putumayo). The Arana account draws on Hemming's Tree of Rivers and on Hardenburg's testimony, and includes the London company board that believed Arana's lies, making slavery, in Mann's phrase, "a British matter".

Rubber as strategic resource and inter-imperial contest. Political and business leaders in Europe and the US were "infuriated" that so vital a material was controlled by foreigners (Hecht's "scramble for the Amazon"): French troops in Guyane, British Guiana's border via a Royal Geographic Society survey, Leopold's offer, Maury's slaveholder-annexation scheme, and a US syndicate buying Bolivian rights to Acre until Brazilian settlers took it in 1902. This is a resource-security geopolitics story that only loosely rhymes with weaponized-interdependence (there the chokepoint is deliberately exploited by a state; here it is a raw material monopoly that rival states scramble to break), and with the world-economy framing of braudel-world-economy, since Belem and Manaus are peripheral nodes financed and run by European merchant houses. Mann is not a machine theorist and makes no such claim.

Boomtown excess as social texture. Belem's "Paris of the Americas" and Manaus's Teatro Amazonas (658 seats, financially foolish) show wealth built on the labor above appearing as spectacle in a place where "the Amazon has little stone". Mann's tone is ironic and reportorial; the brothel and champagne anecdotes are Hemming's, not Mann's own research.

Notable passages

Malaria, drought refugees and the flagelados (c1). The causal core of the opening: malaria capsized Craig's venture, and the boom itself recreated the epidemiological conditions (flagelados fleeing the 1877-79 drought, an estimated 25,000 rubber estates with over 150,000 laborers per Roberto Santos). The Keller-versus-Craig contrast ("saw little malaria" versus "saw little else") compresses the point.

The price anomaly (c2). The unusual rise of price with quantity; Mann hedges that figures are unreliable and treats the New York Times as hyperventilating but "not entirely wrong".

Debt, dormitories and barons (c3). How the labor shortage was met by coercion, and why remote camps and the biology of latex forced this.

Caucheiros and the race to strip forest (c4). The economic logic (the first entrant wins) that made caucho extraction destructive, and how it turned toward Indigenous people, with the understated "there are always people ready to take up such invitations".

Arana and the Putumayo (c5). How a 22,000-square-mile private domain was built from a contested border, weak state presence, bribery, imported enforcers from Barbados and enslaved Indigenous labor.

Maury and Acre (c6). The US strategic interest: Maury's proposal to annex the Amazon as a slave-state safety valve, the Confederate emigres, and the proxy contest that ended with Brazil victorious at Acre.
