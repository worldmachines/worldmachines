The Isthmus of Panama: silver chokepoint and maroon country

Commons · 1493: Uncovering the New World Columbus Created · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Mann narrates how Balboa's 1513 crossing of Panama opened the isthmus as the chokepoint through which Potosí silver flowed to Spain, and how the enslaved Africans and Indians the route required became maroons who defeated Spanish control, allied with Drake, and made the road nearly ungovernable.

Mann opens with Vasco Núñez de Balboa, a debtor-turned-conquistador who crosses Panama in 1513 with about eight hundred men, massacres the Indian lord Quarequa, and sights the Pacific. Mann's own emphasis is on what the Spanish sources noticed and failed to interpret: an entire community of escaped African slaves already living in the mountains, which implies that Africans, not Europeans, were the first transatlantic settlers on the mainland. The chapter then follows the isthmus's fate. After Pizarro's conquest and the 1545 discovery of Potosí, the Panamá to Nombre de Dios road became the single passage for much of the Spanish crown's silver. The route was physically miserable, the local Indian population had collapsed, and so the labor came from imported Indian and then African slaves. By 1565 Africans outnumbered Europeans seven to one, and runaways formed large multiethnic maroon communities that robbed travelers and threatened the silver line. Mann traces the failed Spanish response (the betrayal of the maroon king Bayano in 1556, the deadly disease environment, an incentive conflict over who would pay for a campaign) and closes with Francis Drake's 1572-73 raids, which Mann reads as triumphs for the maroons rather than for the English.

Themes

A chokepoint in the silver economy. Mann presents the road as a single passage "down which flowed much of the monarchy's financial lifeblood" (his phrasing, in his own voice), built with technology Europe could not adequately maintain in a rainforest. The point is that the early global silver system ran through a fragile, poorly controlled bottleneck. This loosely rhymes with braudel-world-economy and, in a different register, the chokepoint logic of weaponized-interdependence, though Mann is describing a logistics failure, not a deliberately weaponized network.

Labor, depopulation, and the turn to Africa. Mann reports the contrast between Oviedo's claim of over two million natives and modern estimates of at most a quarter of a million, then says the exact figure barely matters because the isthmus was depopulated to under twenty thousand by the time Potosí exported silver. Indian slaves were imported; after Spain banned Indian slavery, colonists turned to Africa, beginning with Balboa's thirty captives. The human exchange is thus a derivative of the silver economy's labor demand.

Maroons as a structural outcome. Runaways, joined by escaped Andean and Venezuelan Indian slaves and remnants of local groups, formed multiethnic villages. Mann relies on the historian Jean-Pierre Tardieu for the picture of Bayano's mixed, religiously syncretic community and notes that nobody knows what language they shared. Mann stresses that maroons learned from each failure (avoid lowlands after Felipillo, distrust Spaniards after Bayano, distrust Europeans after Drake's first ambush), that is, they were adaptive agents, not just victims.

Disease as a constraint on empire. Mann folds malaria and yellow fever into the Columbian Exchange: fresh European troops died at horrific rates, Nombre de Dios earned the nickname "Sepultura de Vivos," and the relocated Portobelo was scarcely better. Because roughly two soldiers had to be imported to field one, an anti-maroon force became prohibitively expensive. Yellow fever also halved Drake's party.

Institutions and incentives. Mann frames the paralysis over funding as a "principal agent" problem: the Seville merchants who were short-term agents would not pay for a campaign whose benefits arrived after they left, and the distant crown could not monitor spending. This is Mann's explanatory move, borrowed from economics; it is a small institutional-legibility story (the crown cannot see or measure performance), which touches Legibility only loosely, since Mann does not use that vocabulary.

Unintended consequences. The Spanish imported enslaved Africans to run the silver road and thereby created the population that most threatened it. Mann does not name this as a machine dynamic; the resonance with history-machine-style bottom-up assembly of ungoverned outcomes is at best partial.

Notable passages

Africans before Europeans (c1). Mann reports six Spanish sources attesting to a maroon community two days' walk from Quarequa's village and draws out the implications the sources themselves did not: Africans may have been the first settlers and the first to see the Pacific from the American side, and the isthmus was good country for escaped slaves, a fact that "would come to preoccupy the Spanish crown."

The road (c2). A vivid catalogue of mud, snakes, bats, armor and flooded rivers, culminating in Mann's judgment that sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Europe lacked the means to maintain an adequate highway there.

Runaway numbers (c3). The compact demographic claim that Africans outnumbered Europeans seven to one by 1565 and that runaways formed a maroon force Spain could not manage.

Bayano (c4). The portrait of the maroon leader, his fortress and mixed community, and Ursúa's treacherous peace feast, ending with the lesson maroons drew.

Who pays (c5). Mann's principal-agent analysis of why Spain could not mount a campaign despite rising stakes.

Drake reframed (c6). The chapter's punchline: Nichols's authorized biography presents a failed venture as success, while Mann argues the real winners were the maroons.
