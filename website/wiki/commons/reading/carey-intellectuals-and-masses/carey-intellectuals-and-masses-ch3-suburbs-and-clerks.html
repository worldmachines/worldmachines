The Suburbs and the Clerks

Commons · The Intellectuals and the Masses: Pride and Prejudice among the Literary Intelligentsia · updated 22 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Carey traces how the explosive growth of suburbs and clerical white-collar work between 1880 and 1939 gave English intellectuals a concrete target — "the suburban" and "the clerk" — onto which they projected and then despised an invented, homogeneous mass, with writers from Chesterton and Bullock to Betjeman and Stevie Smith occasionally breaking ranks to reclaim that territory with sympathy.

This chapter locates the intellectuals' contempt for "the masses" in two linked, datable social facts: the suburban building boom that engulfed English countryside between roughly 1880 and 1939, and the parallel explosion in white-collar clerical employment. Carey shows writer after writer — Greene, Waugh, Forster, Shaw, Nesbit, Joad, Byron — recording the same story of a childhood green suburb devoured by jerry-built housing, and then shows how that ecological grievance curdled into class contempt for the people who came to live in the new houses: the clerks. A minority (Chesterton, Bullock, Conan Doyle, Kipling, later Betjeman and Stevie Smith) resisted the consensus, but the dominant intellectual move was to convert a demographic and architectural fact into a moral and aesthetic verdict on an entire class.

Themes

Suburbanization as intellectual trauma and the invention of a category. Nearly every writer surveyed has an autobiographical "ruined paradise" story — Greene's uncle's house at Berkhamsted, Waugh's father's meadow view swallowed by Golders Green, Forster's Rooksnest fictionalized as Howards End. Carey is careful to note that this is a real ecological loss (documented in housing statistics, Times editorials, local newspaper complaints) but that intellectuals then generalized their class-specific grief into a sweeping verdict on suburban people. As Carey puts it, the hostility to suburbs as ecologically destructive "quickly fused with contempt for those who lived in them," and the intellectual's images of "philistine hordes" were less observed than manufactured — "the intellectual delights in portraying (that is, inventing)" the dullness of the middle classes. This is the through-line of the whole book: the mass, and here specifically the suburban clerk, is less a discovered social reality than a rhetorical convenience, a screen onto which the intellectual projects the qualities that justify his own superiority.

Nietzschean aestheticism as the sharpest edge of the contempt. The chapter's darkest material is the strain of anti-democratic aestheticism that treats mass suffering as aesthetically necessary — George Moore's rhapsody on injustice as the price of great art, and Shaw's chillingly literal eugenic proposals in the Preface to On the Rocks. Carey insists these are not fringe cranks: Moore was Yeats's collaborator, Shaw a national institution. Their extremity exposes, in undiluted form, an assumption running through milder anti-suburban writing (Crosland, Connolly, Mrs Leavis): that the many exist to be managed, culled, or aesthetically discounted so that the few can flourish.

The clerk as a stunted, feminized, or redeemable figure. Clerks recur as an image of physical and spiritual diminishment — Leonard Bast's spine "that might have been straight," Eliot's carbuncular house-agent's clerk in The Waste Land, Bullock's Robert Thorne with his narrow chest. Carey tracks a counter-tradition of pro-clerk writing (Conan Doyle's athletic Hall Pycroft, Wells's rescuing stockbrokers' clerks) that answers the slur by reasserting manly vigour rather than by contesting the premise that manliness is what's at stake. Bullock's Robert Thorne and Kipling's "The Finest Story in the World" go further, insisting on the clerk's inner life and thwarted intellectual hunger — Thorne's autodidact reading list, Charlie Mears's devouring of Byron — as a rebuke to a class system that closes off higher education while blaming its casualties for their ignorance.

Two writers who inhabit rather than judge the suburb. Betjeman and Stevie Smith close the chapter as the tradition's exceptions: Betjeman splitting suburbia into a nostalgic Eden (his parents' generation) and a monstrous present (bureaucrats, sodium lamps, Odeons), Smith finding unembarrassed sensory and formal pleasure in Palmers Green — rain on Grovelands park, pink light on brick walls — and building a poetic voice out of the same "unpretentiousness" for which suburbia was despised.

Notable passages

The Times's 1904 leader on suburban sprawl ("a district of appalling monotony, ugliness and dullness") sets the chapter's baseline: a genuine, widely shared alarm at landscape destruction that predates and outlasts any specifically intellectual snobbery. Carey's own summary sentence — that the intellectual's philistine-hordes image was something he "delights in portraying (that is, inventing)" — is the chapter's thesis in miniature, and connects directly to this book's larger argument that the very category of "the masses" functions for intellectuals as a projection screen rather than an observed population; in world-machines terms this is a small case study in how an elite reads and narrates a social transformation it did not choose and cannot fully control. George Moore's paean to injustice ("Injustice we worship; all that lifts us out of the misery of life is the sublime fruit of injustice") and Shaw's proposal that "Extermination must be put on a scientific basis if it is ever to be carried out humanely" show the aestheticized-eugenic endpoint of anti-mass thinking taken at face value rather than softened as metaphor. Kipling's "The Finest Story in the World" is treated as a rare self-aware exception — Carey reads its narrator-scholar dynamic as satire of the intellectual's own posture, "observing that the masses must pass into history before they become suitable for intellectual contemplation." Bullock's aside on Thorne — dissolving into "thousands like him... Slaves of the desk. Twopenny clerks" and yet insisting "He has a soul, this figure that I see in the crowd" — is the chapter's clearest statement of a conatus-like individual persistence against statistical absorption into the mass, a theme Conatus names directly. Stevie Smith's rapturous description of Palmers Green in stormy light closes the chapter as the fullest available rebuttal to the entire anti-suburban consensus it has just documented.
