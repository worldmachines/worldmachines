Natural Aristocrats

Commons · The Intellectuals and the Masses: Pride and Prejudice among the Literary Intelligentsia · updated 22 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Carey traces how early-twentieth-century intellectuals (Nietzsche, Lawrence, Yeats, Pound, Bell, Huxley, Greene, Steiner) invented a doctrine of "natural aristocracy" to reassert distinction and authority once mass literacy and democracy had eroded the old bases of cultural and social hierarchy.

This unit surveys the family of doctrines by which early-twentieth-century writers and critics — Nietzsche, W. B. Yeats, Ezra Pound, T. S. Eliot, Clive Bell, D. H. Lawrence, Aldous Huxley, Graham Greene, Evelyn Waugh, Middleton Murry, and George Steiner — reconstructed a claim to be a "natural aristocracy" once the traditional grounds of intellectual and social distinction (birth, religious authority, monopoly on literacy) had been undercut by the "revolt of the masses." Carey treats these doctrines less as philosophy than as symptom: a set of improvised, often self-contradictory rhetorical strategies for reasserting superiority in a world that had stopped automatically granting it.

Themes

The problem precedes the solution. Carey opens by naming the drive rather than the doctrine: intellectuals, faced with redundancy in a newly literate, democratized culture, needed "new proof of their distinction." The chapter catalogues the candidate grounds for this distinction — esoteric knowledge (Lawrence, Yeats), transcendence of ephemeral commercial time (Eliot), the sacredness of aesthetic rapture (Bell), and outright despotic self-election (Pound) — before settling on Nietzsche as the doctrine's most extreme and paradigmatic exponent. This is a legitimacy crisis in miniature: the old warrants for cultural authority (aristocratic birth, ecclesiastical sanction) have died, and nothing has yet organically replaced them, so intellectuals manufacture replacements under pressure — a dynamic that rhymes with the gramsci-gap pattern of an interregnum between an expired legitimating order and an unborn one, "morbid symptoms" and all, though here enacted in aesthetics and philosophy rather than politics proper.

Nietzsche's rhetoric of embattled nobility. Carey reconstructs Nietzsche's position as a rhetoric rather than a philosophy: an anti-egalitarian, anti-Christian, anti-logical stance built from mountain-and-eagle imagery rather than demonstrable argument. Carey is careful to show its internal collapse — Nietzsche renounces moral judgement while making moral judgements constantly, renounces logic while depending on it to argue, and invokes biology to justify the strong overpowering the weak even as he denies (against Darwin) that the biologically ascendant masses are actually superior. The mountaineering imagery is presented as compensation: a way of giving "objective expression" to a superiority that has no other evidence.

Lawrence's contradiction between hierarchy and uniqueness. D. H. Lawrence inherits Nietzsche's terms ("blood," "breeding," contempt for ideas and logic) but Carey shows him caught in a deeper bind: his poetic conviction that every created thing is singular and incomparable is logically incompatible with ranking human beings into higher and lower orders. Lawrence never resolves this — he asserts both positions with equal fervour, sometimes within the same essay — and Carey reads this not as sophistication but as a symptom of the same underlying anxiety that drives the whole natural-aristocrat project.

Institutionalizing the elite: Clive Bell's slave economy. Bell's Civilization pushes the doctrine to its logical, openly authoritarian conclusion: a "civilizing élite" of aesthetically refined connoisseurs, exempted from labor and supported by public funds, requires — Bell says so explicitly — a supporting class of slaves and an efficient police force to manage their discontent. Carey uses Bell to show how quickly aesthetic elitism converts into frank political domination once its practical implications are drawn out, including Bell's exclusion of housewives and endorsement of prostitution as the only route to female "civilization."

Recruiting God as adjudicator. A recurring late move, from Huxley to Greene to Steiner, is to invoke divine sanction where argument fails: Huxley's mescalin-derived contact with "the divine source of all experience," Greene's Catholic convert as spiritually superior to the decent but Godless Ida in Brighton Rock, and Steiner's claim that great art is "touched by the fire and the ice of God." Carey's closing point is mordant: God, like "the masses," becomes a convenient fiction in intellectual discourse — a figure whose only real job is to ratify judgements the intellectual has already made.

Huxley and the necessity of suffering. Brave New World is read as Nietzschean doctrine in novelistic form: the idea that struggle, hardship, and suffering are necessary for the human spirit to be significant, an idea Carey traces to a fusion of Christian redemptive-suffering ethics and the ideology that legitimated nineteenth-century imperial exploitation — the very system that produced the leisured class Huxley belonged to.

Notable passages

The Pound blockquote ("we who are the heirs of the witch-doctor and the voodoo... about to take over control") is the most naked statement of the doctrine's megalomania, useful precisely because it strips away the more decorous framings offered by Eliot or Bell. Nietzsche's anti-Christian tirade ("I abhor Christianity with a deadly hatred") and his mountain-peak imagery both show Carey's method: quoting extensively and letting the rhetoric's excess do the critical work. The Bell passage on slaves and police is the chapter's clearest demonstration that aesthetic hierarchy, followed to its institutional conclusion, requires coercion. The Huxley/Nietzsche parallel on suffering, and the closing lines on Steiner's God-as-convenient-fiction, bookend the chapter by showing the doctrine's evolution from swaggering assertion to a more anxious, theologically laundered version of the same claim.
