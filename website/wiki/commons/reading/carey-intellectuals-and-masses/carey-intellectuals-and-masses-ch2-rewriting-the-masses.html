Rewriting the Mass: Metaphor as Instrument of Control

Commons · The Intellectuals and the Masses: Pride and Prejudice among the Literary Intelligentsia · updated 22 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Carey traces how early-twentieth-century intellectuals (Ortega, Hardy, Nietzsche, Eliot, Woolf, Pound, Le Bon, Freud, Canetti, Forster, Lawrence, Priestley, and above all Orwell) invented and reinvented images of "the mass" — herd, bacillus, jelly, peasant, pastoral child, prole — in order to strip actual people of individuality and secure the intellectual's own superiority and control.

This chapter argues that "the mass" is not a sociological given but a rhetorical invention: since actual human multiplicity is unknowable and unmanageable, intellectuals from the 1880s to the 1930s built substitute images — herds, bacilli, jellies, savages, peasants, proles — that could be manipulated, degraded, or sentimentalized at will. Carey moves through Ortega y Gasset, Hardy, Nietzsche, Eliot, Woolf, Pound, Le Bon, Freud, Canetti, Forster, Lawrence, Priestley, Greene, and finally a long case study of Orwell, showing two complementary strategies: derogatory dehumanization (mass-as-animal, mass-as-disease, mass-as-fact-obsessed-philistine) and cosmetic dehumanization (mass-as-peasant, mass-as-pastoral-innocent, mass-as-noble-prole), both of which achieve the same end — segregating the intellectual from the mass and licensing control over it.

Themes

Naming is domination. The chapter's thesis, stated through Ortega, is that rewriting the mass lets intellectuals "acquire the control over the mass that language gives." Hardy's own trajectory — from inarticulate night terror at London's population ("a monster whose body had four million heads and eight million eyes") to contemptuous ease once he can fix "these people" in language at the British Museum — is offered as "a paradigm of the modern intellectual's effort to limit and dominate the mass": naming converts formless dread into a controllable object.

The "scientific" tradition turns people into specimens and pathogens. Le Bon's The Crowd (1895) fused crowd and mass into a single irrational, feminine, childlike, barbarous entity destined to destroy civilization; Freud's Group Psychology borrowed Le Bon's model almost wholesale to justify élite suppression of a mass identified with the unconscious and "evil"; Mass Observation reduced individuals to codes like "M 45 D." Carey draws the darkest line through this tradition to Pasteur's bacterial model of society and Hitler's rhetoric of the "Jewish virus" — the same conceptual move (masses-as-microbes) that starts as a defensive metaphor ends, in the Nazi case, as literal extermination logic. This is the chapter's most forceful demonstration that dehumanizing metaphor is not merely literary but has genocidal downstream potential.

Cosmetic rewriting is dehumanizing too. The chapter's subtler argument is that flattering images — Pound's crowd-as-cherry-blossoms, Forster's eroticized, primitivized Italians and the divine "punkah-wallah," Woolf's begging old woman transfigured into an eternal earth-mother, Yeats's swarming-but-innocent birds and fish — perform the identical operation as the hostile images: they erase the actual social and economic reality of the person observed (poverty, caste, the fact of begging for coppers) behind an aesthetic screen the intellectual finds palatable. Forster prefers the punkah-wallah's silent, pre-democratic "definite status" to Leonard Bast's troublesome literacy; Woolf's old woman is not described but eliminated by being merged with soil and geological time.

Orwell as the tradition's most self-aware casualty. Carey gives Orwell an extended, sympathetic but unsparing treatment: raised to feel the working class as simultaneously beloved (in childhood) and physically repellent, Orwell never resolves the contradiction that dirt is both proof of the workers' authentic freedom and viscerally disgusting to him. His self-imposed tramping is read as expiatory theater — dirt as "baptism" atoning for his colonial service — and the same unresolved doublethink recurs structurally in Nineteen Eighty-Four, where the proles are alternately hopeless dupes of Prolefeed and, in the washerwoman scene, pastoral repositories of unkillable vitality — Orwell rewriting the mass exactly as Yeats had in "Sailing to Byzantium."

Convergence with the Frankfurt School. Adorno, Horkheimer, and Marcuse arrive, via a different theoretical route, at the same impasse as Winston Smith: masses seduced by a capitalist "culture industry" into false consciousness, with genuine truth and art retreating to an élite few — a structural echo of Le Bon's aristocracy-versus-barbarian-mass fifty years earlier.

The chapter resonates with legibility-as-machine-core read against the grain: here, legibility is not bureaucratic measurement but a rhetorical/aesthetic technology that intellectuals use to render an otherwise illegible multitude into fixed, manageable types — the same drive toward order and control, deployed culturally rather than administratively. The averaging violence of "M 45 D" and of Le Bon's/Freud's mass-unconscious models also bears on Conatus: each classification scheme suppresses the individuating striving of actual persons in favor of a statistically or metaphorically flattened type.

Notable passages

The Ortega paragraph supplies the chapter's explicit thesis about language and control, complete with one of the source's bare endnote markers left uncleaned ("comprising.1 It seems clear"). Hardy's British Museum diary entry shows the fear-to-contempt pivot in miniature — mockery of the crowd's flippancy toward Mycerinus and Ramases sliding straight into a prophecy that democracy will "merge in proletarian" ruin of art. The Hitler/Pasteur passage is the chapter's moral center of gravity, showing the bacterial metaphor's literal terminus. Forster's punkah-wallah passage exemplifies cosmetic dehumanization at its most ornate — divinizing an anonymous laborer while erasing caste and destitution. Orwell's dirt paradox ("the dirt never worried me... Dirt is a thing people make too much fuss about") is Carey's clearest evidence that even the era's most self-critical intellectual could not fully escape the mass-rewriting reflex. Woolf's transfiguration of a begging woman into a wind-beaten, geological, ever-singing figure of "love which prevails" is the purest instance of pastoral erasure: description that functions, Carey insists, as elimination.
