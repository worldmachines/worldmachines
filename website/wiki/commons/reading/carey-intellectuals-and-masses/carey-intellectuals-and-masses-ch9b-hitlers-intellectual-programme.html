Hitler's Cultural Orthodoxy and the Rewriting of the Mass

Commons · The Intellectuals and the Masses: Pride and Prejudice among the Literary Intelligentsia · updated 22 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Carey argues, using Mein Kampf and Table Talk, that Hitler's cultural ideas—reverence for canonical genius, contempt for mass media and the bourgeoisie, the aristocratic principle set against parliamentary democracy, and a series of interchangeable images of the mass as woman, child, nation, worker, or exterminable vermin—were not an aberration but continuous with mainstream early twentieth-century English intellectual orthodoxy.

This unit is the payoff chapter of Carey's argument: rather than treating Hitler as a cultural aberration, Carey reads Mein Kampf and the Table Talk conversations against the intellectual positions catalogued in the rest of the book — Gissing's starving-student pose, Nietzschean genius-worship, Wyndham Lewis's and Eliot's aesthetic absolutism, Wells's demographic anxiety, Lawrence's peasant-and-body cult — and finds them reproduced almost point for point in Hitler's own writing. The chapter's structural move is to take Hugh Trevor-Roper's dismissal of Hitler's cultural views as "trivial, half-baked and disgusting" and systematically show that every element of that programme (state subsidy of opera houses, veneration of Shakespeare and Goethe, belief in a "divine spark" of genius, scorn for gutter journalism and cinema, the aristocratic principle versus democracy) has a direct counterpart among respected English intellectuals of the period. The chapter's climax reinterprets the mass as the organizing concept of Nazi ideology and the Holocaust as its logical, murderous terminus.

Themes

Hitler as inheritor, not deviant, of intellectual highbrow culture. Carey documents Hitler's autodidact bibliophilia (Nietzsche, Schopenhauer, Cervantes, Goethe, Wagner, the old masters), his advocacy of lavish state arts subsidy, and his belief that "Wars pass by. The only things that exist are the works of human genius" — a position, Carey notes, indistinguishable from that of a George Steiner. The chapter's rhetorical strategy throughout is to show that condemning Hitler's aesthetics requires condemning the same aesthetics wherever else they appear in the intellectual canon.

The divine spark and the theological legitimation of hierarchy. Hitler's claim that genius is "an innate product of divine grace," and that the Aryan race's cultural achievement proves it is "the highest image of God among his creatures," is read by Carey as only a racialized extension of the ordinary intellectual tactic of invoking God's aesthetic preferences to certify one's own cultural superiority — the same move Carey has traced through Lewis and Steiner elsewhere in the book.

The aristocratic principle versus the mass. Hitler's Nietzschean cosmology — nature as fundamentally aristocratic, genius as "always a protest against the inertia of the mass," democracy as a betrayal of "the eternal Will that governs the universe" — mirrors the natural-aristocracy schemes Carey finds in D. H. Lawrence and others: hostility to the nouveaux riches, appeals to "breeding," and a conviction that cultural eminence should confer political power.

Population terror as the engine of political brutality. Carey shows Hitler obsessively tracking population statistics (Germany's growth, India's, Russia's) and using the specter of overpopulation to justify a coming "dreadful struggle for existence" in which humanitarianism will not survive. This argument, Carey argues, "root[s] the politics of brutality firmly in his readers' terror of crowds and numbers" — the same numerical vertigo that Wells channeled into fantasies about "hordes" of Africans and Asians.

The mass rewritten in incompatible forms. Hitler cycles through mutually contradictory images of the mass — as feminine and irrational, as childlike, as a nation to be welded by propaganda, as noble workers versus a despised bourgeoisie, as a healthy peasantry, and finally as the Jew, imagined as literally sub-material ("jelly and slime"). Carey treats this instability as itself diagnostic: the concept of "the mass" is not a stable sociological category but a flexible device intellectuals and demagogues alike use to justify withholding personhood from whomever they need to exclude.

The Holocaust as the terminus of the intellectual mass-concept. The chapter's most severe claim is that industrialized extermination was made thinkable by first accepting that Jews were a mass — "not fully alive people" — making mass transportation, incineration, and the manufacture of fertilizer from ashes seem merely proportionate. Carey explicitly implicates the literary intelligentsia's decades of before-the-fact rhetoric about the soulless, crowd-like, cremation-worthy mass as preparing this ground.

Notable passages

The opening paragraph sets up the chapter's method by contesting Trevor-Roper's dismissal directly. The divine-spark passage (para. 15) is the clearest statement of Carey's central analogical technique — showing a supposedly extreme Hitlerian belief to be structurally identical to mainstream intellectual argument. The aristocratic-principle passage (para. 17) gives the Nietzschean genius-versus-democracy logic in Hitler's own terms. The population passage (para. 23) demonstrates how demographic anxiety converts into justified brutality. The Holocaust passage (para. 39) is the chapter's moral center, naming the mass-concept as the precondition for genocide. The closing paragraph (para. 49) restates the book's thesis in its starkest form: "The tragedy of Mein Kampf is that it was not, in many respects, a deviant work but one firmly rooted in European intellectual orthodoxy."
