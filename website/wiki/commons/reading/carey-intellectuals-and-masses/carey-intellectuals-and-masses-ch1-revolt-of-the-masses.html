The Revolt of the Masses

Commons · The Intellectuals and the Masses: Pride and Prejudice among the Literary Intelligentsia · updated 22 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Surveying Ortega y Gasset, Nietzsche, and a chorus of early-twentieth-century European writers, Carey shows how the literary intelligentsia responded to newly literate mass publics, popular newspapers, and universal education by inventing modernism, eugenics fantasies, and theories of the avant-garde as mechanisms for excluding and dehumanizing 'the masses,' before arguing that the mass itself is only a metaphor that denies individuality to other people.

This opening chapter builds its argument from Ortega y Gasset's 1930 book of the same title, tracing how population growth, universal elementary education, and the rise of the popular press between roughly 1880 and 1939 produced a literate mass public — and how Europe's literary intellectuals, led rhetorically by Nietzsche, responded not with welcome but with dread, disgust, and a series of increasingly extreme fantasies (denial of the masses' humanity, eugenic culling, illiteracy-by-design, deliberately difficult art) aimed at re-excluding that public from culture. Carey closes by arguing that 'the mass' was never a real referent at all but a metaphor intellectuals used to strip other people of individuality.

Themes

Population growth as the originating anxiety. Ortega's statistical alarm — Europe's population rising from 180 to 460 million between 1800 and 1914 — supplies the demographic premise for everything that follows: overcrowding, the 'intrusion' of the crowd into spaces built for elites, and the 'dictatorship of the mass' under hyperdemocracy. Carey treats this as a case where quantification of population becomes the occasion for a program of cultural and political exclusion — the mass is first rendered legible as a threatening aggregate before it is denied any interior life. This has a clear resonance with legibility-as-machine-core: statistical visibility of 'the mass' precedes, and enables, its dehumanization.

Nietzsche as the mass culture's own antagonist-product. Carey's counterintuitive move is to cast Nietzsche not as an external critic but as 'one of the earliest products of mass culture' — generated by it, in opposition to itself. Nietzsche's denunciations of the state, the 'rabble,' and universal education (all echoed by Zarathustra's wish for 'a storm' to shake 'rottenness' from the human tree) become the template that Ibsen, Flaubert, Hamsun, Lawrence, Eliot, and Yeats all draw on, often selectively and inconsistently, to rationalize their own contempt for a newly enfranchised readership.

Literacy, journalism, and the displacement of the intellectual. The 1870/1871 Education Acts created 'a huge literate public' that bypassed traditional literary gatekeepers; Northcliffe's Daily Mail ('giving the public what it wants') and the human-interest story took over fiction's cultural function without needing novelists at all. Intellectuals like Eliot and Leavis responded with organized hostility to newspapers (Scrutiny capped at 750 copies as a badge of purity), while misogyny threads through the account — women readers and Northcliffe's courting of them are treated by male intellectuals (Nietzsche, Holbrook Jackson) as signs of degeneration.

Denial that the masses are fully alive. A recurring and increasingly disturbing move is the claim that mass humanity lacks souls or is already 'dead' — from Hardy's division of 'souls and machines,' through Eliot's Dante-inflected crowds on London Bridge, to Lawrence's explicit ranking of himself above 'the Mexican who drives the wagon for me.' Carey traces the logical terminus of this move: if the masses are already dead, their elimination is imaginatively costless, which licenses Lawrence's and Nietzsche's eugenic and exterminationist rhetoric, and Yeats's active engagement with sterilization science via Cattell. This is the starkest instance in the unit of what WMP calls the suppression of Conatus — the individuating, self-persisting drive of a person — through statistical or metaphysical averaging into an undifferentiated aggregate, i.e. exactly the mechanism capture-resistance names as what liveness must resist.

Modernism and the avant-garde as engineered exclusion. Having failed to prevent literacy itself, intellectuals turned to making literature unreadable: Eliot's dictum that poets 'must be difficult,' Grigson's New Verse founded explicitly against mass values, and Ortega's The Dehumanization of Art, which welcomes modern art's function of segregating the public into 'illustrious' and 'vulgar' castes. Carey names this pattern the theory of the avant-garde — nominally progressive but functionally reactionary, since its purpose is to claw back from democratic educational reform what universal literacy had granted.

Fictional mass men, judged and found wanting (or not). Forster's Leonard Bast and Woolf's Doris Kilman are punished narratively for attempting self-culture, while Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes stands as mass culture's own comforting, newspaper-loving counter-image of the intellectual. Joyce's Bloom is the partial exception — richly interiorized, yet structurally excluded from ever being able to read the novel that renders him, which Carey reads as Ulysses's built-in duplicity: it embraces mass man and simultaneously expels him.

The mass as metaphor, not referent. The chapter's thesis, arrived at in its final movement, is that 'the masses do not exist' — the mass is a metaphysical abstraction, 'the crowd in its metaphysical aspect,' onto which intellectuals project attributes (newspaper-reading, tinned food) precisely because it cannot be individually known. The closing survey of tinned food as a recurring class-marker of soullessness (Forster, Hamsun, Eliot, Betjeman, Greene, Wells, Orwell) through to Warhol's 1962 Soup Tins shows the same anxious symbol persisting for decades after the literary intelligentsia's original revolt.

Notable passages

The Ortega population-explosion passage establishes the demographic premise the whole chapter answers to. The Nietzsche/Zarathustra passage supplies the ideological vocabulary — 'declaration of war on the masses' — that later writers borrow. Lawrence's 1908 letter describing a 'lethal chamber' is the chapter's most chilling evidence that exterminationist fantasy was not confined to Nietzsche's aphorisms but imagined in operational detail by a canonical English novelist. Ortega's Dehumanization of Art passage shows the avant-garde's exclusionary function stated as a program rather than inferred. The closing 'masses do not exist' passage is the chapter's thesis statement, reframing everything preceding it as evidence for a category error intellectuals built their self-image on.
