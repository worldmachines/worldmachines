Narrowing the Abyss: Arnold Bennett as Anti-Elitist Novelist

Commons · The Intellectuals and the Masses: Pride and Prejudice among the Literary Intelligentsia · updated 22 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Carey presents Arnold Bennett as the book's counter-hero, a self-made shopkeeper's son turned bestselling novelist and mass-market critic whose fiction and journalism systematically refute the modernist claim that ordinary, uneducated people live shallower or less worthy inner lives than intellectuals.

This chapter reads Arnold Bennett's life, journalism, and fiction as a sustained rebuttal of the modernist intelligentsia's contempt for the masses. Carey traces Bennett's provincial, shopkeeping origins (Burslem, a pawnbroker father, night-school matriculation, a clerkship at twenty-five shillings a week) and the class-inflected scorn this provoked from Clive Bell, Woolf, Eliot, Russell, Lawrence, and Pound. Against this, Carey assembles Bennett's counter-doctrine: that art is a universal human faculty rather than an intellectual monopoly, that popular taste deserves respect rather than derision, and that a "democratization of art" through education and cheap reprints would narrow — not erase — the gap between the reading public and its self-appointed cultural guardians. The bulk of the chapter then reads Bennett's novels (The Man from the North, The Old Wives' Tale, Clayhanger, Riceyman Steps/"Elsie and the Child", These Twain) as proof-of-concept: fiction that finds tragedy, delicacy, and "aristocratic" refinement in clerks, servants, drapers, and housemaids, and that treats journalism, advertising, plumbing, and vacuum cleaners as proper subjects of a serious realist art.

Themes

Bennett as deliberate mediator, not merely a popular hack. Carey stresses that Bennett's commercialism (the yacht, the syndicated serials, the brisk book-a-day reviewing) was not philistinism but a considered strategy: he wanted intellectuals to write for a wider audience and refused to treat market success as proof of worthlessness. His critical writing (Fame and Fiction, Literary Taste, decades of reviewing for the New Age and the Evening Standard) championed Dostoevsky, Chekhov, Conrad, Lawrence, Eliot, Joyce, Proust, and the unknown Faulkner — a record that, Carey argues, makes the charge of philistinism untenable.

The biological, anti-elitist metaphysics underlying the fiction. Bennett repeatedly frames people as organisms rather than intellects — bound by "the eternal," "the everlasting purpose," reproduction, and death — which levels intellectual and non-intellectual alike, since biology is "no respecter of thinkers." At the same time he holds, almost as a religious conviction, that every individual (however outwardly ordinary) is irreducibly singular and unknowable to others. Carey shows these two ideas, though logically in tension, both serving Bennett's anti-elitism.

Delicacy and dignity found in the socially invisible. The chapter's emotional core is Bennett's recurring device of disclosing hidden refinement — "delicacy," "aristocratic instinct" — inside characters intellectuals would dismiss outright: the draper's apprentice Edwin Clayhanger, the housemaid Elsie Sprickett, the servant girl Minnie. Carey reads this as Bennett's answer to Eliot's sneer about the "damp souls of housemaids."

Youth and age as the universal, non-class-bound theme. Rather than staying with a social-problem register, Bennett anchors his major novels in a theme Carey calls transcendent of intelligence and culture: the perpetual, pitiless war between the young and the old, dramatized through commonplace objects and gestures (castor oil, a satin wedding shoe, a swing in a garden) rather than moralizing.

Domestic technology as a serious subject modernism ignored. Carey highlights Bennett's engagement with labour-saving home design, plumbing, and electricity — matters he treats with the same gravity as art, and which "enriched the lives of millions of women" while no modernist writer took any notice.

The Woolf rebuttal. The chapter closes by turning Virginia Woolf's 'Mr Bennett and Mrs Brown' against itself: Carey argues that Bennett's fiction is full of exactly the unpredictable, incalculable inner shifts Woolf claims only the moderns capture (Hilda Lessways's dispassionate noticing of a moustache during a first kiss, Sophia's strange pride on learning she has been bigamously married), and that Woolf's essay is animated less by aesthetic principle than by class snobbery — the same snobbery Carey has spent the book indicting.

Notable passages

Carey opens by declaring Bennett "the hero of this book," a framing he earns by cataloguing the intellectuals' class-contempt (Clive Bell's "insignificant little man," Eliot's "successful wholesale grocer," Pound's philistine Mr Nixon) before showing how Bennett's own writing answers it. The manifesto passage on art as "a factor in all lives" states Bennett's core anti-elitist claim outright, and the New Age-era line that "the abyss between the mentality of the true leaders and the mentality of the people narrows and must narrow every year" supplies the chapter's title and its optimistic, education-driven theory of cultural change. Aked's speech in The Man from the North — finding "eighty separate dramas" of tragedy in eighty ordinary suburban houses — is Bennett's aesthetic credo in miniature: everyday, unremarkable life as inexhaustible material for serious art. The Elsie-and-Eva bedtime scene is Carey's strongest evidence for delicacy located in an unlikely body: a servant's inarticulate tenderness toward her young mistress, rendered with a seriousness Bennett elsewhere reserves for his most sympathetic protagonists, its guilty refrain of "Wickedness" underscoring how far this scene exceeds patronizing comedy.
