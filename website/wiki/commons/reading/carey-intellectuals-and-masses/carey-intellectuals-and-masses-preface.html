Preface: The Thesis of "The Intellectuals and the Masses"

Commons · The Intellectuals and the Masses: Pride and Prejudice among the Literary Intelligentsia · updated 22 August 2026 · source on GitHub
In the preface to "The Intellectuals and the Masses," John Carey states his book's thesis that modernist literature and art were a deliberate strategy by which the late-Victorian and early-twentieth-century literary intelligentsia sought to exclude the newly literate reading public created by educational reform, and traces the theological genealogy of the word "mass" from Augustine's massa damnata to its terminal expression in Hitler's death camps.

Carey's short preface lays out, with unusual bluntness for a scholarly monograph, the entire argument of the book that follows: modernist literature and art are best understood not as autonomous aesthetic developments but as a hostile reaction by the literary intelligentsia to the sudden emergence of a mass reading public, produced by late nineteenth-century educational reform. The preface also supplies the book's key semantic move — showing that "the mass" is not a neutral sociological category but an inherited theological fiction — before closing with biographical and institutional context (the book grew out of the 1989 T. S. Eliot Memorial Lectures) and acknowledgments.

Themes

Modernism as exclusionary technology. Carey's central claim is functional, almost mechanical: modernist writing's difficulty, obscurity, and hostility to popular taste are not incidental stylistic features but purpose-built devices. Their purpose was to "exclude" the newly educated — the products of compulsory schooling — and thereby to preserve a shrinking intellectual class's sense of distinction. This reframes a century of formal innovation (fragmentation, allusion, difficulty) as a defensive maneuver by an elite reacting to a literacy expansion it could not control. In World Machines terms, this is a strikingly clean case of a legibility-producing reform (universal education, expanding the literate population, an artifact of the modernity-machine's drive toward standardized, scaled populations) provoking a counter-move by an incumbent culture that responds not by re-asserting control over the newly legible mass but by fleeing upward into deliberate illegibility — a retreat that only makes sense against a backdrop of proliferating, ungovernable readership resembling the push logic of a divergence-machine.

"The mass" as linguistic fiction. Carey insists at the outset that "the mass" is "of course, a fiction" — a term whose function is to strip the majority of people of human status, or at least of whatever qualities the term's users claim as their own distinguishing superiority. This is the book's methodological pivot: rather than treating mass culture and its intellectual critics as opposing but equally real social facts, Carey treats "the mass" itself as a rhetorical instrument to be unmasked, setting up the case-study chapters that follow.

Theological genealogy and its terminus in atrocity. Carey traces the word's ancestry to Augustine's massa damnata / massa perditionis — the condemned mass of humanity, minus the elect few God inexplicably chooses to save. He draws a direct, deliberately shocking line from this theological doctrine of the excess, disposable human remainder through to Hitler's death camps, naming the connection explicitly as "this well-established Christian precedent for disposing of the surplus 'mass' by combustion." This is the preface's most aggressive interpretive claim: that the intelligentsia's disdain for the mass reader participates in — and inherits the eliminationist logic of — a much older doctrine of the damned remainder, with modern genocide as its "practical expression."

Institutional and biographical framing. The remainder of the preface is more conventional scholarly apparatus: the book began as the 1989 T. S. Eliot Memorial Lectures at the University of Kent, was extended with additional case-study chapters, and includes acknowledgments (Faber and Faber, Kent colleagues, the Henry James Lecture at Rye, various friends). This material is useful for provenance but carries none of the book's argumentative weight.

Notable passages

The opening thesis statement is the load-bearing sentence of the whole book: modernist writing's purpose was "to exclude these newly educated (or 'semi-educated') readers, and so to preserve the intellectual's seclusion from the 'mass'." Carey then immediately denaturalizes his own key term — "The 'mass' is, of course, a fiction" — before tracing it to Augustine's massa damnata, a passage carrying a bare endnote marker ("...decided to save.1") in the source. The paragraph's final turn, from theological damnation to the crematoria of the twentieth century, is the preface's most provocative move and previews the book's closing chapter.
