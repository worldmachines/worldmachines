Coda: Mass-Phobia After Modernism

Commons · The Intellectuals and the Masses: Pride and Prejudice among the Literary Intelligentsia · updated 22 August 2026 · source on GitHub
In the book's postscript, Carey traces intellectual mass-phobia from Virginia Woolf's self-tormenting fantasy of loathsome working-class women through Rayner Heppenstall's near-genocidal outbursts, then updates the story to the late twentieth century, where real population explosion literalizes the old fear of the swarming mass and post-structuralist "theory" replaces modernist difficulty as the intellectual's new defense against a culturally enfranchised majority.

Carey closes the book by showing that the intellectual contempt for the masses he has traced through 1880-1939 did not end with the period. He opens with Virginia Woolf's 1941 diary entry inventing loathsome, parasitic women out of overheard lavatory chatter, moves to Rayner Heppenstall as a pathological limit-case of the same syndrome, then leaps past his stated period to show two real historical developments — runaway population growth and the rise of television — that force a reckoning with what the early intellectuals feared and got wrong, and one that shows continuity — the emergence of post-structuralist "theory" as a new, more effective version of the old anti-popular strategy.

Themes

Mass-phobia is self-invented and self-tormenting. Woolf's diary scene is admitted to be fiction — "the women in Fuller's are not 'slugs'" — yet the invented mass still generates real rage and loathing in its inventor. Carey generalizes this as the structural feature of the whole intellectual tradition he has been describing: the mass is a projection that then punishes the projector, offering only "the marginal comfort of assuring the intellectual that he or she is different."

Heppenstall as clinical extremity. Carey uses Rayner Heppenstall — Third Programme producer, friend of Orwell and Dylan Thomas — as evidence that the earlier intellectuals' rhetoric of extermination was not merely rhetorical excess but could shade into something closer to derangement: a wish for the "total genocide" of Arabs and Irish, a hoarded suicide potion, a bonfire feud with working-class neighbours that he later rewrote as a fictional multiple murder. Carey's diagnosis is explicitly psychiatric ("perhaps best regarded as insane") and pathetic rather than dangerous, since Heppenstall could barely walk to the shops — the fantasy of violence substitutes for real power.

Population growth literalizes the old fear. Wells and Ortega feared and eugenically fantasized about a swelling, unmanageable mass; Carey shows their heirs got the direction of history backwards. Wells predicted the world population would fall to under a billion by 1960; instead it passed 4 billion by 1975 and stood at an estimated 8.6 billion by 2025 (Carey's postscript, revised for a later edition, updates its own figures past 1992). Carey pointedly notes that demographers' proposed "solutions" — war, plague, famine — echo both Wells's fiction and Hitler's Mein Kampf, and that the impulse to blame "irresponsibly procreative Indians and Chinese" for the crisis is the same self-exculpating logic that let early-century intellectuals blame "the masses" while excluding themselves from the category. This is where the book's whole argument about invented, self-serving categories of the human converges most sharply with legibility-as-machine-core: populations become manageable, and eliminable, only once they are rendered as aggregate, countable figures — a move Carey shows tipping, in Bogota's death squads hunting street children, from statistic into administrative extermination.

Television democratizes; theory re-erects the barrier. Carey credits television with giving ordinary audiences unprecedented access to "traditional culture" and note that contemporary poets (Larkin, Hughes, Heaney) abandoned Eliot's demand for difficulty. But he reads post-structuralism/"theory" — beginning with Derrida in the 1960s — as intellectuals reinventing exclusion once democratized media closed off modernism's old avenue. Theory's deliberate unintelligibility and its doctrine that biography and "human interest" are irrelevant to a text's meaning are shown to directly continue the aesthetics of Clive Bell and Ortega y Gasset, who dismissed interest in "the passion and pain of the man behind the poet" as the mark of the degenerate masses.

Barthes as the old snobbery in new dress. Roland Barthes's celebration of "aristocratic readers," his contempt for plastic as an unnatural bourgeois material, and his romanticization of wooden toys and the vanished craftsman are read by Carey as a direct descendant of the peasant/craftsman idealization and mass-contempt he has traced through the whole book — proof that "the old intellectual prejudices did not die out in 1939, and have not died out yet."

Notable passages

The opening Woolf diary entry and Carey's reading of it ("the scene is, of course, invented") set up the postscript's central move: showing that intellectual disgust manufactures its own object. The Heppenstall material — especially the genocide remark and the bonfire feud rewritten as murder fiction — supplies the darkest evidential anchor for the claim that this disgust could curdle into something pathological rather than merely literary. The population-statistics passages update Wells's and Ortega's demographic anxieties with real numbers and show the argument's stakes rising from rhetoric to policy (the Bogota death squads). The closing passages on "theory" and Barthes complete the book's argument by showing the intellectual defense mechanism evolving rather than disappearing once its original occasion (mass literacy, modernist difficulty) is undone by history.
