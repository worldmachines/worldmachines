The Tree of Life

Commons · Darwin's Dangerous Idea: Evolution and the Meanings of Life · updated 6 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Dennett formalizes the Tree of Life as a graph of every organism's birth-to-offspring time lines to argue that its two biggest forks (the eukaryotic and multicellular revolutions), the boundary of a species, and the moment of speciation itself all resist essentialist or real-time definition and can only be read off the whole pattern, retrospectively, at the right level of oversimplification.

Dennett opens by making the Tree of Life literal: a graph in which every organism gets a time line from birth to death, with offspring lines branching off it, so that the entire history of life is one connected fan-out. He uses this formal object to work through three linked puzzles: what the tree's overall shape looks like at its two largest transitions (the origin of eukaryotes and of multicellular organisms), why "species" cannot be given an essentialist definition even though sexual reproduction gives it a real (if fuzzy) boundary, and why speciation — like the identity of Mitochondrial Eve — can only be recognized after the fact, never at the moment it occurs. He closes by defending deliberately oversimplified, level-appropriate explanations against charges of bad reductionist science.

Themes

The Tree is a formal graph, not just a metaphor, and time is not the same axis as Design. Every organism's time line either branches (has offspring) or doesn't; the whole tree, if drawn right-side-up, would look like a palm tree with a fuzzy, still-unexplored root. Dennett is careful to separate the tree's vertical axis (time, later = higher) from the vertical axis of Design Space introduced in the previous chapter (more designed = higher) — the two must not be conflated, and whether more-designed forms could precede less-designed ones is left as an open question for later chapters. The tree's two biggest forks to date are the eukaryotic revolution (prokaryotes joining into nucleated cells with organelles, ~1.4 billion years ago) and the multicellular revolution (~700 million years ago), after which the familiar fan-out into plants, animals, and fungi accelerates.

Species have no essences — only branches and pragmatic boundaries. Using a thought experiment of color-coding one individual ("Lulu") and all her red-inked descendants and ancestors, Dennett shows that a species just is a connected branch of the tree, bounded only where interbreeding stops — there is no "secret fact," no joint nature was carved at, that settles borderline cases where both daughter branches diverge simultaneously. He extends Darwin's own island/peninsula analogy: asking where a variety becomes a species is like asking when a peninsula becomes an island once you dig a canal — a definition-mongering question with no principled answer, only nominal, pragmatic ones.

Speciation, like Mitochondrial Eve's crown, can only be known in retrospect. Because reproductive isolation is a global, historical property and not a local one, you cannot detect a speciation event while it is happening, even in principle — only later, once its lineage's fate is known, can it be "retrospectively crowned." Dennett makes the same point with Mitochondrial Eve (the most recent common maternal ancestor of everyone alive), who was unremarkable in her own time and would be dethroned or reconfirmed by later population bottlenecks, and with the observation that jokes have no single identifiable author, only a distributed lineage of tellers. This resonates strongly with history-machine — a paradigm that "assembles silently and distributedly from independent components until it lurches into visibility," explicitly opposed to "great men" historiography. Dennett's speciation events and joke-authors are that same authorless, silent-assembly structure, just instantiated in biology and folklore rather than institutions; the fit is not perfect (Dennett's cases are about individual lineages, not civilizational infrastructure), but the underlying epistemic shape — no witness at the time, crowned only by later consequences — is the same move.

Good explanation means finding the right level of deliberate oversimplification. Dennett defends "bean-bag genetics," Newtonian orbital mechanics, and unrealistic neural-net models (against Crick's objection that they are "terrible science") on the grounds that some patterns — traffic jams, a calculator's right or wrong answers, a giraffe's long neck — are simply invisible at the micro level and only explicable by rising to the level where the pattern actually lives. This sets up the book's next move: any acceptable explanation of the Tree's patterns must be contrastive, comparing what happened against what else was possible, which requires a theory of biological possibility (Design Space, and later the "Library of Mendel").

Notable passages

The opening definition of the Tree of Life as a graph of time lines and offspring (c1) sets the ground rules for everything that follows. The eukaryotic-revolution passage (c2) marks the first of the tree's two biggest forks and ties it explicitly back to Design Space. The Lulu color-coding passage (c3) is Dennett's clearest statement that species boundaries are pragmatic, not essential — "there are no secret facts... which choice carves nature at the joints." The retrospective-crowning passage (c4) states the core epistemic claim about speciation directly: it is a property an event has only in virtue of what happens long afterward, never locally or at the time. The joke-authorship passage (c6) restates that same claim through a folklore analogy — jokes, like species, have no locatable author, only a distributed, silently-assembling lineage — which is the passage most worth reading against history-machine. Finally, the giraffe-necks passage (c5) is Dennett's compact demonstration of contrastive, level-appropriate explanation, the method the rest of the chapter has been building toward.
