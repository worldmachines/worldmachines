The Power of Adaptationist Thinking: From the Intentional Stance to the Leibnizian Paradigm

Commons · Darwin's Dangerous Idea: Evolution and the Meanings of Life · updated 6 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Dennett grounds adaptationist reasoning in the intentional stance's reverse-engineering method, then defends it against Gould and Lewontin's Panglossian caricature by proposing the fairer label 'the Leibnizian Paradigm.'

Chapter 9 opens with Dennett's case that reverse engineering — reading an artifact's or organism's design by assuming it is good — is the central method of both engineering history and evolutionary biology. He walks through artifact hermeneutics (the Antikythera mechanism, cathedral vault-supports, obsolete machines repurposed for new ends) and biological reverse engineering (archaeopteryx claws, color vision, endorphins, genomic imprinting) to argue that assuming optimality is not a dispensable heuristic but the load-bearing inference behind most discoveries in the life sciences. The chapter then turns to defend this style of reasoning, "adaptationism," against its most famous critique — Gould and Lewontin's "Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm" — arguing that the critique caricatures adaptationism the way Voltaire's Dr. Pangloss caricatures Leibniz, and proposing a fairer name for the real, disciplined version of the practice: the Leibnizian Paradigm.

Themes

  • Three stances for reading design. Dennett distinguishes the physical stance (predicting behavior from physics), the design stance (predicting behavior from what something is built to do, as with a VCR user who need not know its circuitry), and the intentional stance (imputing reasons to a designer, used in reverse engineering another manufacturer's product). This triad is the chapter's engine: everything that follows is an application of the intentional stance to progressively stranger cases.

  • Reverse engineering artifacts. The Antikythera mechanism is identified as an orrery because that reading makes it a good orrery; Viollet-le-Duc's and Fitchen's competing theories of the cathedral-building cerce are adjudicated the same way — the better hypothesis is the one that makes the artifact's design make sense. "It counts against the hypothesis that something is a cherry-pitter... if it would have been a demonstrably inferior cherry-pitter."

  • Excess functionality and repurposed artifacts. Sadirons become bookends, mainframes become boat anchors, and dedicated word processors run on full-power CPUs doing a fraction of what they could — evidence that current function and original design can drift apart by historical accident even while both remain legible through the same interpretive method. This is the same move Mother Nature makes with vestigial or repurposed structures, and it resonates with the World Machines decay-sequence — infrastructure surviving past its original function by being retained for a new (or merely residual) one — though Dennett's cases are opportunistic human repurposing in a single generation, not the slow civilizational fade the decay-sequence describes.

  • The same method applied to biology. Archaeopteryx's claws, reverse-engineered as adaptations for perching rather than running, support the inference that the creature (or its ancestors) flew. Von Frisch's proof of color vision in bees, the discovery of endorphins from the existence of morphine receptors, and Haig and Westoby's prediction of genomic imprinting (via the IGF-II/type-2-receptor story) are all presented as cases where assuming Mother Nature is a good designer let scientists find things nobody was looking for.

  • Climbing levels of abstraction. The Game-of-Life thought experiment — a chess-playing Turing machine built from gliders and eaters — shows the same interpretive method compressing computation: from tracking individual pixels, to gliders and eaters, to a Turing-machine tape, to chess moves and reasons. This ladder of interpretive levels resonates strongly with the Prime Radiant's hierarchical magnification — reading real patterns out of noisy substrate by choosing the right level of description — though Dennett's ladder is about prediction cost, not about a living, editable historical model.

  • Adaptationism as non-optional. Dennett adopts Lewontin's own definition of adaptationism (assuming traits are optimal solutions to environmental problems) and argues it cannot be dropped without losing biology's standard argument for evolution itself (homology) and much of biochemistry and medicine besides.

  • From Pangloss to Leibniz: renaming the paradigm. Gould and Lewontin dubbed adaptationism's excesses the "Panglossian Paradigm," after Voltaire's caricature of Leibniz's "best of all possible worlds." Dennett's countermove: since Pangloss is an unfair parody of Leibniz, and Gould and Lewontin's article is (he argues) an equally unfair parody of adaptationism, the honest, defensible version of the practice deserves its own, fairer name — the Leibnizian Paradigm. He traces the term "Panglossian" through Haldane, Maynard Smith, and Bateson to show it was already a loaded weapon before Gould and Lewontin picked it up, and recounts the 1983 Behavioral and Brain Sciences debate (including Fodor's claim that the Spandrels paper had shown adaptationism "completely bankrupt") as evidence of how badly the critique was misread.

  • Even the critics can't escape optimality. Eldredge's and Ghiselin's attempts to downplay adaptationist assumptions in their own preferred examples (functional morphology of horseshoe crabs; "what has happened" instead of "what is good") are shown to depend covertly on exactly the optimality premises they claim to reject — you cannot even state a homology, Dennett argues, without the intentional stance.

  • Telling good adaptationism (Leibniz) from bad (Pangloss). The honey guide bird's partnership with human honey-hunters is a Just So Story that held up under testing; the aquatic ape theory (Elaine Morgan) is treated sympathetically as an equally serious, if unconfirmed, story that establishment savanna narratives are no better evidenced against. George Williams' three rules of thumb — don't invoke adaptation where physics, general developmental constraints, or by-product effects already explain a trait — give a working discipline, but Dennett shows each rule can be legitimately overridden (bright maple-leaf color, originally pure chemistry, becomes a real adaptation once human tourism creates a selection pressure for it).

  • Open questions and the "story-shifting" charge. The leatherback turtle's purposeless-seeming extra eggs and the cicadas' prime-numbered life cycles (Gould's own favored Just So Story) show adaptationist inquiry as open-ended rather than dogmatic. Dennett rebuts Gould and Lewontin's charge that adaptationists just keep swapping stories (the Eskimo-face example) by arguing that a paradigm which keeps generating real research — unlike the rival "Dialectical Biology" that never materialized — is doing exactly what good science should.

Notable passages

The chapter opens by defining the physical/design/intentional stances through the VCR example, ending on the claim that reverse engineers "avail themselves not only of the physical stance, but also of...the intentional stance" to reconstruct a designer's reasoning — the definitional passage the rest of the chapter builds on. Midway through, Dennett states his target thesis baldly: adaptationism is "defined by one of its most eminent critics" (Lewontin) as assuming traits are optimal solutions, and to abandon it would mean "the collapse of modern biochemistry and all the life sciences and medicine" — the strongest form of his non-optionality claim. The chapter's title move comes in the passage proposing that, just as Pangloss unfairly caricatures Leibniz, fair-minded adaptationism deserves to be called "the Leibnizian Paradigm" rather than inherit Gould and Lewontin's pejorative. The Game-of-Life passage on climbing from pixels to gliders to Turing-machine tape to chess moves is the clearest illustration of the compression that adopting a higher stance buys. Williams' three rules of thumb for restrained adaptationism, and the maple-leaf passage showing how a rule can be legitimately overridden once a new selection pressure appears, together give the chapter's answer to "how do you tell good adaptationism from bad?"
