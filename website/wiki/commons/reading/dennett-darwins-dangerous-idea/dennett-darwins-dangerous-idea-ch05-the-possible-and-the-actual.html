The Possible and the Actual

Commons · Darwin's Dangerous Idea: Evolution and the Meanings of Life · updated 6 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Dennett builds a naturalized theory of biological possibility by nesting four grades of possibility (logical, physical, biological, historical) and replacing the vague, all-or-nothing question of what is biologically possible with a measurable notion of accessibility inside an imagined "Library of Mendel" — a Borges-style combinatorial space of every conceivable genome — arguing that many apparent laws of biology are really just contingent, historically locked-in restrictions on what that search process can reach.

Chapter 5 sorts possibility into four nested grades — logical, physical, biological, historical — then repurposes Borges's Library of Babel as a "Library of Mendel," the combinatorial space of every possible genome, to replace the all-or-nothing question "is this biologically possible?" with the measurable question "how accessible is it from here?" — a move that lets Dennett treat apparent laws of biology (why there are no horned birds, why Dollo's Law holds) as possibly nothing more than locked-in historical accidents, on the model of the QWERTY keyboard.

Themes

Possibility comes in nested grades, and every boundary between them is embattled. Logical possibility is mere non-contradiction (Superman is logically possible; "Duperman," who moves faster than light without moving at all, is not). Physical possibility adds the constraints of physical law (Superman is not physically possible). Biological possibility adds development, metabolism, and viability (a full-sized flying horse may be biologically impossible even though physically permitted). Historical possibility adds which past opportunities were actually taken up (six-limbed mammals may have been foreclosed the moment four-limbed tetrapod ancestors were selected for). Dennett rejects "actualism" (only the actual was ever possible) but shows, via Quine's fat-man-in-the-doorway puzzle, that ordinary intuitions about these grades dissolve under scrutiny — motivating a more disciplined replacement.

Borges's Library of Babel, repurposed as the Library of Mendel. All the 500-page books printable from 100 characters form a Library that is finite but so large that ordinary hyperbole ("astronomically large," "a needle in a haystack") fails; Dennett coins "Vast" and "Vanishingly" as technical terms for quantities that dwarf any actual physical quantity while remaining strictly finite. Restricting the alphabet to A/C/G/T and book length to genome length yields the Library of Mendel — the space of all possible DNA sequences, of which only a vanishing fraction correspond to viable organisms. This is Dennett's naturalized gloss on Dawkins's claim that there are far more ways of being dead than alive.

A genome is a recipe, not a blueprint, and it presupposes a reader. Just as a musical score means nothing to Martians without instruments and performers, DNA specifies nothing without an elaborate reader-constructor — amino acids, cellular machinery, a developmental environment — so "complete" dinosaur DNA without a dinosaur-DNA-reader (as in Jurassic Park) cannot yield a dinosaur. This grounds the defense of "gene for X" talk: a gene for reading is identifiable only via a gene for dyslexia, and only relative to an environment that includes literacy education — the same gene would mean something else, or nothing, in a pre-literate environment. Genotype-to-phenotype mapping is also non-linear: a minimal genetic change can produce a large phenotypic jump, so intermediate phenotypes (thirty-centimeter tusks between working ten- and forty-centimeter designs) may simply be inaccessible even though easy to describe.

Biological possibility redefined as accessibility, not an all-or-nothing category. Dennett's proposal: x is biologically possible iff x instantiates a genome (or a phenotypic feature of one) accessible from some stipulated starting point in the Library of Mendel, given some means of "travel" (natural selection, breeding, genetic engineering). This makes possibility a matter of degree and distance rather than binary — commensurable with probability — and defers the question of true "laws of biology" (versus laws of physics/logic plus historically local restrictions) by putting the burden of proof on anyone who claims one.

QWERTY as the model for a "law" that is really just locked-in history. Borrowing Seymour Papert's term, Dennett argues that restrictions which look like universal biological laws may instead be temporally and spatially local path-dependencies — like the QWERTY keyboard, adopted to solve a since-irrelevant key-jamming problem and then locked in by accumulated vested interest, with no rational explanation left, only a historical one.

Notable passages

The Library of Babel/Mendel section pauses to mint vocabulary — "We need some terms for the quantities involved" — because no existing hyperbole is adequate to a space that is finite but incomprehensibly larger than any physical quantity; "Vast" and "Vanishingly" recur as load-bearing technical terms through the rest of the book's argument about design space.

The Beethoven's-score analogy is Dennett's cleanest statement of why DNA cannot be read in isolation — a recipe needs a reader-constructor, which is why "complete" dinosaur DNA in Jurassic Park is inert without a dinosaur ovary to read it.

Dawkins's dyslexia example, quoted in full, is doing real argumentative work: it defends gene-talk against the charge of naive genetic determinism by showing that a gene's phenotypic effect — and even its identity as "the gene for X" — is always relative to an environment.

The QWERTY passage is the chapter's payload: a technology "introduced in response to a problem" that outlives its justification and calcifies into apparent necessity purely through accumulated switching costs. This resonates with history-machine's account of a paradigm assembling silently and distributedly (millions of individual typists' habits, not a single deciding authority) until it becomes unquestioned infrastructure, and with the decay-sequence's move away from an original Functional rationale — though Dennett's QWERTY never fully decays to Ornamental/Invisible, since the constraint stays functionally binding even after its reason is gone, which is precisely his point about horned birds and Dollo's Law.

The closing naturalized-possibility definition converts a metaphysical either/or into a matter of relative accessibility from a stipulated starting point — a move that faintly echoes Configurancy's rejection of teleology, though Rao's Configurancy is a relational co-emergence of agents and worlds while Dennett's design-space accessibility is a blind, non-relational combinatorics with no agency at either end.
