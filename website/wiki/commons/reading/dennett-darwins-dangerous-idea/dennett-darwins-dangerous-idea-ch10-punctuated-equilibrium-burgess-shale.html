Gould's Two Revolutions That Weren't: Punctuated Equilibrium and Radical Contingency

Commons · Darwin's Dangerous Idea: Evolution and the Meanings of Life · updated 6 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Dennett argues that both of Stephen Jay Gould's self-styled revolutions against orthodox neo-Darwinism — punctuated equilibrium and the Burgess Shale's 'radical contingency' — collapse back into the modern synthesis once their rhetoric is stripped away, revealing Gould as a covert hunter for skyhooks rather than a discoverer of new cranes.

This unit covers the second half of Dennett's chapter 10, working through Stephen Jay Gould's two most famous challenges to orthodox neo-Darwinism — punctuated equilibrium (co-developed with Niles Eldredge) and the 'radical contingency' thesis drawn from the Burgess Shale fossils in Wonderful Life — and in both cases finds the revolutionary rhetoric dissolving into claims the modern synthesis already accommodates. Dennett's verdict is that Gould keeps discovering cranes (real, mechanistic, if sometimes underappreciated evolutionary processes) while wishing they were skyhooks (a source of freedom or contingency exempt from mindless algorithmic process), and that this wish is best explained by a mix of political and quasi-religious motivation rather than by the biology itself.

Themes

Punctuated equilibrium is a claim about tempo, not mechanism. Eldredge and Gould's 1972 picture — long stasis punctuated by brief, 'geologically instantaneous' bursts of change — looks revolutionary only because of a scale illusion: zoom in far enough on any evolutionary ramp and it becomes a staircase, and zoom in far enough on any 'instantaneous' punctuation and it resolves into an ordinary gradual transition stretched over a thousand years. Dennett shows the real target was never gradualism itself but 'constant speedism,' a position he argues neither Darwin nor orthodox Darwinism actually held.

The hopeful-monster flirtation and its retreat. Gould's position shifted over time: first a conservative correction about what the fossil record should show, then (around 1980) a flirtation with Goldschmidt's saltationism and 'hopeful monsters' — macromutations with a 'creative role' outside selection — and finally a retreat to the much tamer claim that small genetic changes can produce large developmental effects, which no orthodox Darwinian denies. Dennett reads the 1980 moment as Gould reaching for a real skyhook and then backing away once challenged.

Species selection is a crane, not a skyhook. Gould's 'higher-level sorting' (clade or species selection) relocates evolutionary trends to the level of whole species living, speciating, and going extinct — a bait-and-switch move Dennett finds genuinely interesting. But he insists it is a level shift, analogous to explaining software behavior without denying the underlying hardware: new lineages still arise by ordinary gradual micromutation, so species selection builds on gradualism rather than refuting it.

'Replay the tape of life' and the ambiguity of "we." Wonderful Life's central claim — that rewinding and replaying evolutionary history would probably never reproduce anything like us — depends entirely on what "us" means. Dennett walks the reader through a sliding scale from maximally specific (Gould and Dennett personally) to maximally general (any land vertebrate), and argues Gould's intended middle ground (intelligent, culture-creating beings) is an interesting but wholly unsupported hypothesis; Wonderful Life offers no actual evidence for it.

Convergent evolution undercuts contingency. The kiwi — a flightless New Zealand bird that independently re-evolved a suite of mammalian traits in the absence of mammal competitors — is Dennett's (borrowing from Conway Morris) key counter-example: if lineages reliably converge on the same 'Good Tricks' in Design Space regardless of which branch survives, then contingency about which phylum happens to prosper does not translate into contingency about what kind of design eventually results.

Diagnosis: skyhooks, not cranes. Dennett closes with a speculative psychological reading: Gould's taste for revolutionary rupture over gradual reform echoes his Marxism, and his insistence on radical contingency echoes a yearning (which Dennett frames as quasi-religious, in the lineage of Locke's mind-first cosmos) to preserve human freedom and specialness against a 'merely algorithmic' account of life — a project Dennett likens to Gould inverting Nietzsche's eternal recurrence into its photographic negative.

Notable passages

Gould's own gloss on tempo-and-scale is the cleanest statement of why punctuation is not really instantaneous: "An isolated population may take a thousand years to speciate... in a lifetime of species that often live for several million years in stasis" — a thousand years is a blink geologically, but an eternity biologically.

The Goldschmidt episode is captured in the passage naming the doctrine directly: Goldschmidt's catchphrase "hopeful monsters" and the claim that "such leaps were necessary for speciation to occur" — the notorious, and ultimately disowned, saltationist reading of Gould's early work.

Dennett's verdict on species selection states the crane/skyhook framework explicitly: species selection "is no skyhook," because new lineages still arise by ordinary micromutation, so "Gould may have helped discover a new crane" even though Dennett suspects "he has all along been hoping for skyhooks, not cranes."

Wonderful Life's thesis is stated in Dennett's own paraphrase of its central conclusion — that "if the tape of life were rewound and played again and again, chances are mighty slim that we would ever appear again" — which he then spends the rest of the section unpacking and, ultimately, rejecting.

The kiwi passage delivers the strongest counter-evidence to contingency: a bird that "pretends it's a mammal" through pure convergence, which Conway Morris (quoted by Dennett) calls "the fatal weakness in his case for contingency."

The chapter's closing move — turning "Nietzsche upside down" on eternal recurrence, ending with "a little bit of Chance, a little bit of Ever. That's Darwin's dangerous idea, like it or not" — is Dennett's own rhetorical flourish tying the chapter title's baseball pun (Tinker to Evers to Chance) back to the book's master theme.

World Machines resonance

The tempo debate rhymes, loosely, with WMP's history-machine concept: a history-machine assembles silently and distributedly until it "lurches into visibility," much as Dennett's punctuated equilibrium is really gradual change that only looks sudden at low resolution — both frames deny that apparent suddenness implies a special, non-incremental cause. Gould's contingency thesis, by contrast, reads as closer to a "great men" (or "great contingent events") historiography than to a history-machine account, which sharpens rather than dissolves the analogy: Dennett's algorithmic, crane-built alternative is structurally the history-machine position applied to biology. The punctuation events themselves — brief, disruptive transitions between long stable states — also echo the world-machines Dawn/Day/Dusk cycle and its transitional gramsci-gap, though the fit is partial: world-machine transitions are read as periods when legitimacy has died but nothing has yet replaced it, whereas Dennett's punctuations are, on his account, not really discontinuities at all once properly time-scaled.
