Redesigning Morality

Commons · Darwin's Dangerous Idea: Evolution and the Meanings of Life · updated 6 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Dennett argues that no ethical theory, utilitarian or Kantian, has ever produced a computable decision procedure, and that real moral reasoning is instead Herbert Simon's "satisficing" all the way down, so a workable ethics must be redesigned as situational, a-rational "conversation-stoppers" rather than a completed algorithm.

Chapter 17 turns the book's recurring critique of the "great design process" onto ethical theory itself: since all actual exploration of Design Space is myopic and time-pressured, no system of ethics — utilitarian or Kantian — could ever be a computationally tractable decision procedure, and Dennett spends the chapter showing why, staging a thought experiment ("Judging the Competition") to expose real moral reasoning as an instance of Herbert Simon's "satisficing," before sketching what a redesigned, honestly practical ethics would have to look like: not a proof but a "Moral First Aid Manual" of rule-like conversation-stoppers that terminate reflection well enough, most of the time, without ever being fully rationally grounded.

Themes

No algorithm for the good. Mill's own metaphor — utilitarianism as a moral Nautical Almanac, precomputed and ready for the rational sailor — is turned against him: astronomy's domain is closed and orderly enough to tabulate, ethics's is not. Bentham's "felicific calculus" and its mnemonic jingle are the reductio of this hope; Mill's retreat to "in principle, not in practice" concedes the whole game without admitting it. Kantians fare no better — Maxim-following just relocates the same intractability to the prior step of framing the maxim.

The Three Mile Island Effect. Consequentialism's computational problem isn't merely intractable, it's underdetermined at the root: Dennett shows we cannot even fix the sign (positive or negative, all things considered) of a value like the Three Mile Island meltdown, let alone its magnitude. The chess analogy sharpens this — heuristic search-pruning and a "principle of quiescence" manage real-time pressure in a bounded game with a checkmate at the end; life has no such terminal state to retrograde-analyze from.

Satisficing as the universal design signature. The "Judging the Competition" thought experiment (250,000 fellowship applicants, an impossible deadline) forces a mixed strategy of cut rules, delegated lottery, and committee review — Herbert Simon's satisficing (1957, 1959). Dennett's key move is to show this pattern recurs fractally: in the department meeting, in a single person's momentary decision whether to keep arguing, in "swift, unconscious processes" of perception, and, reaching back further, in the "design decisions" natural selection settled for when building the deciding organism in the first place. This is the chapter's closest continuation of the book's cranes-not-skyhooks argument, applied now to moral cognition rather than biological form.

Optimality claims evaporate, and so does any Archimedean point. Having shown satisficing is basic, Dennett resists the further, "inviting" claim that it is therefore best — there is no single stable perspective (no view from which one feature is fixed and all others malleable) from which to certify any design, moral or cognitive, as optimal. The Prisoner's Dilemma literature's slippery talk of "ideal rationality" is diagnosed as a version of the same Panglossian fallacy the book has been chasing since its early chapters, now relocated to game theory.

Rules as conversation-stoppers, not proofs. Since any demand for justification threatens infinite regress, a workable design needs brute, a-rational terminators: "But that would be murder"; rights-talk as Bentham's "nonsense upon stilts" reread as good nonsense, valuable precisely because it stands "on stilts" high enough to end debate. "Rule worship" is thereby partly rehabilitated — not because any rule is provably best, but because having rules works and not having them doesn't work at all, provided the worship stops short of literalism.

The fragility of rule-following. Hofstadter's "Wolf's Dilemma" shows how a single flicker of doubt about others' rationality can unravel an "obvious" cooperative solution through "reverberant doubt" — the sharper the reasoners, the more exposed to the instability, not less. This caps Dennett's case that no amount of further reflection on "ideal rationality" makes the underlying design problem go away; it can only be lived with, the way the department "drops it" after a decision is made.

Notable passages

  • The Nautical Almanac paragraph, where Mill's own analogy for utilitarian practicality is shown to presuppose a closed, tabulable domain ethics doesn't have — the clearest statement of why moral reasoning resists full legibility. Read against World Machines' legibility-as-machine-core, Bentham's felicific calculus is exactly a bid to make moral judgment legible — measurable, ordered, calculable like a sailor's position at sea — and Dennett's argument is that this legibility project fails not from lack of effort but in principle, since no procedure can compute even the sign of an outcome's value.

  • The Three Mile Island Effect passage, the chapter's sharpest impossibility result against consequentialism, paired with the chess/quiescence analogy to show real life has no terminal position to reason backward from.

  • The "Judging the Competition" fellowship problem, the thought experiment the rest of the chapter's analysis is built on — all the needed information is "at your fingertips," yet considering all of it is still physically impossible.

  • The satisficing definition ("Notice how the pattern repeats itself, rather like a fractal curve"), naming Herbert Simon's concept as the chapter's master pattern, self-similar from natural selection down to a single unspoken micro-decision.

  • The conversation-stoppers passage and its rereading of Bentham's "nonsense upon stilts," Dennett's positive proposal for what a redesigned ethics actually needs instead of a proof. The chapter's closing concession that there might be several different Moral First Aid Manuals, each pitched to a different audience, pulls against the single universal ambitions both Bentham and Kant shared and toward something closer to World Machines' divergence-machine — a plurality of locally effective, non-convergent heuristics rather than one legible system — though Dennett's plurality is audience-relative rhetorical strategy, not divergence's proliferation of genuinely incompatible ontologies, so the rhyme is partial.

  • Hofstadter's Wolf's Dilemma, a vivid, self-contained illustration of "reverberant doubt" unraveling an obvious cooperative equilibrium purely through the passage of time and the possibility of others' doubt.
