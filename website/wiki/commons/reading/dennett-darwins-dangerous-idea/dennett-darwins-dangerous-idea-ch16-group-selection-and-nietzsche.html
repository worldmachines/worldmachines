Two Just So Stories of Morality: Hobbes's Social Contract and Nietzsche's Genealogy

Commons · Darwin's Dangerous Idea: Evolution and the Meanings of Life · updated 6 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Dennett reads Hobbes's social-contract account and Nietzsche's Genealogy of Morals as rival Darwinian "Just So Stories" of how an amoral state of nature gave way to morality, using the genes-eye puzzle of multicellular cooperation — solved by Brian Skyrms's "Darwinian Veil of Ignorance" — to explain why a body's own cells do not rebel, and credits Nietzsche with an early, independent statement of the origin/function distinction Dennett calls exaptation, against the Social Darwinists' genetic fallacy.

This unit is the first two sections of Chapter 16, "On the Origin of Morality." Section 1, "E Pluribus Unum?", reads Hobbes's Leviathan as the first Darwinian "Just So Story" — a rational reconstruction, not a history, of how an amoral state of nature gave way to a social contract and, with it, to morality — and then uses the genes-eye puzzle of multicellular cooperation (via Brian Skyrms's "Darwinian Veil of Ignorance") to ask why a body's own cells cooperate where genuinely independent lineages would defect. Section 2, "Friedrich Nietzsche's Just So Stories," turns to Nietzsche's Genealogy of Morals (1887) as a second, darker sociobiological genealogy, contrasts it with the Social Darwinists' naive "natural therefore right" reasoning, and credits Nietzsche with an early, independent statement of what Dennett (following Gould) calls exaptation.

Themes

Hobbes as the first sociobiologist. Dennett opens with the Leviathan frontispiece image of the commonwealth as an "Artificial Man," and argues Hobbes saw "the fundamental Darwinian task" two centuries before Darwin: explaining, without a fossil record, how a state of pure amoral competition (good spears, good suppers, but no good or bad persons) gave way, via a single "mutation" of strategy — cooperation for mutual benefit — to a social contract, and with it, for the first time, right and wrong. Dennett is explicit that this is a rational reconstruction "of sorts," not real history, and no worse for it.

Hobbes conflated with Rawls, then distinguished. Dennett traces the same move through Rousseau and Locke to John Rawls's Theory of Justice, whose "veil of ignorance" thought experiment he treats as Hobbes's true descendant — but insists on a key difference: Hobbes claimed to describe something that actually happened, while Rawls's is a purely normative thought experiment about what would be right. Hobbes, "greedy reductionist that he was," tried to do both jobs (explain morality's origin and justify its norms) at once, which Dennett flags as overreach even while admiring the attempt.

Group solidarity has to be engineered, not assumed. Taking the genes-eye view (via David Sloan Wilson and Elliott Sober), Dennett poses the real puzzle beneath the Hobbesian metaphor: if genes are "selfish," why don't the genes in a thumb cell ever rebel against the genes in a neighboring finger? The answer turns on Weismann's germ-line/soma-line distinction: somatic cells, being reproductive dead ends, have nothing to gain from defection, while true symbionts (which retain their own lineage and reproductive stake) retain a genuine "choice" to cooperate or not. Group rationality, Dennett stresses, is never free — it "has to be achieved," a "major design task," whether among prokaryotes or among human ancestors.

The Darwinian Veil of Ignorance. Philosopher Brian Skyrms's mechanism explains why: meiosis blindly and randomly assigns each gene-copy to the germ line or the soma before any gene could "know" its own reproductive fate, so there is nothing for a somatic-line gene to gain by selfish competition. This is explicitly parallel to Rawls's veil, with one difference the chapter refuses to let slide: the resulting "harmony and coordination" of somatic cells is also, in Dennett's words, "the unquestioning obedience of zealots or zombies" — not a model to emulate, since humans, unlike our own cells, are "guided missiles" always free to defect, switch allegiance, or reconsider.

Nietzsche as the second great sociobiologist. Unlike Hobbes, Nietzsche wrote after Darwin but, per Dennett, probably never read him directly — his contempt was aimed at Herbert Spencer and the "English" Social Darwinists, not at Darwin himself. Dennett is careful to separate the Social Darwinists' bad argument (is → ought: because nature favors the strong, the strong ought to dominate) from Nietzsche's actual target, which was their historical naivete — their Panglossian faith that reason smoothly bridges the state of nature and morality.

Nietzsche's two-phase genealogy. Where Hobbes told one origin story, Nietzsche's Genealogy of Morals (told, confusingly, out of order) tells two: first, in the Second Essay, how a promise-keeping, debt-remembering animal had to be bred into existence through literal, ancestral cruelty (a possible case, Dennett suggests, of the Baldwin Effect); second, in the First Essay, a later "transvaluation of values," the "slave revolt in morality," in which priests trick the strong into adopting inverted values, turning the aristocrats' own cruelty back against themselves.

The genetic fallacy and exaptation. Dennett's strongest claim for Nietzsche is that, whatever his faults, he grasped — independently of Darwin and ahead of many later sociobiologists — that a trait's origin and its current use are separate questions ("the genetic fallacy" is inferring one from the other). Quoting Nietzsche's own line that a thing's origin and its "eventual utility... lie worlds apart," Dennett reads this as convergent with Darwin's own view (quoted from 1862) that organs are constantly repurposed, and explicitly equates it with Gould's "exaptation": all adaptations are exaptations, in cultural evolution as in biological. Nietzsche's residual "will to power" talk, by contrast, Dennett dismisses as skyhook hunger.

A partial resonance with WMP's non-teleological emergence. Dennett's insistence that cooperation must be achieved by a "mindless 'voting' of differential reproduction," never assumed as group rationality, rhymes with Configurancy's picture of agents and worlds co-emerging without a governing telos — though the fit is loose, since Configurancy describes agents actively relating, while the mechanism here (meiosis, the Darwinian Veil of Ignorance) operates one level below agency, on genes that have no perspective to co-emerge from at all. The somatic cells' fixed, non-negotiable "summum bonum" is also a limit case worth naming against Conatus: if conatus is a thing's own striving to persist and expand on its own behalf, the somatic line has had its conatus redirected wholesale into the body's from the moment of conception — a resonance with the concerns behind capture-resistance, though inverted, since here total "capture" by the whole is what a functioning organism requires, not what it must resist.

Notable passages

  • The Hobbes epigraph (Leviathan, 1651): the "Artificial Man"/Leviathan image that frames the whole chapter's reading of society as an engineered group-organism.
  • Dennett's compressed retelling of Hobbes's state-of-nature story, his clearest single statement of the "Just So Story" logic he then applies throughout the chapter.
  • The multicellular-cooperation puzzle (thumbs, fingers, an eagle's wings and gonads), which reframes group solidarity as something that must be explained, not assumed.
  • The introduction of Skyrms's "Darwinian Veil of Ignorance," the chapter's mechanistic answer (via meiosis and the germ/soma split) to why our own cells don't defect.
  • Nietzsche's own formulation, in the Second Essay, of "the paradoxical task" of breeding an animal capable of making promises — the question his Genealogy sets out to answer.
  • Nietzsche's origin/function distinction from the Second Essay, which Dennett treats as an independent, pre-Gould statement of exaptation and the strongest praise he offers Nietzsche in the chapter.
