Biology Is Engineering: Function, Fitness, and the Origins of Meaning

Commons · Darwin's Dangerous Idea: Evolution and the Meanings of Life · updated 6 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Dennett argues that engineering is not a demeaning metaphor but the literal, obligatory organizing principle of Darwinian biology, tracing how mindless molecular mechanisms — from Eigen's quasi-species dynamics to Monod's code-plus-environment solution to protein folding — give rise, by gradual and non-teleological steps, to function, error, and finally meaning itself.

Chapter 8 of Darwin's Dangerous Idea makes the positive case that engineering — long treated as intellectually second-class next to "pure" science — is in fact the deepest and most literal lens for understanding Darwinian design. The chapter moves through four movements: first, a defense of engineering's intellectual status against Aristotelian and Two-Cultures prejudice; second, an account of Manfred Eigen's "quasi-species" theory of molecular/viral evolution, which extends rather than overturns classical fitness-landscape Darwinism; third, Jacques Monod's solution to how a one-dimensional genetic sequence specifies a three-dimensional, functional protein shape — the code alone cannot do it, the environment must supply the rest; and fourth, an account of how function, error, and eventually meaning itself emerge from mindless molecular machinery, climaxing in a direct inversion of John Locke's Mind-first cosmology.

Themes

Engineering is the obligatory organizer of Darwinian thinking, not an optional metaphor. Dennett anticipates the reader's "Philistine, reductionist" objection to his chapter title and answers it by tracing engineering's low intellectual status back to Aristotle's secundum/contra naturam divide, then arguing that Darwin's algorithmic account of design collapses the distinction: if there is only one Design Space in which both bodies and minds are R-and-D products, the wall between engineering and "real" science (and between the natural and human sciences) cannot hold.

Evolution discovers reasons retroactively rather than pursuing them in advance — a strictly non-teleological explanatory schema. Eigen's quasi-species work shows that viral populations travel as a "cloud" of variants around a wild type that Dennett stresses is "really just an abstraction, like the Average Taxpayer" — there need be no individual that is the canonical genome. The same non-teleology governs why DNA is double-stranded or why organic molecules share a single chirality: these are not designed-in necessities but historical accidents ("frozen accidents," QWERTY phenomena) that, once they open up downstream opportunities, get "a retroactive endorsement" from the very success of the lineages that exploit them. This is a genuine, if partial, rhyme with Configurancy's picture of agents and worlds co-emerging through a non-teleological relational process — Dennett's version runs on molecules and populations rather than Rao's social actors, but the shared move is denying that a goal precedes the process that reaches it.

Function is jointly authored by code and environment; meaning cannot be read off a sequence in isolation. Monod's puzzle — how a 1-D amino-acid string can specify a 3-D functional shape whose "data content is richer" than the string itself — is resolved by noting that the missing information comes from the protein's environment at the moment of folding. Dennett generalizes this: DNA requires co-evolved "readers" it does not itself fully specify, and any functioning structure (a seagull's wing, a dishrag's bacteria) carries implicit information about the environment its function presupposes. The deconstructionist claim that "the reader brings something to the text" turns out to apply to DNA as much as to poetry.

Error is the precondition of meaning: "no taking without mistaking." Before any copying process could go wrong, nothing could be right or wrong either — there was no perspective from which error was discernible. The first copying mistake creates, for the first time, an objective distinction of better/worse, and every subsequent norm (including species boundaries, understood as competing error-correcting regimes) descends from that original error-making capacity. Dennett names this mechanism, half in jest, "Original Sin": unlike its Biblical namesake it "provides an explanation that makes sense." Tellingly, selection pressure pushes copying fidelity toward perfection but can never quite reach it — "if it did, evolution would grind to a halt" — which resonates, loosely, with WMP's legibility-as-machine-core/divergence-machine tension: total legibility (error-free replication) would foreclose exactly the variety that selection needs to act on, though here the register is molecular information rather than institutions or political economy.

Intentionality percolates up from below, inverting Locke's Mind-first cosmology. Locke's God is the "Unmeant Meaner," the ultimate source from which all aboutness descends. Darwin turns this upside down: the first "meaning" in nucleotide sequences is mere "semi-semantics" or "as if intentionality" (Searle's term), acquired gradually and diachronically over the deep history of Design, with Artificial Intelligence offering a synchronic parallel — a mind built from many semi-intentional, mindless parts. Dennett's punchline: your ancestors, and your own molecular machinery, are robots ("macros") all the way down, and this is not a problem for consciousness or meaning but their only possible ground.

Notable passages

The chapter's thesis statement (c1) stakes out the whole chapter's ambition against anticipated "reductionist" backlash. The quasi-species passage (c2) undercuts any essentialist notion of "the" genome — a strong instance of Dennett's recurring anti-essentialism. The DNA-reader passage (c3) extends the deconstructionist reader/text analogy to genetics, showing meaning as jointly constituted by code and environment (ending, characteristically, on the homely image of a dishrag's bacteria). The retroactive-endorsement passage (c4) gives the clearest statement of the chapter's non-teleological explanatory schema: reasons discovered after the fact, not pursued in advance. The Original Sin passage (c5) is the chapter's pivot from mere function to normativity — the birth of a right/wrong distinction from copying error. The final passage (c6) delivers the chapter's culminating claim, inverting Locke: intentionality comes from below, not from on high.
