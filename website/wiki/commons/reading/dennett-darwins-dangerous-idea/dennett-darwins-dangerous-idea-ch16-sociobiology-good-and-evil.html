Sociobiology: Good and Bad, Good and Evil

Commons · Darwin's Dangerous Idea: Evolution and the Meanings of Life · updated 6 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Dennett tries to separate sociobiology's two faces — careful science of nonhuman social behavior versus reckless megaphone claims about human nature — by insisting that in humans, unlike other species, adaptive plausibility never by itself licenses an inference to a genetic cause, since culture is always available as an alternative explanation.

Dennett surveys the sociobiology wars in order to clear ground for a naturalized ethics. He accepts Kitcher's diagnosis that the field has "two faces" — a careful, low-key science of nonhuman social behavior and a megaphone-amplified set of pronouncements about human nature — and argues the discredit earned by the second face has unfairly tarred the first. The chapter's spine is a methodological correction, borrowed from Gould and developed by Kitcher, that Dennett endorses without reservation: in humans, and only humans, the standard sociobiological move from "this trait is adaptive" to "this trait is genetically fixed" fails, because culture supplies a permanent rival explanation that has no analogue in nonhuman species. He illustrates the point with his own "Maze War" video-game anecdote, indicts E. O. Wilson for forgetting his own caveats about territoriality, and then holds up Cosmides and Tooby's cheater-detection account of the Wason selection task as a model of evolutionary psychology done right. The chapter closes on the "do ducks rape?" controversy as a test case for how much of sociobiology's bad reputation is deserved versus manufactured by its critics.

Themes

Two faces, one field. Kitcher's epigraph — animal sociobiology conducted with "eyes carefully focused, lips pursed judiciously," human sociobiology delivered through a megaphone — sets up Dennett's organizing complaint: legitimate, technically excellent work (Hamilton on kin selection, Trivers, Maynard Smith, the whole apparatus of Darwinian reverse-engineering applied to termites, elephant seals, and naked mole rats) gets discredited by association with a handful of overreaching claims about human nature. Dennett's verdict on the field as a whole: "some of this work is great, some is good, some is good but false, and some is bad — but none of it is evil."

Ethics can't wait for comfortable facts. Pinker's epigraph and Dennett's opening pages establish why this chapter has to precede the book's turn to ethics (chapter 17): if human beings are not perfectly equal, rational, and malleable, the Enlightenment assumptions of Equality and Perfectibility that many people think ethics depends on look shaky. Dennett's response is not to deny the biological facts but to insist that ethics has to be built on whatever the facts turn out to be, not on a wish about how the brain works — an application of Pinker's own point in the chapter's opening epigraph.

Kin selection and the naked mole rat: sociobiology at its best. Hamilton's kin-selection framework solved Darwin's old puzzle about insect eusociality; Richard Alexander then predicted, from first principles, that a mammal could in principle evolve the same sterile-caste arrangement — a prediction spectacularly confirmed when he learned, after giving the lecture, that his hypothetical beast already existed as the naked mole rat. Dennett offers this as the showcase case for what rigorous, non-megaphone sociobiology looks like.

Why "if adaptive, then genetic" breaks down for humans. The chapter's methodological core, credited to Gould and elaborated by Kitcher: normal Darwinian adaptationist reasoning infers a genetic story from evidence of adaptive design, but for humans there is always a second possible source of the same adaptive fit — culture — so the inference to genes is never automatic. Dennett generalizes the point even to nonhuman behavior that is an obvious "Good Trick": the more obviously excellent a move is, the weaker the inference that it must have been copied down through the genes rather than independently discovered. His own autobiographical illustration — backing into a corner during an early MIT video game and recognizing his own posture as that of a moray eel — makes the point vivid: no one needs a "moray-eel gene" to explain a strategy any sufficiently non-stupid agent would hit upon, any more than every human culture needs a "pointy-end-first gene" to explain why hunters throw spears point-first. E. O. Wilson is Dennett's cautionary example of forgetting his own rule: despite explicitly restricting genetic hypotheses to the "least rational" human behaviors, Wilson treats the near-universality of territoriality, and of similarities among early Egyptian, Mesopotamian, Indian, Chinese, and Mesoamerican civilizations, as evidence of shared genetic wiring, when ubiquity alone is equally consistent with independent cultural reinvention.

Good Tricks, forced moves, and reinventing the wheel. This is the same convergence problem that troubled Dennett's discussion of memetics in chapter 12 (cultural convergent evolution undermines inference to shared descent), now redeployed against naive human sociobiology: octopuses without culture still convergently discover Good Tricks through general intelligence rather than instinct, and humans do so even more readily through cultural transmission, so behavioral ubiquity is weak evidence for a dedicated gene. This convergence-without-common-cause pattern — order and uniformity arising from many independent local processes rather than one centralized lineage — is a loose but genuine echo of the World Machines idea of a history-machine (a paradigm assembling silently and distributedly from independent components until it lurches into visibility, as opposed to "great man" causation); the fit is partial, since Dennett's forced moves are about convergent engineering optima under similar problem constraints rather than a paradigm coalescing from distributed contributions, but both resist explaining widespread uniformity by appeal to a single common source.

Evolutionary psychology done right: cheater detection. Cosmides and Tooby's account of why the Wason selection task is hard in its abstract form but easy once reframed as a social-contract/cheater-detection problem is Dennett's model citation for good Darwinian psychology: a testable hypothesis, not a just-so story, that treats the mind as a collection of special-purpose "Darwinian algorithms" — a "Swiss-army knife" of gadgets rather than one general-purpose reasoner. Dennett uses this to restate his own view against the "Standard Social Science Model" (culture as an autonomous system fully insulated from biology): his "Only Slightly Nonstandard Social Science Model" has culture growing from a biological base but growing indefinitely away from it — an image consistent with his book-wide theme that human minds are cranes of unusual power, not skyhooks.

Do ducks rape? Naming, blame, and manufactured outrage. Dennett takes the vocabulary fight over sociobiological terms like "rape" (in ducks), "harem," "lesbian" gulls, and sexual "cannibalism" in spiders as his test case for the "evil" half of the chapter's title. He grants Fausto-Sterling's point that reusing morally loaded human terms for nonhuman behavior is "at least insensitive," but rejects her further move of holding sociobiologists responsible for how defense lawyers might misuse the science, calling the analogy to blaming Shakespeare scholars for stage portrayals of rape apt. His verdict: the terminology dispute generates "more heat than light" and the harshest charges against the field are largely unearned.

Notable passages

The chapter opens by borrowing Kitcher's "two faces" image to frame the whole inquiry, then earns its claim about legitimate sociobiology with the naked mole rat story — Alexander's armchair prediction of a eusocial mammal, confirmed after the fact by field biologists who told him his hypothetical beast already existed. The methodological heart of the chapter is the passage explaining Gould's point about why adaptive plausibility does not imply genetic causation in humans, which Dennett immediately dramatizes with his own Maze War anecdote about unwittingly reinventing "moray-eel behavior" at a computer terminal. The Cosmides-and-Tooby material on cheater detection and the "Swiss-army knife" mind is Dennett's positive case study, offered in deliberate contrast to Wilson's territoriality overreach. The chapter ends on the "do ducks rape?" episode, which crystallizes the good/bad/evil distinction of the chapter's title into a single concrete controversy.
