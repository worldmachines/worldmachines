The Meme's-Eye View: A Doubtful Science, an Essential Philosophy

Commons · Darwin's Dangerous Idea: Evolution and the Meanings of Life · updated 6 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Dennett argues that a rigorous science of memetics is unlikely, because cultural information has no uniform syntactic medium the way genes have DNA, but that the meme's-eye view remains philosophically indispensable for explaining how minds, selves, and autonomy are themselves products of meme colonization rather than its sovereign judges.

This unit closes chapter 11's case for memes by asking two questions in sequence: could memetics ever be a real science, and — whatever the answer — what does the meme's-eye view do to our philosophical picture of minds, selves, and free will? Dennett answers the first question pessimistically and the second one enthusiastically, then closes with capsule previews of chapters 12 and 13.

Themes

The gene/meme analogy breaks down exactly where a science would need it to hold. Genes have a double identity: a semantic one (what they are "about," e.g. making hemoglobin) and a syntactic one (their spelling in DNA), and the two align well enough that biologists can identify genes either way. Memes have only the semantic identity — there is no shared "brain language," so a meme is "primarily a semantic classification, not a syntactic classification." Dennett illustrates this with the homeobox gene for eyes (nearly identical in mice and fruit flies) versus the meme for bifocals, which is realized in wildly different brain-states in Benjamin Franklin and in a child today, yet is recognizably the same meme. Without syntactic identity, a science of cultural cladistics can't cleanly separate homology (shared descent) from analogy (convergent reinvention) — the Romeo and Juliet / West Side Story case shows how confidently we identify semantic sameness while having almost no principled way to rule out independent reinvention absent an author's own testimony.

Cultural transmission also violates the high-fidelity, purely-divergent topology that makes Darwinian description tractable. Where DNA copying is exquisitely faithful and lineages only ever split, minds routinely mutate memes at high, often directed rates (Pinker's point about the theory of relativity), and — following Gould — cultural lineages recombine ("anastomosis") in a way biological lineages never do. Dennett's grandson's unwitting mutation of a nursery rhyme ("Pop! goes the diesel") stands in for the ordinary, constant creativity that makes tracking meme descent close to hopeless in practice, even if descent-with-modification is still what's happening in principle.

Dan Sperber's objection — that abstract, intentional objects can't be causes — looks fatal to a science of memes, but Dennett turns it into the very rationale for one. What kills the dinner guest is a genetically-triggered protein cascade, not "the Mornay sauce recipe in the abstract"; only the concrete chain of public and mental representations is causally real. Dennett's reply: this is true of gene selection too (genes act only via indirect, statistical phenotypic effects), and abstraction from causal noise is exactly what lets science find patterns — the moth/bat camouflage prediction is the parallel case. Sperber's own "Law of the Epidemiology of Representations" (memorable representations survive oral transmission; hard ones don't) shows the abstract, substrate-neutral level paying off as a lever into facts about human memory.

Meme fitness and organism fitness can radically diverge — but not usually, and that non-randomness is the philosophical payoff. As with genes, a meme's staying power has no necessary connection to being good for its host: a suicidal meme can spread via well-publicized martyrdom, exactly as more virulent disease strains persist by keeping a host mobile long enough to transmit. This licenses a Newtonian-style shift in what counts as needing explanation — under the "normal view" we ask why an idea is rejected despite its truth or beauty; the meme's-eye view treats replicative success itself as the default fact needing no further gloss. Dennett insists the two perspectives are not usually opposed: the memes that flourish tend, by and large, to be good "by our standards" — but immediately adds the important twist that our standards (our summum bonum) are themselves memetic products, so this reassurance is not independent confirmation, it's closer to a system checking its own priors.

Human minds are themselves meme-built artifacts, which forces a redefinition of autonomy rather than its abandonment. "The haven all memes depend on reaching is the human mind, but a human mind is itself an artifact created when memes restructure a human brain in order to make it a better habitat for memes" — so the "independent mind" resisting alien memes is a myth, since prior meme infestations already constitute who "we" are. Dennett's answer to the resulting worry about free will and responsibility is the Viking-spacecraft analogy: autonomy just means the switch from remote to local self-control over goals installed at one's "birth," not freedom from having been programmed at all. The celibate priest (a meme complex biologically deleterious to fitness but often welcomed by its host) and the closing image of personal identity as shifting "coalitions of memes" — no one meme rules, governing coalitions do — cash this out at the level of a single life.

A loose, qualified WM resonance: the "normal view" Dennett attacks — that ideas spread because a discerning author or public correctly recognized their truth or beauty — is the individual-cognition analogue of "great men" historiography; the meme's-eye view instead has ideas assembling, mutating, and recombining across many minds with no single author owning the result (the acronym-as-meta-meme, the "Pop! goes the diesel" anecdote, nobody claiming "authorship of special creation"). That is the same shape as WMP's history-machine — a paradigm that "assembles silently and distributedly from independent components until it lurches into visibility" against a great-men narrative — though Dennett's unit works at the scale of single minds and generations, not centuries-long institutional infrastructure, so the rhyme is in the mechanism (undirected, distributed, unauthored assembly), not the scale. Similarly, the claim that a mind is itself an artifact continuously co-built by the memes it hosts, with personal identity as an evolving coalition rather than a sovereign self, rhymes with Configurancy's non-teleological relational co-emergence of agents and worlds — though again Dennett keeps this at the scale of one brain across one lifetime, not agents and worlds jointly emerging.

Notable passages

The Gould topology quote (c1) states the sharpest formal reason memetics resists cladistic science: biological lineages only ever split, while "transmission across lineages is, perhaps, the major source of cultural change" — anastomosis has no clean biological analogue.

The Romeo and Juliet / West Side Story passage (c2) is Dennett's cleanest demonstration that meme-identity is semantic, not syntactic — "the story, not the text; the characters and their personalities, not their names and speeches" — the very property that makes homology vs. analogy so hard to adjudicate in culture.

Sperber's causal objection (c3), quoted directly, is the strongest anti-memetics argument the unit engages, and Dennett's answer to it (that abstraction earns its scientific keep by ignoring causal noise) is the hinge on which the whole "could memetics be a science" question turns.

The suicidal-meme passage (c4), quoting Dawkins on martyrdom, is the cleanest statement that meme fitness and organism fitness can come apart — the premise the entire second half of the unit needs before it can ask what should ground our values instead.

The "haven all memes depend on reaching" passage (c5) is the unit's philosophical center of gravity: it is where Dennett commits to minds themselves being meme-built artifacts, which is what forces every subsequent claim about autonomy and responsibility.

The Viking-spacecraft passage (c6) supplies Dennett's positive redefinition of autonomy as local self-control over installed goals — the move that lets him say selves survive being "built" by memes without free will collapsing into illusion.
