Why Capital Shunned Industry, and How Goods Actually Moved by Road

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Industrial profit rates are historically almost unmeasurable and were structurally dwarfed by commercial profit, pushing capital away from production, while old-regime overland transport — dismissed as primitive — actually carried far more goods than inland waterways and stayed deliberately unrationalized because its fragmented, competitive structure suited the merchants who depended on it.

The unit opens by confronting the near-impossibility of measuring historical industrial profit rates, argues that this gap in the record tracks a real structural fact — commerce dwarfed and starved industry of capital — and sketches, via Jean-Claude Perrot's Caen studies and Werner Hoffmann's British data, a recurring "parabola" of industrial rise and fall; it then turns to transport, showing that overland carriage, though written off as backward, in fact carried the bulk of old-regime goods traffic, and that merchants kept it fragmented and competitive on purpose rather than rationalizing it into a capitalist enterprise.

Themes

Industrial profit is structurally unmeasurable, and structurally inferior to commercial profit. Braudel laments the absence of a documented series of profit rates comparable to the price-and-wage series historians already possess, and traces the gap to more than an archival accident: because commerce "commanding the market, was all-powerful," industrial gains were "regularly dwarfed by trading profits," so capital treated industry — like agriculture — as a trap to be avoided rather than entered. This is the market/anti-market distinction (braudel-three-storey-house) operating at the level of individual capitalist choice: the transparent, competitive market absorbs industrial producers, while capitalism proper positions itself above and outside production, extracting from circulation instead.

A method modeled on Simiand and Labrousse's price history is needed, and Perrot supplies the closest working example. Braudel calls for "a methodical model, the model of a model" and sketches its research program: collect every known profit figure regardless of quality, plot it against a provisional 10% threshold of "success," classify by industry, region, and circumstance, and push the series back before the statistically over-represented late eighteenth century. Profit, in his phrase, is "the approximate point of intersection of innumerable lines" to be reconstructed, not read off a ledger.

Industrial rise and fall traces a repeating "parabola," from a single town's sectors to national industry. Perrot's four Caen industries (cloth, bonnet-making, calicoes, lace) succeed one another almost mechanically, each rising as the last declines, constrained by the town's finite capacity for labour and outlets rather than by capital. Braudel extends this with his and Spooner's sixteenth-century production curves and, more surprisingly, Hoffmann's three-stage law (expansion / plateau / decline) applied to nineteenth- and twentieth-century British industry — suggesting the pattern is a general economic rhythm, not a pre-industrial peculiarity, though old-regime cycles ran shorter and sharper because production kept meeting a ceiling in "raw materials, labour, credit, technology, energy, or the home or foreign market" — a formulation that echoes Braudel's own limits-of-the-possible from Vol. I, here re-applied to the lifespan of a single industry rather than a whole economy.

Transport is an ancient industry capitalism never fully colonized. Even contemporaries (Montchrestien, Defoe, Petty, Galiani) recognized transport as "a species of manufacture," yet capital only committed to trunk routes that "really 'paid'"; the vast bulk of ordinary traffic was left to whoever would accept modest returns. Capital investment ranked overland transport lowest, inland waterways only "of limited significance," and maritime transport somewhat more attractive — an index, in Braudel's words, of each mode's "modernity or archaism."

Sombart's recalculation overturns the "common-sense" ranking of transport modes. Using late-eighteenth-century Germany, Sombart shows overland carriage (carts, pack animals) moved roughly five times the tonnage of inland waterways — a finding Braudel treats as broadly generalizable across Europe (with Holland and England as partial exceptions), corroborated by Dutens's 1828 French figures showing a roughly 1:10 water-to-land ratio.

The system stayed deliberately unrationalized because fragmentation served the merchant, not despite it. Peasant carters worked seasonally and cheaply, "paid for, like all sidelines, at less than its true cost price"; a 1782 edict enshrined haulage as a trade that "should be absolutely free"; and repeated attempts by the royal messageries to monopolize transport failed against merchant opposition. The closing argument makes the logic explicit: the merchant "simply swam with the current" rather than organizing transport in capitalist fashion, because abundant, unorganized competition among independent hauliers let merchants — not carriers — set the price. It is the same anti-market logic that keeps capital out of industry, restaged at the scale of a single service sector: capitalism prefers to sit above a competitive substrate it does not need to own.

Notable passages

The Caen lace paragraph states the core claim plainly — industrial gains were "dwarfed by trading profits" — and immediately complicates it with a concrete case (a collapsed lace industry revived only once a wholesale trader folded it into his commercial operation), showing profit and trade already fused at the point of revival.

The call for "a methodical model, the model of a model" is the unit's methodological hinge, naming Simiand and Labrousse as precedent and laying out, in list form, what a future profit-rate historiography would require.

The Hoffmann quotation gives the parabola its most explicit theoretical statement — three stages of expansion, plateau, and decline — applied not to a single eighteenth-century town but to two centuries of British industry generally.

The Sombart paragraph is the empirical core of the transport argument: a back-of-envelope tonnage calculation ('kilometre tons' for carts versus boats) that inverts the assumed hierarchy of transport modes.

The closing passage on merchants "swimming with the current" is the unit's thesis statement for its second half, tying the transport material back to the same market/anti-market logic that opened the profit discussion.
