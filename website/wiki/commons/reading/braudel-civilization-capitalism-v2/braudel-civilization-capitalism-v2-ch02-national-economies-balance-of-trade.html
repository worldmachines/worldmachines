The Balance of Trade as an Instrument of Domination

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel treats the (crude, often misleading) balance of trade as the best available map of domination and subjection between national economies, tracing it from Anglo-French and Anglo-Portuguese bilateral rivalries through Baltic multilateral credit to the structural deficits that delivered India and China into colonial subjection.

Braudel takes up the balance of trade — the crude, contemporaneously-invented ledger of a nation's exports against its imports — not as reliable economic data but as the best available diagnostic of how national economies measured, dominated, and subjected one another before a true "national market" existed; the chapter moves from the mercantilist doctrine that coined the concept, through a series of bilateral case studies (England–France, England–Portugal, the Baltic), to the aggregate view of "overall balances," and closes with the structural deficits that unravelled the Mogul and Manchu economies into European colonies.

Themes

The balance of trade as an imperfect but indispensable map. Braudel is explicit that he distrusts the instrument even as he reaches for it: it is "not the best or the only way of tackling the problem," merely "the only figures we possess" for reconstructing "equal and unequal exchange, balance and imbalance of trade, domination and subjection." The mercantilist maxim behind it — never buy more from strangers than you sell them — is traced to the Discourse of the Common Weal (1549, attributed to Thomas Smith) and to Thomas Mun and Edward Misselden in the 1620s, and Braudel shows territorial states acting on it well before it had a name: Louis XI restricting bullion export to Rome in 1462, England structuring war logistics in 1703 around keeping silver inside the kingdom. This is a state apparatus inventing an accounting technology to render international exchange legible and controllable, in step with the domestic instruments (double-entry bookkeeping, cadastres) that did the same for internal life — a strong instance of what this project calls legibility-as-machine-core, and squarely material for the modernity-machine's construction period, since Braudel notes "all these ideas emerged, as one might expect, at the same time as the territorial states."

Figures lie, and interpretation is contested from the start. The "Interpreting the figures" section is a running argument against taking the ledger at face value: Spanish America's bullion exports aren't a "deficit" because silver there is itself a commodity (Father Mercado, Turgot); and the 1786 Russia–England dispute, where the Russian consul John Newman insisted Britain was the true beneficiary of a nominal Russian surplus once shipping, re-export, and price mark-ups were counted, is presented as an "intuitive premonition of modern theories of the trade balance" — Braudel using an eighteenth-century amateur to gesture at a structural, rather than merely bilateral, reading of trade advantage.

Bilateral drama versus archival fact: the Anglo-French case. The chapter's most developed vignette is the decades-long English conviction that France profited from cross-Channel trade — reasserted in Parliament, in Genoese diplomatic correspondence, and in popular sentiment (bonfires and a weavers' procession against the 1713 commercial treaty) — collapsing when the French negotiator Anisson checked the English customs record himself and found the opposite had been true "for over fifty years." Braudel compounds the irony by noting a 1787 estimate that two-thirds of Anglo-French trade was contraband, so that even Anisson's correction rests on incomplete data: official trade statistics, on his account, are as much objects of "official hypocrisy" and self-deception as they are evidence.

Credit and monopoly beneath the visible ledger. Repeatedly the bilateral balance is shown to conceal a deeper structure of credit and monopoly control rather than transparent exchange — Braudel's recurring distinction, elsewhere named the three-storey house, between the market economy proper and capitalism's "anti-market" of privilege and finance (see braudel-three-storey-house). Aristocratic France's habitual deficit with Italy is not settled by trade so much as drained through the Lyons fairs, "a very effective vacuum pump operated by Italian capitalism"; Portugal's Brazilian gold is captured by English merchants who supply the Brazil trade "on credit" and thereby immobilize Portuguese capital in "a long circuit"; and in the Baltic, Amsterdam — not any bilateral balance — "fixed the prices, called the tune."

Structural deficit as the mechanism of subjection: India and China. The unit's culminating argument is that a temporary imbalance is survivable but a permanent one rots an economy from within — Braudel's examples are Spain's copper inflation and revolutionary France's capital flight, but the extended cases are India after 1760 and China after the 1780s. Centuries of an Asian trading system that required Europeans to pay in silver gave way, after roughly 1730, to a reversed balance: Plassey (1757) and the plunder of Bengal financed a "free 'primitive accumulation'" that produced Clive-era "nabobs," while China's balance turned only after Indian opium flooded the market in the 1780s, "paid in smoke," culminating in the Opium War (1839–42) and a century of "unequal treaties." This is Braudel doing the world-scale comparison (Europe against India and China) that the project should recognize as one of his own signature moves, distinct from any single instrument like the balance of trade.

Internal weakness or external shock? Braudel refuses to resolve the India/China cases into a single cause — Mogul and Manchu internal disorder mattered, but so did the "external shock" of European capital and shipping — and ends on the flat verdict that "Europe upset for its own ends the systems of exchange and the ancient balances obtaining in the Far East." Read against this project's vocabulary, the missing ingredient on the Asian side is close to what would here be called capture-resistance: the old Indian Ocean trading networks absorbed early European "depredations" and restored "the ancient equilibrium" for two centuries, but had no institutional defense against the specific mode of capture — chartered-company monopoly backed by credit and, eventually, artillery — that followed. The resonance is only partial: capture-resistance in this project's framing is a property to be cultivated against a coming Liveness-era capture regime, whereas Braudel is diagnosing a historical capture that already happened; the concept illuminates what was absent, not something Braudel himself theorizes.

Notable passages

The opening framing (c1) states Braudel's method for the whole chapter: an admittedly rudimentary instrument is nonetheless pressed into service because it is the only available way to trace domination and subjection between economies. The Thomas Smith quotation (c2) supplies the mercantilist maxim in its original, OCR-preserved form, and Braudel's aside that it "sums up all we need know about the trade balance" signals how little theoretical refinement the doctrine required to organize centuries of state policy. Anisson's 1713 discovery (c3) is the chapter's sharpest demonstration that official trade belief and archival fact can diverge for half a century unnoticed. The Lyons fairs passage (c4) is the clearest single image of capitalism as credit-and-monopoly extraction operating underneath an apparently favorable balance sheet. The Bengal passage (c5) names the mechanism — "primitive accumulation" — by which a shift in India's trading balance became outright colonial subjection. And the closing lines (c6) are Braudel's own refusal of a single-cause explanation for that subjection, worth preserving verbatim as the chapter's final word.
