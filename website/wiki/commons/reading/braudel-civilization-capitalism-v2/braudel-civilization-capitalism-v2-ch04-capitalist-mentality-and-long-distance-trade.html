Choice, Information, and the Anti-Market

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel argues that capitalist superiority rested not on a fixed psychological 'mentality' but on strategic flexibility, privileged information, and control of long-distance trade, and traces the schooling, correspondence networks, and rule-bending monopolistic practices that made that flexibility possible.

This unit assembles Braudel's positive case for what actually made merchant capitalism superior to ordinary market exchange: not an innate 'spirit' of calculation or gain, but the structural capacity to choose where to intervene, to know things before others did, and to bend or escape the rules that bound everyone else. It moves from a direct polemic against psychological explanations of capitalism (Sombart, Weber, Schumpeter), through a defense of long-distance trade (Fernhandel) as capitalism's backbone against revisionist historians who stress its small share of total volume, into the concrete infrastructure — merchant schooling, double-entry bookkeeping, and above all correspondence — that made informational advantage exploitable, and closes with a catalogue of monopolistic 'anti-market' practices, from Parisian meat cartels to a rigged grain-quay rule at Dunkerque.

Themes

Capitalism as strategic choice, not mentality. Braudel opens by defining capitalism's superiority as the fact that it "had the means to create its own strategy or to alter that strategy if necessary" — a structural freedom, not a fixed set of psychological traits. He then explicitly dismantles the Sombart/Weber move of explaining capitalism through an inner 'spirit' (calculation, lack of feeling, unbridled appetite for gain, the Protestant ethic): capitalists were as varied as anyone, some calculating and some reckless, some geniuses and some merely lucky beneficiaries of position (the La Bruyère anecdote of stupid men handed water at the river's source). His alternative is resolutely multi-causal: "economics played a part, politics played a part, society played a part, and culture and civilization played a part" — an explicit refusal of single-factor history that echoes his 'set of sets' framing of society.

Long-distance trade as backbone, defended against the revisionists. Braudel concedes the "good reasons" modern historians (Heers, Mathias, Godinho, Lütge) have for showing that internal/regional trade vastly outweighed long-distance exotic trade in sheer volume — grain, wool, and salt over pepper and spices. But he insists volume is the wrong measure: long-distance trade's importance lay in concentrated capital reproduction, not bulk. The Fernhandler (import-export merchant) was "a category apart," inserting himself between artisan and raw material, and between producer and distant market, thereby controlling production (the Paris merciers' 1684 petition) without owning it outright.

Profit from distance and mutual ignorance. The chapter's clearest mechanism: superprofits arise because two markets are "very far apart, with supply and demand in complete ignorance of each other," bridged only by the middleman — illustrated by pepper multiplying five-to-fifteen-fold in price between the Indies and European consumers, and repeated across Potosi silver, Siberian furs sold to China, the Acapulco–Manila galleon, and Javanese rice and salt arbitrage. Distance itself, not any special commodity, is what manufactures the margin.

Education and correspondence as the machinery beneath the advantage. None of this exploitation was possible without an acquired competence: Florentine botteghe teaching 8,000–10,000 children to read, a further cohort trained in algorismo and abbaco through adolescence, double-entry bookkeeping (Fugger learning partita doppia in Venice), and — most of all — merchant correspondence, whose real content was less the transaction than the intelligence: political news, harvest reports, price and exchange-rate postscripts. "To be well informed was even more important than to be well-trained."

Information as capital, sustained across centuries. Braudel stacks a 1360 merchant's maxim (open your own mail before passing on others'), Louis Greffulhe's 1777 anxiety about deals leaking to competitors, Amsterdam speculators chartering ultra-fast boats to beat the regular post in 1779, and a 1973 French subscription bulletin boasting that "once information becomes public it loses 90% of its value" — a longue-durée demonstration that being first to know, not any modern innovation, has been the tradable asset all along.

Anti-market mechanics: monopoly built into the rules. The closing section, ironically titled "Competition without competitors," catalogues how capital captured or evaded the supervised public market at every scale: Parisian meat and Bordeaux butter/cheese cartels, iron-merchant cabals, a nine-year glass-bottle monopoly in the Argonne, and — most vividly — Dunkerque's rule that no grain could be sold on the quayside in lots smaller than 100 razières, pricing ordinary buyers out and reserving the cheaper quayside rate for the wealthy few. This is capitalism as Braudel elsewhere calls the "anti-market" operating inside the nominal market, resonant with braudel-three-storey-house's upper storey "where the great predators roam" — here shown mechanically, rule by rigged rule, rather than asserted abstractly. The unit's merchant-correspondence infrastructure is also a partial, private-sector echo of legibility-as-machine-core: it builds the same recording/reporting apparatus the Modernity Machine uses for administrative control, but merchants deploy it to gain private, asymmetric advantage and evade rules rather than to make a population legible to a state — the resonance is real but inverted in orientation. See also fernand-braudel and braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1 for the material-life ground floor this upper-storey activity rests on.

Notable passages

The opening definition of capitalism as strategic freedom ("the means to create its own strategy") frames everything that follows. The rejection of Sombart and Weber's "idealist" mentality-explanation, replaced by an irreducibly plural causal account, is Braudel's methodological signature. The pepper-price passage supplies the actual mechanism — distance plus mutual market ignorance — behind every superprofit example that follows it. The Florence education passage grounds capitalist competence in ordinary, spreading literacy and accountancy training, not genius. The 1360–1777–1979 information sequence is Braudel at his most characteristically longue-durée, collapsing four centuries to show a structural constant. The Dunkerque quay rule closes the unit with the clearest possible micro-case of monopoly engineered directly into a market's own procedural rules.
