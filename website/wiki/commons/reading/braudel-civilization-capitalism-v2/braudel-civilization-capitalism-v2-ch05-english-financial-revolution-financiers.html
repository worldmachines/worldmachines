The English Financial Revolution, State Budgets, and the Financiers

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel traces England's slow, unplanned nationalization of public finance into a self-sustaining perpetual national debt after 1688, then uses the near-total absence of real budget forecasting across early modern states to attempt a rough estimate of the ratio between taxation and national product, before defining the financiers as a distinct, state-tied sector of capitalism running from Jacques Coeur through the Amsterdam loan houses that bankrolled Europe's princes.

This unit covers three linked sections from the chapter on the state and capitalism. First, it narrates England's 'financial revolution' of 1688-1756: a century-long, largely improvised nationalization of the fiscal machinery that culminated in a national debt which never had to be repaid, only serviced, because subscribers could sell their titles to a third party. Second, it turns to budgets as historical evidence, arguing that Europe's states barely knew their own finances in real time, and attempts a tentative calculation of the ratio between tax revenue and national product using Venice as a test case. Third, it defines the 'financiers' as an ambiguous, hybrid class -- part tax-farmer, part banker, part merchant -- structurally welded to the state's chronic insolvency, tracing the type from fifteenth-century France to the international loan houses of eighteenth-century Amsterdam.

Themes

The financial revolution was a slow, unplanned convergence, not a single act. Braudel is emphatic that Dickson's label is accurate only in outcome, not in process: England's finances in 1640-1660 looked much like France's -- fragmented among tax-farmers, royal moneylenders, and office-holders who owned their posts -- and the 'nationalization' (Crown Commissions over customs and excise, the 1714 Treasury Board, eventual state control of the Bank of England founded in 1694) proceeded 'steadily and with discretion, though without any discernible plan of action.' This slow, distributed assembly of a paradigm-shifting institution -- unplanned components accumulating for decades before the result becomes visible and gets a name (Dickson's own 1967 coinage) -- rhymes with history-machine, though Braudel's causal register stays institutional and economic rather than Lohse's paradigm-shift framing.

The 'miracle' was making debt permanent and liquid at once. The real innovation was not borrowing itself but converting floating short-term debt into a perpetual, consolidated debt that the state never had to repay, combined with post-1692 transferability of title so a lender could recover his money on demand by selling to a third party. This decoupling of the state's obligation from the individual creditor's liquidity is the structural core of the 'anti-market' capitalism Braudel describes elsewhere in the volume -- credit and privilege operating one level above transparent exchange -- and connects to braudel-three-storey-house.

Public credit produced its own social fracture. The system required earmarked new taxes (land-tax, consumption taxes) whose payers saw themselves as funding a parasitic class of 'moneyed men' -- rentiers, moneylenders, untaxed businessmen -- who had every incentive to want war, since war meant more loans and higher interest. Braudel treats the 1739 war with Spain as substantially their doing, and frames the whole apparatus as pragmatic improvisation later mythologized ('the standing miracle in politics') rather than the product of deliberate design.

Budgets are poor windows onto real economies, and legibility itself was the missing piece. Across Venice, Burgundy, Castile, France, the Ottoman Empire and China, surviving fiscal records are partial and the concept of forward budget forecasting barely existed -- Naples' Sommaria could not even reconcile a single tax year across its own provinces, and double-entry auditing only reached French public finance in 1716, and even then only as a check on spending already committed, not a forecast. This is a striking, only partly-fitting resonance with legibility-as-machine-core: the episode shows the Modernity Machine's signature project (rendering a polity's finances readable, measurable, orderable) still failing at the state's own core accounting function well into the eighteenth century -- legibility as a slowly, unevenly won capacity rather than an accomplished baseline.

A tentative fiscal-to-national-product ratio. Braudel risks a real quantitative claim: taxation might represent 10-15% of gross national product in a dense city-state like Venice (extrapolating an 8-12 million ducat national product from a 1,200,000 ducat income in 1600), but perhaps only around 5% in a larger, less urbanized territorial state -- suggesting territorial expansion was partly encouraged by the lower fiscal burden it could get away with. He is careful to flag this as 'conjecture,' but treats even rough national-income ratios (5-15% of income spent on war, per another historian's estimate for fifteenth-century western Europe) as more illuminating than no measurement at all.

The financier is a distinct, ambiguous sector of capitalism, defined by proximity to the state. Braudel explicitly separates financiers from the market economy discussed in the prior chapter: they are 'closely and firmly associated with the state,' blending tax-farming, moneylending, and private commercial ventures in a single career. Jacques Coeur (Charles VII's finance minister and a Levant shipowner), Mazarin's Italian bill-of-exchange agents, and the Castanier and Crozat/Gilly dynasties in France (spanning tax-farming, manufactories, colonial trading companies and banking across Carcassonne, Paris, Cadiz, and Amsterdam) all instantiate the same hybrid 'merchant-banker-entrepreneur-shipowner-financier' type across three centuries.

A recurring, migrating hegemony of dominant financial groups. Braudel closes the unit by naming a long historical sequence of single dominant creditor-groups that each in turn held a state financially hostage: the Fuggers and then Genoese hombres de negocios over Spain, Italian merchants over France, the bankers of Lucca and Florence over fourteenth-century England, and finally 'international Protestant bankers' and Amsterdam's finance houses (Hope & Co.'s nineteen Russian loans between 1787-1793) over much of eighteenth-century Europe. England's supposed exceptionalism -- eliminating dependence on foreign or parasitic moneylenders by nationalizing its own finances -- turns out to be only partial: Dickson's own research shows a small oligopoly of insider speculators dominating ostensibly open public loan subscriptions, vindicating Sir John Barnard's parliamentary attacks on the 'undertakers' even after 1747-1748 reforms meant to open subscription to the public directly.

Notable passages

  • The 'miracle' of perpetual, transferable debt (c1) is the mechanical heart of the financial revolution: the state converts floating debt into consolidated debt and never repays it, while the lender can cash out at will.
  • The French trade envoys' 1713 letter on English administration (c2) is Braudel's best incidental evidence for how thoroughly the English state had been bureaucratized -- 'Tout est ici en regie' ('everything here is under government control') -- to the point that bribery no longer read as corruption to the bribers themselves.
  • The 'moneyed men' passage (c3) names the social resentment the funding system generated: taxpayers versus untaxed rentiers and speculators accused of warmongering for profit.
  • The Venice national-product estimate (c4) is Braudel's rare attempt at a real fiscal-tension ratio, offered as tentative but methodologically serious.
  • The definition of the financier (c5) draws the analytic line separating this 'special sector of capitalism' from the market economy proper, on grounds of its structural dependence on the state.
  • The closing survey of successive dominant creditor powers (c6) -- Fuggers, Genoese, Lucca/Florence, Amsterdam, the 'international Protestant bankers' -- frames England's financial revolution as one late instance of a much older European pattern of financial dependency migrating from state to state.
