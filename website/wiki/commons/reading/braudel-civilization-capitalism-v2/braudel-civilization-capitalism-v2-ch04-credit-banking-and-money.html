Who Put Up the Money: Investors, Three Banking Take-offs, and the Tides of Capital

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel surveys the full social ladder of early modern Europe's capital suppliers, traces three successive Western banking take-offs (Florence, Genoa, Amsterdam) that each collapsed short of institutionalizing financial capitalism, and shows money oscillating between hoarding and circulation according to confidence rather than pure scarcity.

This unit runs across three linked subsections of Braudel's argument: first, who actually supplied early modern Europe's investment capital, from menial servants to the newly-named 'capitalists'; second, a comparative history of Western banking's three separate take-offs — Florence, Genoa, Amsterdam — each of which collapsed before financial capitalism could institutionalize (that would wait for the nineteenth century); and third, a meditation on money's perpetual oscillation between hoarding and circulation, governed by confidence and social mores as much as by raw quantity.

Themes

Capital had a full social ladder of suppliers, and interest rates were the barometer of its abundance. Braudel opens by inverting Marx's 'Accumulate, accumulate!' into 'Borrow, borrow!', arguing capitalism only fully arrives once accumulated capital is used near capacity. He reads the near-continuous fall in European interest rates between the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries — strikingly low in Genoa around 1600, then in Holland and London — as proof that accumulation had outrun what trade could absorb, not that money was becoming intrinsically cheaper to produce. Below the 'capitalists' (a term that only crystallizes by the late seventeenth century) and middling investors sat a mass of small savers: valets, domestic servants, and 'the money of the innocent', whose modest subscriptions Braudel treats as evidence that capital-supply reached society's humblest ranks, not just its grandees.

Credit was a personal, reputational relation before it was an institution. The extended case of Robert Dugard's linen works at Darnetal and his partner Louvet's begging rounds through Paris (1749–1750) shows abundant French capital sitting idle because lending carried social stigma and no institutionalized money market existed to match borrowers with backers. Braudel contrasts this explicitly with Holland, where 'Holland's triumph meant the triumph of easy credit, even for foreign merchants', and with London, where goldsmiths and bill-brokers had begun improvising institutional roles cash-scarcity forced onto them.

Western banking took off three times and collapsed three times before the nineteenth century. Braudel's comparative core: Florence (before/after 1300, ending with the 1345 collapse of the house of Bardi under Edward III's debts), Genoa (bills of exchange centered on the Piacenza fairs, collapsing around 1622), and Amsterdam (eighteenth-century paper credit, undone by the French crown's 1789 bankruptcy). Each was 'a sophisticated credit system, created at the topmost level of European affairs', that proved 'unable to maintain its position for more than a very short time... as if these new experiences were beyond the capacities of ancien regime economies'. This rhymes, loosely, with world-machines's construction-then-collapse cadence — three infrastructures each assembled, dominated, and were dismantled — though the scale (decades, not a millennium) and the trigger (a discrete shock: a royal default, a silver glut) are unlike a world machine's slow legibility exhaustion; Braudel's pattern is closer to a recurring structural ceiling than a full civilizational cycle.

Money's rhythm was hoarding and release, and abundance often outran the era's capacity to invest it. Coin was hidden in wartime (Brittany in 1708, Genoa after the 1627 Spanish crown bankruptcy) and released again once confidence and peace returned. But Braudel's closing argument is structural, not just psychological: even at its most abundant — Milan's post-feudal capital windfall, France's unused eighteenth-century reserves, England's industrial revolution not needing to 'summon up all her reserves' — capital regularly exceeded productive demand, because 'every society has its own ways of saving and spending, its own prejudices and motivations and its own restraints on investment'. This is close kin to limits-of-the-possible: the ceiling here is not physical or technological but social and institutional, a limit on what a given society's habits and taboos will let capital do.

Notable passages

The opening thesis ('Accumulate, accumulate!... capital formation') states Braudel's measure of capitalism as capital utilization, not mere possession, and ties the pan-European interest-rate decline directly to over-accumulation. The valet-and-servant anecdote ('It is not without significance... not... to be taken literally') is Braudel's concrete answer to the unit's title question, pushing 'who put up the money' down to society's least visible investors. The Dugard/Louvet correspondence dramatizes credit as a fragile personal favor-economy in a France that, paradoxically, was awash in capital. The 'three occasions in the West' passage frames the Florence/Genoa/Amsterdam comparison that structures the whole 'Credit and banking' section, and the Genoese 'mill-wheel' passage gives Braudel's own preferred mechanism for why that second take-off failed: bills of exchange only profit from differential discount rates, and a glut of silver after 1590–1595 submerged the wheel that made the system turn. Finally, the Rennes informant's 1708 letter is a primary-source snapshot of hoarding as it actually looked to a contemporary — tens of millions of piastres and specie withheld from circulation pending the resolution of a currency crisis, tying the section's closing argument to lived testimony rather than aggregate reasoning.

Braudel's own three-storey framing of the economy — braudel-three-storey-house — is implicit throughout: this is the top storey, the 'anti-market' zone of credit, banking, and monopoly finance sitting above ordinary market exchange, continuous with the argument of braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1 and the author page fernand-braudel.
