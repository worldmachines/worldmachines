The World Outside Europe: Markets, Pedlars, and Indian Bankers

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel widens the frame beyond Europe to show markets as a universal, elementary phenomenon from Brazil to China, then dismantles J.C. Van Leur's picture of Indian Ocean traders as humble "pedlars," using the Armenian agent Hovhannes's eleven-year credit-financed journey and India's saraf banking system to argue that Asia's long-distance trade already possessed capitalism's contracts, credit, and interest.

Braudel asks whether Europe occupied the same "stage of exchange" as the world's other densely-populated regions, and answers first with the sheer ubiquity of markets and shops — in Brazil, Black Africa, the Islamic souks, Indian villages, and the calendared hierarchy of Chinese market-towns studied by G. William Skinner — before turning to the more contentious question of long-distance trade. There he takes aim at J. C. Van Leur's influential thesis that Indian Ocean and East Indies merchants were mere itinerant "pedlars," unchanged since Rome, arguing instead — via the 1596 Dutch landfall at Bantam, the harsh credit contracts behind supposedly humble traders, the Armenian agent Hovhannes's documented eleven-year circuit from Isfahan to Lhasa, and India's saraf banking system of bills of exchange and maritime insurance — that Asia's commerce already ran on capital, credit, and contract at a scale rivaling Europe's.

Themes

Markets are an elementary, world-wide layer, prior to civilizational choice. Braudel opens by insisting that "production, exchange and consumption" at this basic level are universal obligations that "have no frontiers" — markets appear from the pulperias of the Argentine pampas to the souks of Islam to every Indian village (forced there by the need to convert dues into cash for landlords or the Great Mogul) to the strict two- or three-day market cycles of small-town China. This is the same ground-floor, near-invariant material life Braudel mapped in Vol. I, continued here across the whole planet rather than just Europe (compare braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1).

China's market hierarchy is a social hierarchy. Drawing on Skinner's fieldwork, Braudel describes market-town "hexagons" scaling inversely with population density (echoing Vidal de la Blache's French "pays"), a coordinated calendar that let peddling artisans, coolies, and moneylenders circulate without markets clashing, and a nesting of village into market-town into city in which "the market hierarchy in fact described a social hierarchy" — small towns embedded in peasant life, cities escaping it to face the wider world.

The Van Leur "pedlar" thesis underestimates Asian capitalism. Braudel directly contests the idea that the swarm of merchants who boarded Houtman's ships at Bantam in 1596 were simply small-time itinerants; he reads the same evidence as showing "agents of altogether higher status, sometimes even wholesalers," backed by silent shareholders, moving increasingly bulky cargoes, and bound by contracts as harsh as (or harsher than) the Italian commenda. This is Braudel's market/anti-market distinction (braudel-three-storey-house) applied outside Europe: what looks like humble peddling from the market-economy level conceals capitalism's credit and privilege underneath.

Concrete credit networks prove the point. The Armenian commission-agent Hovhannes's account book — an eleven-year (1682–1693) journey from Julfa to Surat to Lhasa and back, financed by his khoja's capital plus borrowed money and bills of exchange at rates from 0.75% to 20–25% per transfer — is Braudel's star exhibit: a functioning, long-distance capital market with no European involvement. He compares Hovhannes not to a pedlar but to the new English "private trader" reshaping the home market.

India ran a parallel banking system. The sarafs (Banyan money-changers) issued hundi (bills of exchange) and bima (maritime insurance), a system Irfan Habib compared structurally to Western banking; financiers like Virji Vora and Abd ul Ghafur amassed fortunes and shipping fleets rivaling the English East India Company, and Tavernier's travel account shows a merchant crossing India entirely on borrowed money, "paying the old with new" at compounding transfer rates.

The method is explicit world-scale comparison. Europe, Islam, India, and China (with a glance at Japan via Dutch borrowing from Kyoto) are set side by side as variably-developed instances of the same underlying phenomena rather than a European invention diffusing outward — a comparative move that loosely rhymes with world-machines's insistence on reading multiple coexisting infrastructures rather than a single civilizational telos, though Braudel's comparison is economic-institutional where WMP's is civilizational-infrastructural.

Notable passages

The opening claim that markets are planet-wide and pre-civilizational is anchored by the Latin American and Argentine detail (c1). The China section's payoff line — "the market hierarchy in fact described a social hierarchy" — is curated in full context (c2). The Bantam scene that Van Leur read as proof of unchanging petty trade, and that Braudel reads as misleading, is quoted in full (c3). Hovhannes's itinerary is quoted for its sheer improbability as a data point (c4). The saraf/hundi/bima definition paragraph anchors the India-banking claim (c5), and Tavernier's own words on "paying the old with new" anchor the credit-mechanics claim (c6).
