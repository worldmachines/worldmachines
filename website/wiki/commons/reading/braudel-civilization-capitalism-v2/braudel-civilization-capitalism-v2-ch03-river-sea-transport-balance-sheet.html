River and Sea Transport, and Capitalism's Negative Balance Sheet

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel surveys river and sea transport — slow, toll-ridden rivers, sea lanes worked mainly by small competitive carriers, and ship ownership evolving from a communal joint venture into divisible capital shares and speculative maritime loans — and closes the chapter with a negative verdict: pre-industrial capitalism scarcely penetrated production, transport included, remaining instead a creature of distribution and market position.

River traffic was slow, hazard-prone, and toll-choked even where waterways were praised as the age's cheap option; sea traffic was worked everywhere, in bulk, by small independent carriers rather than big capital; and across both, ship ownership shifted over centuries from an archaic communal joint venture into divisible capital shares and speculative maritime credit — all of which Braudel assembles into the chapter's closing verdict that pre-industrial capitalism's inroads into production, transport included, were “pretty negative.”

Themes

River transport never delivered the ease its reputation implied. Rivers were slow (a Loire convoy from Nantes to Orleans could take three months instead of the speed the current promised), subject to “the caprices of the rivers,” obstructed by wrecks, weirs and sandbanks, and taxed by dozens of tolls on routes like the Loire and Rhine “as if to discourage shipping.” Canals traded these hazards for lock delays (30 locks in 18 leagues on the Orleans canal). Boatmen formed a distinct, closely regulated caste (censused by “ports” on the Seine, embroiled in disputes like the 1723-24 Seine gribaniers-versus-Amiens-merchants tariff fight), and at Roanne a genuine class split opened between master-boatmen working their own boats and merchant-carriers who owned boats worked by hired factors — “a separation between the worker and his means of working.” Braudel allows this was “some kind of capitalism,” but one hemmed in by guild and administrative custom.

Sea transport, too, ran mostly on small-scale, competitive carriage, not concentrated capital. Fleets of small coasters and fishing boats worked the Baltic, the Norwegian fjords, the Mediterranean and the Aegean, carrying “anything and everything.” Competition among small operators kept freight margins thin — English interlopers undercut Dutch and French shipping in Amsterdam in 1725, recorded Dutch voyage profits averaged only 6%, and two attempts (1629, 1634) to found an Amsterdam maritime-insurance monopoly failed because merchants judged the premiums would exceed the profits they were meant to protect.

Ship ownership moved from an archaic communal form toward tradable, accountable capital shares. Medieval vessels sailed communiter — “as a joint venture” — owned per loca by “several companions” who crewed the ship themselves, shared a common kitty, and needed “no book-keeping ... from start to finish”: a self-governing “miniature republic” under a master who was merely first among equals. As hull sizes outgrew what such venturer-crews could finance, ownership shifted per partes into fixed fractional shares (usually 24 carats), paying an annual dividend enforceable before a magistrate and held by absentee investors scattered, in the Ragusan case, “throughout the Christian ports of the Mediterranean.” Read against the World Machines frame, this rhymes — only partially — with the Modernity Machine's drive toward legibility-as-machine-core: an unaudited communal enterprise gives way to divisible, quantified, transferable ownership, but the engine here is ship-scale economics and merchant accounting, centuries before the Machine's own construction period and with no state legibility project behind it.

Maritime credit became a speculative layer detached from the voyage it nominally financed. The grosse aventure loan — money lent on a voyage at 30-50% interest, then itself insured at a further premium — let a lender come out ahead whichever way the voyage went, a device Braudel likens to the “Stock Exchange practices” of speculating “with money one did not actually possess.” Court cases arising from failed voyages (the Carnate's rerouting dispute, the bankruptcy of Harelos, Menkenhauser & Co.) show the risk being litigated rather than absorbed at sea. This is capitalism in Braudel's strong sense — credit, privilege, and long chains of commerce standing apart from and above the competitive carrying trade — and resonates with the anti-market storey of braudel-three-storey-house.

Fixed capital began to outweigh circulating capital (wages and provisions) over the eighteenth century. Saint-Malo ships bound for the Pacific in 1706 show circulating-to-fixed capital running about 2:1; by the century's end, accounts for three Nantes ships show the ratio roughly reversed. Braudel sets this beside Davis, North and Walton's finding of a 50% productivity gain in North Atlantic shipping between 1675 and 1775, and floats — without resolving it — the idea that the ship was becoming “a sort of machine” whose rising capital cost was displacing labour as the dominant expense.

The chapter's verdict: pre-industrial capitalism's balance sheet in production, transport included, was negative. The “important merchant” of this period was rarely a farmer, a craft-master, or a transport entrepreneur in his own right; owning a boat, as with the Pellet brothers of Bordeaux or the Saint-Malo shippers who fitted out Pacific voyages, was a secondary asset in service of trade, not a commitment to production. Braudel dates capitalism's real invasion of production to the industrial revolution, when machinery made industry itself a profit-making sector — after which capitalism kept its old habit of moving between sectors rather than fusing permanently with any one. The claim sharpens Vol. II's central distinction between the transparent, competitive market economy (the small carriers, the thin-margin coastal trade) and capitalism proper, “the sector in which real profits were made” through distribution and market position rather than production; see fernand-braudel and braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1.

Notable passages

  • River traffic's real pace (c1) — the litany of delays, caprices and tolls that undercuts the myth of easy river carriage.
  • The archaic communal ship (c2, c3) — sailing “as a joint venture,” owned by “several companions,” run as a bookkeeping-free “miniature republic.”
  • From per loca to per partes (c4) — the shift to carat-shares as hulls outgrew the old venturer model, producing absentee capital-owners.
  • The grosse aventure loan (c5) — insured maritime credit that let a lender come out ahead regardless of the voyage's fate.
  • The negative balance sheet (c6) — Braudel's direct statement that the important merchant stayed a man of distribution, not production.
