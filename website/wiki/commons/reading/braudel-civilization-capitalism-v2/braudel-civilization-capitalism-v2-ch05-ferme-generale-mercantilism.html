France's Privatized Finance and the Mercantilist State

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
The French monarchy never built centralized public finance, instead ceding tax collection for centuries to an interlocking financial aristocracy that culminated in the monopolistic Ferme generale (1726-1776), while mercantilism, an incoherent label Braudel keeps anyway, named the modern territorial state's improvised drive to hoard precious metals against its rivals.

Braudel traces how the French monarchy's finances were never centralized but instead ran for centuries through private intermediaries — traitants, partisans, and eventually the great tax-farming syndicate known as the Ferme générale — and then turns to a second, more theoretical problem: whether "mercantilism" names any real, coherent policy at all, or only the modern territorial state's improvised instinct to hoard precious metals and out-compete its neighbors.

Themes

State finance without a state. France had "no public finances at all, no centralized system," so neither control nor forecasting was possible: the Crown drew on roughly a hundred separate funds, with the nominal central Treasury receiving only about half of royal revenue, and depended on towns, provincial estates, the clergy, and tax-farmers to actually collect money. Braudel treats this structural privatization — not the receipts-versus-expenditure balance — as the deep, long-run cause of the monarchy's collapse, in pointed contrast to the Revolution's ability to reform finance quickly because its problem was "social and institutional" rather than merely fiscal.

Traitants as an interlocking financial aristocracy. The 1661 judicial inquiry against Fouquet exposed roughly 230 partisans, drawn overwhelmingly from the nobility (76.5%), whose cash "advances" to the king were themselves financed by aristocratic landowners. Braudel follows Daniel Dessert in reading this not as corruption bolted onto a separate noble order but as constitutive: marriage among financiers and aristocrats was "no longer a bargain in which one exchanged money in return for an ancient name, but rather a capital partnership." The nobility was already inside what Braudel flatly calls "a vigorous form of capitalism" — the anti-market, privilege-based upper storey of the economy described across braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1 and formalized in braudel-three-storey-house.

The Ferme générale as institutionalized monopoly. Tax farming consolidates in stages: Colbert-era "syndicates" (1669), the Fauconnet lease grouping several taxes (1680), and finally the Ferme générale in its "definitive form" (1726), maturing with the addition of the tobacco monopoly (1730). Its forty fermiers-généraux, bonded by enormous deposits that made them nearly impossible to dismiss, formed what Braudel calls "a mighty clan" bound by intermarriage — a case, in his words, of "the iron law of minority control, that structural concentration of capitalist activity." The Ferme's heyday (1726–1776) ends precisely with Necker's arrival as its declared enemy, and its members are executed during the Terror in 1794.

Mercantilism as a useful fiction. Braudel sides with critics — Hutchinson calling it "one of the vaguest and most irritating 'isms' in the language," a later historian noting "there are as many mercantilisms as there are mercantilists" — who doubt that "mercantilism" ever named one coherent doctrine. He keeps the word anyway as shorthand for the modern territorial state's improvised, self-interested reflexes from the thirteenth century onward, arguing pointedly that no single architect designed it: "it was the mercantilists who invented the nation... unless it was the nation... which, by inventing itself, invented mercantilism at the same time." That picture of a policy assembling itself piecemeal out of scattered, uncoordinated acts until it becomes a nameable pattern resonates with history-machine — though Braudel's mercantilism is a policy-reflex rather than a paradigm shift, and his own skepticism about the label cuts against treating it as a clean, unified machine at all. The underlying drive of "territorial or modern states... pushing them all towards modernity" also brushes against the state-building, boundary-drawing ambitions of the modernity-machine, though here the aim is extraction and hoarding of specie rather than legibility for its own sake.

Bullionism as structural necessity, not mere error. Richelieu called gold and silver "tyrants"; Sombart summarized centuries of state-building as "viel Silber (später Gold) so viel Staat." Braudel refuses to dismiss this as naivety: war's voracious appetite for cash, and the stubborn real-world refusal of soldiers, sailors and ordinary people to accept paper at par (French bank notes discounted 40–50%, English sailors' pay tickets bought up cheap by the jobber Thomas Guy), meant precious metal genuinely was the load-bearing infrastructure of the ancien régime economy. This maps directly onto the braudel-three-storey-house distinction: "the upper level was the realm of paper," where traitants, bankers and businessmen operated, while everyday life below stayed stubbornly tied to metal cash.

Notable passages

The unit opens by describing the structural privatization of French royal finance — the "no public finances at all" passage — which sets up Braudel's central claim that private capture, not bad accounting, killed the monarchy's fiscal capacity.

The Fouquet-inquiry passage on aristocratic financing of the tax-farmers is the evidentiary core of the "financial aristocracy" theme: it is where Braudel shows the nobility's own capital, not just its patronage, sustaining the traitant system.

The institutional history from the 1669 syndicates through the 1726 Ferme générale traces the consolidation Braudel treats as the apex of ancien régime tax-farming, immediately followed by the "iron law of minority control" passage, Braudel's own theoretical gloss on why the Ferme's forty families behaved like a single capitalist clan.

The Montaigne/Voltaire quotation on mercantilism as "a policy of each for himself" is Braudel's crispest one-line definition, chosen precisely because it survives his own skepticism about the label.

The two-planes passage — specie below, paper above — is the theoretical payoff of the bullionism discussion and the clearest bridge to the three-storey-house framework elsewhere in Braudel's argument.
