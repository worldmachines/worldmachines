Back to the Heart of Europe: Capitalist Agriculture from Norfolk to the Kingdom of Naples

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Testing a four-part definition of capitalist agriculture derived from England's Norfolk model against Brie, Venice's Terraferma, the Roman Campagna, Tuscan sharecropping, and France, Braudel finds capitalist agriculture was a genuine but minority current running against Europe's persistent and often intensifying feudal and seigniorial order.

Braudel tours the "heart of Europe" — the continent west of a Hamburg–Venice line — to test how far a genuinely capitalist agriculture penetrated the world of lord and peasant, using England's Norfolk model as a reference grid and then checking it against Brie, the Venetian Terraferma, the Roman Campagna, and Tuscany, before conceding that such capitalism remained, across Europe as a whole, a minority phenomenon set against vast and often intensifying feudal survivals. The chapter continues fernand-braudel's climb, begun in braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1, from the ground floor of material life toward the market economy and capitalism proper, now tested case by case in the countryside rather than the town.

Themes

A four-part working definition, built from England. Rather than argue in the abstract, Braudel derives his test for "capitalist agriculture" from the Norfolk case: the disappearance of feudal tenure (quoting Marx on the Stuart Restoration's conversion of feudal into private property), the leasing of land to capitalist tenant-farmers, the rise of a wage-earning proletariat with nothing to sell but labour-power, and a resulting vertical three-tier division of labour — landowner-rentier, tenant-entrepreneur, wage-labourer. Every subsequent regional case in the unit is measured against this template rather than against a fixed ideological definition of capitalism.

Organization can outrun — or fail to meet — technology. At Brie, urban-owned estates leased to rich tenant-farmers who hired wage labourers reproduce the English three-tier structure almost exactly, yet Braudel insists that here "nothing changed in the shape of technology until the nineteenth century": capitalist social relations and technical stagnation coexist, and the high cost of leases combined with cereal specialization left these farmers acutely exposed to grain-price swings. This resonates with Braudel's own limits-of-the-possible — the outer technical ceiling within which social and economic forms can vary without breaching it.

Capitalism migrates between storeys: Venice's retreat to the land. After the crisis of the early seventeenth century, Venetian patrician capital abandoned long-distance Levant trade — the "anti-market" zone of privilege Braudel elsewhere calls capitalism proper — for large, wage-labour estates on the Terraferma like Anguillara, run "with method and discipline" under a watchful steward, with profits reaching 130% in a good year against Brie's 12%. Contemporary Bergamask villagers already had the vocabulary for it ("Vi sono capitalisti qui?"). Braudel reads the move less as flight from risk than as a shift of capital toward secure income and investment — but ultimately judges it, against Genoa's continued commitment to trade, "not the best capitalist choice." This touches braudel-three-storey-house, though loosely: it is capital descending from the top storey toward organized market production rather than a clean market-economy/capitalism split.

A "deviant case" complicates Adam Smith. Sismonde de Sismondi's 1819 account of the Roman Campagna describes more than ten classes of migrant labourers — Sabine day-labourers, workers from the Marches and Tuscany, men from the Abruzzi, casual idlers from Rome's piazzas — each assigned a fixed task under negozianti di campagna (capitalist tenant-farmers subletting land owned by absentee nobles and religious houses). Braudel uses this to push back on Smith's claim that division of labour operates in industry but not in agriculture, arguing that the ancien-régime countryside was full of task specialization once one looked past the ploughman-sower stereotype.

Sharecropping as capitalism's limit case. Tuscany's podere system, "a work of art" expressing Florentine "rationality" (Elio Conti), shows landowners maximizing yields through close supervision (Morelli's Ricordi) and calculated return on investment, concentrated on marketable cash crops like oil and wine. But Braudel is careful: the mezzadro is not proletarianized — the oste "was not dealing with a peasant deprived of his means of production" — he retains direct, generational ties to land he manages himself. Capitalist rationality and market orientation can coexist with a form well short of full wage labour, and Braudel treats this gap as the real measure of how far the system can be called capitalist at all.

The advanced zones are a minority against a deepening feudal majority. Southern Italy after the 1647 Masaniello revolt, Sicily's "second serfdom" still intact in 1789, Aragon's Gutsherrschaft, southern Spain, the Scottish Highlands and Ireland — Braudel catalogues a periphery where seigniorial power intensified rather than dissolved. Paolo Mattia Doria's testimony on Neapolitan barons, and the statistic that up to 88% of the population in some provinces remained under feudal justice even in the age of enlightenment, anchor the claim that "the role played by agrarian capitalism was in the end rather small."

France as a contested synthesis. France's "seigniorial reaction" of 1730–1780s is read by Eberhard Weiss, and with qualification by Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie, as simultaneously a capitalist reaction — landowners and tenant-farmers restructuring estates, speculating, entering trade — layered onto genuinely archaic feudal grievances. Braudel notes the irony that peasants, lacking a vocabulary for the new capitalist phenomenon, reverted to the old language of anti-feudalism in the cahiers de doléance of 1789, leaving the counterfactual of an English-style agrarian evolution "for ever unanswered."

Notable passages

The four-criterion definition (c1) functions as the unit's methodological spine — Marx's Stuart Restoration quotation gives it historical warrant before Braudel applies it region by region. Brie's paired capitalist hierarchy and frozen technology (c2) is the unit's clearest statement that social/organizational and technical change can decouple. The Anguillara material (c3) supplies rare direct testimony — "capitalisti," used unprompted by barely literate Bergamask mayors — that the term is not an anachronistic imposition on the period. Sismondi's Roman Campagna description (c4) is the unit's richest primary quotation, doing double duty as evidence against Smith's agriculture/industry distinction. The Tuscan passage (c5) carries the unit's most important qualification: capitalist rationality without proletarianization. Doria's account of baronial power (c6) is the counterweight that keeps the chapter from over-claiming capitalism's reach, placed deliberately under the heading "Advanced areas: the minority."
