Neither Protestant Ethic Nor Rationalism: Braudel Against the Sombart–Weber Origins Debate

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel rejects single-cause explanations for both the North's economic overtaking of the Mediterranean South (geography, cost competition and timing rather than the Protestant ethic) and for the origins of capitalism itself (rationalism and double-entry book-keeping, against Sombart), then sides with Sombart's dating of a new bourgeois mentality to Quattrocento Florence before dissolving the whole Weber-Sombart debate as a period piece of an outdated Eurocentric confidence in Western reason's world-historical destiny.

This unit closes out Braudel's survey of rival explanations for capitalism's rise (Chapter 5's concluding stretch). It moves through four connected arguments: a geographic/economic account of why the Protestant North overtook the Mediterranean South that displaces the Weberian Protestant-ethic story; a demolition of Sombart's claim that rationalism (paradigmatically, double-entry book-keeping) is what produced capitalism; an endorsement, against Weber, of Sombart's dating of a new bourgeois "art of living" to Quattrocento Florence via Leon Battista Alberti; and a closing reflection that the entire Weber–Sombart quarrel (1904–1912) is itself a period artifact of a Eurocentric certainty about Western reason's historical destiny that later history has undercut.

Themes

Retrospective geography beats the Protestant ethic. Braudel's own preferred explanation for the North's rise is not confessional but structural and contingent: an old, exhausted, already-"colonized" Mediterranean/Latin Europe versus a poor, late-developing northern periphery with lower wages, cheaper transport and "unbeatable industry"; a Mediterranean-wide economic downturn around 1590 that hit the South's more complex, developed economy hardest while handing the North a run of opportunities; and businessmen who "flocked into Holland from Germany, France and indeed Antwerp" once the balance tipped. "The victory of the North was undoubtedly in a way the victory of the proletarian, the underdog" — a story of competitive costs, not superior ethics. Braudel does not discard the Reformation entirely: he grants it gave the northern countries political "coherence," cross-border Protestant trading solidarity, and (via a weakened Church elsewhere) a more flexible social order suited to capitalist expansion — but treats these as reinforcing rather than causal. "I find this a persuasive explanation. There are more things in the world than the Protestant ethic."

Capitalism does not equal rationalism. Braudel takes on Werner Sombart's thesis that Western rationalism — embodied above all in Luca Pacioli's 1494 codification of double-entry book-keeping (partita doppia) — is the "same spirit" that produced Galilean physics and modern capitalism alike. He dismantles this on several fronts: Fibonacci's Liber Abaci (1202) actually demonstrates Arabic, not Latin, mathematical rationality, learned from his merchant father's contacts in North Africa; Pacioli was not first — Cotrugli's Della Mercatura (1458) already discusses orderly book-keeping, and Federigo Melis's archival work shows partita doppia already active in Florentine merchant houses (the Compagnia dei Fini and Compagnia Farolfi) by the late thirteenth century, two centuries before Pacioli's book; and double-entry book-keeping diffused extremely unevenly — some of the biggest firms in Europe, including the Dutch East India Company (founded 1602) and London's Sun Fire Insurance Office, did without it for centuries (the latter until 1890). Citing de Roover and Yamey, Braudel argues single-entry bookkeeping was an adequate, flexible instrument in its own right, and that accounting technique never dictated the decisions of a firm's head. This is a clear instance of a paradigm assembling slowly and unevenly from scattered, independent merchant practices rather than arriving as a clean invention by a credited genius — resonant with history-machine's critique of "great man" historiography, though Lohse's frame concerns historiographical paradigms rather than commercial techniques, so the fit is more structural analogy than direct application.

Two logics within one economy. Braudel's deepest objection to the rationalism thesis is definitional: it silently identifies capitalism with the market economy, then borrows the market's impersonal, quasi-natural rationality (Adam Smith's "hidden hand," Lange's "natural computer") and credits it to capitalists themselves — an "arbitrary equation." Against this he insists on two coexisting logics: "There is the logic of free competition; and there is the logic of monopoly, speculation and power." Endorsing (with qualification) Lenin's observation that monopoly grows out of free competition without abolishing it, Braudel restates his own thesis: capitalism "never entirely eliminates the free competition and market economy from which it has grown... but continues to exist over and alongside them." This is the unit's clearest statement of the braudel-three-storey-house architecture — transparent competitive exchange as one storey, the monopolistic "anti-market" as another — and it reframes rationality itself as plural: what counts as rational varies "not only from one culture to another but from one set of circumstances to another." Braudel even toys with reframing capitalism's motor as risk-taking and gambling rather than calculation, though he sets the idea aside as too neat.

Florence, not just the Reformation, as the mentality's birthplace. Turning to when a genuinely new economic mentality appears, Braudel sides with Sombart against Weber: not sixteenth-century Protestantism but Quattrocento (and even earlier) Florence, evidenced by Leon Battista Alberti's Libri della Famiglia (1433–1441) — "Money is 'the root of all things'"; time is "one of man's possessions which he would do well not to waste"; and a rule of thrift ("your expenditure should never exceed your income") aimed pointedly at the rich, not the poor. Weber's rejoinder — that Alberti merely repeats classical, Ciceronian household-management wisdom (Hausvaterliteratur), not a genuinely capitalist ethic — is answered with independent testimony from Paolo Certaldo (idle money is worse than money working "in vain," a dialogue speaker's line anticipating "Time is money"), which Braudel judges decisive: "If capitalism can be recognized as a 'mentality' and be measured by words, then Weber must surely be wrong." But Braudel's real payoff is methodological, not partisan: word-debates about a "mentality" cannot settle the question at all, and "we must move out of the charmed circle of words, and look to real life" — the concrete, thirteenth-and-fourteenth-century capitalist practice of Italian cities, a position that also credits Marx's own instinct to look there.

A period-piece debate, and history without a destined winner. The unit's final movement steps back from the whole Weber–Sombart quarrel and reads it as a symptom of its own moment (1904/1912): both men assumed "the indisputable structural superiority of the western 'mind'" as something needing explanation rather than something to be doubted, and Weber pictured capitalism as "a culmination, the promised land of economic development, the final stage of progress," never "a fragile and possibly transitory regime." Braudel refuses both the superiority premise and the teleology: "why should one civilization be more intelligent and rational than another for all time?" He sharpens this with an explicit counterfactual in world-scale comparative history — what if Chinese junks had rounded the Cape of Good Hope in 1419, during Europe's Hundred Years' War recession, handing world domination to "that other pole of the populated world"? — refusing to "rewrite world history in support of a cause." This is Braudel's own version of history without a predetermined winner or destined center, close in spirit to Configurancy's refusal of teleological co-emergence, though Braudel is doing comparative civilizational history rather than modeling agent/world co-emergence. And his closing line — capitalism "no longer appears to us to be the last word in historical evolution," undergoing what "we can see... taking place in front of our eyes" as mutation or possible death — rhymes with world-machines's picture of a dominant machine's Day mistaking itself for permanent before its own Dusk, though Braudel offers no cyclical apparatus of his own and is speaking of his present (the 1970s) rather than positing a structured succession.

Notable passages

The curated passages track the unit's arc: the geography-over-ethic argument for the North's rise; Melis's archival evidence undercutting Pacioli's-invention story of double-entry book-keeping; Braudel's two-logics statement of the market-economy/capitalism split; the Certaldo evidence against Weber's reading of Alberti; and the closing Eurocentrism critique with its Chinese-junks counterfactual and its refusal to grant capitalism a destined, final-stage status.
