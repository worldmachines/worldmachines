The Three-Storey House and the Case for a Provocative Word

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
In the foreword to Vol. II, Braudel lays out a three-storey model of material life, the market economy, and capitalism as an opaque anti-market zone of power, defends his anachronistic use of the word capitalism for 1400-1800, and previews the book's chapter plan and comparative, longue-durée method.

The foreword sets out the three-tier architecture of the whole volume — material life, the market economy, and capitalism — and explains why Braudel calls the top tier "capitalism" despite the word's twentieth-century birth, before sketching the book's chapter plan and its comparative, longue-durée method.

Themes

The three-storey house. Braudel frames the whole project as a climb "upstairs from the ground-level of material life" to "the highest level of all, the action of capitalism," with a middle storey of "economic life" — the market economy of "great trading cities, Stock Exchanges and fairs." The floors meet only at "thousands of humble points of intersection: markets, stalls, shops," below which lies self-sufficient, non-economic material life bounded by the "fateful threshold of 'exchange value'." This is the source image behind the project's own braudel-three-storey-house page, and picks up directly from the ground floor covered in braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1.

Capitalism as anti-market, not super-market. Braudel insists the market economy (transparent, competitive, rule-governed) and capitalism (the top storey) are not the same activity at larger scale. Capitalism is "a shadowy zone, a twilight area of activities by the initiated," and at root "an accumulation of power ... a form of social parasitism." This half-echoes the project's legibility-as-machine-core distinction between transparent, standardized zones and opaque ones that resist it — though here the opacity of capitalism serves predation and privilege, not the capture-resistant autonomy the project elsewhere associates with opacity.

An anachronism owned, not apologized for. Braudel concedes "capitalism" only reached full maturity around 1900, long after the period he applies it to, but defends the choice on the historian's usual license to coin retrospective labels (the Hundred Years' War, the Renaissance, the Reformation): he needed one word for "this zone which is not the true market economy, but indeed often its exact opposite."

Method: observation, comparison, and the longue durée. The inquiry proceeds by "observation — repeated until the eyes ached," borrowed help from the human sciences, and above all "systematic comparison" in Marc Bloch's tradition, using "the perspectives of the long term, la longue duree." Because pre-industrial systems changed slowly, Braudel treats comparable cases across centuries as "virtually a series of experiments" rather than chance comparisons — a method-first stance that rhymes with the project's own ambition to fuse qualitative historiography with something closer to comparative, falsifiable modeling, though Braudel's aim here is retrospective explanation, not forecasting.

The book's plan, closing on the "set of sets". Chapters 1-2 catalogue the visible surface (markets, pedlars, shops, fairs); chapters 3-4 turn to capitalism "away from home" and "on home ground," fixing the meaning of capital, capitalist, and capitalism and trying to "situate capitalism by sector" so as to "reveal the limits of capitalism" — a narrower, sectoral sense of limit than the Vol. I limits-of-the-possible, though both are Braudel hunting for a system's boundary. The final chapter, "Society or the 'set of sets'", is called the most necessary of all: it replaces economy and capitalism inside "the overall context of social reality, outside which nothing can have full meaning."

A candid, unfinished voice. Braudel closes on an unusually personal note — chapters written and rewritten four or five times, delivered first as lectures, compared to Matisse's discarded drafts — and admits doubt about his own clarity, steadying himself with Maitland's line that simplicity is technical subtlety's outcome, not its starting point.

Notable passages

  • The three-storey house (c1) — the governing metaphor for the entire volume, stated before any argument begins.
  • The threshold of exchange value (c2) — where material life gives way to economic life, and why Braudel treats the boundary as a set of discrete contact points rather than a continuum.
  • Capitalism as accumulation of power (c3) — Braudel's working definition of capitalism as opaque and predatory, plus his claim that the storeys are hierarchically interdependent.
  • Justifying an anachronism (c4) — the deliberate, self-aware decision to name a 1400-1800 phenomenon "capitalism."
  • The comparative, longue-durée method (c5) — the explicit statement of how the whole inquiry is meant to proceed.
  • From circulation to the "set of sets" (c6) — the chapter-by-chapter plan and its holistic destination in the final chapter.
