Shops and Pedlars: The Market's Fixed and Mobile Doubles

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Shops splinter off from the periodic market as artisans' open workshops become fixed points of sale and then middleman enterprises run on credit, while pedlars fill the gaps shops and markets leave unserved and persist by evading formal control.

Braudel traces the shop's slow separation from the periodic market — an 'elementary instrument of exchange' that is nonetheless constantly confused with it in practice — from its origin as an artisan's open workshop through the rise of the 'real' shopkeeper-middleman, the specialization and guild hierarchies that followed, the seventeenth- and eighteenth-century 'conquest' of towns by shops, and the credit relations that made even the humblest shopkeeper 'a capitalist in a very small way.' He then turns to pedlars, the market's itinerant complement, who filled distributional gaps shops and markets left unserved and who persisted, adaptively, through every crisis and every attempt to suppress them.

Themes

The shop/market boundary is a European taxonomic convenience, not a universal fact. Braudel opens by cataloguing exceptions before he even proposes the rule: a Muslim souk is a street of shops mistranslated as 'market'; Szechwan's scattered yao-tien function as permanent markets without ever being one; seventeenth-century Ceylon had shops but no markets at all; Parisian booths banned in 1776 were market-like shops open every day. The confusion is itself the point — before capitalism imposes its own order, distribution takes whatever local shape fills the gap, and Europe's market/shop grid is only one solution among several (curated as passage A below).

Urban authorities preferred the market to the shop because the market could be watched. The first shops were simply artisans' open workshops; town governments forced bakers, butchers, and the like to sell in the market hall 'as the market was easier to supervise than the shop, where every man was virtually his own master.' This is a strikingly early, explicit statement of the logic legibility-as-machine-core later formalizes as a state project: supervision precedes and motivates the organization of exchange, though here it is guild and town oversight doing the watching, centuries before a modern cadastral state.

The 'real' shopkeeper is where Braudel plants the boundary between market economy and capitalism. Once middlemen — rather than artisans selling their own output — take over the shop, Braudel reaches for Marx's formula: the capitalist merchant runs M-G-M ('he begins with money... and returns regularly to money'), while the peasant and the artisan still run G-M-G, converting goods into money only to reconvert it into goods. This is the ground-level version of the braudel-three-storey-house distinction between the transparent, competitive market economy and the anti-market where capital reproduces itself — playing out not among great merchant houses but at the level of the corner shop.

Credit, not scale, is what makes the shopkeeper structurally capitalist. The unit's central causal claim about the eighteenth-century 'boom' in shops is not population growth or urbanization (though both matter) but the extension of credit at every link of the chain — wholesaler to retailer, retailer to customer — which leaves the shopkeeper 'a capitalist in a very small way,' suspended between debtors and creditors and permanently exposed to ruin. The correspondence of unpaid Lyon merchants and the bankrupt Paris baker's ledger are Braudel's evidence that this precarity, not prosperity, is the shop economy's normal condition.

Shops 'conquered' the towns as a visible, datable social transformation, and stratified as they spread. From Lope de Vega's Madrid ('everything has turned into shops') to London's mercers multiplying sixfold in a generation, the growth is not just economic but produces its own hierarchy — Arti Maggiori over Arti Minori in Florence, the six Corps of Paris, the Cinco Gremios Mayores of Madrid, the London Merchant Companies — an intersection of the economic and social-hierarchical registers of the same 'set of sets' Braudel elsewhere separates analytically.

Pedlars are defined by the gaps they fill and the authority they evade, not by a fixed trade. Named a dozen ways across Europe (colporteur, hawker, Hocke, buhonero, seyyar satici), peddling ranges from destitute packmen to comfortably-off forains and manufacturers' agents 'saving their wealth.' Braudel treats its persistence — surviving into the twentieth century, reviving during the Revolution, the Russian Civil War, and wartime shortages, despite repeated predictions of its extinction and repeated bans — as itself a finding: an 'eminently adaptable system' that returns whenever formal distribution breaks down. That resilience of an informal structure beneath successive formal orders rhymes with limits-of-the-possible, and the unit's closing claim that peddling is 'a way of getting round the sacrosanct market, a way of cocking a snook at established authority' gives it a capture-resistance-like character, though the resonance is economic evasion rather than the deliberately constructed autonomy WMP means by the term.

Notable passages

A. The Szechwan/Ceylon comparison is Braudel's most explicit world-scale move in the unit: it uses non-European cases to show the market/shop pair is not a natural category but a description that keeps breaking down.

B. The line on market supervision gives the reason, in the authorities' own logic, for privileging the market over the shop: visibility, not efficiency.

C. The Marx M-G-M / G-M-G passage is where Braudel names, precisely, the moment an ordinary seller becomes a capitalist in miniature — the conceptual hinge of the unit.

D. 'The shopkeeper then, a capitalist in a very small way...' compresses the credit-and-precarity argument that follows into a single sentence.

E. The closing line on peddling as evasion is Braudel's summary verdict on two thousand years of the trade, from the Late Roman Syri to Soviet-era sackmen.
