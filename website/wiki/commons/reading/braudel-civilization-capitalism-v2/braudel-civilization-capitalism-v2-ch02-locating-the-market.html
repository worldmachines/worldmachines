Locating the Market

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Closing his two chapters on exchange, Braudel rejects both the economists' self-regulating market and Polanyi's embeddedness thesis, proposes synchronized price fluctuation as the empirical test for a market economy, and lands on his three-tier model of a non-economy, a market economy, and the anti-market zone of capitalism above it.

Closing the two preceding chapters on exchange, Braudel asks where exactly "the market" belongs in economic life. He rejects the economists' picture of the market as an autonomous, self-regulating mechanism and Karl Polanyi's substantivist claim that a "true" self-regulating market only arrived with nineteenth-century capitalism; against both he proposes an empirical historical test — do prices across a region fluctuate in unison? — traces the market's institutional history from the archaic and ethnographic (itinerant village markets, Kabylia, Dahomey, the Trobriand Islands) through the Greek agora and the merchant-city bourses of medieval Italy, and ends with his signature architecture of economic life: a non-economic substratum, the market economy proper, and, above it, the anti-market zone that is capitalism's real home.

Themes

  • The word 'market' is equivocal and must be disambiguated before use. Braudel opens by separating "market" as a loose synonym for exchange, circulation and distribution from "market" as the name of a specific historical system, insisting the concept only makes sense embedded in an economic and social life that is itself constantly changing — it "never has the same meaning or significance from one minute to the next."

  • The economists' self-regulating market is too clean a picture. Smith's division-of-labour regulator, the "hidden hand," and Oskar Lange's image of the market as "the first computer mankind ever had" all treat the market as an autonomous mechanism; Braudel accepts this as a useful simplification but not a historical description — "it cannot satisfy the historian, who does not view the market as a simply endogenous phenomenon."

  • Braudel rejects Polanyi's embeddedness thesis on evidential and methodological grounds. He credits the underlying insight — the economy as a mere "sub-division" of social life — but attacks the claim that only the nineteenth century produced a genuine self-regulating market as resting on a grab-bag of ethnographic exceptions (potlatch, kula, "ports of trade") rather than engagement with the actual price-history literature (Labrousse, Abel), calling the theory's definitional rigor "an almost theological taste for definition."

  • He substitutes his own empirical criterion: synchronized price movement. A market economy exists, in Braudel's view, "when prices in the markets of a given area fluctuate in unison" — a condition he finds already established across Europe by the twelfth century, which decouples "market economy" from any single nineteenth-century threshold and pushes its origin deep into the medieval period.

  • The market has an institutional history that long predates capitalism. Beginning as a kind of "itinerant village," the market was later absorbed and scaled up by towns; the Phoenicians and the Greek agora institutionalized it, and Italian merchant cities eventually built "an embryo Stock Exchange" out of near-daily merchant meetings — the decisive step being the town's appropriation of the market, not any single invention.

  • A brief anthropological digression broadens the frame beyond Europe — Kabylia, Dahomey, the Red River delta, Bahia, the Trobriand Islands — gesturing at Braudel's larger habit of world-scale comparison, though here it is archaic/ethnographic survival rather than the civilizational Europe/Islam/India/China/Japan comparison that recurs elsewhere in the volume.

  • The chapter closes with Braudel's three-tier model of economic life, folding Galbraith's "two economies" and Lenin's imperialism/competition split into a stack he treats as a European constant since the Middle Ages: a lowest "non-economy" that capitalism's roots reach but never fully penetrate; above it the "favoured terrain" of the market economy, with its horizontal, self-coordinating price signals; and above that, "the zone of the anti-market, where the great predators roam and the law of the jungle operates" — which he names outright as "the real home of capitalism," today as in the pre-industrial past. This is arguably the clearest single statement of the architecture behind braudel-three-storey-house in the whole volume, and belongs with fernand-braudel's broader habit of testing theory against price-series evidence.

  • A minor resonance worth flagging without overreading it: Lange's description of the market as a "self-regulating machine which would itself ensure the equilibrium of economic activities" echoes the mechanized, self-organizing framing behind WMP's own world-machines concept — though Lange's machine is a single coordinating mechanism inside an economy, not the civilization-spanning infrastructure of institutions and assumptions a world-machine names; the rhyme is in the "self-regulating machine" language, not the scale.

Notable passages

Locating the market's equivocation (c1) — Braudel's opening move is definitional housekeeping: "market" names both any exchange whatsoever and a specific historical "system," and the chapter is an attempt to hold those apart without pretending the referent stays fixed.

The self-regulating market as theory (c2) — the strongest form of the economists' picture, built through Smith, Lange's "first computer" line, and D'Avenel's liberal credo that price "would never let itself be subjugated by anyone."

Against Polanyi (c3) — Braudel's sharpest methodological complaint: Polanyi's dating of the "great transformation" rests on scattered ethnographic exceptions rather than the price-history record, a habit he calls "an almost theological taste for definition."

An empirical test for "market economy" (c4) — Braudel's own operational substitute for Polanyi's threshold: synchronized price fluctuation, attested across Europe from the twelfth century onward.

The three-tier architecture (c5) — the chapter's destination and the clearest statement in the volume of non-economy / market economy / anti-market, with capitalism named flatly as "the zone of the anti-market, where the great predators roam."
