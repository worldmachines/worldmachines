Usury's Long Retreat and the Weber Thesis on Puritan Capitalism

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel traces the centuries-long, practically-driven erosion of the Christian ban on usury through scholastic loopholes and universal practice, then tests Max Weber's Protestant-Ethic thesis against counter-evidence like Calvin's own permissive economics and Catholic Genoa's rock-bottom interest rates.

Braudel closes his chapter on Christendom and commerce with two entwined arguments: a centuries-long chronicle of how the Church's absolute ban on lending at interest was doctrinally maintained yet practically dismantled by scholastic casuistry, state toleration, and universal habit; and a critical engagement with Max Weber's claim, in The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1904), that Puritan religious psychology produced the capitalist mentality — which Braudel treats as a real but overstated correlation rather than a clean causal law.

Themes

  • A doctrine built to be circumvented. The Deuteronomic ban on lending "upon usury to thy brother" (permitting it against a "stranger") was universalized by Christian teaching into a total prohibition, hardened by canon law (the second Lateran Council, 1139) and given philosophical teeth when Aristotle's writings entered Christendom via Aquinas — "money produces no offspring," pecunia pecuniam non parit. Yet scholastic theologians themselves opened the loopholes that let real credit expand: interest was licit if the lender bore a risk (damnum emergens) or forwent a gain (lucrum cessans), which legitimized currency exchange (cambium), only condemning its purely fictional cousin (cambio seco, "dry exchange"), and licensed loans to princes and profit-sharing partnerships (the Genoese commenda, Venetian colleganza, Florentine societas). Scholastic doctrine ends up manufacturing the very legal technology of early capitalist credit while formally denouncing its object — a slow-motion version of what Braudel elsewhere calls the "anti-market" upper storey improvising its own rules of privilege; see braudel-three-storey-house.

  • Formal law lagged practice by centuries. Usury remained nominally illegal in France until 1789, and "excessive" interest was still being legislated against as late as 1935 — yet Braudel shows usury "practised by the whole of society: princes, the rich, merchants, the humble, and even the Church," concealed, moralized over, and tolerated in the same breath ("one went as furtively to visit the moneylender as one went to visit a whore... but one went all the same"). Jewish moneylenders absorbed a disproportionate share of the blame — the "stranger" clause of Deuteronomy repurposed as a legal fiction — even though Christian bankers (Lombards, Tuscans, Cahors men) lent openly at interest alongside them and both used identical fictional-sale and false-bill techniques. Braudel's own gloss — "mentalities had not changed as quickly as economic practice" — reads as a case of the limits of the possible: a mental/normative constraint eroding far more slowly than the material economy it nominally governed; see limits-of-the-possible. It also has a family resemblance to the World Machines decay-sequence (Functional through Ornamental to Absent): the usury ban survived as ever more ornamental law — reasserted by Benedict XIV in 1745, litigated in 1769 and 1777 — long after it had stopped functioning as an actual constraint on practice; the fit is partial, since Lohse's sequence describes physical or civic infrastructure decaying, not a moral-legal prohibition.

  • Weber's Protestant Ethic, summarized and interrogated. Braudel walks through Weber's argument in some detail — the 1895 Offenbacher survey of Baden Protestants and Catholics, Benjamin Franklin's Advice to a Young Tradesman ("time is money... credit is money... money is of a prolific generating nature"), and above all Richard Baxter's Puritan doctrine of the calling: worldly success as a providential sign of election, joined to an ascetic ban on luxury that redirects profit back into productive use. Braudel names his own method-level "allergy" (shared with Lucien Febvre) to Weber's retrospective, ideal-typical argument, but insists Weber's thesis has been distorted by both defenders and attackers (Sombart chief among the latter) into a stronger claim — Protestantism as the source of capitalism — than Weber actually made.

  • Counter-evidence Braudel weighs against a strong thesis. Calvin's own 1545 letter on usury is measured, not liberationist: interest is legitimate between merchants at modest rates (about 5%) and illegitimate only where it offends charity — hardly "breaking any taboos," since the scholastic loopholes had already done that work. And Catholic Genoa, "the beating heart of a capitalism already of worldwide dimensions," had Europe's lowest interest rates (1.2%) — suggesting, in Braudel's own suspicion, that causation may run from capitalism's scale to cheap credit rather than the reverse. Braudel also notes, via Weber himself, that hostility to usury recurs across Greek, Muslim, Jewish, Indian and Chinese ethical systems alike — a world-scale comparison consistent with Braudel's broader Vol. II method of setting Europe against Islam, India, China and Japan rather than explaining European capitalism as sui generis.

Notable passages

  • c1 opens the unit with Braudel's civilizational frame: Christendom, unlike Islam, had no "benevolent religion" bonus and so fought out religion-versus-economy from first principles.
  • c2 gives Aristotle's foundational case against usury (money as barren, "interest" as the unnatural "offspring" of money), the doctrinal target every later loophole had to work around.
  • c3 shows the loopholes themselves — risk, forgone gain, currency exchange, partnership — the scholastic machinery that let interest-bearing credit expand while the ban stood in name.
  • c4 documents the gap between official prohibition and universal practice, and the scapegoating of Jewish lenders alongside functionally identical Christian ones.
  • c5 is Braudel's own summary of Baxter's doctrine of the calling, the psychological mechanism at the center of Weber's thesis.
  • c6 is Braudel's closing rebuttal: Catholic Genoa's rock-bottom interest rates as evidence that Weber's causal arrow may run backwards.
