International Monopoly, the Currency Snare, and the Anatomy of Exceptional Profit

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Long-distance merchants captured extraordinary profits through international monopoly, currency arbitrage between gold, silver, and base coinage, and privileged access to capital and information — practices Braudel treats as the defining, anti-market core of capitalism rather than incidental abuses of it.

This unit moves through three linked demonstrations of how big long-distance merchants extracted profits unavailable to ordinary market participants: a survey of international monopoly practices (Dutch wine-buyers in the Loire, the English Levant Company, German mining cartels, Amsterdam warehousing, the Genoese silk monopoly in Sicily) capped by the 1787 cochineal speculation of Hope & Co. as a monopoly that failed; a section on how the coexistence of gold, silver, and base coinage let the rich run a permanent, near-invisible currency arbitrage against everyone else; and a closing survey of trade profit rates, the poor evidentiary record behind them, and Braudel's thesis that capitalism's essential trait is its capacity to shift between trade, industry, and land whenever profits in one sector decline.

Themes

International monopoly is capitalism's normal mode, not its exception. Entry to "these extremely profitable circuits" required capital, credit, being "well-informed and well-connected," and associates abroad who were "a party to one's secrets" — barriers of information and trust, not just money. Braudel catalogues instances across scales: Dutch buyers cornering Loire wine before the harvest and dictating price; the English Levant Company deliberately delaying its 1718 sailings to inflate prices at both ends of the route; Bordeaux merchants timing cargo to Martinique for the same effect; the Fuggers, Welsers, and Hochstetters monopolizing German mercury, copper, and silver until Charles V's edicts protected them from a hostile Reichstag; Amsterdam's warehouses ("bigger and more expensive than a large ship") stockpiling whale oil, silk, sugar, saltpetre, and copper to be released only as prices rose; and the Genoese lock on Sicilian raw silk, defended by land purchases in the silk-growing districts and by underselling any French challenger. This is the concrete texture of what the brief calls the "anti-market" upper storey — braudel-three-storey-house — where transparent, competitive exchange gives way to privilege and concealment.

Even a well-capitalized monopolist can lose the bet. The 1787 cochineal venture by Hope & Co. is Braudel's deliberate counter-case: Henry Hope planned to buy three-quarters of Europe's cochineal stock simultaneously across exchanges, force up the price, and sell — but underestimated the true size of stocks, was slowed by mail delays, and ended up sitting on twice the inventory he expected amid a wartime slump in demand. Braudel uses the episode to attack historian P.W. Klein's Schumpeterian defense of monopoly as a source of "security" that promotes investment and the general good; Braudel's rejoinder is blunt — this was "a speculative gamble," and Amsterdam's dominant position "was the pursuit not of security but of domination."

Currency itself functioned as a hidden monopoly of the rich. Because the wealthy alone habitually held large stores of gold and silver while ordinary people transacted only in copper, two currencies effectively coexisted inside one economy "as if a strong and a weak currency were to co-exist... with artificial attempts to maintain fixed parity - doomed to failure." Those who understood the gold-silver ratio and the exchange rate between base and precious coinage — a "cabal understood by few," per Malestroit — could profit systematically from those who didn't; foreign merchants, especially the Dutch, exploited the same asymmetry by paying with debased coin and hoarding the best coin they received in return. This is a private, unevenly distributed legibility of market and monetary conditions rather than a state-built one, so it only loosely rhymes with legibility-as-machine-core — the asymmetry here is a class privilege maintained by concealment, not an infrastructural project to make a whole population newly readable.

Inflation redistributes rather than enriches. Braudel quotes Charles Mathon de La Cour's 1788 argument at length: new gold and silver "increase the amount of coin" without making nations "really become more wealthy" — prices rise while wages lag, so that "the gold mines have provided weapons for the egotism of the rich, enabling them more and more to oppress and enslave the industrious classes." Braudel endorses the substance of this: money was "a miraculous agent of exchange, but it was also a confidence trick serving the privileged," pushing back on economists who treat money as a neutral medium.

Trade profits outran industry and agriculture, but the evidence is deliberately opaque. Braudel likens the historian of merchant profit to "a journalist venturing into a classified area": figures are "either incomplete, or invented, or both." What survives is suggestive — Hope & Co.'s visible 10% return concealed the real, unrecorded profits partners made privately; the French India Company reported an unexplained "average profit of 96.12%"; Compagnie des Indes tobacco once returned 500%; individual voyages ranged from 28% to 200%; a good Venetian sheep-farm yielded 40% a year. Long credit chains and payment delays (one merchant, Bertrand fils, waited twenty years for his 1777 profits) were the hidden cost of these returns, and capital was always migrating toward whichever sector or region currently paid best — what Braudel calls a "differential geography of profit."

Capitalism's defining trait is its capacity to change shape. The unit closes on Braudel's larger thesis: from thirteenth-century Italy to the present, capitalism's "unlimited flexibility" — its readiness to slip between trade, industry, banking, and land "at a moment's notice" whenever profit rates fall — is what has let "the capitalist class... direct and control change... to preserve its hegemony," and he explicitly rejects any stage-theory that treats industrial capitalism as uniquely "true." This shape-shifting-to-preserve-position bears a partial, suggestive resemblance to capture-resistance — though what capital is resisting here is a falling profit rate rather than legibility or capture as such, so the rhyme is at the level of adaptive persistence, not the same mechanism.

Notable passages

The unit opens by defining monopoly's entry requirements — capital, credit, being well-connected, and associates "party to one's secrets" — which frames everything that follows as a matter of structural access rather than individual cunning. The Hope & Co. cochineal narrative is the unit's one extended failure case, useful precisely because it shows the mechanism (buy up stock quietly, then force the price) breaking down against imperfect information and bad timing. Braudel's rebuttal of Klein supplies his clearest thesis statement on monopoly as domination rather than security. The currency-asymmetry passage and the Mathon de la Cour block quote together carry the section on money as a class instrument rather than a neutral medium. The closing passage on capitalism's "unlimited flexibility" is the chapter's — and arguably the volume's — governing claim, tying the monopoly and currency material back to Braudel's larger argument in fernand-braudel's work about what capitalism, as distinct from the market economy, actually is.
