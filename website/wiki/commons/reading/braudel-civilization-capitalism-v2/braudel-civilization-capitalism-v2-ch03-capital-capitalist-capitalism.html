Capital, Capitalist, Capitalism: From a Study in Vocabulary to a Topology of Capital

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Opening Chapter 3, Braudel defends his continued use of the word 'capitalism', traces the separate word-histories of capital, capitalist, and capitalism from medieval Italy to the twentieth century, then reworks capital as a material and quantifiable category to argue that pre-industrial capitalism occupied only a narrow, protected sector of an economy otherwise governed by a vast 'non-capitalism'.

This unit opens Chapter 3 ("Production: or Capitalism away from Home") of Vol. II. Braudel first defends his continued, reluctant use of the word 'capitalism' against calls to ban it as anachronistic or ideologically loaded, then undertakes a word-by-word etymological history of capital, capitalist, and capitalism from twelfth-century Italy to the twentieth-century dictionaries, and finally pivots from words to the thing itself: capital reconceived as 'capital goods' (fixed vs. circulating), an attempt to quantify capital formation in the ancien régime economy via Alice Hanson Jones and Simon Kuznets, and a closing argument that capitalism's home was narrowly the 'sphere of circulation' rather than the whole economy.

Themes

Refusing to discard a contested word. Braudel opens by admitting he has used 'capitalism' sparingly and that it invites charges of anachronism when applied before the industrial era, but he refuses calls (from Herbert Heaton, and even Lucien Febvre) to abolish it: "if capitalism is thrown out of the door, it comes in through the window," because the phenomenon it names — a form of economic activity distinct from ordinary market exchange — existed well before industrialization even if the word came later. Historian and present converge: "To the historian, understanding the past and understanding the present are the same thing."

A vocabulary study as a check on anachronism. Following Henri Berr and Lucien Febvre's method (ask where a term-of-art comes from and whether it misleads), Braudel traces capitale (from Late Latin caput, 'head') emerging in twelfth–thirteenth-century Italy in the sense of funds or interest-bearing money, competing for centuries with rivals — fonds, richesses, avances, biens — before finally winning out only around Turgot's time (c.1770). He grounds this in primary documents: Datini's 1399 letter about insuring 'il chapitale', St Bernardino of Siena's Latin sermon calling capital 'that prolific cause of wealth', Savary's 'capital funds', and Condillac's observation that a science's vocabulary always lags its concepts ('people begin by speaking and writing and the language remains to be composed').

Capitalist and capitalism as political, pejorative coinages. Capitalist dates from the mid-seventeenth century (Hollandische Mercurius, 1633) and was almost never used neutrally — Marat, the comte de Custine, and Cambon all deploy it as an epithet for a predatory elite, and Rivarol blames the Revolution's course on 'sixty thousand capitalists.' Capitalism itself is by far the youngest and, Braudel says, 'the least real' of the three: coined with its modern sense by Louis Blanc in 1850 and academically launched only by Sombart's Der moderne Kapitalismus (1902), long after Marx's death and never used by Marx himself — a fact that did not stop it from being retrofitted into a Marxist stage-model (slavery/feudalism/capitalism).

The anachronism debate and capitalism's 'former topology'. Braudel takes on historians (Polanyi, Van Leur, Veyne against Rostovtzeff) who forbid the word before the nineteenth century, and rejects Gerschenkron's collapse of capitalism into 'the modern industrial system.' His counter is structural rather than lexical: capitalism in the past was real precisely because it was defined against its context — "It was nevertheless a world apart, different from and indeed foreign to the social and economic context surrounding it... capitalism was what it was in relation to a noncapitalism of immense proportions." This is the chapter's real thesis, and it resonates strongly with braudel-three-storey-house: capitalism is not a whole economic system but an upper storey occupying only certain "channels of trade" — as Braudel puts it of the pre-industrial economy, "it was in the sphere of circulation, trade and marketing that capitalism was most at home," never the whole of circulation, only the parts it could control.

Capital reconceived as a material thing: capital goods. Braudel then drops the word-history and asks what capital is, materially: something that is the 'result of previous labour' and that must be reabsorbed into further production to count — grain sown (capital) versus grain eaten (consumption); coal fed to Newcomen's engine (capital) versus coal burned in a fireplace; a ship, a managed forest, a working mill, all capital goods, while a miser's hoarded coin or an unused forest are not. Money itself, when 'launched on to trade circuits,' is a capital good — Braudel invokes Hume's claim that money is 'nothing but the representation of labour and commodity.'

Fixed vs. circulating capital, and their unequal fragility. Via the eighteenth-century economist Heinrich van Storch's thought experiment — barbarians who strip a rich nation of its circulating capital (food, raw materials) but leave its houses and workshops (fixed capital) intact — Braudel shows that circulating capital is more urgently indispensable to keep production running at all, even though fixed capital is what eventually wears out for good ('the road deteriorates, the bridge collapses'). Production is 'a kind of two-speed engine.'

Quantifying capital in a pre-statistical economy — and a limit on savings. Braudel turns to Alice Hanson Jones's calculation of private wealth in 1774 New Jersey/Pennsylvania/Delaware (capital roughly three to four times national income, uncannily matching Keynes's own C≈4I ratio) and to Simon Kuznets's long-run backward extrapolation of English growth data to 1500. Kuznets's key finding — that no society, even the richest today, seems to save above roughly 20% of production, and that pre-modern gross capital formation was mostly consumed by maintenance and replacement rather than accumulating as durable fixed capital — reads as a genuine, if economic rather than material, instance of limits-of-the-possible: a structural ceiling that pre-industrial societies could approach but essentially never cross, until "the industrial revolution was above all a transformation of fixed capital" itself. Braudel folds in social hierarchy too: with an elite of 5% controlling 25% of income, savings capacity was concentrated in a narrow privileged stratum — a claim that also echoes the material-life register of braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1 (land as 'a very fragile kind of capital,' crop rotation, endless small repairs to dykes, bridges, roads).

Sector analysis as method. The chapter's payoff, and Braudel's own methodological signature fernand-braudel, is that because agriculture and 'industrial' production suffered chronic capital deterioration and low, unreliable returns, capital concentrated instead in commerce — 'the topology of capital provides us with a reverse topology of the weaknesses and unprofitable sectors of former societies.' The unit ends by previewing the question the next chapter will answer directly: why capitalism withdrew from agriculture, mining, and lending to peasants across sixteenth-century Europe whenever profits there dried up, following, even then, 'the hunt for and maximization of profits.'
