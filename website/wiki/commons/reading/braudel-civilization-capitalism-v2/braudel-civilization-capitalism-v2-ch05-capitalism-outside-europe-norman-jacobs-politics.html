Capitalism Outside Europe: Long-Distance Trade, Norman Jacobs, and the Politics of Dynastic Accumulation

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Comparing China, Japan, the Ottoman lands, and Mogul India, Braudel argues capitalism's precondition everywhere was long-distance trade, but its completion depended on whether a society's political structure let upper classes accumulate and transmit wealth across generations, a test only Europe and, partially, Japan passed.

Braudel closes his account of capitalism as a world phenomenon by asking why it "succeeded in Europe, made a beginning in Japan, and failed... almost everywhere else," despite every civilization having had the same raw materials of trade, credit, and currency; he surveys thriving long-distance trading enclaves in China, Japan, and India, works through Norman Jacobs's structural comparison of Japan and China, and then supplies his own answer, centered not on culture or religion but on whether a society's political structure lets an upper class accumulate and hand down wealth across generations.

Themes

Long-distance trade is capitalism's universal precondition, but it only ever colonizes narrow enclaves. Wherever ships, caravans, and rivers created circulation, Braudel finds the same pattern repeating at every scale: a thin, cosmopolitan, wealthy trading strip against a vast, inward-looking, poor hinterland - sixteenth-century coastal Fukien against inland Fukien, Japan's Seto no Uchi ("a tiny Japanese Mediterranean") against the rest of the archipelago, boomtown Surat on the Gulf of Cambay (with its Banyan bankers reputedly worth millions) against the huge, "untouched" areas around it. Circulation is necessary but not sufficient - it produces capitalist-looking enclaves (capital, brokers, wholesale merchants, even an "artisanal proletariat") without producing capitalism as a systemic, self-reproducing order.

Norman Jacobs relocates the explanation from culture to long-established political structure, and Braudel adopts the method while contesting the execution. Jacobs's The Origin of Modern Capitalism and Eastern Asia (1958) rejects the idea that Japanese industrial capitalism simply imitated Europe, and instead compares Japan to China (culturally close, structurally different) and to Europe (culturally distant, structurally similar) across centuries - a longue-duree procedure Braudel explicitly endorses as akin to his own habit of reaching back to Europe's eleventh century to explain modern outcomes. But Braudel also flags real defects: Jacobs takes European pre-capitalism as already understood, folds India into the Chinese model "mutatis mutandis," omits Islam entirely, and overdraws a "black/white reversed image" between China and Japan. Jacobs's instinct to look for durable, centuries-deep institutional structure behind a society's capacity (or incapacity) for capitalism resonates with world-machines's idea of deep, slow-moving infrastructure setting the possibilities of an era, though Jacobs's "structures" are specifically political and legal inheritance regimes, not the full institutions-technologies-assumptions bundle the world-machine concept names.

China had a fully developed market economy but no capitalism - the ground-level/upper-level split Braudel has argued all along, now tested at world scale. Against any suggestion that no market economy implies no capitalism, Braudel insists China's "swarming population of small artisans and itinerant merchants" and "busy shopping streets" made for a "solidly-established market economy," brisk at ground level. What China lacked was the upper storey: a centralized, Confucian-moralized bureaucracy patrolled towns street by street, controlled the entry and exit of every vessel, taxed proximity-to-market land precisely to suppress unearned advantage, and treated conspicuous individual wealth as a moral offense - mandarin fortunes (like Heshen's, confiscated and its owner put to death in 1799) died with their holder rather than passing to heirs. This is braudel-three-storey-house's market-economy/capitalism distinction confirmed outside Europe, and it is also a strikingly premodern instance of totalizing legibility - a state that patrols, registers, and taxes precisely to make wealth visible and interceptable rather than to enable its accumulation, an inversion worth noting against legibility-as-machine-core's more usual pairing of legibility with capital's expansion rather than its containment.

Japan's decentralized feudal pluralism let merchant capital take root, but the 1638 closure cut off the foreign trade capitalism needed to complete itself. Where China had one unbreachable stratum, Japan's Ashikaga-era "liberties" (guilds, free towns, merchant groups "answerable to no one") produced a genuine plurality of competing local powers, closer to Europe's fragmented feudal order than to China's totalizing state. Merchant dynasties - Konoike, Sumitomo, Mitsui - survived "far beyond the time spans suggested by Henri Pirenne," financing shoguns and daimyo alike and controlling the silver-gold-copper currency axis between Osaka and Edo. But the necessary "crucial ingredient of a capitalist superstructure - foreign trade" was severed when Japan sealed itself off after 1638; two centuries of enforced isolation held back a capitalism whose seed, in Takekoshi's image, was "the grain growing under the snow."

The real hinge, in Braudel's own synthesis, is whether wealth can accumulate and pass across generations in a competitive "snowball process" between rival classes. Europe had (with exceptions like Venice, Genoa, and Augsburg, where a merchant patriciate sat at the top from the start) two classes, nobility and bourgeoisie, each capable of durable, heritable accumulation, so that one could patiently absorb the other's waste over generations - illustrated at length through the usurious Seguier family preying on the Nevers, Montmorency, and Sully fortunes. Everywhere else this second condition failed for structural, not cultural, reasons: Chinese mandarin wealth was non-heritable, since recruitment ran by examination rather than blood; Ottoman timars and sipahiliks were "life-grants," not property, until a real feudal aristocracy belatedly entrenched itself in the Balkans; and in Mogul India, Bernier's testimony that "there are no dukes or marquises nor any family of wealthy landowners" because all land reverted to the crown on death describes what Braudel calls "a sort of perpetual New Deal, with the cards being dealt again automatically." Only India's merchant-banker class, the Banyans, proved durable enough to defend itself "with the weapon it knew best - money," as Clive's own testimony to Parliament about the corruption of young English "writers" in Bengal illustrates.

Notable passages

"A spectre haunting every form of society" (c1). Braudel opens by endorsing, with qualification, Deleuze and Guattari's image of capitalism as latent everywhere, then immediately narrows it to his real question: not whether the seeds existed, but why only Europe (and partly Japan) grew them to completion.

The Fukien/Hunan contrast (c2). Research on coastal versus inland Chinese provinces gives Braudel his clearest empirical statement of circulation as an "elementary determinism" operating "on every level and in every region of the world" - the master pattern behind every other case in the unit.

Market economy without capitalism (c3). This is the unit's theoretical center of gravity: Braudel uses China specifically to refute any equation of market economy with capitalism, insisting the two can and do come apart when the state monopolizes the upper storey.

Merchant dynasties in Japan (c4). Naming Konoike, Sumitomo, and Mitsui gives concrete, centuries-spanning weight to Braudel's claim that Japan, alone outside Europe, produced heritable merchant capital, exactly the condition he goes on to argue is decisive.

Bernier's "perpetual New Deal" (c5). A first-hand seventeenth-century witness account, used as Braudel's clinching case that Mogul India's failure to produce capitalist dynasties was a direct, mechanical consequence of non-heritable land tenure rather than any cultural deficiency.

Europe's two accumulating classes (c6). The unit's payoff sentence: Braudel's own answer to "capitalism as a world problem," stated as a positive condition (competing, wealth-transmitting classes) rather than as a list of other places' deficiencies.
