Bourgin's Fourfold Classification and the Restless World of Pre-Industry

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel adopts Hubert Bourgin's 1924 fourfold typology of pre-industrial production (family workshop, dispersed manufacture, concentrated manufactory, mechanized factory), tests it against Africa, colonial America, China and India, and then complicates it by showing pre-industry had no clean border with agriculture, was often poverty's own remedy, and was carried by a chronically mobile, insecure workforce moving between town and countryside.

Braudel first justifies calling pre-nineteenth-century manufacturing "pre-industry" rather than "industry," then borrows a 1924 model from Hubert Bourgin that sorts all manufacturing activity of the fifteenth to eighteenth centuries into four categories — the tiny family workshop, dispersed (putting-out) manufacture coordinated by a merchant-entrepreneur, concentrated manufactories under one roof, and machine-powered factories. He tests the grid outside Europe (Black Africa, colonial America, China, India), then spends the rest of the unit dismantling its tidiness: pre-industry had no settled frontier with agriculture, was frequently an improvised refuge from poverty rather than a chosen path, and was staffed by a workforce so mobile and insecure that contemporaries called artisans "rootless men."

Themes

Bourgin's four categories, and the limits of the model. Category A is the family workshop — cutler, blacksmith, cobbler, baker, butcher — undifferentiated, "monocellular," and half outside the market economy. Category B is dispersed manufacture: individual workshops spread over a wide area but coordinated by a merchant-entrepreneur who advances raw material, routes it through spinner, weaver, fuller, dyer and shearer, and pockets the final profit. Category C is concentrated manufacture (forges, breweries, tanneries, state and private manufactories) bringing labor under one roof for supervision and division of labor. Category D is the machine-powered factory, which Braudel explicitly places outside this book's chronology except as an early anticipation (Agricola's sixteenth-century mines, Cantabrian water power, the Saardam naval yards). Crucially, Braudel uses a manufactory/factory survey from Ansbach-Bayreuth to insist the four types were "roughly chronological" but did not supersede one another in a clean sequence — siding with Sombart against Marx that there was "no natural and logical transition from the manufactory to the factory."

Testing the model against the world, and the limits of the possible. Extending Bourgin's grid outside Europe, Braudel finds Categories A and B dominant almost everywhere (Black Africa's blacksmiths and weavers, the craft base of colonial Mexico and Peru), with Category C appearing chiefly in China and India — Songjiang's 200,000 cotton workers, Suzhou's thousands of silk looms, Jingdezhen's three thousand porcelain furnaces. Yet this same skilled, ingenious Chinese and Indian workforce used startlingly simple tools: an Indian traveller marvels that local crafts "employ few machines using only their hands and two or three tools to work with, where we would use over a hundred," and Braudel lingers on the portable Chinese blacksmith's forge and the tree-mounted horizontal loom. He reads this not as backwardness but as a rational response to cheap, abundant labor — citing Montesquieu's complaint that machines are "pernicious" because they idle workmen, and the Encyclopedie's converse claim that dear labor forces mechanization ("wherever labour is dear, it must be supplemented by machines"). This is effectively a restatement, applied to manufacturing, of the limits of the possible explored for material life in Vol. I — limits-of-the-possible — where available technique tracks relative factor cost rather than a society's inventiveness. The comparative sweep across Africa, colonial America, China and India is also a clean instance of Braudel's world-scale method, one of the through-lines the World Machines project watches for braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1.

No border between agriculture and industry. Braudel insists pre-industry was "barely distinguishable from the omnipresent agricultural life" around it. Winter cottage industry across Europe was dictated by the farming "calendar": harvest and haymaking always took priority over the loom, coalminers left the pits in August, and Vauban reckoned the artisan worked only about 120 days a year. Attempts to overturn the rhythm (Rouen's 1723 order forbidding textile work during harvest, or the reverse case of continuous weaving threatening the wheat crop) show the boundary was porous and contested rather than fixed by geography, against both Goudar's industry/agriculture split and Roger Dion's line dividing craft-north from wine-south France.

Industry as poverty's remedy, not choice. Repeatedly, pre-industry appears where agriculture could not support the population: Lucca's silk trade grew from a shortage of land ("the Republic of Ants"), Norfolk's stocking industry absorbed fisherwomen idle outside the herring season, Russia's craft industry took root among the poorest "black" peasantry, and Swabian and Silesian mountain linen-weavers or Scottish crofters-turned-coal-miners compensated for meagre farmland. Savary des Bruslons's line — "the prodigies of industry spring from the heart of necessity" — is the unit's thesis in miniature: Colbert's supposed genius mattered less than taxation and hardship in pushing France toward manufacturing.

A workforce that could not put down roots. Because artisans depended entirely on market wages rather than land, they were, in Mirabeau's phrase, essentially "rootless men," prone to sudden migration whenever wages fell or manufactories closed — a pattern illustrated by Reims, London, and Languedoc weavers "without bread" forced abroad, and by full-blown flights after the Revocation of the Edict of Nantes or famine in New Spain. State policy toward this mobility itself shifted with the century: draconian pursuit and forced repatriation of runaway workers gave way, by 1757, to an official order abandoning the chase as a waste of public money — a small but telling sign of eighteenth-century industrial expansion overtaking older controls.

Town and countryside as two sounding-boards. Van Houtte's study of the Netherlands traces industry's centuries-long pendulum between town and countryside, driven partly by guild rigidity and fiscal regimes. Domenico Sella's account of Venice around 1590-1630 shows the same dynamic sharpened into crisis: facing prohibitive urban wages, Venetian industry pursued rural putting-out, luxury specialization, and hydraulic mechanization simultaneously — three different answers to one factor-cost problem, reinforcing the unit's broader point that available equipment and organization were chosen, not given.

Notable passages

The Category A definition (c1) gives Bourgin's baseline unit — the family workshop "half outside the market economy" — against which everything else in the unit is a departure. The Category B passage (c2) isolates the merchant-entrepreneur who profits from coordination alone, a miniature of the "anti-market" capitalism braudel-three-storey-house that sits above the transparent market economy the project's context distinguishes. The China/India tools passage (c3) is the clearest statement of the limits-of-the-possible argument: skill and ingenuity did not translate into equipment where labor was cheap. The calendar passage (c4) and the Savary des Bruslons quotation (c5) together make the case that pre-industry was seasonal and poverty-driven rather than a separate, freely chosen economic sector. The Mirabeau quotation (c6) supplies the unit's most vivid image of the pre-industrial workforce's structural precarity.
