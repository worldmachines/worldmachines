Society as a 'Set of Sets': Hierarchy, Pluralism, and the Vertical Elevation of the Few

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Opening Chapter 5, Braudel defines society as a 'set of sets' of interacting economic, hierarchical, political and cultural systems, argues that every society is at once inescapably hierarchical and internally plural (illustrated through feudal Europe's five coexisting 'societies'), and closes with a cross-continental statistical survey showing that wealth and power concentrate in a strikingly small, self-perpetuating elite almost everywhere.

Opening Chapter 5 of Volume II, fernand-braudel pivots from the economic argument of the preceding chapters to lay out his working model of "society" itself: he defines it as a "set of sets" — a superordinate collection of variables (economy, social hierarchy, the state, culture) that can never be fully separated from one another — then works through two further claims: that every historical society, however small or "primitive," is inescapably hierarchical, yet that hierarchy is never a single clean order but a plurality of coexisting sub-societies; and, in a closing statistical survey drawn from Venice to Poland to Mughal India, that power and wealth have concentrated in an astonishingly small elite (often under 5%, sometimes under 1%, of the population) across virtually every documented society — a "law" he flags as empirically undeniable but theoretically unexplained.

Themes

Society as an irreducible "set of sets." Reacting against Georges Gurvitch's 'société globale' (a single transparent "container") and against disciplinary carve-ups (economics vs. sociology, Marx's infrastructure/superstructure, Trevelyan's "history of the people with the politics left out"), Braudel proposes treating total society as a mathematics-borrowed "set of sets": economy, social hierarchy, politics/state, and culture, each an internally coherent "system" but none reducible to, or fully separable from, the others. He is explicit this is a heuristic, not a metaphysics — "the point of a definition is to provide an approach to a problem" — but insists any one of the four sets "requires and implies the existence of other collections of variables outside itself." This four-part decomposition sits alongside, but is distinct from, the storeys framework — material life / market economy / capitalism — that structures the rest of braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1 and braudel-three-storey-house; here Braudel cuts the same total-history problem along a different axis (synchronic domains within one society, not vertical zones of exchange).

No sector claims permanent primacy — though economy tends to dominate post-1500 Europe. Braudel refuses "a priori" that any one set (state, economy, culture) permanently outranks the others; he specifically rejects "the sacrosanct primacy of the state." Yet he concedes a historically specific asymmetry: in Europe after the eleventh–twelfth century, and "even more markedly" after the sixteenth, "a rapidly-developing economy often seems to have dominated other sectors," forcing other domains "to define themselves in relation to itself" — a dynamic he ties to "the precocious modernity of the continent." This is a genuinely qualified, historically-bounded claim about shifting structural dominance, not a fixed causal hierarchy; it loosely rhymes with world-machines's picture of coexisting infrastructures whose relative dominance shifts over time, though the fit is partial — Braudel's "sets" are synchronic subsystems within one society, not ~1000-year civilizational machines, so the resonance is one of shape (irreducible plurality, shifting not fixed primacy) rather than mechanism.

Hierarchy is universal, but never simple. Every documented society, Braudel argues, is "openly hierarchical" — even Berber clans that "had no King" (per startled Portuguese observers in 1446) or Formosan villagers "at war... one village against another" (per the Dutch in 1630) turn out, on closer inspection, to have chiefs and structure; "even the gods of Olympus had their hierarchy." But this vertical fact coexists with a horizontal one: societies are "diversified pluralities," internally divided against themselves. His extended example is "feudal" society (a label he uses reluctantly, following Bloch and Febvre's preference for restricting the word to the fief itself): following Gurvitch's reading of Bloch, he decomposes it into at least five coexisting "societies" — seigniorial, theocratic (the Church), the nascent territorial state, feudalism proper (the lordly hierarchy), and the towns — which "managed perfectly well to live together, mingled their elements" rather than remaining foreign to one another as Gurvitch supposed. Braudel treats the towns as the pivotal fifth term, "the prototypes of modern times."

The vertical cut: a near-invariant law of the tiny elite. Having established that hierarchy always coexists with plurality, Braudel takes "a vertical section through society as a whole" and finds, in every case he can document, "the fundamental inequality of both wealth and power dividing the mass from top to bottom" — a "structural law that admits of no exception." He marshals a dense comparative run of figures: Venetian Nobili at roughly 5% of the population before 1575 (collapsing to a bare 14–15 people fit for high office after the 1630 plague); Genoa's ruling nobility at 700 men out of some 80,000 inhabitants; Nuremberg's 150–200 patricians out of 40,000; fewer than 200 merchants running London in 1603; the Dutch Regents at 10,000 out of two million; oligarchies of a few dozen families in Lyons, Antwerp, Seville, Le Mans, Dunkerque and Marseilles; England's roughly 150 truly powerful families per Gregory King's and Massie's estimates; and Poland's unusually broad (8–10%) but mostly impoverished nobility. He extends the comparison outward — Peter the Great's nobles, Chinese mandarins, Japanese daimyos, Mughal rajahs — as evidence of a cross-civilizational constant. Crucially, Braudel refuses to explain the constant away: "we are once more stating a fact without entirely understanding it," and he closes by weighing Marx's "needs," Fourquet's "desires," and finally Weber's "political qualifications of the dominant... classes" as competing, incomplete accounts. This is longue-durée structural analysis in the register of limits-of-the-possible: a near-universal ceiling on how thinly power can be spread, holding across centuries and continents almost regardless of political form, offered as an observed constant rather than a mechanism.

Notable passages

The chapter's methodological core is the "set of sets" definition (c1): a deliberately provisional, mathematics-borrowed heuristic for holding economy, hierarchy, state and culture together without collapsing one into another.

Braudel's claim that hierarchy is a cultural universal (c2) uses two colonial "no-king" anecdotes — the Portuguese among Saharan Berber traders in 1446, the Dutch among Formosan villagers in 1630 — precisely to show that apparent statelessness still yields clans, chiefs, and organized warfare: structure by another name.

The pluralism thesis condenses into one aphoristic passage (c3) — "not one society then but several... We must think of everything in the plural" — the chapter's hinge between the hierarchy argument and the feudal-Europe case study that precedes it.

The "vertical elevation" section-opener (c4) states the chapter's second thesis in Braudel's most exposed rhetorical register: power as something that must be "captured" at "the commanding heights of society."

Finally, the Venice/Genoa comparison (c5) is the leading edge of the chapter's evidentiary run — the passage where the "insidious law giving power to the few" stops being an assertion and becomes a demonstration, anchored in named, dated, quantified cases.
