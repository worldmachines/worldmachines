Individual Firms and the Three Generations of Commercial Association

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel traces the individual firm's legal evolution from the medieval societas maris and family compagnia through the limited partnership to the joint stock company, then argues that formal progress far outran practice: most firms stayed small, family-run, and patriarchal until the state itself drove a genuine increase in scale.

Braudel surveys the legal and organizational history of the Western merchant firm — from the medieval two-person maritime partnership through the family-based compagnia and the limited partnership to the joint stock company — and closes by arguing that this tidy sequence of "three generations" describes the law far better than it describes practice: outside a handful of large houses and state-linked ventures, firms stayed tiny, family-run, and nearly patriarchal households well into the eighteenth century.

Themes

The individual firm is a market-economy instrument; the great trading company belongs to capitalism proper. Braudel opens by insisting the two must not be conflated: the individual firm lies at the very heart of capitalism as a form, but it is the big chartered companies (the East India Companies) that are entangled with both capital and the state, forcing capitalists to submit, protest, or withdraw as state power grew. This is his three-storey-house distinction in miniature — braudel-three-storey-house — the individual firm as ground-and-first-floor market activity, the state-entangled company as the second-floor "anti-market" zone of privilege and monopoly.

From commenda to compagnia: mobilizing capital through kinship. The earliest form, the societas maris (also collegantia or commenda), paired a stay-at-home financier (socius stans) with a traveling partner (socius tractator) for a single voyage — Braudel notes it blurs any clean division of capital from labour, since the tractator usually put in money too. Inland cities without direct sea access instead produced the compagnia, a family firm (cum panis, "with bread") bound by joint-and-several liability "ad infinitum." Its family character was compatible with real scale: the Bardi held the whole of Christendom in fee, and Germany's Ravensburg-based Magna Societas ran eighty partners and subsidiaries from Genoa to Cologne to Paris for a century and a half.

The limited partnership formalizes — and sometimes conceals — liability. The commandite let passive investors cap their exposure to their stake while active partners retained full liability, a change Braudel treats as resolving new problems of long-distance, discreet partnership. But its legibility cut both ways: the same instrument that made capital and liability calculable also let concealment-seeking parties disappear from the record — foreign merchants evading national shipping law, colonial officials hiding commercial interests, nobles protecting their rank. Braudel's own aside ("Paris was not London or Amsterdam") makes the discretion, not just the liability cap, the point of the French uptake — a reminder that a legibility-as-machine-core-style instrument can be adopted precisely to produce opacity for some parties even as it renders others' stakes newly calculable.

The joint stock company: capital detached from persons, but slow to spread. Where the family firm and the limited partnership are still associations of people, the joint stock company is an association of capital alone, formed into a single mass in which partners hold shares. Braudel pushes the form's ancestry back centuries before the Muscovy Company (1553-5) — ship-shares in Venice, Genoa and Marseilles, mine shares in Siena and Styria, mill shares in Toulouse traded as real estate — but insists that outside a few privileged companies the form spread extremely slowly, especially in France, where even the vocabulary (action, actionnaire) lagged the practice and carried a pejorative tone.

Formal progress outpaced practice: most firms never left the family household. Braudel's closing section undercuts the whole "three generations" schema: a Paris counting-house employed twenty or thirty people at most, servants and apprentices lodged with the merchant and ate at his table, and even under Queen Victoria big trading firms opened the day with family prayers. Real growth in scale, he concludes, came only where the state itself was involved. This is Braudel's own version of a limits-of-the-possible argument: legal and institutional innovation (limited liability, negotiable shares) existed for centuries before it could dislodge an entrenched, small-scale, patriarchal default. It also half-rhymes with the divergence-machine's picture of proliferation resisting standardization — though that machine is a modern (1600–2000) creature and Braudel's small firms predate it by centuries; the resonance is in the pattern (formal legibility available but widely unused), not the period.

Notable passages

The opening distinction between firms and companies sets up the unit's stakes and is the clearest single statement of Braudel's market/capitalism split here (c1). The commenda passage (c2) shows the earliest, simplest partnership form and Braudel's aside on why it resists a clean capital/labour reading. The compagnia passage (c3) traces the family firm from its "bread-sharing" etymology to the Bardi's continent-spanning power. The limited-partnership passage (c4) gives the 1673 ordinance's liability-capping language alongside a concrete 1786 Marseilles contract for a female partner, showing the doctrine made operational. The joint-stock passage (c5) states the form's definition and its deep, often-overlooked medieval antecedents. The closing passage (c6) delivers Braudel's real thesis for the unit — the family-patriarchal household persisted underneath the formal legal evolution, and only the state broke the pattern.
