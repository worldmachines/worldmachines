Raynal's History of the Indies: why it caught fire, slavery, and the author's end

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley argues that Raynal's History of the Two Indies owed its vast popularity to feeding the age's hatred of religion and its passion for justice and freedom, made subject races, slavery and the slave-trade a matter of right and duty in French opinion, and ended with Raynal's recoil from the Revolution in 1791 and his impoverished death.

Morley continues his account of Raynal's History. He claims the book's deeper popularity lay in supplying fuel to two "master emotions" of the age: hatred of religion and the passion for justice and freedom. He compares Raynal's use of the Spanish conquest against Catholicism to Gibbon's use of church history. He then turns to the book's positive side, its engravings and its sermons on Negroes and Indians, sets Raynal's furious tone on slavery against Montesquieu's more analytic one, quotes Raynal's appeal to kings and his prophecy of a black avenger, and closes with Raynal's 1791 letter to the National Assembly against the Revolution's excesses and his death in poverty in 1796.

Themes

The popularity had a deeper source than scandal (Morley's thesis). Morley says the commercial and political material was only "setting and framework"; the real subjects were anti-religious feeling and the passion for justice. Attacks on the Church were by now "cheap", so the book needed the eighteenth century's positive side: zeal for humanity, better laws and the amelioration of the common lot. This matches the frame of the whole study, a movement of ideas (compare intellectual-vs-revolutionary, history-machine) preparing a revolution, though Morley's moralised, positivist rhetoric of "zeal" is not WMP's structural vocabulary.

Colonial cruelty as an indictment of Catholicism. Morley judges the Spanish conquest "profoundly instructive" about papal Christianity's narrowness, and says it made extermination or enslavement a duty to the Church. He hedges as a "philosophic historian": Aztec human sacrifice was worse in some respects, and Las Casas, whom Raynal also recalls, was a Catholic. He ties the 16th-century failure to the creed that murdered Calas and La Barre. This is Morley's own judgment, and a polemical one; he also says the doctrine of obligations to lower races had not yet taken its place in Europe, and uses the phrase "lower races" himself, with its Victorian hierarchy intact.

Humanitarian sentiment as political education. The book, in Morley's view, brought "the lower races finally within the pale of right and duty" in French opinion, and the same movement intensified the notion of rights for the suffering people of France. He claims the sentiment shaped the youth of those who carried it into revolution some twenty years later. It was popular and concrete where Rousseau's Social Contract was concentrated but dreary to many (see jean-jacques-rousseau). The engravings (Auri sacra fames, Nature nursing white and black infants, Inkle and Yarico, Quakers) show a sentimental, visual rhetoric.

Raynal against Montesquieu: revolt against the historic method. Morley contrasts Montesquieu's rational account of slavery's growth with Raynal's refusal to treat it as anything but an offence to reason. In the quoted passage Raynal invokes Clarissa and Lovelace, the right of the enslaved to kill, and the claim that the oppressor will be a victim in turn. Raynal looks to kings for redress, as Turgot and the Economists did (a hedge on enlightened despotism), and calls for a black Spartacus: "how terrible will the White Code be!" Morley says such words acted upon Toussaint.

Raynal's retreat in 1791. Morley contrasts Raynal with Condorcet (see nicolas-de-condorcet), who kept faith with the movement. He calls Raynal "of weaker mould", with a "stratum of cant and borrowed formulas" in his eloquence. Raynal's May 31, 1791 letter attacked a government "enslaved by popular tyranny", defended persecuted priests, and served Malouet's constitutional royalists. Robespierre excused it as the apostasy of age. Raynal lost his property and died in poverty in 1796. Morley closes on sympathy: he had been "a powerful pleader for noble causes".

Notable passages

  • Two master emotions (c1). Morley's explanation of the book's popularity, and the Gibbon parallel, with the observation that Raynal "invented" an imaginary happy native simplicity as a reproach to Christianity. Morley treats this as a rhetorical device, not ethnography.
  • Spanish conquerors and Catholic duty (c2). Morley's anti-Catholic reading of conquest, including the charge that Christianity "nursed a still more cruel passion".
  • The book's moral effect (c3). The claim that Raynal's work brought the lower races within right and duty, and the engravings that set its keynote.
  • Slavery and Montesquieu (c4). Raynal's impassioned attack, set against the historic method, with a dagger-stroke for apologists.
  • Kings and the new Spartacus (c5). Appeal to kings, the prophecy of black vengeance and the link to Toussaint.
  • Raynal's end (c6). The 1791 letter, Robespierre's reply, and the abject death.

For WMP, the unit shows ideas circulating through a print network (eisenstein-printing-revolution) and a moral sentiment shaping a coming rupture, with a Victorian author reading it as a staged prelude to the Revolution; the 1791 recoil of a precursor is a small case study in how Enlightenment critics related to the revolution they prepared.
