Diderot's Salons: Greuze, the Moral Picture and the Critic's Digressions

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley surveys Diderot's Salon criticism of Greuze, Boucher, Fragonard and others, arguing that Diderot's sentimental moral aesthetic, technical knowledge, improvising 'emendations' and free digressions made him a vivid critic and a philosophical thinker about art.

Morley presents Diderot as an art critic. Greuze, whose domestic, moral, pathetic subjects Diderot adored, is his chief favourite, and the Accordée de Village (1761) is the showpiece. Diderot's exhortation to Greuze to "fais de la morale en peinture" is set against Hogarth, whose ferocity Morley thinks would have revolted him. Boucher, the idol of the age, is the one painter Diderot never spares; Fragonard falls from favour once he turns toward Boucher's manner. Morley then turns to Diderot's method: his technical knowledge, his habit of improving pictures by imagining how they should have been painted, his moral asides and digressions, and the philosophical side of his writing on art (the Vernet dialogues of 1767). Morley's own judgments (that mythological allegory is dead, that Hogarth would have horrified Diderot, that the digressions are a source of charm) are mixed with Diderot's quoted views, and the two are kept distinct below.

Themes

Art as moral and sentimental instruction. Diderot values subjects of the family, the common people and "l'honnête". Morley says Paris, "weary of mythology and sated with pretty indecencies", welcomed Greuze's simple village story in the year of the New Heloïsa. Diderot's own demand is that painting join dramatic poetry in "instructing us, correcting us, inviting us to virtue". Morley's gloss (his judgment, not Diderot's) is that Diderot would improve men by "warming and softening the domestic affections" rather than by Hogarthian horror. This is sensibility as a social technology; it rhymes with the modernity-machine's project of moral improvement, though nothing in the text frames it that way.

Criticism: its ease and its cost. Chardin's remonstrance (a contemporary voice, quoted) reminds critics of the labour behind even bad pictures. Diderot half-agrees, calling criticism a "foolish occupation" while still dismissing incompetence (Parrocel, Brenet). Morley adds his own moral: the wise do not reproach a man for what he cannot do.

Taste against the fashionable. Boucher earns an income of fifty thousand livres; Diderot concedes his fecundity but wants discipline and severity, and still admits he would like to own one of his pictures. Fragonard loses Diderot's favour by drifting from "l'honnête" and "le beau moral" (the "omelette d'enfants" jibe). Morley treats the moral criterion as Diderot's governing one.

Technical knowledge and the critic as emender. Morley says Diderot, unlike later critics, studied the painter's material conditions, extracting technique from artists as he did from weavers and dyers for the Encyclopédie. His characteristic move is to rewrite a picture: the Psyche of Vien, the Flight into Egypt, Deshays's Saint Benedict (altering one figure alters all, "the force of unity"). Morley criticises Diderot's own advice to La Grenée (Mars and Venus for Peace) as trapped in dead allegory, and prefers the "Honest Model" subject given to Greuze as more in Diderot's vein, though he calls it "commonplace sentimentalism".

Digression as method. Morley argues that Diderot's moral asides (the Calas apostrophe, Van Loo's lost friend, the silence of birds under a cloud, the three classes of a nation, the unreliability of language) reproduce the free movement of his mind. They are a source of charm rather than disorder. One quoted aside, that a man ahead of his generation should be thankful for oblivion, is a small theory of how minds above the common level go unperceived. Another, "Nos qualités tiennent de prés à nos défauts", is called by Morley the base of wise tolerance.

Art criticism as philosophy. Morley closes by claiming that Diderot was also a thinker about the abstract laws of art, with a gift for "analysis, comparison, classification", citing the Vernet landscape dialogues with an abbé, which turn out to be invented from the sea-pieces exhibited.

Notable passages

  • The Accordée de Village (c1): Morley's account of why the picture's story-telling and gentle emotion won Paris, tied to the sentimental moment of Rousseau's New Heloïsa.
  • "Courage, my good Greuze" (c2): Diderot's programmatic call for moral painting, with Morley's Hogarth contrast.
  • Boucher (c3): the one painter Diderot never spares, and the ambivalent concession.
  • The digressions (c4): Morley's defence of Diderot's asides and the Greuze vanity example.
  • The Honest Model (c5): Diderot's own invention of a sentimental subject, as Morley's example of his best vein.
  • Vien's Psyche (c6): the specimen of Diderot's criticism, imagining the scene as lived (the mother with a lamp at a cradle).
