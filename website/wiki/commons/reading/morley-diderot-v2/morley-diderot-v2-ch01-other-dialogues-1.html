Diderot's moral dialogues: the Father's law-abiding conscience and the fickle public judgment

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley summarizes two minor Diderot dialogues, the Conversation of a Father with his Children (law versus private judgment of special cases) and On the Inconsistency of the Public Judgment on our Private Actions (the Desroches story), praising their dramatic ease while faulting Diderot's satire of 'Society' as unable to improve anyone.

Morley moves from the larger works to two short dialogues in which Diderot "puts questions of morals in an interesting way". The first, the Conversation of a Father with his Children (published 1773), stages a family evening in which the elder Diderot recounts nearly burning a forgotten will that would have enriched a wealthy Paris bookseller at the expense of poor kinsfolk. Interleaved cases follow: the doctor who cures a condemned criminal, the journeyman tempted to keep his dead wife's marriage portion, adultery and the freeing of the other spouse, and the cobbler of Messina who becomes a private executioner of criminals. The father and the abbe side with the supremacy of law; the younger Diderot argues for the sage's discretion. The second piece, published in 1798 under the title On the inconsistency of the Public Judgment on our Private Actions, tells of Desroches, whose infidelity is followed by the deaths of his wife, child, mother-in-law and brother-in-law, and whom the public condemns by an ever-shifting measure. Morley then offers his own verdicts: he admires the form, thinks Diderot's irony 'fails to press to the root of the matter', and uses the second dialogue to contrast French denunciation of Society with English appeal to the Individual.

Themes

Law against private judgment (Diderot's father, the abbe, and the casuist). In the Father's tale the curé Bouin repeatedly asks "Who authorised you" to interpret the dead man's intentions, weigh the justice of a bequest, or decide what the testator owed. These are views voiced inside Diderot's dialogue, not Morley's thesis, though Morley endorses the conclusion: the abbé defends the law because evading it in one case "is to open the door to the sophistries of all the knaves in the universe". The younger Diderot's closing whisper that "there are no laws for the sage" is answered by the father: he would not live in a town where all thought that way.

Morley's historical diagnosis: casuistry and the old regime. Morley claims (his own judgment) that the 'moral corruption of France' under the old regime owed something to Jesuit directors who made moral rules 'flexible and conditional'; habitual loose superseding of principles in individual cases produces demoralisation after generations, 'just in proportion as the principles are sound'. He reads the dialogue's younger Diderot as holding the casuist attitude while leaving its refutation to the father. This is an explicit link from a private-conscience dialogue to the long-run decay of institutional legitimacy, a half-rhyme with the history-machine idea of slow, distributed assembly of a rupture, though Morley's frame is moral-causal and Jesuit-centred rather than infrastructural.

The cobbler of Messina. A vigilante who, in the face of corrupt judges and a negligent viceroy, runs a secret court and kills about fifty criminals is judged by the father and abbe to be 'simply a murderer'. The case frames the question of who may substitute private justice for failing public institutions.

Public judgment as shifting, imagination-driven. In the Desroches dialogue Diderot attacks the 'imbecile crowd' that measures praise and blame by the 'shifting event', holding Desroches answerable for deaths he could not foresee. Morley concedes the logic is indefensible but replies (his own view) that people have 'imagination as well as reason', and that verdicts often really express that a person's 'associations have become disagreeable and doubtful to them', not a judicial sentence. This is a small theory of reputation and collective opinion as an affective, not rational, mechanism.

French denunciation of Society versus English individual responsibility. Morley argues that improvement comes from 'broader views, higher motives, and sounder habits of judgment' addressed to each person, not from satire against Society, which is 'only a name for other people'. He contrasts Diderot and Victor Hugo with Milton and Mill. This is Victorian liberal-individualist framing and should be read as Morley's own position, not Diderot's; it also sits near the WMP interest in whether ideas change systems top-down or person by person, cf. intellectual-vs-revolutionary, though Morley is not discussing revolution here.

Sociability and the limits of Stoic consolation. Morley observes that Diderot, typical of the 'immense sociability of the eighteenth century', never treats exclusion from the circle of friends as no evil; life for Diderot was 'an agitated scene of living passion'. Morley closes with a Latin quotation (of an unnamed 'good man') on the emptiness of rhetorical consolation to the afflicted.

Notable passages

The curé Bouin exchange is the core of the Father dialogue: a chain of questions that ends 'No one has any right to infringe the laws, to enter into the intention of the dead, or to dispose of other people's property.' It is a rule-based answer to a sympathetic case.

The cobbler and the concluding whisper gather Morley's reading of the whole piece: the abbé's verdict, the sage's exemption, the father's reply, and Morley's casuistry-and-decay diagnosis.

The Desroches condemnation gives Diderot's catalogue of the public's fickleness ('The important thing is, not to know, but to talk').

Morley's critique of Diderot's method (opening a subject brilliantly but not leaving it 'readjusted') and his 'Society is after all only a name for other people' passage are the unit's main evaluative claims; the Turgot motto 'A great enemy to indifference, a great friend to indulgence' is quoted contemporary testimony offered as an ideal.

The 'Life was real to Diderot' passage defines, in Morley's terms, what separates Diderot from Stoic and homiletic consolation.
