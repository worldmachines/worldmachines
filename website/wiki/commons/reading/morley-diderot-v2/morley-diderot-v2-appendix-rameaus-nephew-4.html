Rameau's Nephew (translated), part 4: the parasite's creed, the fool's wisdom, and the expulsion

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In this stretch of Morley's translation of Diderot's dialogue, Rameau's nephew (He) explains to the philosopher (I) how parasites flatter the rich, how he reads moralists to master the tone of vice, why he cannot be a sage, and how he was thrown out of his patrons' house.

This is a middle stretch of Morley's English translation of Diderot's dialogue Le Neveu de Rameau; the words are Diderot's, not Morley's argument, and Morley (per the brief) has omitted passages on obscure personages and technical music, so ellipses mark cuts. The speakers are _I._ (the philosopher-narrator) and _He._ (Rameau's nephew, a failed musician and professional hanger-on). He mocks the actresses Dangeville and Clairon and admits the mockery is the patter retailed to a patroness; he sketches the table of a rich house as a pack of famished beasts who abuse Buffon, Montesquieu, Rousseau, Voltaire, D'Alembert and Diderot, and links this to Palissot's play The Philosophers; he explains how he reads Theophrastus, La Bruyere and Moliere; he argues that being the great man's fool is the best role going; he narrates the quarrel over the abbe (the "Observateur litteraire" priest) that got him expelled; and he closes with a theory of a "tacit compact" between patrons and parasites, policed by nature as a second public prosecutor. I presses him on sincerity, benefaction and virtue; He answers with a candid interest-based amoralism.

Themes

Flattery as the social currency. He holds that society swallows "any lie that flatters us" and takes truth "drop by drop"; the parasite's craft is supplying the lie. I treats this as either irony or knavery and asks whether anyone dares dissent; He says this is "the sentiment and language of the whole of society" (He's claim, not I's, and not Morley's).

Reading moralists for tone, not conscience. He's reading of L'Avare and Tartufe is inverted: "Keep the vices that are useful to thee, but avoid their tone and the appearances that would make thee laughable." He distinguishes vice from the characteristics of vice, and boasts he does systematically what others do by instinct. I's expected answer ("the love of virtue, the hatred of vice") is deflated.

Interest as the measure of virtue. He claims there is "nothing absolutely, essentially, and universally true or false" in manners, only what interest requires; had virtue led to fortune he would have been virtuous or feigned it. Vice and virtue may be swapped in vocabulary if "we came to explanations". This is the sharpest ethical claim of the unit, a relativism voiced by a self-confessed rogue, set against I's conventional moral language.

The fool and the sage. The king's fool existed; the "king's sage" never did. He argues that whoever keeps a fool is no sage, and may be the fool of his own fool. This reverses the hierarchy of patron and dependent and parallels the philosophe's own uneasy dependence on the powerful.

Patron and parasite as a compact. The household is a school of "humanity" and a menagerie; He claims a tacit compact (patrons know what they take in) and that nature, the second public prosecutor, punishes vices the laws miss: "Give yourself up to debauchery, and you will end with dropsy". I counters that benefactors should not be ridiculed; He replies it is folly, not wickedness, that deserves accusation.

Satire of the philosophes' enemies. The table talk names Palissot, Freron, Dorat, the abbes Le Blanc, d'Olivet and Batteux, and Robbe, showing a literary underworld that lives by insulting the Encyclopedie's figures. I's reply is that being insulted by such people is an honour.

Resonance with World Machines (hedged). The compact and its policing by nature loosely echo themes of institutional dependency and the interested social machinery of an era, and the insistence that morality tracks interest rather than principle has a distant kinship with the utility theme of this volume; but the text is a satirical dialogue, not an analysis of institutions, and nothing here argues for these frames. For the wider setting see voltaire and jean-jacques-rousseau, both named in the quarrel as targets of abuse, and counter-enlightenment for the attacks on the philosophes that Palissot's play represents (the dialogue itself does not use that frame).

Notable passages

Why flattery works (c1). He's claim that society swallows lies that flatter it and sips bitter truths, with I's astonishment.

The infamous table (c2). He describes the "school of humanity" of fallen poets, musicians and actresses, famishing like wolves and tigers and abusing the great names of the Enlightenment; Palissot's The Philosophers is said to follow this plan.

Reading Moliere for the tone of vice (c3). The inversion of moral instruction: be a hypocrite, but do not talk like one.

Fool, sage and interest (c4). The reversal of the king's fool, the relativism of manners, and the swapping of virtue and vice.

The expulsion scene (c5). The brief repartee ("Rameau, you are an impertinent." "I know I am, and it is on that condition that I was received here.") shows He's self-awareness about his role.

The tacit compact and nature's prosecutors (c6). The menagerie analogy and nature as the prosecutor of the vices the laws miss.
