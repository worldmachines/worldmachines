Holbach's System of Nature, concluded: a social ethic, a thorough atheism, a revolutionary polemic

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley closes his account of Holbach's System of Nature by defending its social ethics against the charge of egoism, faulting it as unhistorical, and then presenting its second-half assault on theism and its antithesis of Nature and Religion as the polemic that fed a political revolution.

Morley finishes his exposition of Holbach's System of Nature (1770) in three movements. First, the end of Part I: its defence of suicide and contempt for death, and its ethics, which Morley insists is a social and not a selfish hedonism, though badly thought out and blind to moral history. Second, Part II on the Deity, an attack on theism itself rather than merely on Church or Christianity, which Morley rates as unsurpassed in vigour but unphilosophic in tone, with Holbach's refutation of the a priori proofs and his defence of atheism against the charge of denying a moving force in nature. Third, the famous antithesis of Nature and Religion, which Morley reads as a fair account of the Encyclopaedists versus the French Catholic Church of the day, and a coda on Holbach as propagandist who died in 1789. The frame throughout is Morley's own: the book is "not so much philosophy as the literature of a political revolution".

Themes

Death, suicide and the revolutionary temper. Holbach (the author Morley is expounding) argues with "some malice" that Samson, Eleazar and Christ were suicides, and that fear of death makes only "cowards" or "fanatics or melancholy pietists". Morley's own gloss links this to the revolutionary generation, citing the suicides of Condorcet, Roland, Valazé and others, as proof the doctrine "had entered their souls". This is Morley's historical inference, not Holbach's claim. See nicolas-de-condorcet.

Ethics: universalistic, not egoistic, hedonism. Morley defends Holbach against the charge of reducing virtue to far-sighted egoism. Using Sidgwick's classification, he says Holbach's real base is "the happiness of the greatest number"; virtue means placing one's interest in what accords with the interest of others, and "no man can be happy alone". Morley adds a sociological reading: in a society kept miserable by a privileged few, this principle was "the indispensable weapon" and "the ethical side of every popular and democratic movement"; the Church, nominally built on the Golden Rule, had become "the great organ of sinister self-interest", while the Atheists taught public spirit, which "could only mean revolution". This resonates with the utility-and-legislation line from Helvetius to Bentham, treated as social machinery, though Morley does not use that language.

Morley's criticisms: no analysis, no history. Morley judges that Holbach fused several distinct questions (sanctions, motive, the mark of right and wrong, the origin of conscience) under one "basis of morals", and ignored the cumulative inheritance by which one generation's moral gains become the next's starting point. He says this is inconsistent with Holbach's own doctrine of necessity: conscience should have been treated as imposed by the past rather than calculated personally. Morley's frame here is Victorian evolutionary and positivist. He also reads the System as, in substance, "a protest against ascetic and quietist ideals", citing Holbach's "action is the true element of the human mind".

Part II: the attack on theism itself. Morley says Holbach condemns "theism, in its purest as in its impurest shape", and that no more "elaborate, trenchant, and unflinching attack" on the foundations of theology exists, cosmological, ethical and metaphysical. His reservations: the tone is not "truly philosophic" because Holbach treats God as a morbid excrescence and never tries to understand the human needs behind theology, and he omits theology's contributions to morality and civilisation. This is Morley's verdict, offered with praise and censure together.

The a priori proofs and incommensurable ways of thinking. Holbach rejects the proofs of Descartes, Samuel Clarke, Malebranche and Newton by repudiating any a priori basis for belief; experience is the "source and limit" of knowledge. Morley cites Locke's remark that Descartes' argument allows "senseless matter" to be the first eternal being. His own comment is that the two sides argued "on two different planes of thought" with no common criterion, and that the force of Holbach's attack lay in exposing the gap between the seventeenth-century notion of demonstration and the eighteenth-century one. Compare isaac-newton, baruch-spinoza and enlightenment-monism; the half-fit is that Holbach's matter in motion is a closed, necessitarian nature, which Morley reports without endorsing.

Defence of atheism. Morley credits Holbach with rebutting the charge that atheists deny a moving force or attribute everything to chance. If by Atheist you mean someone denying a force inherent in matter, "an Atheist would be a madman"; atheists perceive only "matter, essentially active and mobile" and decline to attach that force to a being outside the whole. Matter in motion under fixed laws is the materialist core; closest WMP rhyme is the Enlightenment's closed-system view of nature, but nothing here concerns historical machines directly.

Naturalism versus theism as a basis for morals. Morley says the System is austere, not licentious: Holbach argues a Book that shows God as now just and now capricious cannot ground virtue, that morality is "the science of the duties of man living in society", and that theological sanctions are "not only needless, but mischievous". Then the long antithesis, Nature versus Religion, ranging from self-love and reason to politics: Religion makes the subject a slave, obeys tyrants, yet incites rebellion against princes who neglect priests; Nature would teach princes "that they are men and not gods". Morley's verdict: if Nature means the Encyclopaedists and Religion the French Catholic Church of that moment, the antitheses are "a tolerably fair account", and the political side "hardly less just", though it "appalled men like Voltaire" (a contemporary reaction reported by Morley). See voltaire and counter-enlightenment for the opposing camp.

Propagandist and the long shadow. Morley notes Holbach's popular abridgment Good sense, his flood of tracts "inciting their readers to an intellectual revolt", and his death in spring 1789; he imagines what Holbach would have seen five years later: Notre Dame consecrated to Reason and a procession whose hero was an ass crowned with a mitre. Relevant to the idea of a pamphlet network as part of a history-machine that assembles unseen before it lurches into view, and to eisenstein-printing-revolution and intellectual-vs-revolutionary; Morley himself frames it only as a vivid ironic contrast, not a theory.

Notable passages

Suicide and the revolutionary generation (c1). Holbach's argument that death is "a resource" for oppressed virtue, followed by Morley's catalogue of revolutionary suicides.

Virtue as social sympathy (c2). The quotation from ch. xv and Morley's rebuttal of the egoism charge, including the claim that the true base lay "in social sympathy" and the reading of atheist public spirit as revolution; followed by his critique of the unanalysed "basis of morals".

Part II as an attack on theism as such (c3). Morley's judgment of the second half: comprehensive, merciless, unphilosophic in tone, and silent on theology's contributions.

Atheism defended (c4). Holbach's argument that the real atheist denies no moving force, and Morley's remark on the "unreasonableness" of the charge of chance.

Nature versus Religion (c5). The first block of Holbach's antitheses and Morley's reading of them as the Encyclopaedists versus the French Church.

Holbach the propagandist (c6). The coda: Good sense, the tracts, death in 1789, and the cult of Reason's procession of the ass.
