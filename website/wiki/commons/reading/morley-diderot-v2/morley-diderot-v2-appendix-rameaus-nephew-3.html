Rameau's Nephew: idioms of vocation, the flatterer's craft, and the happiness of good actions

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
In this stretch of Morley's translation of Diderot's dialogue, the Nephew (He) defends parasitic flattery and vice as 'idioms of vocation' within a general social conscience, while the philosopher (I) answers with a happiness grounded in good actions, and the two reach no agreement.

This unit is the middle of the appendix translation of Diderot's dialogue Le Neveu de Rameau, rendered into English by Morley. The words are Diderot's (in Morley's English), not Morley's own argument; positions below belong to He (the Nephew, "Lui") or I (the philosopher-narrator, "Moi"). The Nephew has just described his frantic performance of being busy and in demand, and the unit follows him from that self-presentation through a cynical theory of social morality, a hypothetical fantasy of what he would do if rich, a clash with the philosopher over duty and happiness, and a comic demonstration of his craft of flattering a gloomy patron and a vain woman of fashion. It closes with his boast of invention and the philosopher's ironic assent.

Themes

Morality as grammar, with trades as idioms. He argues that there is a "general conscience, just as there is a general grammar", and that each calling has its exceptions, "idioms of vocation". The older the institution and the worse the times, the more idioms multiply. I draws the dry conclusion that there are few honest callings and few honest men in them; He agrees, adding that only the "assiduous" who never leave their shops grow rich and esteemed. Social morality is presented as a set of conventions local to roles, not as universal law. This sits oddly with Enlightenment universalism, and also rhymes loosely with the modernity-machine's way of making roles legible and tradeable, though nothing in the text speaks of that frame.

Society as mutual predation. He justifies his old thefts from pupils by saying that "in nature, all species devour one another; so all ranks devour one another in society". He names a chain of retaliation (milliner, jeweller, cook and others avenging the financier). Only "the imbecile or the sloth" suffers without inflicting injury. I answers only "I admire yours". Here a naturalistic image of struggle is used to excuse conduct; it is He's rationalisation, not Diderot-as-narrator's endorsement.

Vanity against duty. In reply to I's list of goods (country, friends, position, children), He repeats "Vanity" and substitutes self-interest: gratitude is "a burden", "there is native land no more; I see nought from pole to pole but tyrants and slaves", duty leads only to "jealousy, trouble, persecution", and the secret is to "serve their vices, praise their injustice". He also insists that his own pleasures are the typical pleasures of the rich ('I represent the most important part of the town and the court') and that philosophy and virtue are not "made for all the world". The fantasy of what he would do if rich (command, paid flatterers, mocking Voltaire, Buffon, Montesquieu and D'Alembert) shows that he claims no ideal of his own beyond inverting his humiliation.

The philosopher's counter-ethic. I distinguishes the dulled, idle pleasure of the worldly from the pleasure of having "succoured the unfortunate", written "a good page", or fulfilled one's duties. I says he would rather have won justice for the memory of the Calas than have written Mahomet (a play of voltaire), and tells the story of a younger son who returns from Carthagena to restore his ruined parents. I's claim is that "it is impossible to be unhappy under the shelter of good actions". He's reply, that honest people are unhappy and the dishonest happy, is left standing; I attributes He's plight to failing to "provide a resource independent of servitude".

Temperament, hypocrisy, and the pride of the parasite. He argues that his vices are natural, easy, and suited to his protectors, while virtue would be "a standing accusation" that "freezes the marrow" and would make him a hypocrite (a "Catonise"-ing Rameau). Religious people are harsh because they are straining against nature. Yet he claims a residual dignity: he will be abject "without constraint", on his own terms, like a worm that turns when trodden on.

Performance as livelihood. He demonstrates his mimicry of the sour patron (an automaton-like "pagod" with a string tied to its chin) and the pretentious prude, and catalogues the craft of timed applause, vocal tones and bodily gestures of admiration. The text treats flattery as a technical art with its own limits ("Wit and art have their limits"), and I's closing lines ('carried the talent of playing the madman, and of self-debasement, as far as it can possibly be carried') are ironic rather than approving.

Notable passages

Idioms of vocation (c1). He's grammar analogy for morality is the unit's cleanest theoretical statement: a general conscience plus role-specific exceptions. Within a few lines I strips it to its content (few honest callings), and He sharpens it into a joke about the "assiduous".

All ranks devour one another (c2). The chain of avengers (milliner to financier) is He's account of redistribution without law, and the clearest naturalistic argument in the unit, voiced by the less reliable speaker.

Vanity, vanity (c3). The question-and-answer sequence on country, friends, position, children and wife is the dialogue's sharpest dramatisation of ethical nihilism, with I's replies supplying the stakes. He's "tyrants and slaves" line is a political observation placed in a cynic's mouth.

The philosopher's happiness and the Calas (c4). I's one long speech gives the counter-position, with a reference to the Calas affair and the Carthagena story as exempla of sentiment-driven goodness. This is the Enlightenment sentimental-moral register, placed against He's materialism of the stomach.

Vice as fit with one's nature and patrons (c5). He's longest justification of his way of life, combining temperament, market, and hypocrisy: vices that go with "the manners of my nation" and are "more in harmony" with the small needs of his protectors.

The worm that turns (c6). A compact image of dependent dignity: He will crawl, but only unbidden. It qualifies the picture of complete abjection.

For World Machines, the dialogue is mostly of interest as an ancien-regime portrait of patronage as a social mechanism (courts, financiers and flatterers) and of ethics as role-bound convention; the fit with WMP frameworks is only loose and the text itself does not state it.
