Raynal's History of the Indies: authorship, popularity, merits and faults

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley introduces Raynal's History of the Indies (1772) as a once-immense, now-forgotten bestseller of uncertain collective authorship, weighs its breadth of view against its credulity, borrowed rhapsody and invented facts, and explains its popularity by readers' curiosity about the wider world and France's post-1763 colonial humiliation.

Morley opens his chapter on the abbé Raynal's Philosophical and Political History of European settlements and commerce in the two Indies with a paradox: Grimm predicted immortality for it, yet "not one hundred persons now living have ever read two chapters" of it. He sketches Raynal (born 1711, a needy priest turned bookmaker, then rich and a generous founder of academy prizes, including the question whether the discovery of America had helped or harmed humanity), his habit of cross-examining dinner guests at Holbach's table, and the widespread belief that Diderot wrote a large share of the book. He then treats the reception (twenty-odd editions, readers from Franklin to Toussaint Louverture, Frederick the Great's sudden silence), weighs the book's real merit (a broad, consequence-minded view of events) against its faults (no dates, references or criticism; myth turned to history and history to idyll; the invented Polly Baker episode), and closes by accounting for the popularity: variety, a new curiosity about distant peoples, and the French loss of Canada and India in 1763. The unit stops before the History's treatment of colonial subjects proper; it is about the book as a cultural object. All judgments of merit and fault are Morley's, with Grimm, Walpole, Gibbon, Franklin and De Brosses quoted as contemporaries.

Themes

Collective, anonymous authorship as a feature of the age. Morley says the book has "no unity of composition, no equal scale, no regularity of proportion" and reads like "half a dozen writers". He generalises (his own thesis) that the eighteenth-century French insurgents suppressed authorial vanity, as the Jesuits did, because of the constant need for secrecy: the System of Nature, Voltaire's denied pieces, Turgot's anonymity in the Encyclopaedia, and Diderot's unpublished masterpieces. Rousseau is excepted as "a reactionist". This is a picture of the Encyclopaedist circle as a distributed workshop rather than a set of named authors, which resonates with the history-machine idea of a paradigm assembling from many hands, though Morley's point is about publication conditions and personal vanity, not historical mechanism.

Reach and reception. Morley lists readers: Franklin, Gibbon (who praised the title's "political and philosophical" epithets but condemned the lack of references), Robertson, a young religious who compiled L'Esprit de Raynal, and Toussaint Louverture, who found there "the first definite expression" of thoughts the brutalities of slavery had stirred. Frederick the Great admired it until he met a passage rebuking him; when Raynal later visited Berlin the king answered "I don't know them". Contemporary views are reported as such; the Frederick passage is Raynal's own apostrophe, quoted by Morley.

Merit: history measured by consequences. Against a hostile critic (the quack on a waggon), Morley judges that "the instinct of the gaping crowd was sound". Raynal is below voltaire and Robertson in judgment, but has "a luminous breadth of outlook": he notes that the weightiest effect of the Portuguese conquests was the check on Ottoman power. Morley adds a Victorian jab that the conception of history may have "gone back" into pedantic accumulation of facts, a stance of his own.

Faults: no method, myth and idyll. No dates, no authorities, no criticism. Raynal ignored De Brosses's maxim to study man himself and turned the Peruvian Manco-Capac legend into sober narrative (Morley calls this Euhemeristic). Conversely he idealised savage and distant life in idylls (a fashion Morley traces to Rousseau and Bernardin de Saint-Pierre), borrowing and inflating Poivré's account of the Chinese emperor ploughing. Morley credits the idyll fashion with one merit: teaching that savage life is coherent, not chaos. This overlaps the noble-savage motif, though Morley is judging it as a historiographical vice with a modest pedagogical benefit.

Polly Baker and the cost of invented evidence. Raynal's eloquent plea for an unmarried mother in Puritan New England is, Morley says, too close to Diderot's manner to be authentic, and Franklin confessed he had invented her as a newspaper filler. Raynal's reply ("I would rather relate your stories than other men's truths") becomes the emblem of the book's attitude to fact. The passage also shows the anti-Puritan, anti-religious animus of eighteenth-century reformers toward seventeenth-century ones.

Why it sold. Morley's causes: variety and animation (Walpole's catalogue), a new but childlike curiosity about the world beyond France, a systematic and concrete form compared with Montesquieu's fragmentary treatment, the readers' taste for sensuous description veiled by moral disquisition (reminiscent of Diderot's Supplement to Bougainville's Voyages), and the national mortification after the Seven Years' War, with Guiana as consolation utopia ("Equinoctial France"). Colonial loss thus becomes demand for a colonial book.

Notable passages

Readers across the world (c1). Franklin, Toussaint Louverture, Gibbon and Robertson show the book's range; Toussaint's reading is Morley's account of how a slave found his thoughts articulated.

Who wrote it (c2). Morley's argument that the work was multi-handed and that Diderot was popularly credited with at least a third of it.

The merit of consequence (c3). The Ottoman example captures what Morley thinks good eighteenth-century history did.

Faults of method (c4). Gibbon's complaint, Morley's list of absences, and the De Brosses principle.

Polly Baker (c5). The anecdote of Franklin's fiction and Raynal's retort.

Popularity (c6). Walpole's list and Morley's explanation of why Raynal's cosmorama hit the hour.
