Diderot's aesthetics: Essay on Painting, Goethe's rebuttal, Beauty, Lessing and music

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley sets Diderot's Essay on Painting against Goethe's stringent commentary (nature versus art, rule versus imitation), then briefly surveys Diderot's Encyclopédie essay on Beauty, his anticipation of Lessing's Laocoön on the limits of poetry and painting, and his negligible writing on music, ending in praise of the Salons as criticism turned fine art.

Morley concedes that Diderot is no systematic aesthetician, then argues his value is provocation: quoting Goethe, "the highest influence of mind is to call out mind". The bulk of the unit stages a disagreement between two critics. Diderot's Essay on Painting (written 1765, published 1796) holds that nature is the sole source of artistic inspiration and that academies, studio models, anatomy and the antique breed mannerism. Goethe, who praised the essay to Schiller but translated parts of it with a commentary of protest, replies that Diderot confounds nature and art. Morley sides with Goethe, while granting that the state of French painting may have justified Diderot's exaggeration. The unit then runs through the Encyclopédie essay on Beauty (found wanting), Diderot's early anticipation of Lessing's Laocoön on the boundaries between poetry and painting, and his thin, good-natured writing on music, and closes by praising the Salons.

Themes

Nature versus art (Diderot against Goethe). Diderot, in Morley's account, praises nature as never incorrect even in apparent monstrosity, and ridicules the academic life model, the study of anatomy and the weight of the antique: "Mannerism comes from the masters, from the academy, from the school, and even from the antique." Goethe's reply, as quoted by Morley, is that art represents only "the surface of an appearance" and has its own laws and aims; to scorn rules is to destroy the conditions that make painting possible. Attribution matters here: the Diderot position is Morley's paraphrase and summary of the essay; the Goethe position is Goethe's own words; the verdict that Goethe "must be pronounced to have the last word of reason and wisdom" is Morley's. See johann-wolfgang-von-goethe. Morley also says Goethe's drift is in fact the thesis of Diderot's own Paradox on the Comedian.

Genius, morality and extremity. A Salon passage Morley quotes has Diderot speculating that there may be a morality peculiar to artists, the reverse of the common one: poets plunge into extremes, happiness lies in the just mean, and the heroes and philosophers "make poetry in their life, and that is their bane". Morley reads Goethe's Tasso, with its poet and the worldly Antonio, as the idealised version of this figure, and says Goethe sympathised with Antonio. The contrast Morley draws is between a deep critic who ties art to character and conduct and a mere virtuoso.

Beauty as relations (a weak essay). Morley judges the Encyclopédie essay on Beauty less fertile than Diderot's other contributions: it reviews Hutcheson and others, locates beauty in perceived "relations" (order, proportion, symmetry, unity), but leaves unanswered why some relations are beautiful and why judgments vary. Morley notes Diderot lists twelve sources of diversity in judgment and came near Alison's associationist theory without adopting it.

Limits of the arts (an anticipation of Lessing). Diderot's 1751 letter on Deaf Mutes asked why a situation admirable in a poem becomes ridiculous in a painting, taking Virgil's Neptune and Polyphemus as examples. Morley says this anticipates Lessing's Laocoön, where each art is limited by its material. Diderot's counsel to the poet is to give one or two great traits and leave the rest to the reader's imagination (Armida, Helen against Ariosto's Alcina). Morley treats these as fragmentary beside Lessing's sustained mastery, and quotes Diderot's own admission that he had not used half his strength.

Music and the Salons. Morley says Diderot wrote little worth attending to on music, citing his good-natured dialogue-form rewriting of Bemetzrieder's harmony lessons (which modern teachers consider contemptible). He pardons this because the Salons show how criticism of art can itself become a fine art.

Notable passages

Diderot on nature's harmony and the academy (c1). Morley's summary of the Essay on Painting's central claim: nature coordinates even unusual features into a harmony, so human "rules" of proportion are poor measures.

Goethe's counter-thesis (c2). The most developed statement in the unit of the opposing view: nature organises the living and indifferent, the artist the dead but significant; art needs rules, measure, and the guardianship of academy and antique. It also contains a chiasmus-like contrast of real versus apparent that is the theoretical heart of the unit.

Twilight reverie on artists' morality (c3). A long Salon quotation (Diderot's words) on whether artists have a morality opposite to the common one, and why the "poetry" of exceptional lives is paid for in misery.

Virgil's Neptune and Polyphemus (c4). Diderot's early, concrete argument that painting cannot render what poetry can, with Morley's linking comparison to Lessing.

One great trait (c5). Diderot's advice to leave detail to the imagination, the most "just and true" of his passages on poetry versus painting by Morley's estimate.

Diderot's unused strength (c6). Morley's verdict that Diderot came near decisive truths without consecutive study, ending on Diderot's confession of dabbling ("baguenaudé").

WMP resonance is slight. The Goethe-Diderot contrast loosely parallels a tension between the imitative, natural and the formal, rule-governed, though this unit concerns aesthetics, not machines or infrastructure; no stronger link is claimed.
