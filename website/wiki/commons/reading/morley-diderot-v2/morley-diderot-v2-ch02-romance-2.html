Sterne, Diderot's Jacques le Fataliste, and Morley's verdict on it

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley traces Sterne's reception in Paris and the transmission of Diderot's Jacques le Fataliste to Germany and France, then judges the book a heavy, often gross and pointless work by a sentimentalist who could not play the buffoon, while granting why Goethe and Schiller relished it as a solvent of formalism.

Morley opens by saying Sterne founded a French school as Richardson had, though Sterne himself was a distant follower of Rabelais. He recounts Sterne's 1762 Paris visit, his dinners at Holbach's where he met Diderot, and the success of the Sentimental Journey (1768) with French taste. He then turns to Diderot's one surviving Sternean imitation, Jacques le Fataliste (composed about 1773). He traces its odd manuscript history (copies in Germany, Schiller's 1785 translation of one episode, a complete German version in 1792, a French transcript only in 1796 via Prince Henri of Prussia). He quotes Goethe's enthusiastic 1780 verdict, and then gives his own largely negative assessment. In Morley's view Diderot is too conscientious and too much a preacher to be a free buffoon, the characters are thin, the stories insipid or obscene, and only the Madame de Pommeraye tale has meaning. He closes by explaining, with qualification, why great German minds could still enjoy the book.

Themes

Sentiment as a cross-Channel fashion. Morley (quoting Sterne, as reported) has Sterne remark that the French "make such a pother about the word sentiment" without a precise idea of it. Sterne states his own aim in the Sentimental Journey as teaching "us to love the world and our fellow-creatures better". Morley says this fitted Parisian modes "with such sentiment as that of Diderot most of all". This extends the Richardson thread from the preceding material: sensibility travels from England to France, and on to Germany, where Diderot, Rousseau, Richardson, Sterne and Goldsmith "kindled the glow of sentimentalism, both in its good and its bad forms". It sits within the wider Enlightenment network of ideas moving between salons, translators and princes, though Morley frames it as literary taste rather than infrastructure.

Transmission and reception as an international traffic. The manuscript circulated through Grimm's subscribers, travelled via Diderot's Dutch friends, was first printed in German (Schiller), and reached France only through Prince Henri of Prussia, brother of Frederick the Great, who gave a copy to the Institute. Morley reads this as another instance of "the eagerness of the best minds of Germany to possess and diffuse the most original products of French intelligence and hardihood". Compare the Goethe link in johann-wolfgang-von-goethe; here Goethe is a quoted contemporary (his 1780 letter) whose appetite Morley doubts modern readers will share.

Morley's verdict: the sentimentalist as poor reveller. This is Morley's own judgment, not Diderot's. He holds that Diderot has "the German heaviness" because he "had too much conscience", and echoes Diderot's own contention that excess of sensibility makes a mediocre actor. Perfect fooling, he says, belongs to Aristophanes, Rabelais and Shakespeare, and Diderot "must rank in the second class". The contrast with voltaire's Candide is a foil (wit, verve), though Morley also finds malice in Candide and self-consciousness in Tristram Shandy. This is a Victorian moral-aesthetic standard: sincerity and purpose are demanded of art, and the book is condemned for "want of point and want of meaning, either moral or æsthetic".

Imitation versus adaptation. Morley concedes that whole sentences are transcribed from Tristram Shandy, yet argues that Richardson and Sterne gave Diderot only "the suggestion of a method and a sentiment", from which his own figures loomed. He likens the process to the adaptation of The Natural Son from Goldoni. His caveat is that "nothing became blithe in its passage through his mind".

Weak characters, strong episode. Jacques, with his catchword "Il était écrit là-haut", and his master have no personality compared with Trim, Uncle Toby or Sancho. The fatalism of the title is thus treated by Morley as a mere tic, not as philosophy; he does not discuss necessity in this unit. The one exception is the tale of Madame de Pommeraye and the Marquis d'Arcis, a revenge plot that Morley calls a "crude foreshadowing" of Feydeau and Flaubert, and one Balzac "would have made a masterpiece". Morley calls the rest insipid or obscene and quotes Diderot's imagined objector and his reply to the reader.

Why the geniuses still liked it. In a more generous turn Morley says the book "breaks roughly in upon that starved formalism", with its license, contempt of conventions, and common people and coarse passions acting as "a dissolvent" of frigid rules. Its grossness left no stain on "great wholesome minds" and stimulated originality in others. His final charge is the lack of poetry: no "beautiful fancy" to redeem its irony, unlike Rabelais's Cupid or Sterne's recording angel.

Notable passages

Sterne in Paris and the French imitations (c1). Morley's account of Sterne's 1762 visit, the dinners at Holbach's, the aim of the Sentimental Journey, and the claim that only Diderot's Jacques le Fataliste survives among the French imitations.

Goethe's 1780 letter (c2). A contemporary's praise, quoted by Morley, of the manuscript as a meal "prepared and dished up with great skill", swallowed in six uninterrupted hours. Morley then doubts that modern readers will match this appetite. Goethe's view is not Morley's thesis.

Imitation versus invention (c3). The paragraph arguing that Diderot's debt to Richardson and Sterne was a starting point, and that he "was too much of a preacher to be an effective humorist".

The lack of creation, and the one good story (c4). Morley's critique of Jacques and his master, the summary of the Pommeraye plot, and the judgment that it is "at least a story with meaning, purpose, and character".

Dissolvent of formalism, and the lack of poetry (c5). Morley's reconciliation of his own distaste with the admiration of Goethe and Schiller, ending with the image of the book as a dish lacking "the pinch of salt and the handful of herbs".

The unit's World Machines relevance is modest: it shows sensibility and the novel as a transnational current among the Encyclopédie's circle (Holbach's table, Grimm's manuscript subscriptions, German translators), and a Victorian positivist critic holding that art must carry moral or aesthetic purpose.
