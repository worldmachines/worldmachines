Diderot at St. Petersburg, the return to the Hague, and Hemsterhuys

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley narrates Diderot's five months at Catherine II's court (1773-74), her verdict that his reforming theories were 'fine books, but very bad business', his return journey and months at the Hague, and his encounters with Hemsterhuys and Princess Galitzin as a meeting-point of French philosophy and the German-Dutch reaction against it.

Morley follows Diderot from his arrival in St. Petersburg (late 1773) to his departure from the Hague (September 1774). At court Catherine banishes etiquette and talks with him daily, while foreign diplomats imagine political intrigue; Catherine herself later told Ségur that his plans were impracticable. Morley then sets Grimm's smoother court success against Diderot's, traces the return journey, and uses the Swedish scholar Björnstähl's account of the Hague months (Diderot's simplicity, his preference for artisans over courtiers) as the only glimpse of him abroad. The unit closes on a wider reflection: at the Hague Diderot meets the younger Hemsterhuys and Princess Galitzin, figures of the Platonist-sentimental reaction against the French philosophy of sensation, and Morley ends by looking ahead to the ten years left to Diderot, the American republic, Turgot, and the end of the old French monarchy.

Themes

The philosopher and the sovereign. The key exchange is Catherine's own retrospective account (reported through Ségur, so it is Catherine's self-presentation, not Morley's judgment): Diderot's principles "would make fine books, but very bad business" because he works "on paper" while she works "on the human skin". This is a classic statement of the gap between theory and administration, and of the limits of enlightened despotism as a channel for philosophic reform. Morley adds his own hedge: Catherine rated Diderot "at his true worth as a great talker" without either wholesale enthusiasm or disparagement. See also voltaire, to whom Catherine wrote flatteringly while Frederick reported that people at St. Petersburg found Diderot tiresome.

Diplomatic misreading. Cathcart (English ambassador) and the French minister and ambassador, all quoted contemporaries, assume Diderot's access to the Empress must be political. The French hoped to detach Russia from Prussia after the first Partition of Poland; Morley judges the French government 'shiftless and feeble' and ignorant of Catherine's "strong and positive mind". This is a small case study in how a network of ideas (the Encyclopedists) is mistaken by states for an instrument of policy.

Diderot against Grimm: temperament and the court. Madame Geoffrin (quoted) judges Diderot lacking in tact and 'always in himself', and Grimm the more successful courtier. Morley calls Grimm one of the shrewdest heads in the party yet as ecstatic about the Empress; he records Grimm's later quarterly packets of daily letters to Catherine and his role as unofficial intermediary with Versailles until 1792, a concrete instance of philosophers as an informal communication channel between courts.

Frederick and the style of Diderot's books. Frederick (quoted via D'Alembert) cannot endure Diderot's 'self-sufficiency' and 'arrogance' and does not invite him; Morley notes in passing that Kant, soon to deliver 'so heavy a blow to the Encyclopaedic way of thinking', was at Konigsberg as Diderot passed.

The Hague: simplicity, the useful, and sentiment. Björnstähl's report (a contemporary witness, 'obscure' in Morley's words) has Diderot shine in conversation but not in large companies. Diderot's own Scheveningen description and his preference for peasants and handicraftsmen over courtiers because the former can be asked about 'useful and necessary things' are Diderot's views, and Morley reads them as 'after Diderot's own heart'. Morley also notes that English sentimentalism (Spectator, Clarissa Harlowe) had reached Dutch letters, echoing the Richardson theme of the volume.

Two tides of thought meet. Morley's own interpretive claim: in Diderot and the younger Hemsterhuys 'two great tides of thought meet for a moment, and then flow on'. Hemsterhuys, a Platonist, leads a reaction against 'the French philosophy of illumination--of sensation, of experience, of the verifiable', feeding German mysticism and pietism and Jacobi's defence of feeling against the understanding. This resonates with counter-enlightenment framing (and, via Jacobi's borrowing from Rousseau, with jean-jacques-rousseau), though Morley writes it as a Victorian positivist watching a rival tradition rise and notes its later patriotic and political uptake after the revolutionary war.

Princess Galitzin and the demand for proof. Her biographer (a partisan source, quoted) says her 'Why?' and 'How?' routed Diderot's atomist proselytising; Morley notes she later became a Catholic (1784) and her son a missionary. Goethe's later impression of her is cited (johann-wolfgang-von-goethe).

Looking ahead. Diderot's 'ten years' in his wallet and Morley's closing sentences link his remaining decade to the American republic, Turgot's last serious experiment in orderly reform, and the final decay of the old monarchy; Catherine's 1792 banishment of Voltaire's busts to the cellars marks the cooling of Russian enlightenment under revolutionary pressure.

Notable passages

Catherine on Diderot as a politician (c1). Her account of working 'on the human skin' against his work 'on paper' is the unit's central passage, with the caveat that it is Catherine's recollection, told to Ségur years later.

Etiquette banished (c2). Diderot hitting the imperial knees and Catherine sheltering behind a table; the setting for the diplomats' mistaken suspicion.

Hemsterhuys and the two tides (c3). Morley's own synthesis of the intellectual fork in the road.

Princess Galitzin (c4). The hostess who demands proof of atomism, and Morley's list of philosophers drawn to clever, sympathetic women.

Peasants and courtiers (c5). Diderot's remark, through Björnstähl, preferring useful artisanal conversation to courtiers.

Ten years in the wallet (c6). Diderot's farewell calculation and Morley's panoramic coda on the decay of the old order.
