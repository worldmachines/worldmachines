Diderot's Closing Years (I): Death-Musings, the Apology for Seneca, and the Rousseau Quarrel

Commons · Diderot and the Encyclopaedists · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Morley opens his chapter on Diderot's last years with Diderot's own meditations on death after his return from Russia, then treats his apology for Seneca (the Essay on the Reigns of Claudius and Nero) and the way that book became a vehicle for his quarrel with Rousseau and the Confessions.

Morley opens the chapter on the last phase of Diderot's life with a passage in which Diderot, closing his notes on anatomy and physiology, imagines life as a gaming-house and philosophy as an "apprenticeship for death". Morley then describes Diderot's return from Russia (Morley's text prints the autumn of "1744"; the Russian journey belongs to the 1770s, so this is evidently a misprint in the source, and the unit gives no other date), his cheerfulness and new languor, and a 1762 letter to Mdlle. Voland on the acceptability of death, which Morley sets beside Plato's Cephalus. The main body of the unit is Diderot's late Essay on the Reigns of Claudius and Nero, an enthusiastic defence of Seneca: Morley reads it as a test case for Diderot's situational ethics (the statesman versus the moralist) and then follows its digressions into a long, acrimonious attack on Rousseau, prompted by the prospect of the Confessions. Morley judges the quarrel "now dead", the attack a lapse from Diderot's own rule of generous reading, and the book's reputation damaged by La Harpe's later revenge.

Themes

Death as natural rest, not terror. Diderot's letter and the closing notes treat death through nature's "incessant, ever renewing circle": a busy, innocent life resigns itself to the common lot, and the coffin is "a bed of rest". Morley frames this with Plato's Cephalus, so the serenity is partly Morley's classical gloss (a Morley comparison, not Diderot's). The notes' skeptical epistemology ("Forms. And what besides? Forms.") and the idea of philosophy as rehearsal for death sit beside a materialist acceptance of nature's order. This rhymes loosely with the project's interest in mechanism and necessity, though here it is a personal and consolatory register rather than a systematic one.

Ethics by case, not by absolute rule. Morley says Diderot was "always very near to the position that there is no such thing as an absolute rule of right and wrong" and that each act must be judged on its circumstances. Seneca, minister to Nero, is a "case in which the general rules of morality condemn, but common sense acquits". Morley partly endorses and partly qualifies Diderot's defence: the statesman might be applauded for clinging to the helm, but Seneca was also a moralist, and "the contrast between lofty writing and slippery policy" harmed his fame, as it did Francis Bacon's. This is a Morley verdict, not Diderot's. The theme touches philosophers who serve power (compare Diderot's own courting of Catherine, treated elsewhere in the volume), though the unit makes that connection only implicitly.

The apology as self-defence; Rousseau. The Essay's second edition (1782) is so full of replies to Rousseau's partisans that Morley doubts whether it is "an apology for Seneca or a vindication of Denis Diderot". Grimm, quoted by Morley, mocks the leaps between Rome and Paris, and Morley adds that the reader confuses Seneca, Burrhus and Thrasea with Turgot, Malesherbes and Necker. Morley's account of why Diderot was stung is psychological: indifferent to attacks on his opinions, he was sensitive to "innuendoes against his private benevolence and loyalty", in an age that put amiability at the head of the virtues. Morley also notes the Confessions' treatment of Madame d'Epinay and Madame d'Houdetot (Grimm being d'Epinay's lover) and, as one possible further motive, "the harm that Rousseau's sentimentalism had done to sound and positive thinking", while concluding Diderot's own sentimentalism was the stronger motive. Morley calls Rousseau the "reactionary philosopher of the eighteenth" century here. This is a Victorian positivist's judgment; compare jean-jacques-rousseau and the counter-enlightenment reading of him, though Morley's framing is a moral-temperamental one, not a systematic intellectual one.

A rule for reading history. Diderot's quoted method is to scrutinise stories that dishonour humanity and to lean toward charity when an action is noble. Morley calls this "not unworthy of imitation" and says the attack on Rousseau was "perhaps Diderot's only breach" of it. The consequence, Morley says, was La Harpe's retaliatory lecture on Seneca, which fed the "vulgar prejudice" against the philosophes in the years of reaction.

Notable passages

The closing notes and the Voland letter (c1, c2) are Diderot's own words on death. The Seneca passage (c3) is Morley's analysis of the case for situational morality and of the statesman/moralist split. The Rousseau digression (c4) and the explanation of Diderot's sensitivity (c5) carry Morley's account of the quarrel. The reading-of-history passage (c6) is Diderot's quoted principle, which Morley uses as the standard against which to judge Diderot's own conduct. Voices to keep apart: Diderot (the notes, letter, essay), Galiani and Grimm (quoted contemporaries), Rosenkranz (cited), and Morley (the verdicts).
