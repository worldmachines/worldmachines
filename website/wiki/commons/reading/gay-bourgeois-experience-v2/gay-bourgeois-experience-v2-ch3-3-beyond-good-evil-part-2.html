The oedipal triangle in fiction, and the uneven opening of the reading public

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay argues that the erotic triangle, above all the oedipal one, was the nineteenth-century novel's indispensable device, reads David Copperfield, First Love, Henry Esmond, Pepita Jimenez and Fort comme la mort as (often unwitting) oedipal texts, and uses readers' and censors' reactions to show that bourgeois tolerance of candor grew unevenly from the 1880s, with the avant-garde mistaking an anxious public for a prudish consensus.

Gay moves from the general utility of the amorous triangle to its oedipal core. He first surveys triangles across European fiction (Austen to Dostoevsky, Machado, Galdos, Fontane), including the rare same-sex variants in James and Hardy, and argues that a delayed resolution mirrors the rhythm of erotic gratification. He then reads a run of novels as oedipal texts: Dickens's David Copperfield (the fullest case), Turgenev's First Love, Thackeray's Henry Esmond, Valera's Pepita Jimenez and Maupassant's Fort comme la mort. The second half turns from texts to readers and regulators. Reception evidence (reviews, bowdlerized translations, prosecutions, editorial dots) is used to argue that the fiction-reading middle class did not move smoothly from repression to candor, and that the avant-garde's picture of a hypocritical bourgeois enemy misread an anxious, internally divided public.

Themes

The triangle as narrative engine. Gay treats the triangle as overdetermined for writers: it sustains uncertainty, permits noble resignation or jealous rage, and lets a story withhold and then grant resolution. He ties this to the structure of desire itself, since a cleverly delayed happy ending multiplies pleasure. Zola's half-jesting claim that legal divorce would ruin literature is Gay's emblem of how much fiction depended on marital unhappiness. Triangles could also be false trails (Daniel Deronda) or allegories of ideas (city versus country). Bisexual or same-sex variants (Hardy's Desperate Remedies, James's The Bostonians) are resolved heterosexually, and the openly bisexual triangle of Mademoiselle de Maupin stayed nearly unique outside pornography.

Novelists as innocent knowers. In David Copperfield, Gay argues, Dickens builds a whole society of incomplete families in which a surviving parent and child form an undisturbed dyad (Clara and David, Mr. Wickfield and Agnes, Uriah and Mrs. Heep). Dora is a replica of the mother; Agnes, the opposite, marks the Oedipus complex overcome, so that love is transference and also transference overcome. Dickens never diagnoses any of this. Gay cites Freud's envy of poets who arrive at insight by empathy, and coins the formula that novelists often found things without knowing what they saw: 'innocent, ignorant knowledge'. This is Gay's own psychoanalytic reading, applied with hedged confidence to a text whose author had no such vocabulary.

Autobiography, displacement and the primal scene. Turgenev's First Love and Thackeray's Henry Esmond are read as thinly disguised confession. Gay reads the riding-crop scene as a displaced primal scene and the son's dream as a second displacement, and links Thackeray's plot to his own fixation on his mother, quoting his 1852 letter about a mother who cannot be all in all to him. Attribution matters: Viardot's complaint about the sewer of the modern novel is anal imagery, in Gay's reading, that dodges the oedipal conflict.

Denial as reception evidence. Readers of Henry Esmond (Eliot, Chesnut, Lewes, Forster, Oliphant, Brimley) supply Gay's best evidence of how the culture handled oedipal material. Eliot's misreading of the plot, Forster's refusal to believe it, and Oliphant's outrage all count as defenses; Brimley's phrase about filial affection and an unconscious passion 'curiously blended' is praised as prescient. Gay thus uses reviews as data on collective psychic defense, in line with his volume-wide theme of bourgeois-defenses-against-eros.

Wish-fulfillment and moral packaging. Pepita Jimenez keeps appearances by marrying the lovers and uniting conjugal love with love of God, while letting the son defeat his father with impunity; Gay calls it the novel as wish-fulfillment. The unpunished transgression, and Maupassant's matter-of-fact treatment of adultery, incest wishes and half-willed suicide, mark for him a shift in what a public would accept, with only Boissin loudly indignant.

The 1880s as transition, but not a clean one. Gay sketches a move from an age of reticence to one of candor (Goncourt, Zola, van Deyssel, Wilde) and then complicates it. Pressures for restraint intensified around mid-century (Dickens dropping the word prostitute for Nancy, the Montepin and Flaubert prosecutions), national stereotypes about French permissiveness are unreliable (Serrano's bowdlerized Queiros, Napoleon III's and the Third Republic's prosecutions), and censorship continued after the 1880s (Vizetelly, Wilde, van Deyssel's self-censorship, Dehmel, the Norwegian Jaeger). The novel is 'that undependable mirror', reflecting politics and medicine but going its own way.

Anxious, not hypocritical. Flaubert, van Deyssel and Fontane cast the bourgeois as mendacious philistine enemy of literature. Gay replies that the bourgeois was anxious, not simply hard-hearted, that middle-class literary education produced real tolerance (Tolstoy, Eliot), and that avant-garde writers mistook resistance to change for the bourgeois consensus. The 'community of bourgeois fantasies' had fractured into segments of reading publics. This bears on the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction and bourgeoisophobia: the philistine is partly the avant-garde's construction, though Gay concedes real prudes (Podsnap survives) and real prosecutions.

Notable passages

The triangle as delayed gratification (c1). Gay's compact statement of why the device works, tying the storyteller's withholding to the rhythm of intercourse and the relief of perils overcome.

Dora and Clara (c2). The core of the David Copperfield reading: Dora as a replica of the mother, the second marriage reviving the oedipal struggle with Murdstone in its most destructive form, and Dora as mother restored to life.

Innocent, ignorant knowledge (c3). Dickens's moral style, his lack of a vocabulary for what he depicted, and Freud's envy of poets. It is the clearest statement of Gay's method of reading fiction as evidence of unconscious cultural knowledge.

Henry Esmond and the misreading (c4). Eliot's summary that the hero loves the daughter and marries the mother, and Gay's inference that a reader's inaccurate perception signals intractable inner conflict. It sits close to the book's larger theme of defenses.

Decorum intensifies as bourgeois power grows (c5). Against the expectation that security breeds tolerance, Gay finds mid-century fiction inhibited by vindictive decorum, exactly when the middle classes consolidated power. This cuts against any simple story in which a rising class relaxes its emotional culture, and is the passage most useful for tracing how a dominant class's norms are built and later shift (roughly the 1850s to 1890s). It is a historian's hedged observation, not a model; the modernity-machine framing would be an outside gloss.

The anxious enemy (c6). The reversal of the avant-garde image of the bourgeoisie. It exposes a gap between official decorum, enforced by prosecutors and editors, and a more tolerant private reading practice, which resonates loosely with the gramsci-gap idea of legitimacy eroding before a replacement exists, though Gay's is a change in reading conventions rather than a political interregnum.
