Bourgeois Eros as Seen in the Mirror of the Lower Orders

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay argues that nineteenth-century bourgeois accounts of aristocratic, peasant and working-class love were less reports than projective self-portraits, poised between condescension and envy, and that the evidence, once gathered, shows the poor sharing the same drives and defenses while loving under conditions of privation, exposure and exploitation.

This opening section of Gay's epilogue asks how nineteenth-century middle-class people used the sexual lives of other classes to define their own. Aristocrats were trivialized as debauched and comic; the lower orders were treated earnestly and with a charged mixture of horror and longing. Gay sorts the bourgeois verdicts into three leading views (sensual and happy, sensual and unhappy, chaste and happy) and argues that the contradictions say more about the observers than the observed: the reports are "unwitting exercises in cultural autobiography." He then lays two fables side by side (Michelet in 1846, Freud in 1917), turns to Freud's 1883 letter on the price and reward of bourgeois self-restraint, and moves to the evidence itself: workers' autobiographies (Bromme, Holek, Baader, Perdiguier, Nadaud), social surveys (Wagner's pastors), and Gattel's 1898 study of Viennese neurotics. His conclusion is that working-class love was as varied as bourgeois love and shaped by the same drives and defenses, but was pressed by poverty, crowding, workplace coarseness and the sexual predation of employers, so that its ground tone was resignation where the middle-class one was often exuberance.

Themes

Class psychologies as projective test. Victorians believed each class had its own moral code (Freud to his fiancee, the radical James Dawson Burn). Aristocrats were the easy butts of satire, yet the satire was mixed with emulation; the poor were read in earnest, and inconsistently. Gay's key move is that observations of others disclose bourgeois "satisfied with their own sexual performance—or anxious about it." Attribution matters: the Goncourts, Abel Hugo, Dickens's Steerforth, Meredith and the Saturday Review are each cited as instances of the bourgeois gaze, not as evidence of how the poor lived.

Three views and the split between deplored and celebrated license. The poor were seen as sensual and happy, sensual and unhappy, or chaste and happy. Villerme, John Simon and the Saturday Review found incest and promiscuity in overcrowded housing and treated the poor's freedom as a kind of slavery; Debay claimed rural sex was brutal and joyless; Zola's peasants were offensive or refreshing depending on the reader. Others, Hugo among them, held up rustic chastity as a reproach to aristocratic imitation by the bourgeoisie.

Michelet versus Freud: the two fables. Michelet (Le peuple, 1846) urged young bourgeois to marry women of the people to escape a calculating marriage market and a sickly, exhausted class. Freud's 1917 parable of the caretaker's and the landlord's daughters runs the opposite way: the proletarian girl stays guilt-free and neurosis-free, the bourgeois girl represses and falls ill. Both fictions are about bourgeois sexuality. Gay's reading of Freud is that neurosis is what bourgeois civilization pays to atone for its delicacy and its restraint. This is the volume's theme of repression and its costs (see bourgeois-defenses-against-eros) restated at the level of class.

The paradox of black-coated Eros. Freud's 1883 letter ("We keep ourselves for something, we know not what") shows renunciation producing refinement and deeper feeling. Gay treats it as a self-portrait of a young educated man in love, not knowledge of the poor. The paradox: postponement risks neurosis and marital misery, yet trained sensitivity should also enlarge the capacity for erotic play, and the middle classes had access to erotic knowledge (books, pictures, travel) the poor lacked. Hence the middle classes were "at once more likely and less likely" to secure gratification. Bertrand Russell's phrase "black-coated Respectability, the living God" frames the epilogue.

The evidence problem and the sources. Before the 1880s middle-class knowledge was thin (Thackeray on Wapping); peasants were reticent; workers' autobiographies were rare, tract-like and often prompted by editors such as Paul Gohre. French ones were politically minded and reticent; German ones (Bromme, Holek) are unusually frank. Later inquiries into budgets, health and prostitution dragged private life into sociological view. Gattel's 100 Viennese patients showed the poor with the same anxiety, phobias, masturbation and hysteria, "almost, one might say, like bourgeois."

Same drives, same defenses, different circumstances. Working-class girls too repressed and practised learned ignorance, yet the poor "knew more, and repressed less": shop-floor banter, crowded rooms and early traumas gave a joyless sexual education that bourgeois adolescents largely escaped. Gay adds that the erotic education came from above as well: employers, managers and masters exploited dependent women (Harriet Kidd, Holek's Marie and Luise, Lucy Luck, Spann's illegitimacy statistics on domestic servants), though he judges claims that bourgeois seducers filled the brothels exaggerated.

Love among the poor, and Gay's comparative verdict. Navvy and laborer testimony, the Wagner survey of rural sexual morals, and Holek's seven-year "wild marriage" show real tenderness and passion under pressure of unemployment, illness, motherhood and lack of contraception. Gay refuses to milk the pathos (Burn: "every body of men has its own standards of perfection"; some rural communities welcomed premarital pregnancy). Still, the ground tone was "stolid endurance" and resignation, where bourgeois lovers had glowing metaphors. Freud's point that romance is a luxury of high civilization is endorsed by Gay, though he is describing tendencies and evidence, not claiming a rule.

Notable passages

The central methodological passage (c1) is the claim that middle-class investigations of peasants and workers were exercises in self-definition, poised between condescension and envy. Gay's account of Michelet and Freud (c2) shows two opposed fables making one point about the bourgeoisie, and the 1883 letter (c3) supplies the volume's clearest statement of what restraint costs and buys. The three-views passage (c4) names the core dispute and its self-referential function. The employer-exploitation passage (c5) qualifies any idyll of lower-class sexual freedom by showing the power relations shaping it, and the closing contrast of Wenzel and Luise Holek with Charles and Fanny Kingsley (c6) is Gay's own verdict on class-specific emotional tone, with envy named as a form of anxiety.

Connections

The section extends Gay's case against the caricature of the prudish, hypocritical bourgeois: see the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction and bourgeoisophobia. Its defenses argument links to bourgeois-defenses-against-eros, and the observation that the poor lacked privacy, cleanliness and health while bourgeois love presumed them ties to bourgeois-privacy-as-conquest and infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness; the workers' autobiographies contrast with the middle-class habit of self-writing discussed in self-construction-through-writing-gay. For World Machines, the section bears on how a class constructs an emotional culture partly by contrast with an imagined other, and on the gap between official respectability and private practice; the fit with world-machines is loose, since Gay offers a social-psychological history rather than an infrastructural one. Gay does not name Foucault in this unit, so any contrast with michel-foucault's repressive hypothesis is the reader's, not Gay's. See also the-bourgeois-experience and peter-gay.
