Prostitution Literature, Fiction and the Turn to Reform, 1840s-1914

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay traces how, from the 1840s to 1914, bourgeois writers, novelists, vice commissions and Magdalen-home reformers shifted from denial or hysteria about prostitution toward remorse, social responsibility and rehabilitation, while noting the ambivalence, sentimentality and limited results of that shift.

Gay continues his account of how the bourgeoisie moved "from denial or hysteria about prostitution to a measure of understanding and attempts at reparation." He first shows that prostitution was an "open secret" before 1848, already surveyed by Parent-Duchatelet, Mayhew, Tait, Wardlaw and others, and follows the Prussian bordello controversy of 1845-56, where policy reversed itself repeatedly. He then reads novels (Dumas fils, Maupassant, Zola, Huysmans, Goncourt, Galdos, Gaskell, Dickens, Collins) as a "suggestive, though far from reliable, barometer" of what readers could tolerate. He ends with the municipal and Progressive-era vice commissions and the Magdalen homes, and argues that the rehabilitation of the prostitute became a fixed duty of the bourgeois conscience, in what he calls the erotic economy of the late-nineteenth-century middle classes. This is part of the-bourgeois-experience by peter-gay.

Themes

An open secret, then a policy problem. Gay stresses that the literature on prostitution was already large before Greg's 1850 review. The German pamphlets show reformers' "honest doubts" and officials' ambivalence. Prussia closed its brothels in 1845, reopened them in 1851 and closed them again in 1856. Gay reads these reversals as officials implicitly saying "that sensuality is always out of control." Reformers, "growing more aggressive though no less nervous," would take a less fatalistic line. The episode is about an institution (police regulation and medical inspection) trying to manage intimate life, and failing in both directions.

Fiction as barometer of tolerable attitudes. French novels long romanticized the courtesan. Dumas fils's Marguerite Gautier and Maupassant's tales let readers indulge "pressing and improper desires" without any critique of hypocrisy. Gay reads Zola's Nana as a moral tract against devouring lust, not a realist exposé. Huysmans, Goncourt and Galdos are the clinical counter-current. Russian writers, Gaskell, Dickens and Collins show the prostitute as a person and prepare "a new compassion on which humane philanthropic actions could build." Gay attributes the sentimental stereotypes to burghers' fantasies and to what they would accept, and he does not present them as evidence about prostitutes themselves.

From hysteria to remorse. The section's central claim is that mid-century onward, the shift is one of moral stance, not only of volume. The later reports are cooler, more statistical and more official, but above all "ready to hold society responsible" and focused on rehabilitation. Gay is careful to say the shift was "never complete": administrators and some physicians still wanted segregation and harsh punishment, and figures such as Dr. Corlieu mocked the reformers as sentimental.

Vice commissions as a genre and an institution. The Committees of Twelve, Fifteen or Twenty share personnel (minister, priest, rabbi, physicians, lawyers, social workers) and rhythm. Their recommendations lean on sex education and economic improvement. Gay observes that their intentions were conservative but their implications "radically subversive," since they implied that abolishing poverty and spreading the facts of life would end vice. The reports connect to international social-hygiene congresses and to the privileging of a larger cultural frame ("social evil").

Magdalen homes: benevolence, punishment, and inflated success. Penitentiaries and shelters multiplied (sixty English Magdalen homes in 1856, 308 half a century later). Success rates were routinely reported by fund-raisers in optimistic terms, and many homes were "strict, punitive, and vengeful." Urania Cottage, shaped by Dickens's letters, is Gay's model of humane practice: Dickens saw the girls as victims of a society that "has used her ill," and insisted on colour. Gay also notes the mutual incomprehension across class lines, and that recipients might read middle-class guilt as "a rehearsal of self-indulgence."

Remorse and the bourgeois superego. Gay uses psychoanalytic vocabulary for the moral change. The prostitute "insinuate[d] herself into the agenda of the bourgeois superego," and Jane Addams attributes earlier neglect to "emotional resistances." The rescue campaign is thus tied to guilt about erotic desire, and Gay ends by placing it in the "erotic economy" of the class more than in its craving for degraded love objects. This continues his argument in bourgeois-defenses-against-eros terms: a defense (denial, hysteria) gives way to a more honest but still self-regarding response.

World Machines resonances

The passage bears on how a class's emotional culture is regulated by institutions (police regulation, print, medicine, reform societies) and on how dominant attitudes change between roughly the 1840s and 1900s, in line with Gay's transition story. It resonates loosely with legibility-as-machine-core: Parent-Duchatelet's positivist "reaching for precision," the tables and street-by-street surveys of the vice commissions, are attempts to make a hidden practice statistically readable and governable, though Gay presents them as motivated by moral anxiety, not administrative control alone. The gap between the fiction of the reclaimed prostitute and the modest facts of rehabilitation is a small case of the distance between official ideology and private practice. Gay is a historian, and no machine framework is at work in the text.

Notable passages

The courtesan with a heart of gold. Gay explains why the Dame aux camelias worked: it condensed improper desires into one doomed, redeemable figure, and Maupassant's amusing tales could not be mistaken for critique (c1).

The mark of remorse. The distinction between 1840 and 1900 literature lies in its readiness to hold society responsible and to reform the prostitute (c2).

Conservative intentions, subversive implications. The vice commissions' recommendations on sex education and poverty imply a radical conclusion (c3).

The bourgeois superego. Addams on philanthropy's neglect of prostitution; Gay's conclusion that the whore "insinuated" herself into the superego's agenda (c4).

Urania Cottage. Dickens's letters and the interim balance sheet, showing both empathy and modest results (c5).

Rehabilitation in the erotic economy. Gay's closing judgment on why the campaign for the prostitute mattered more than the supposed bourgeois craving for degraded love objects (c6).
