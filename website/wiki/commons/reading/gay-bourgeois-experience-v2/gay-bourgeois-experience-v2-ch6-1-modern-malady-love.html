Nervousness as the Modern Malady of Love

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay traces how "nervousness" became the nineteenth century's favorite diagnosis of bourgeois civilization, shows the diagnosis to be more a projection of anxious diagnosticians than a measured fact, and credits Freud with relocating its cause in sexual repression while faulting him for accepting the premise and for generalizing from his patients.

Gay opens Chapter Six by saying that around mid-century physicians and social observers decided that being bourgeois was itself a strain, and that the strain surfaced in two phenomena: nervousness and prostitution. Chekhov's "An Attack of Nerves" and Jane Addams's tract of 1912 show the two joined. Contemporaries read both as the price of a repressed way of loving, and Gay announces flatly that "They were wrong." The first section takes up nervousness. He places it in a long lineage of laments about estrangement (Plato, Augustine, Schiller, Hegel, Marx, Durkheim, Tönnies) that are implicitly about love's absence. He then follows the word "nervous" as it shifts from "vigorous" to "tender, irritable" (Johnson, 1783), and shows the diagnosis spreading through letters, diaries, novels and advertisements. German physicians and popularizers (Kisch, Erb, Krafft-Ebing, Lamprecht) made it a national theme. The center of the section is the American neurologist George Beard, who coined "neurasthenia" and treated it as the price of civilization, with compensations. Gay's own verdict is that the flood of diagnoses was unmeasured and largely a projection of anxious experts. Freud's 1908 paper then turned "nervousness as the price of culture" into "nervousness as the price of repression". Gay credits the insight but calls it "an unsubstantiated claim, at best a specialized discovery."

Themes

Nervousness as the diagnosis that carried a class's self-image. Terms like alienation, anomie and Gesellschaft were critics' words. "Nervousness", unlike them, entered common parlance and shaped bourgeois self-perception. Gay ties it obliquely to erotic life: Otto von Leixner's diary links his nervous excitement to friends, mother and "a new love which can only end in renunciation", and Symons writes that "The modern malady of love is nerves." Most contemporaries "took care not to see" the connection. This bears on how an era's emotional culture gets a vocabulary, and on the gap between official ideology and private practice. Compare bourgeois-defenses-against-eros.

Gender and class inflections. Symons treats nerves as the splendid defect of aesthetes, the moralists Gay cites as a decline from manliness (Basil Ransom, Kisch, Falkenhorst), and Beecher and Alcott mainly as a woman's condition. The anti-feminist and feminist camps used the same material in opposite directions. Gay sides with the feminists, who "read nervousness not as a cause but as an effect." Cures were prescribed in a virility idiom of sports, cold showers and male superiority.

Beard: civilization's price, half-celebrated. Beard attributes the rise of neurasthenia to "steam power, the periodical press, the telegraph, the sciences, and the mental activity of women", as well as clocks, schedules and "repression of emotion". His metaphors are hydraulic and electrical: the mind as a power plant or bank account of nervous energy. He thinks the nervous races and classes are the more civilized, and he finds compensations such as the beauty of the American girl. His life, Gay says, refutes his own warnings. This resonates loosely with the modernity-machine and its legibility-driven infrastructure of clocks and telegraphs, but Beard's account is a nineteenth-century somatic theory of an individual's nerve reserves and not a theory of machines. Gay is not doing machine theory either.

Gay's skeptical historiography. The claim that nervousness was rising rests on "anecdotal case reports, casual observations, seductive metaphors, literary fancies", with no statistics, and the new alertness may reflect new sensitivity to an old ailment. Modern urban life could as well be exhilarating. "The diagnosis of nervousness was a form of auto-intoxication on the part of the diagnosticians", which Freud would call projection. Nervousness was "less a pervasive symptom of bourgeois society than of those who detected it." The point rhymes with bourgeoisophobia and the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction: the bourgeoisie's critics, and its own experts, drew a caricature of it. It also has a clear antecedent in Gay's argument that the legend of the repressed Victorian is overdrawn, and michel-foucault's repressive hypothesis is its natural foil.

Freud's move and Gay's dissent. Freud's 1908 paper reverses the accepted view that nervousness comes from debauchery. It roots nervousness in culture's excess suppression of sexual life: the ideal of abstinence until marriage, contraception that impairs pleasure, and lifelong fidelity. Modern marriage is "the continuation by other means" of sexual unease, and the fusion of the tender and sensual currents is rare. Gay's reservations: Freud accepted the rising-incidence premise uncritically, underestimated the erotic knowledge and openness of respectable bourgeois, and drew social generalizations from patients who "were the casualties of their culture". Hence Freud is "a representative as much as a critic of his time." This is the Gay-versus-Freud tension that runs through the volume, and is a hedge worth carrying downstream.

Notable passages

The opening framing sets the chapter's thesis (nervousness and prostitution as the extorted tribute) and immediately rejects the contemporary conclusion. The passage on women shows the same evidence recruited by both anti-feminists and feminists. Beard's catalogue of causes gives the period's own list of what it thought modern civilization consisted of, and it includes the printing press, telegraph, clocks and the repression of emotion. The "auto-intoxication" paragraph is Gay's methodological center. The Freud paragraph states the price-of-repression thesis, and the closing evaluation is Gay's hedged verdict on it.
