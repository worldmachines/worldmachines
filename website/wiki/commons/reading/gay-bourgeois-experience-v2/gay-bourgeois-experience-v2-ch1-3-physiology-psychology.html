Theories of Love: From French Cynicism to Freud's Two Currents

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay traces nineteenth-century theories of love from French cynicism through Schopenhauer's and Nietzsche's metaphysics of sexual will to Freud, whose developmental account of the tender and sensual currents, unconscious memory and transference bridged physiology and psychology while reading love pessimistically.

Gay opens with the foreign legend that the French monopolized worldly, cynical writing about love, then shows that many Frenchmen shared the censorious view and that the search for love's essence was pan-European. The bulk of the unit is an intellectual genealogy: Schopenhauer's metaphysics of sexual love as the will's instrument, Nietzsche's inversion of that pessimism into a celebration of passion 'spiritualized' as love, and finally Freud, who is presented as both heir to these amateur psychologists and their scientific superior. Gay's own concern is how Freud made love a developmental, unconscious and inherently precarious achievement, and how this theory offers a way to think systematically about bourgeois love. Part of the-bourgeois-experience by peter-gay.

Themes

Philosophers of love import their own erotic history. Schopenhauer's thought is a 'photographic negative' of his life: a pessimist who enjoyed life, a misogynist who sought out women. Gay reads the gap between public demeanor and private need as the management of imperious impulses, explicitly 'not from hypocrisy'. His reading of Schopenhauer's childhood (a father's probable suicide, a cold and quarrelsome mother) is psychoanalytic conjecture, flagged with 'almost certainly' and 'seems only too likely'.

Sex as will, and the fashionable reception. Schopenhauer treats sexual infatuation as rooted in the drive that serves the species, indifferent to individuals. His ideas were ignored for decades and then found a large following after the 1850s, fitting a widespread fear of women in late-century literature and art. Gay calls him a 'metaphysical Diderot' and notes the parallel to Freud's fusion of the love of life and the love of love.

Nietzsche: sublimation as love. Nietzsche keeps the erotic will but makes it the champion of the passions. Gay stresses that his misogyny, like Schopenhauer's, reads as anxiety, 'personal malaise disguised as cultural concern', while it also serves a defense of passion against Christianity's 'castratism'. Love is 'the spiritualization of sensuality'; virtues are refined passions; bourgeois marriage is society's licensed sexual gratification with little to do with love. This links the unit to bourgeois-defenses-against-eros and to sublimation as Gay's recurring concept.

Freud's debts and independence. Gay treats Freud as a brilliant condenser of German and French ideas who deliberately avoided reading Schopenhauer and Nietzsche, and who owed more to a physician, Charcot ('toujours la chose genitale'), than to a philosopher. The Charcot anecdote also illustrates repression at work in Freud himself. Freud's own credit to poets is noted: 'Everything has already been said, certainly in the domain of love.'

The two currents and development. A normal love unites the tender and the sensual currents. Don Juan, far from a triumph, is read as neurotically unable to unite them. The affectionate current is older, rooted in early childhood; the sensual current arrives with puberty after long latency; the incest barrier forces a transfer to new objects still chosen after the infantile model. Freud thereby 'threw his bridge between physiology and psychology': the libido is biological yet expressed in feelings.

Unconscious memory, transference and loss. Gay calls the role of unconscious memory in love Freud's most original contribution: earlier writers (Stendhal, Balzac, Collins) knew love was irrational, but Freud made it a script from the repressed past. Being loved early underwrites the capacity to love (object constancy). Education in love is an education through loss and threatened withdrawal of love.

Freud's pessimism, and Gay's reservation. Perfect fusion of the component drives and the two currents is 'fragile, rather uncommon'; fixation explains the varieties of sexual taste; lovers' overvaluation of the beloved recalls narcissism and compulsion. Gay adds that Freud neglects benign idealization (reflexive crystallization), that his own marriage was less conflicted than his theory, and that his pessimism 'outran his evidence and his experience', while the explanatory range of libido theory was 'enormously impressive'.

Notable passages

The Schopenhauer passage (c1) states the sexual drive as the will's affirmation and the ground of the pessimism Gay finds 'seductive'. The Nietzsche pair (c2, c3) gives the sublimation thesis and the attack on bourgeois marriage. c4 is Gay's compact statement of Freud's 'two currents' and the Don Juan diagnosis. c5 gives the developmental sequence from tenderness to puberty's sensual current. c6 states the claim that unconscious memory shapes love.

World Machines resonance

The unit bears on how a class's emotional culture is theorized and, arguably, partly produced by its intellectual apparatus: a whole 'metaphysics of love' rises to dominance from the 1850s while bourgeois marriage is criticized as loveless. Nietzsche's charge concerns the gap between official ideology (marriage as honorable) and private practice, which touches the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction and bourgeoisophobia, though Gay is a historian of feeling and this is only a loose rhyme with a machine framing. The recurring defense and sublimation vocabulary connects to bourgeois-defenses-against-eros.
