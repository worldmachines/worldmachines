Denial as Protection: Homoerotic Attachment in a Culture of Reticence

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Opening on Proust's joyless lesbian scene at Montjouvain, Gay argues that the bourgeois culture of denial and reticence about homoerotic love gave sexual heretics a surprising zone of impunity (Albert Dodd, Amiel, Mabel Barrows, Holtei, Vaughan) that was lost when late-century exposure, the Wilde trials and clinical inquiry made the matter public.

Chapter Four of The Tender Passion opens with Proust's Montjouvain scene, in which Mademoiselle Vinteuil and her lover act out a ritual of desecration before her dead father's photograph, and asks why Proust could publish it in 1913 with almost no outcry. Gay's answer is that the public was already saturated with press reports of sexual irregularity (the Eulenburg affair), that Sand and Balzac had prepared the ground, and above all that Proust made his homosexuals joyless: a fate rather than a vice, and so publishable. The first section proper, "Dividends of Denial," then argues that the bourgeois ideal of love left whole territories unnamed, that this reticence was not simple hypocrisy but one side of an unresolved bourgeois dilemma between denial and candor, and that for most of the century it protected homosexual lovers. Case studies follow: the Yale student Albert Dodd's journal, Amiel, Holman Hunt and Millais, the schoolgirl Mabel Barrows, Karl von Holtei, Gogol, and Dr. C. J. Vaughan of Harrow. The section closes on the irony that the late-century shift toward exposure worsened the fate of homosexuals.

Themes

Suffering as the price of publishability. Gay argues that Proust's depressed portrayal matched his own view of his sexuality and was also prudent. It was safe to speak of "aberrations" as a physician, moralist or patriot, in technical terms, or as a foreign disease. "A fate is not a vice," and pain, not pleasure, made an account of homosexuals easier to publish. The one thing respectable opinion agreed on was that "pederasty" did not deserve to be called love (the journalist Ernest-Charles is quoted for this, not Gay).

Denial as cultural defense, and its dividends. Gay's central claim is that the fading reticence was "not hypocrisy" but one way of handling a "familiar bourgeois dilemma": the middle classes "could never quite decide whether denial was a sounder cultural defense than candor." The paradoxical result is that lovers were safer before the 1880s. Bachelors and women sharing quiet households drew praise; Tchaikovsky married to give the lie to rumor; George Sand was notorious for publicity, not for lovers. The 1811 House of Lords finding for two schoolmistresses, and Dickens's Miss Wade, show impunity resting on a "sheer failure of sexual imagination." This is a strong statement of the gap between official ideology and private practice, and it sits well with bourgeois-defenses-against-eros and with Gay's foil against the repressive hypothesis (michel-foucault). He is explicitly ambivalent about Durkheim-style claims that society manufactures deviants: the bourgeois style "complicates this generalization."

The Wilde trials as backlash against exposure. The press response, in Gay's reading, is less moral outrage at homosexuality than fury at a violator of "the supreme social rule of reticence" and at the anxiety his display provoked. The St. James's Gazette's plea for "a dash of wholesome bigotry" and the Times's "naturally" show a wish to return to "protective ignorance." The moralists' claims are theirs; Gay's inference is that reticence itself was the value being defended.

Passionate friendship and the innocent reading. Dodd's journal, with its oscillation among Julia, John Heath, Anthony Halsey and Elizabeth, Amiel's "Search out men more," the Hunt-Millais "sacred friendship," Mabel Barrows's crushes at St. Margaret's, and Holtei's autobiography show attachments felt and written about without shame, and often equated with love for the opposite sex. Gay reads Dodd's cut page, his dashes and his dreams psychoanalytically (dream work reversing the direction of desire; libido in a "pure" love). He also calls the innocence thesis "complacent and self-serving": it rested on the cult of friendship and on denying infantile sexuality, and "the power of denial" let the culture worry about ardent adolescent friendships and treat them as innocuous at once. Dodd's "impunity is a tribute to the unsuspected openness of his culture"; that he apparently overcame his homosexual appetites (he "contemplated marriage") raises a question Gay leaves open: "We shall never know how expensive this victory must have been to him." The diary as a place for self-analysis resonates with self-construction-through-writing-gay and bourgeois-privacy-as-conquest.

Discretion as a bargain, and the irony of exposure. Gogol shrouded his love for Vielhorsky in "elevated friendship"; Vaughan, surprised with a pupil, was contained rather than exposed, blocked from a bishopric, and kept his church place and reputation. "Fenced in but not exposed." After the 1880s, hesitant openness "invited blackmail, ruined careers, drove some into suicide." Gay closes with the reversal from "vices which have no names" to "the love that dare not speak its name."

For World Machines the unit is a case study in how a class's emotional culture can hold a large zone of tolerated practice open by not naming it, and how a transition in the infrastructure of naming (newspapers, trials, clinical categories, on Gay's telling from about the 1880s) converts tolerated ambiguity into a regulated category. It fits legibility-as-machine-core loosely, with the caveat that Gay never theorizes it: here legibility is the danger, and illegibility the shelter. Compare also the ideology-versus-practice theme in the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction. Gay is a historian of sensibility, and the machine reading is ours, hedged.

Notable passages

Why Proust could show it (c1). Gay lists the conditions for Proust's reception and then the decisive one: joyless homosexuals. The passage links the sexual and the literary marketplace and names the "doomed race" trope Proust supplied.

Denial versus candor (c2). The programmatic statement that reticence was a defense, not hypocrisy, and that "most bourgeois chose the spurious safety of ignorance over the risky benefits of knowledge."

The St. James's Gazette on Wilde (c3). Contemporary voices demanding intolerance and forgetfulness, with Gay's framing of the exposure as a rupture of reticence.

The paradox of permissiveness (c4). Durkheim complicated by the bourgeois mix of euphemism and candor; Mrs. General, Lord Gillies, and Dickens's Miss Wade.

Dodd's impunity (c5). The summing-up of Albert Dodd's career: bisexual attachments innocent because appearances were preserved, with the honest admission of not knowing the cost.

Vaughan and the reversal (c6). The bargain imposed on sexual heretics and the closing irony of exposure, ending in Douglas's "love that dare not speak its name."
