The Return of the Repressed: Law, Science and Literature Confront Homosexuality

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay argues that the contradictory laws, hostile satire, defensive medical Latin and coded literature surrounding homosexuality in the later nineteenth century show the return of the repressed in culture, as a new candor from the 1870s to 1890s ran together with shame, pathologizing and moralizing.

Gay opens with the patchwork of nineteenth-century sexual legislation (France, the Netherlands and Italy decriminalizing; Prussia's Paragraph 175; Britain's Labouchere Amendment and the Wilde trials). He reads this as Freud's return of the repressed operating at the level of culture: a defense that fails produces a distorted compromise between the need to know and the need to stay ignorant. He then traces the growth of a literature on homosexuality: heralding novels, the Krafft-Ebing/Westphal/Ulrichs vocabulary of "inversion", the mid-1890s spread of explicit writing, and the Wilde scandal. He argues that the new frankness had several causes (Darwinian and sociological secularization of sex, purity crusaders who publicized what they attacked, a medical profession expanding its jurisdiction) and yet stayed "shame-faced". The unit then follows the moralizing that persisted: antifeminist sexual insult, Symonds's failed attempt to pin down Whitman, physicians who retreated into Latin, and the exceptional Havelock Ellis. It closes with the coterie literature of the 1890s (the Chameleon), its links to Anglo-Catholic piety, and Protestant demagogues who transferred the innuendo used against feminists onto ritualists.

Themes

The return of the repressed as a cultural, not only individual, process. Gay borrows Freud's account of defenses failing and applies it to statute law and its capricious enforcement. Laws were hard to enforce (about 500 convictions a year under Paragraph 175), and easing penalties in Denmark increased prosecution. The structure is a compromise formation, not a simple prohibition. This is Gay's implicit answer to the legend of straightforward Victorian silence, and a natural foil to the repressive hypothesis of michel-foucault, though Gay stays inside a psychoanalytic vocabulary and does not engage it here. See also bourgeois-defenses-against-eros.

A vocabulary assembled from within a hostile culture. "Homosexuality" (Benkert, 1869) and "contrary sexual feeling" (Westphal, 1869) come from physicians, and homosexual writers such as Symonds adopted them, half defiant and half conformist. Ulrichs's claim that inversion is fate, not crime, was the one reading of the term welcome to homosexuals. Gay's remark that the 1870s to 1890s "invented homosexuality" is hedged ("too much to say, but not by a great deal") and belongs to a wider claim that Victorian reticence began to lift about two decades before the Queen's death.

Multiple, unintended causes of candor. Darwinism, sociology, purity crusaders and the medical profession each widened discussion for reasons unrelated to liberation. Medical engrossment of the sexual domain meant that it proposed all forms of homosexuality to be diseases. The result: discussion, but on the terms of pathology. This resonates loosely with WMP interest in institutions (medicine, print, reform societies) that make intimate life legible and regulable (legibility-as-machine-core), though Gay does not frame it as machinery and the fit is partial.

Gap between what physicians would say and how they said it. The physicians catalogued in detail while retreating into Latin "to keep the unauthorized from wading in these cesspools", and some drew hidden pleasure from the work. Ellis is the exception: sympathetic, empirical, but still treating inversion as an abnormality like colorblindness, whose sufferers should not be crushed by shame.

Displacement of sexual anxiety onto other targets. Early feminists, their male supporters and Anglo-Catholic ritualists were all met with sexual innuendo of "unmastered anxiety". Gay also notes that homosexual religious-literary coteries themselves fused sexuality with piety, in an echo of the eroticized religion treated elsewhere in the volume (see the-bourgeois-experience).

Class and audience. Gay stresses that few ordinary bourgeois saw the coterie writings; a spectacular trial or alert editor gave them currency. Social Democrats (Bebel, Bernstein) welcomed the scandals as reflecting on decadent bourgeois society, a reminder that the bourgeois emotional order was attacked from within and without (bourgeoisophobia).

Notable passages

The passage that names the thesis (c1) is short and central: legislation and its capricious application demonstrate "the return of the repressed in culture". The account of the causes of the new candor (c2) is Gay's most direct statement on how a change in a class's emotional-sexual culture came about, and on the 1850s-1890s shift; it stresses convergence of unrelated developments rather than any single driver, a shape that rhymes with the history-machine idea of distributed assembly, though Gay is describing a discourse shift, not a paradigm. The antifeminist passage (c3) shows sexual insult as a defense against the redistribution of roles. The Symonds-Whitman exchange (c4) is a compact case of private practice at odds with public statement: Whitman's invented six children and "terrible" denial, and Symonds choosing to believe him. Ellis's list of possible views of inversion (c5) marks the edge of what a sympathetic bourgeois expert could say. The Latin case histories (c6) are the clearest picture of shame surviving inside candor.
