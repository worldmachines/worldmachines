Fiction Beyond Good and Evil: Confession, Sensuality and the Incest Theme

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay traces how the nineteenth-century psychological and realist novel, from confessional first-person fictions through Gautier, Flaubert and Queiros, treated love as a contagion beyond good and evil and escaped or defied moralizing, and shows that the incest theme, the most heavily defended, persisted in Victorian-era fiction as an unconscious return of the repressed.

Gay argues that the quarrels between moralistic critics (the Saturday Review, Louis Proal) and novelists had a source older than mid-century campaigns: the psychological novel, from Constant's Adolphe (1816) onward, which made lovers' irrational fantasies "the common coin of literature." He follows it through Fromentin's Dominique, the retreat from poetic justice (Thackeray, then the punishment of the unloving rather than the unwise), Gautier's defiant Mademoiselle de Maupin, Flaubert's Madame Bovary, and Queiros's O Primo Bazilio and Os Maias. The last leads into a general discussion of incest as an ancient, irresistible and delicate theme, which Victorian-era literature mostly could enter only obliquely, as an unconscious disclosure. This is a unit about the literary front of the gap between public propriety and private feeling, read through Freud.

Themes

The confessional novel as a product of the age of privacy. Gay's paradox: in the very age that raised the wall between what people discussed and what they felt, fictional heroes and heroines became garrulous about passions ordinary readers scarcely admitted to themselves. Fiction thus served as a licensed channel for what social conversation withheld; compare bourgeois-privacy-as-conquest and infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness, and self-construction-through-writing-gay for the diary and letter counterparts. The first-person form enacts what Gay calls "erotic isolationism": lovers, with their libido invested in the beloved, have little left over for public interests.

Love as an experience beyond good and evil. Psychological novels made the omniscient, sententious authorial voice hard to sustain; love appears as infection or contagion, not policy or sin, and "One does not argue with obsessions." Realism and naturalism, with their mechanistic and biological theories of motive (Zola's determinism is Gay's extreme case, which he calls justly maligned), gave a vocal minority of writers and readers a way out of didacticism. Gay is explicit that this was a minority that grew confident by Victoria's last decades, while sentimental fiction "just a nice story" stayed the best-selling staple. Serious novelists came to punish the failure to love (Casaubon, Karenin, Sue and Jude) rather than loving unwisely.

Memory, infancy and love (Fromentin). Dominique is read as documenting that love feeds on memory. Fromentin's Jenny shared his mother's name, so his love was "infected with recollections of infantile pleasures"; writing the novel converted passive suffering into activity, an aim of overcoming rather than recapturing memories. The gain, Gay says, is psychological rather than moral. The same Freudian motif returns with Flaubert's Frederic Moreau, whose feelings for his first love mix with "an aversion like the dread of incest" and a mother's kiss.

Provocation versus consequence: Gautier and Flaubert. Gautier's preface attacks the "great affectation of morality" and bourgeois utilitarianism ("Nothing is truly beautiful unless it is useless") and praises Fourier's harnessing of drives; his heroine's androgynous, sensual education is polemical rather than tragic. Gay explains his relative impunity by the intransitive, blithe quality of that sensuality: nothing follows from it. Flaubert, admiring but pitiless, shows undisciplined fantasy nourished on bad romances producing debt, affairs and suicide. Gay thinks the attempted suppression of Madame Bovary clumsy yet an anxious but perceptive reading of its threat to proper notions of love. Reception (Baudelaire's "hymn to beauty," Mirecourt's "poison in a diamond goblet") is the other party's testimony and stays theirs.

Time and place change what is tolerable (Queiros). More than twenty years after Bovary, O Primo Bazilio is a novel of appetites that provoked no disapproval; its audacity is in treatment, not theme, with its hint of "adultery with a bit of incest." Os Maias, by contrast, makes incest central, but unwitting, ending in a sad rather than revolting separation without the guilt that, on Gay's psychoanalytic reading, accompanies even the wish. Note Gay dismisses symbolic readings of Portugal's decadence as inhospitable to Queiros's light touch.

Incest and the return of the repressed. Gay's Freudian claim: incest is a perennial theme (Old Testament to Thomas Mann), proving "love begins at home"; impermissible wishes cannot be destroyed but reemerge distorted as symptoms, tastes or stories. Because the taboo obstructs even its discussion, psychoanalysis suits the historian of love. Periods of candor (Greek myth, Sophocles, Euripides's Phaedra as stepmother mask, Ford, Byron, Shelley's The Cenci) contrast with reticent Victorian literature, where the theme appeared, if at all, as unconscious disclosure that the public absorbed unconsciously. See bourgeois-defenses-against-eros for Gay's wider account of defenses.

World Machines resonance (hedged). The unit fits WMP's interest in the gap between official ideology and private practice and in print as an institution regulating intimate life; compare the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction and bourgeoisophobia for the avant-garde attack on bourgeois respectability, and michel-foucault as the foil to Gay's repression-and-return model. This does not map onto machine cycles; Gay tracks a minority of writers gradually gaining confidence, not a system transition, and he is a historian rather than a machine theorist. See also the-bourgeois-experience and peter-gay.

Notable passages

The opening claim about the age of privacy and garrulous fictional lovers (c1) sets the unit's paradox. The short "beyond good and evil" paragraph (c2) is the compact thesis about authorial voice. Fromentin's aim to overcome memory (c3) is the clearest psychological reading, and the passage on punishing the unloving (c4) shows the moral centre of fiction shifting while conventional romance persisted. The Gautier/Flaubert contrast (c5) explains why sensuality with no consequences escaped prosecution while consequential eroticism did not. Finally the Freudian incest passage (c6) and the Victorian reticence passage (c7) state the return-of-the-repressed argument and its historical application.
