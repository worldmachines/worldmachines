Love as the Novel's Business, and Fiction as a Community of Fantasies

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay argues that love was the governing subject of the nineteenth-century novel, and, using Freud on fantasy, that novels are disciplined collective fantasies through which a class like the bourgeoisie shows what it wanted, feared and could tolerate seeing of its own erotic life, while most readers preferred soothing happy-ending fare to the demanding masters.

The opening pages of Chapter Three of the-bourgeois-experience (Vol. II, by peter-gay) survey nineteenth-century fiction and find love its inescapable preoccupation, whatever else (money, class, politics, the railroad, the slum) novelists studied. Trollope, Turgenev, Gogol, Eliot, James, Balzac, Zola, Galdos and others are enlisted as witnesses; the bildungsroman makes love the supreme educator; and the commercial fact that love was 'the novelist's most saleable ware' is traced back to Bishop Huet's definition of the novel and forward to Stendhal's and Schopenhauer's remarks on readers' expectations. Section 1, 'A Community of Fantasies', then supplies the theory: drawing on Freud's reading of Oedipus Rex and his essay on the writer and daydreaming, Gay treats fantasies as wish-fulfilling scenarios of desire that sit on the boundary between personal and collective imagination, and fiction as fantasy 'disciplined, organized, embellished'. He then complicates the old 'mirror on the highway' theory (Stendhal, Taine, Fontane), and turns to the reading public: most bourgeois readers wanted happy endings, escape and 'just a nice story', while a minority of demanding masters forced open the conflicts their culture was driving from awareness.

Themes

Love as the novel's constant. Gay's evidence is deliberately wide (English, French, German, Russian, Spanish, Portuguese, American), aimed against the claim that only the French made love central. Trollope's failed attempt at a loveless novel is the comic proof. Politics and money are shown as entangled with erotic quests (James, Trollope, Balzac, Zola, Maupassant), and some novels (Moby-Dick, Huckleberry Finn) hint at attachments from which women are excluded, a foretaste of the volume's later 'problematic attachments'. This is Gay's argument by accumulation, not a measured survey.

Fiction as collective fantasy. Following Freud, fantasies are 'unsatisfied wishes' corrected in imagination; they are not rigid but 'change with every variation in the human situation'. A group with a strong hold on its members (a sect, a social class) imposes styles of feeling, channels appetites and so 'organizes communities of fantasies'. The individual supplies drive and anxiety, culture the materials, aesthetic form the architecture. This is why fantasies are 'rich historical material': they are historically shaped, not merely fantastic. It fits the volume's larger aim of reading the bourgeoisie as a family of desires and anxieties, and it fits bourgeois-defenses-against-eros in that the novel is one place where defenses (censorship, stylization, disguise) are visible in the product.

The mirror is cracked. Gay grants that the 'mirror traveling along a highway' (Stendhal, Madame de Stael, Hazlitt, Taine, Fontane) became a truism, but insists the mirror is 'curiously devised, badly discolored, sometimes cracked': the writer's own needs, rages and talents, and a 'professional superego' oriented to other writers, matter as much as milieu. Period labels (realist, naturalist, symbolist) are 'more useful for combat than they are for diagnosis'. The novel's value as a witness lies less in journalistic accuracy than in its capacity for 'analyzing, representing, and in significant ways distorting the erotic experience of contemporary culture'. Carry that hedge: the source is evidence of what a class could bear to see, not a transparent record of practice.

Comforting fiction and the happy ending. Most middle-class readers, served by lending libraries, railway stalls, reading circles and book clubs, wanted romances that trivialized real love's instability. Family periodicals sold fantasies of love bringing social advancement (Marlitt in Die Gartenlaube); more 'slippery' texts served solitary or venal sexual release. Serious fiction was darker (Collins, Zola, Fontane, Machado, Turgenev, Proust, Fromentin) yet made concessions to the pleasure principle, as when Dickens rewrote the ending of Great Expectations at Bulwer Lytton's urging. The middle-class public never coalesced into one reading public: taste ranged as widely as income and education.

What readers avoided, and what the masters forced. Gay's methodological turn is that the palatable fare tells the historian 'what squeamish and anxious readers wanted to avoid', i.e. the guise under which passions could be tolerated in print. The demanding writers 'forced access to the very conflicts, particularly the conflicts of love, that their culture was doing its best to drive from the stage of awareness', though they too used melodrama (the reprieve scene in Adam Bede).

World Machines resonances (hedged)

The most productive rhyme is the gap between official emotional ideology (love ends in marriage, blessed by parents and unvaried felicity) and the conflicts the culture would not acknowledge: print, lending libraries and serial magazines act as institutions that route intimate life into approved channels, which is also where the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction and the legend of the loveless Victorian are contested. The idea that a class perpetuates itself 'by imposing styles of feeling and expression' is a small-scale version of a dominant emotional culture being built and policed; Gay, a historian and Freudian, offers no machine theory, and the single-class, Euro-American frame is narrow.

Notable passages

The opening claim that novelists could not escape love (c1) sets the thesis, with Gogol's evasion as 'indirect tribute'. The Freudian definition of fantasy (c2) and its bridge role between personal and collective imagination (c3) carry the theory. The 'cracked mirror' passage (c4) is Gay's methodological caution about fiction as evidence. Jane Welsh Carlyle's amused pleasure in a sentimental novel (c5) is the case study of regressive, self-aware reading; mabel-loomis-todd's youthful reading list appears in the passage on middle-class taste and its limits (c6), which contains the historian's payoff for using popular fiction as evidence.
