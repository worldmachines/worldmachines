The Lion in Love: Geefs's Sculpture and the Volume's Conclusion

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay reads Guillaume Geefs's 1851 sculpture The Lion in Love as a dream in plaster that condenses the volume's themes, and concludes that bourgeois love was a vital, often happy compromise between erotic drive and defense, more profoundly erotic than its reputation and hard to read because its owners protected it.

Peter Gay closes The Tender Passion by reading a single object: Guillaume Geefs's plaster group The Lion in Love, shown at the Crystal Palace in 1851 (a nude young woman clips the claws of a smiling lion). He first establishes Geefs as a prosperous, thoroughly bourgeois portraitist and the theme as widely popular (Solomon, Tenniel, Bell's Una and the Lion, Punch), then contrasts the benign contemporary reception with the grim Aesopian fable, in which the woodsman kills the defanged lion. Gay treats the sculpture as an overdetermined wish, a dream image that condenses the themes of the first two volumes of the-bourgeois-experience. He then generalizes: the bourgeoisie lived through an anxious age of transition, held to a demanding ideal of married love, met censors and satirists who were louder than they were influential, and built a defended emotional fortress. His verdict is that the defensiveness was a tribute to passion, and that Victorian middle-class love was more erotic and more often happy than the legend allows.

Themes

The sculpture as dream and wish-fulfilment. Manifestly the work shows serene domestication, "the power of beauty over savage nature" as one visitor read it; latently it hints at "an astutely disguised castration." Gay uses Freudian dream-work vocabulary (condensation, overdetermination, wish presented as fact) on a cultural artifact. The interpretive method is itself the argument: bourgeois evidence needs a reading that goes past manifest surfaces.

Doctrine of distance. Allegory, myth and history let art show the nude and still claim elevated aims; art conveyed sensual information otherwise hard to come by. Compare the wider theme of defenses in bourgeois-defenses-against-eros.

The fatal woman as male anxiety made public. Gay denies that the devouring-female motif (Moreau, Munch, humor magazines) was mainly a backlash against feminism; it drew on older, timeless masculine fears. What was distinctive of the century was that liberal culture let men voice those anxieties publicly and rarely censored them, while also proving able to rethink its defenses and, eventually, accept the woman's movement ("the angel in the house was no angel and need not stay in the house").

Transition and the paradox of civilization. Bourgeois solidity was "as much a defense hastily erected as it was a goal happily reached," built on innovation (Hartmann's average expectable environment). Good news produces anxiety as much as bad, and civilization demands instinctual sacrifice. Love is both the enemy of order and a promise of permanence. For World Machines this rhymes with the way a dominant infrastructure of feeling is assembled under pressure and experienced as stable only in retrospect (cf. modernity-machine, world-machines), though Gay is offering psychological history, not machine theory.

The two currents joined in marriage. Citing Dr. George Napheys (late 1860s), whom Gay takes as speaking for respectable opinion, love has a physical and a moral nature, and only their union in marriage is true love. Gay maps the lion (raw sexual power) tamed by beauty onto this definition and calls the group a cherished ideal. Ideals are wishes that condense the cultural superego; the bourgeois erotic ideal was married love, and Shaw's Don Juan calls marriage the most licentious of institutions.

Censors and satirists, and their proportion. Gay concedes the real damage done by purifiers (Moore's Literature at Nurse, circulating libraries, the Vizetelly trial) and the fair targets of Byron, Flaubert, Ibsen, Nietzsche and Shaw. But the censors' notoriety exceeded their numbers and influence, and surviving letters and diaries (the Roes, the Kingsleys) show many erotic exceptions. This is Gay's counter to the anti-bourgeois legend (see bourgeoisophobia, the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction), and the gap between official ideology and private practice.

Public reticence, private frankness, and the historian's problem. The sharp bourgeois separation of private from public means more, and more frankly erotic, evidence than expected, but in restrained, self-protective accents; middle-class people "were generally ill at ease not so much with their amorous emotions as with giving them voice." See bourgeois-privacy-as-conquest. The fortress of feeling is hard to infiltrate, yet the defensiveness testifies to passion's power, giving the paradox that Victorian culture was at heart more profoundly erotic than more casual ages. Gay ends with Eliza Wilson's line "Happy marriages are not uncommon" as confirmed, and "The smile of Geefs’s lion is not forced." Implicitly he is arguing against a repressive-hypothesis picture such as michel-foucault's, though he does not name him here.

Notable passages

The sculpture as a dream in plaster (c1). Gay rejects the reading of the group as merely amusing and states his interpretive program: a wish represented as a fact, concealing conflict behind a picturesque facade and touching candor versus reticence, moralizing, and the defusing of erotic themes into acceptable form.

Doctrine of distance (c2). The nude allegory as a licensed vehicle for sensual knowledge, taken to be moral in essence. Gay presents this as the general method by which bourgeois culture conveyed erotic information.

Fatal woman and liberal culture (c3). The fullest statement that anxiety became public and negotiable in nineteenth-century liberal culture; feminism is not the sole root of the motif, and Gay hedges that innovating artists captured mostly universal masculine concerns.

Napheys and the ideal (c4). The lion tamed by beauty as an illustration of the union of sensuality and tenderness under matrimony; Gay reads it as a statement of an ideal rather than a description of practice.

Reticence and the need for interpretation (c5). Earnest age, uneasy balance of self-scrutiny and self-deception, the private/public split, and why the evidence needs reading past manifest surfaces.

The emotional fortress (c6). The volume's closing verdict, in which defensiveness becomes a tribute to passion, marriage is both a source and a resolution of conflict, and the reader is urged to learn to read the compromises between drives and defenses. See also peter-gay.
