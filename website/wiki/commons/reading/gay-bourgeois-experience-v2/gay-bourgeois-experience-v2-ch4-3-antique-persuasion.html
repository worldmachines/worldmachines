Greek Alibis and Freud's Way Out: Homoerotic Culture and the Bourgeois Dilemma

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay argues that the classical world gave nineteenth-century homosexual writers a dignified alibi and coded vocabulary (Pater, Symonds, Carpenter, George, Kuzmin), that bourgeois culture could neither confront nor absorb the subject openly, and that Freud's developmental, bisexual theory of homosexuality broke with both apologetics and stigma.

Gay surveys how homosexual men in the nineteenth century used the Greek inheritance as cover, dignity and program, then turns to Freud as the thinker who reframed the whole question. He moves from the appeal of Ganymede, Achilles and Plato, through the scholarly evasions of Jowett and the classicists, to Walter Pater's coded essay on Winckelmann, John Addington Symonds's unpublished A Problem in Greek Ethics and his open Venetian life with Angelo Fusato, Edward Carpenter's Dorian-solution advocacy, the Schweitzer affair defended by Lassalle, Stefan George's circle and Kuzmin's novella Wings. He argues that open advocacy only sharpened a dilemma the bourgeoisie preferred to evade, and that Freud's theory (the unconscious, bisexuality, polymorphous perversity, homosexuality as localized developmental arrest) was the one scandalous and humane alternative.

Themes

The classical alibi. Greek culture was remote enough to be an elite preserve yet close enough to supply models. Its "spirituality" (no overt homosexuality in Homer, Plato's ladder toward ideal beauty) let modern homosexuals borrow dignity from it. Gay calls the device transparent but popular. Many, he says, gratified themselves without any Greek apologia; the refined version was reserved for those educated to write poetry or translate.

Official evasion versus coded speech. Scholars (Jowett on Plato, debates over Sappho's "virginal purity") bowdlerized, changed pronouns and read advocacy as irony. Pater, Symonds and others wrote in euphemism not from prudishness but from policy, so that only the equipped would understand. This is Gay's picture of a gap between what a culture officially permits to be said and what it lets be read between the lines. It is close to bourgeois-defenses-against-eros, though the cover here is authorial strategy as much as unconscious defense.

Symonds as divided self. A Problem in Greek Ethics (1873) adopts the vocabulary of "sexual inversion" and calls boy-love a "vice" while designing its argument to make readers think better of it; Gay suggests its first intended reader was Symonds himself. Gay reads its conservatism (Achilles and Patroclus not pederasts, Rome debased, Christianity's "nobler synthesis" in the elevation of woman) as both tactic and self-protection. Symonds later acts out openly, and Gay uses his friends' acceptance of Angelo as evidence of late-Victorian patience and flexibility. Symonds denounced the bourgeois world while carrying its conscience, in Gay's telling.

Variety of national and social forms. Carpenter's simple life and Whitmanesque libertarianism, Schweitzer's private vice tolerated by a working-class party because Lassalle invoked the Greeks, George's elite circle offering young bourgeois an escape from the boredom, triviality and materialism of their class, and Kuzmin's unapologetic, fragmentary Wings (Russia, 1906) show the same repertoire adapted to different milieus.

The bourgeois dilemma. Kuzmin goes beyond Symonds in claiming superiority for male love, but advocacy called attention to a plight most bourgeois wanted to treat as gossip about decadent aristocrats or bohemians. Once public, it was undeniable that homosexuality touched the middle class. Early incidence estimates were small (Ulrichs, Benkert), and the honest, unblinking public airing that might have resolved the matter was something bourgeois culture could never quite adopt. Ellis, honored mainly abroad, is offered as proof.

Freud's reframing. Gay presents Freud as different in kind from Ellis (a collector) because he tied homosexuality to the unconscious, shifting erotic zones, infantile sexuality and bisexual endowment. Exclusive heterosexual interest becomes "a problem requiring elucidation," heterosexual genital love an achievement, and homosexuality a localized developmental arrest with plural causes and no implied moral, intellectual or artistic deficit. Therapy aims at self-acceptance; Freud opposes segregating homosexuals as a group and pleases neither reformers nor moralists. Gay notes Freud retained the normative hierarchy, a hedge worth carrying; Gay himself is a psychoanalytically informed historian, so this is sympathetic exposition.

Notable passages

The passage on Higginson's review of Wilde shows a contemporary seeing through the Greek alibi. The paragraph on Jowett and the classicists documents the official evasion. The Pater-on-Winckelmann passage is Gay's model of "covert theatrics of homoerotic propaganda" that bourgeois culture was preparing to absorb; it speaks to how an erotic vocabulary is built and legitimized inside a dominant class culture, an angle that bears only loosely on world-machines questions of how cultural infrastructure assembles, and Gay does not frame it that way. The Symonds and Angelo passage shows private practice outrunning public ideology, complementing bourgeois-privacy-as-conquest. The Kuzmin passage states Gay's thesis about why advocacy deepened the problem. The Freud passage supplies the theoretical hinge. Foil: this differs from michel-foucault's account, since Gay stresses concealment and tolerated exceptions rather than the production of a homosexual category, though Gay does trace how Westphal's, Ulrichs's and Benkert's vocabulary was adopted.
