Conflicting Legacies: the Inherited Debate over Eros

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay opens Volume II by arguing that nineteenth-century theorizing on love was a 'literature of reproach' that nonetheless voiced bourgeois anxieties, that nearly everyone agreed true love joins tenderness to sensuality (Freud's 'two currents'), and that this consensus sat atop two conflicting legacies, Christian asceticism versus Enlightenment and Romantic revaluations of eros, none of which the respectable bourgeois could comfortably live by.

The chapter opens by describing the voluminous nineteenth-century writing on love as a 'literature of reproach': work by novelists, philosophers and psychologists who neither sought nor won popular approval, better at recording bourgeois failures than the 'undoubted but unheralded successes'. Gay reads it as both evidence and ingredient of bourgeois culture: evidence of the war between the avant garde and the 'philistine', and a condensation of middle-class anxieties that bourgeois lovers could not articulate. Against this backdrop he asserts a near-universal consensus, from cynics to Freud, that true love is the conjunction of concupiscence with affection (Freud's 'two currents', the tender and the sensual, and the psychological impotence of men who 'where they love ... do not desire'). The section proper, 'Conflicting Legacies', then surveys the inheritance: the Hebraic-Hellenic typology (Heine, Arnold), the internally ambivalent Christian record on sex (Jerome, Augustine, Capellanus, Lombard, the Puritans, Pfister), the Enlightenment's frank valorization of passion (Hume, Diderot), and the Romantics' idealizing, imaginative, marriage-questioning alternative (Novalis, Schlegel, Byron, Shelley, Wordsworth, Coleridge).

Themes

The literature of reproach as symptom. Gay treats the critical writing on love as unjust but revealing: it 'condensed middle-class anxieties' and uncovered 'unconscious conflicts' that bourgeois morality imposed and compelled its devotees to conceal. This is Gay's methodological frame for the whole volume: hostile sources are read as data about what the class felt, not as verdicts. It ties to bourgeoisophobia and the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction, since the 'philistine' is here the avant garde's construct, though Gay also grants that the criticism hit real failures.

The consensus of the two currents. The ideal of joining attachment to lust is presented as ancient (Plutarch, scripture, Plato) and uncontroversial; Diderot's 'voluptuous rubbing of two intestines' and the celestial ascetics are dissenting extremes, and a 'broad center party' holds the majority. The real issue is not the definition but the assignment of proper places to eros and affection. This sets up the volume's later argument about defenses and the split between tenderness and sensuality (bourgeois-defenses-against-eros).

Christianity: ascetic ideal, adaptive practice. Gay refuses the freethinker's caricature. Heine and Arnold saw Hebraism as a sometimes necessary corrective; Gay adds that theology was itself ambivalent (Paul's 'better to marry than to burn', Augustine's sinless Edenic intercourse, Lombard echoing Jerome that 'all ardent love for one's own wife is adultery'), that Puritans praised the marriage bed, and that pious people evidently combined doctrine with erotic satisfaction. Yet the Catholic cult of virginity and Protestant restriction of love to lawful procreation left 'deposits of guilt and depression', and subordinated concupiscence to affection within lifelong marriage as a 'regulative if not always an attainable ideal'. The Immaculate Conception is read psychoanalytically as an elaborate family romance. The gap between doctrine and practice, and the persistence of the doctrine as a regulative norm, is the recurring pattern.

Enlightenment: eros without original sin. The philosophes attacked Christianity for slandering the passions, praising pride and sensuality while despising religious 'enthusiasm' as lower-order excitement. Diderot's aphorisms (the 'bit of testicle' beneath sublime sentiments; the little savage who would 'strangle his father and sleep with his mother') are read as proto-sublimation and proto-Oedipus insights; Hume and Montesquieu relativize sexual codes. Gay's caveat is that they had 'a far more intense interest in sex than in love', and that their work 'seemed written in water' until Freud. The bourgeois glimpsed these vistas mainly through shamefaced recourse to libertine literature.

Romanticism: love as applied religion, and a reproach to marriage. The romantics reacted against the philosophes' 'play of glands' and against philistines, without forming a school. Love becomes imagination's showpiece ('applied religion', Novalis), sensuality is purified but subordinate to the spiritual, and the ideal (a 'slow, continuous embrace') demands mutuality, female cultivation, and experience, which puts lifelong marriage in question. Gay notes the romantic gender split (Byron's 'woman's whole existence', Michelet) fit domestic ideology perfectly even as the ideal offered women wider vistas. Schlegel's Lucinde is a manifesto of reproach: bourgeois marriage 'institutionalized indifference'. The bourgeois struck back by reading only its obscenity; the notorious lives of Shelley, Musset and Byron were a scandal that bourgeois could only deplore and, 'in their most candid moments, envy'. Gay also credits Wordsworth's evidently loving marriage against Coleridge's sneer, a reminder to keep each writer's claim his own.

Relevance for World Machines (hedged). Gay is describing how a class's emotional culture inherits, filters and domesticates competing older repertoires (Christian, Enlightenment, Romantic) rather than adopting one; the official ideal (affection over concupiscence, in lifelong marriage) coexists with divergent private practice and with a hostile literature that both indicts and shapes it. That is loosely analogous to layered inheritance across machines (world-machines) and to the gap between ideology and practice (gramsci-gap only in the weak sense of an ideal losing legitimacy), but Gay makes no such claim and his frame is psychoanalytic and cultural, not infrastructural. The unit's foil to michel-foucault's repressive hypothesis is implicit in the picture of Christianity as accommodating rather than uniformly repressive. See also the-bourgeois-experience and peter-gay.

Notable passages

The consensus (c1). Gay states the one point 'on which cynics, metaphysicians, researchers, and ordinary bourgeois could cheerfully unite', quotes Freud's 'two currents', and introduces the neurotic split: 'Where they love they do not desire, and where they desire they cannot love.' This is the volume's organizing thesis in miniature.

Two legacies (c2). Gay pivots from consensus to conflict, naming the Hebrew and Hellene types via Heine and Arnold and noting that behind Arnold's decorous terms lay 'the hoary debate over Eros and his true nature'.

Protestant accommodation (c3). The Puritan defense of the marriage bed and Pfister's remark that the Reformation was 'an analysis of Catholic sexual repression' (a very inadequate one) anchor Gay's insistence on the complexity of religious attitudes, and his point that nineteenth-century Protestants remained guilt-ridden regardless.

Diderot as proto-Freudian (c4). Diderot's 'bit of testicle' aphorism is offered as an early comment on sublimation: 'the most exquisite flower of love grows from the soil of erotic urges'.

Love as applied religion (c5). Novalis and Schlegel show the romantic elevation of love and the imagination's transfiguring power, the counterpart to the philosophes' physiology.

Lucinde and the literature of reproach (c6). Coleridge's sneer at Wordsworth and Schlegel's charge that bourgeois marriage is concubinage or 'institutionalized indifference' close the unit by returning to its opening frame.
