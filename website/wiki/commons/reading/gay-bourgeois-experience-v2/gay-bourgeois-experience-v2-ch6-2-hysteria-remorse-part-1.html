Prostitution as the Bourgeois Century's Anxious Fantasy: Trade, Estimates, Reticence and the Safety-Valve Paradox

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay argues that nineteenth-century bourgeois anxiety about prostitution, visible in inflated estimates, rituals of reticence and the doctrine that the whore safeguards respectable homes, was a mix of real social evils with fear, fascination and repressed desire (reaction formation), and that it exposed the price the class paid for its repressions.

Gay opens the second half of his chapter on the costs of repression by pairing prostitution with nervousness: nervousness was "a fantasy encapsulating a tiny core of reality," prostitution "a reality enveloped in a dense fog of fantasy." He first sketches the real trade (its venues, price ranges, clienteles, national styles and specialized services), then argues that bourgeois reactions to it were driven by more than rational concern. Inflated head-counts, ritual apologies before speaking of the subject, and the doctrine that the whore guards respectable homes all reveal fear, fascination and buried desire. The unit closes with the licensing-versus-abolition controversy and a qualified defense of the reformers' laments as a starting point for candor. It is Gay's account of how a class's public discourse about vice was built, and what that discourse concealed. Compare bourgeois-defenses-against-eros and, for the wider argument about the nineteenth-century middle class, the-bourgeois-experience and peter-gay.

Themes

A real trade under a fog of fantasy. Prostitution ranged from unsanitary shacks to middle-class houses valued for music and lounge atmosphere to luxurious establishments; it employed pimps, white slave traders, physicians, blackmailers and corrupt officials, and left misery, alcoholism, venereal disease and early death. The legendary grandes horizontales were untypical and obscured the fate of the far more numerous poor. The clientele was equally varied: reformers such as Napheys insisted it included "the father of the family" and "the member of the church." Gay lists the needs the trade served, from the sailor and the traveling salesman to the "unsatisfied husband," and notes that it competed with registered prostitutes, clandestine streetwalkers, courtesans and kept women.

Not all bourgeois were reformers. Down to the 1850s and beyond, many regarded commercial sex with equanimity or resignation. Acton called it "ineradicable." The Goncourts represent a third stance, disillusioned connoisseurship (the question "Is that all?"), which Gay says most bourgeois felt they could not afford in the face of the "social evil." National-style anecdotes (Huysmans in Hamburg, the Goncourts' Saint Anthony story, Taine on English gravity in adultery) are reported as observers' claims, several of them plainly embroidered. Gay flags them as such.

Reaction formation in the statistics. Estimates of London's prostitutes repeat the figure 80,000 across sources and decades; Paris is put at 120,000; Wookey claims nearly 150,000 in England. Gay reads this as "the defensive maneuver of reaction formation": fear of disease and of corrupted adolescents was real, but the panic also came from "deeply repressed desires" for the sensual adventurism the respectable had learned to censor. The anonymous 1846 author of Prostitution in Berlin is quoted as naming the fear of "the waves of the proletariat, poverty, and particularly immorality" engulfing civilization. Gay adds that campaigns against vice served self-control as much as social purification. This is Gay's inference, and the sources quoted do not themselves say so.

The rhetoric of the unspeakable. Dalton, Stursberg, Virmaitre and the New York reformers write of dread, darkness and horror. Dickens, Sanger, Acton, Greg and Lippert all follow what Gay calls a convention: protest that nothing can safely be said, then say it. He credits this formula with giving the century "space for an impressive measure of frankness while hinting that frankness was out of reach." Acton's claim to be breaking silence is, in Gay's judgment, self-flattering, since reticence had been less extreme than he suggested. Greg's 1850 Westminster Review article is heavily defensive in its prologue but then substantive and sympathetic, tracing entry into prostitution to poverty and low seamstress wages.

Mandeville updated: the safety valve. Parent-Duchatelet held that prostitutes protect "your daughters and your servants." Lecky called the whore "the most efficient guardian of virtue." The Saint-Simonians, Dostoevsky, Bebel and Grant Allen turned the same idea into an indictment of bourgeois hypocrisy. Gay's summary is "the price that bourgeois society was willing to pay for its repressions," and he sets it beside the paradox of nervousness. Guyot's dialogue with "Prudhomme" and Flaubert's dictionary entry show critics treating the doctrine as cliche, and Gay follows Flaubert in inferring that its being recorded as a commonplace suggests it was probably nonsense. Each of these authors has a polemical agenda, and Gay reports them as such.

A controversy over means, not ends. Licensers and abolitionists shared the ideals of personal purity and public health. The alliances were strange: roues and devout purifiers for licensing, feminists (angry at the double standard) and social conservatives against. Needham's remark that men bear more blame for infected offspring is presented as a feminist intervention. Gay closes on a measured assessment: the laments habituated the public to candor and "prepared the way for a shift in attitudes."

World Machines resonances

The unit is a case study in how an emotional-cultural regime coexists with its own unofficial infrastructure. Bourgeois respectability depended on a parallel market it publicly condemned, which fits the gap between official ideology and private practice. Gay's account also stresses the institutions that regulate intimate life: police registration, medical inspection, print and reform societies, and the statistics they produced. The statistics illustrate a proto-legibility project, since classification, tables and counting were applied to sex, although they were often inflated by emotion (compare legibility-as-machine-core and modernity-machine; the fit is loose, because Gay is describing a discourse rather than a state apparatus). The tension between exposing and hiding echoes the concerns of bourgeois-defenses-against-eros and, as a foil, michel-foucault's repressive hypothesis, since Gay shows a proliferating discourse about vice that nonetheless keeps performing reticence. The charge that the system rests on hypocrisy links to bourgeoisophobia and the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction. Gay is a historian here, not a machine theorist, so these are rhymes and not claims he makes.

Notable passages

Reaction formation in the estimates (c1). Gay's key interpretive move: the numbers were "the offspring of emotion rather than of counting." He explicitly names fear and buried wishes together and treats the crusaders and critics alike as ambivalent.

Hysteria and self-control (c2). The Goncourts' cynicism is set against the bourgeois inability to afford it. The anonymous Berlin author's admission of "powerful, unmistakable fear" of the proletariat and of immorality lets Gay conclude that anti-vice campaigns "served self-control as much as social purification."

The convention of the unspeakable (c3). Gay's compact statement of how Victorian discussion of sex advanced by announcing its own impossibility.

The safety valve and its critics (c4). Parent-Duchatelet's doctrine and the chain of Lecky, Dostoevsky, Bebel and Allen, culminating in Gay's paradox of the bourgeois preserving "the chastity of his women by corrupting the chastity of others."

Strange alliances and sober findings (c5). Licensing and abolition each drew incongruous coalitions, and local investigations found exploitation and networks of vice, crime and officialdom (one in six of York's clients was a policeman).

Resignation as complacency (c6). Acton's "ineradicable" and the argument that pessimism about human nature could mask complacency, which is the counterpoint to the reformers' panic.
