The Rescue Fantasy: Reparation, Philanthropy and Gladstone's Nights

Commons · The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Gay argues that the nineteenth-century bourgeois crusade to rescue prostitutes was driven by rescue and reparation fantasies rooted in childhood ambivalence and guilt, made socially possible by middle-class prosperity and women's exclusion from work, exemplified by Gladstone's tormented rescue work, and that prostitution was a sign of repression's failure rather than its price.

Gay closes his account of prostitution and its reform by treating the nineteenth-century campaign as more than public-health policy or moral outrage: it carried a "nonrational, partly repressed dimension" of collective fantasies of rescue and reparation. He roots these in childhood (disillusionment about parental sexuality, the management of ambivalence, the forming superego), shows how women turned them into real careers (Butler, Addams, Burdett-Coutts, Ellice Hopkins), explains why the bourgeois century in particular could activate and institutionalize them, and then descends to a single case study, William Gladstone, whose nocturnal rescue work was entangled with his own arousal, shame and self-flagellation. The unit ends by drawing a verdict against the safety-valve theory of prostitution and by summing up, for the whole volume, what was distinctively bourgeois about loving.

Themes

Rescue as a psychoanalytic special case of reparation. Gay borrows the analytic account (he attributes it to psychoanalysts generally) that a child who learns the facts of intercourse may degrade the mother into an available woman and then, in fantasy, rescue her, restoring the "angel in the house." The rescue fantasy is a "special case" of the wider repressed fantasy of reparation, and both are "antidotes to ambivalence": children who harbor hostile wishes against loved persons later seek, under a demanding superego, to make good unasked. Arnold Toynbee's "You have to forgive us, for we have wronged you" is offered as the social echo. Gay is careful that this is his interpretive layer on top of real motives (syphilis, white slavery were not imaginary), and that it is an "in concert with other motives" claim, not a reduction.

Against the hypocrisy reading. The crusade "was not a hypocritical search for a moral holiday." It descended from Enlightenment social engineering (rehabilitating criminals, reeducating misfits), aimed to let reformed prostitutes marry, and so sought "an ethically and emotionally acceptable place for sensuality itself." This is Gay's standing quarrel with the legend of the Victorian hypocrite (see the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction, bourgeoisophobia), and, more distantly, with the repressive-hypothesis picture that michel-foucault later attacked from another direction.

Women's activism and its permitted shape. Women were not ballast on rescue committees but working partners who risked abuse and assault. Jane Addams saw the work as an outlet for educated young women with "no recognized outlet for their active faculties"; Ellice Hopkins and an anonymous 1839 writer supplied the rationale that woman is "the conscience of the world" and that her mission is "the flow of maternal love." Gay stresses that society tolerated this because it extended the traditional picture of woman's mission, so rescue work was both a real widening of women's public role and something the male order could absorb. mabel-loomis-todd appears briefly, moved to tears at a Chicago refuge.

How social circumstances shape universal traits. "How the world invades the mind": surplus capital wanting to be invested in doing good, burghers newly assertive against aristocratic tutelage, and middle-class women exempt from economic activity together made the 1840s-50s unusually favorable for translating remorseful fantasies into institutions. Magdalens and charity were ancient; the bourgeois novelty is the mixture of psychological pressure, economic opportunity and political space. This is the unit's clearest bearing on how a class's emotional culture is constructed and given institutional form (compare infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness, bourgeois-defenses-against-eros); it is a historian's causal sketch, not a machine model, and it fits the World Machines frame only loosely, as an example of a dominant class channeling private guilt through voluntary institutions.

Gladstone: fantasy at work, and its costs. Gladstone's diaries show the strains: from the mid-1840s he specialized in prostitutes, walked the London streets at night from 1849, told his wife, and yet found the work bitter (by 1854, of 80 to 90 women, one demonstrably reclaimed). His real distress was that the time was "impurely spent": the encounters aroused him. Gay links this to his frustrations (Catherine's pregnancies and absences), his secret Italian-language record of pornography, his self-flagellation (itself suspected of pleasure), and a written inventory of "channels," "incentives" and "remedies" that reads as converting longings into phobias. The mission "became, not part of his solution but part of his problem." Labouchere's jibe is quoted (his claim, not Gay's) and Gay concedes he was right about the arousal but "wholly missed his anguish." Gladstone is an outsize case, but what set him apart was his sensitivity to his conscience's reproaches, not the conscience itself.

Prostitution as failure of repression, and the split in love. Thackeray's letter about "investing a trull in the street with that priceless jewel my heart" shows lust and affection running in "divergent directions" and thus the split between Freud's two currents of love. The reformers aimed to rejoin them. Gay's verdict: the notion that prostitution was a necessary safeguard for the middle-class family (Lecky and allies; Mandeville's private vices, public benefits) has "even less merit" than the nervousness thesis, since "Prostitution was far less the price of repression than a sign of its failure."

Closing synthesis on bourgeois love. Love cannot be reduced to a formula; bourgeois were much like other classes and ages, yet set distinctive accents: the rage for privacy (see bourgeois-privacy-as-conquest), rehabilitation of prostitutes, rediscovery of perversions, imaginative disguises for sensuality, strident slanders of woman alongside new respect. Drives and defenses endure but are "malleable, plastic, impressively mobile." The last line, the bourgeoisie's "black-coated Eros," ties back to gay-bourgeois-experience-v1 and the series as a whole (the-bourgeois-experience, peter-gay).

Notable passages

Rescue and reparation as defenses against guilt (c1, c2). Gay defines the fantasies as ways of facing "the urgent, often conflicting pressures of sexuality and aggression" and of warding off guilt, enacted in the world or in the mind (Dickens as adviser to Burdett-Coutts; David Copperfield imagining a rescue by ladder). The next passage supplies the childhood template, the boy who degrades then rescues the mother, and the practical upshot that rehabilitated women were permitted to marry, so the crusade sought a place for sensuality "complete with happy ending."

Addams on the rescuers' need (c3). The most vivid evidence that reform served rescuers as much as the rescued: the "desire for action" of cultivated young women, and Huxley's remark on the "sense of uselessness." Gay reads the crusade as a "longed-for, gratifying-and permissible-opportunity."

The world invades the mind (c4). Gay's compact statement of why the bourgeois century, and the 1840s-50s especially, was the right moment for remorseful fantasies to become institutions.

Gladstone's balance sheet (c5). The 1854 diary entry counting his reclaimed women, and Gay's reading that the real burden was erotic arousal in the midst of a philanthropic mission.

Failure of repression (c6). The unit's thesis on prostitution and the split between the currents of love.
