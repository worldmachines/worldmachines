The Last Horse Powers: Breeding Failures, Imperial Pageantry, and the Petroleum Transition

Commons · Raiders · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
In the decades before and during World War I, Russian and British colonial breeding schemes to industrialize Central Asian and Indian horse stock repeatedly failed against local practice and ecology, even as both empires staged their largest-ever cavalry forces and pageantry, only for airpower and petroleum to render horse power strategically obsolete within a single generation.

This unit closes the book's arc: Russia and Britain, having spent the nineteenth century conquering the horse-breeding peoples of Central Asia and India, spend the early twentieth trying and failing to industrialize those breeding traditions into standing cavalry stock, staging one last spectacular display of horse power at the 1911 Delhi durbar and in the mobilizations of 1914, before World War I and the 1919 Third Anglo-Afghan War reveal that airpower and mechanization have already superseded the horse — a supersession the chapter frames as the definitive end of a three-thousand-year world order built on equine power, replaced by petroleum as the new strategic resource.

Themes

Colonial breeding schemes fail against the ecology and culture they try to extract from. Russian attempts to scale up Akhal-Teke and Kazakh horse breeding through state stud farms and requisitioned mares collapse for structural reasons the empire cannot fix by decree: pacification itself removes the raiding economy that made a superior warhorse valuable ("when the country is pacified, there is no need for such a horse"), railroad-driven export demand strips the steppe of its best stock, and agricultural colonization eats the pastureland. The breeding stations post absurd fertility rates (5 percent versus 50 percent on the open steppe) because mares bred in the wild will not couple in a stable, and herders will not perform the alien labor of stockpiling winter forage. Britain's parallel program in India fares no better, weighed down by racial theories of equine heredity (that stallions transmit more than mares, that Thoroughbred blood improves any line) that Marco Polo had already refuted six centuries earlier. Both empires eventually abandon systematic breeding for the same solution: buy horses that already work — Kazakh stallions on Turkmen dams for Russia, imported Australian Waler geldings for Britain — rather than force local practice into an imperial mold. legibility-as-machine-core names this pattern precisely: an imperial project to make horse breeding standardized and countable runs directly into the divergence of local practice and terrain, and legibility loses.

Local horse-breeding peoples resist incorporation on their own terms. Kazakh herders slow-walk the covering stations by sending pregnant or immature mares; Turkmen Yomuts respond to a horse requisition by fleeing wholesale into Iran, "where they live to this day." Kipling's Mahbub Ali supplies the chapter's epigraph for this theme — "a Kathiawar mare taken from the sands of her birthplace and removed to the west of Bengal founders... each has merit in its own country" — and Chaffetz draws the explicit parallel: "Colonial attempts to exploit Asia's horse power foundered for the same reasons that colonialism itself failed." This is a clean instance of capture-resistance: horse-breeding communities defend a way of life (and a genetic stock adapted to it) against a state that wants to extract and standardize it, and largely win by simple non-compliance and flight rather than confrontation.

Imperial pageantry peaks exactly as its material basis is disappearing. By 1900 Russia fields more mounted troops than any power since Qianlong, Akbar, or Taizong, and Britain answers by restoring the Taj Mahal, moving the Raj's capital to Delhi, and staging the 1911 durbar — a full Mughal-style court ceremony with ten thousand horsemen and the king-emperor entering the Red Fort on a "coal-black waler." Yet the chapter immediately punctures the display: Balfour himself had already called the Great Game unlikely to produce real war, and the Indian public asked why the "most expensive army in the world for the poorest people in the world" existed at all. The princely regiments parading in chain armor and family colors were performing a martial order — cavalry as the arbiter of Asian empire — that had already become theater. This is close to decay-sequence's Performative stage: infrastructure (mass cavalry, the horse-trade economy underwriting it) still fully built but already running on inherited prestige rather than the function that once justified it, and the durbar's ironies (Gaekwad's snub, the maharajas who once fought Britain now saluting it) mark a gramsci-gap — old legitimizing rituals persisting after their material logic has quietly voided.

World War I converts cavalry from shock weapon to mobility and reconnaissance, then makes even that role obsolete. Chaffetz undercuts the romantic image early: "the cavalry almost never actually ran into the defenders' line," relying instead on the appearance of an unstoppable force. In 1914 the logistics of moving Russia's mobilized million horses by rail take so long that the "Russian Steam Roller" arrives too late to matter, and reserve-held cavalry never reaches fighting condition — a telling contrast with Genghis Khan, who "rode to war when his horses were ready." The war's one great cavalry triumph, the 1918 Battle of Megiddo, is immediately reframed as secondary to what T. E. Lawrence does next: coordinating aircraft bombing with cavalry and armored cars into what becomes Blitzkrieg, then recommending the RAF replace ground troops entirely for imperial policing. The chapter is explicit that Lawrence's technological turn, not the cavalry charge, is the era's real hinge.

The 1919 Third Anglo-Afghan War stages the horse's literal last stand and its final replacement. Skinner's Horse rides out against Ghilzai raiders in what the chapter frames as the first Khyber Pass invasion in over a century — but the war is decided not by cavalry (both sides field far weaker horse forces than their ancestors) but by RAF bombing of Jalalabad and finally of the royal palace in Kabul, which breaks King Amanullah's will to fight. Twenty-two years later, in 1941, the same regiment trades horses for tanks in Burma. This double transition — horse to air power in 1919, horse to armor in 1941 — is the chapter's concrete image of a world-machines changeover: petroleum displacing the horse as "the strategic resource that both enabled empires and was indispensable to their defense," with Churchill's 1912 naval fuel switch as the quiet, prior cause that the cavalry spectacle obscured. Genghis Khan, Timür, and Nader Shah become, in one line, "as remote as the Paleozoic forests" — the kind of abrupt-seeming lurch into visibility that history-machine describes for a paradigm that has actually been assembling underground for decades.

The horse's three-thousand-year world order dissolves along with its aristocracies. The chapter's closing paragraph inventories the wreckage: Central Asia goes from wealthy emporium to one of the most isolated, impoverished regions on earth once the caravan trade stops; Sultan Kilich Giray and Prince Shcherbatov have no place in Soviet Russia; Qing bannermen fade in Republican China; Indira Gandhi abolishes the Indian princely maharajas in 1971. Only Afghanistan — "the Land of the Horsemen" — retains the horse's social and economic weight, setting up the book's epilogue.

Notable passages

The Russian animal-husbandry expert's line on the Akhal-Teke crystallizes the whole colonial-breeding paradox in miniature: a horse bred for raiding and defiance has no purpose once its people are pacified, so pacification itself destroys the very asset the empire wanted to capture. Kipling's Mahbub Ali, quoted directly, supplies the chapter's clearest statement of ecological and cultural specificity defeating imperial standardization — a horse (like a people) has "merit in its own country" and nowhere else, which Chaffetz explicitly generalizes to the failure of colonialism itself. The durbar passage is the chapter's set-piece of imperial theater: the Taj Mahal restoration, the capital moved to Delhi, and George V riding a Waler into the Red Fort while ten thousand princely horsemen parade in chain armor — a performance of continuity with the Mughals staged at the exact moment the underlying military logic (mass horse cavalry as the arbiter of Asian power) has become moot. The Lawrence passage marks the technological pivot away from the horse: his shift from cavalry charges to coordinated air-and-ground "combined operations," and his own quoted description of a Turkish column that "melted away" under nine tons of RAF bombs, is the chapter's clearest demonstration that the war's real innovation lay elsewhere. Finally, the Skinner's Horse/petroleum passage and the closing inventory of dissolved horse aristocracies together deliver the book's thesis in concentrated form: a resource that built and defended empires for three thousand years is replaced within two decades by a resource pulled from underneath the very steppes the horse once ruled — "geologists found the new black gold underneath the old steppe lands."
