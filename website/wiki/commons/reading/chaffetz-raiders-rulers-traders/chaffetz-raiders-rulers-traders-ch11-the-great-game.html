The Great Game: India's Horse Powers and the Scramble for Central Asia

Commons · Raiders · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
The eighteenth-century collapse of Mughal power touched off a scramble among Indian horse powers (Marathas, Afghans, Sikhs) that the cavalry-poor British ultimately won through money and diplomacy rather than horseflesh, setting up a century-long, horse-starved Anglo-Russian Great Game across Central Asia that climaxed in the Qing bannermen's defeat by sea powers and Russia's brutal conquest of the last independent Turkmen horse breeders.

This unit traces two intertwined stories: the eighteenth-century free-for-all among Indian cavalry powers that followed the Mughal collapse, and the nineteenth-century Anglo-Russian rivalry over Central Asia's remaining horse-producing steppe — the Great Game — that grew directly out of the winners' horse poverty. It runs from the second Battle of Panipat (1761) through the Sikh Empire, the East India Company's rise, William Moorcroft's doomed mission to Bukhara, the Opium War defeat of Qing bannermen, and Russia's 1881 annihilation of Turkmen independence.

Themes

Horse quality decided Indian supremacy long after Mughal legitimacy had dissolved. The chapter opens on the vacuum left by Nader Shah's 1739 sack of Delhi, filled in turn by Marathas, Ahmad Shah's Afghans, and finally the Sikhs. At the second Battle of Panipat (1761), the outcome turned on horseflesh as much as tactics: Ahmad Shah's Turkmen-mounted cavalry ran down the Marathas' lighter Kathiawar and Arab stock, and the chase-and-capture of Maharaja Mahadji Scindia is narrated almost as a horse-breed morality tale — his fast but tireable Kathiawar mare eventually stumbling while the Turkmen horse that hunted him "never tired." This is the book's recurring thesis in miniature: political fortune tracks access to superior bloodstock, not the reverse.

Anarchy created a horse economy of opportunists. With no single power able to enforce order, whole communities — the Indo-Afghan middlemen, the Kattra of Kathiawar, freelance warlords like Amir Khan — built fortunes breeding, dealing, and raiding on horseback, exploiting marginal agricultural land that horses monetized better than crops could. Tipu Sultan's Mysore shows the flip side: forced to subsidize breeding artificially (paying more per year to raise a single warhorse than most families earned) because the region lacked natural grassland, an arrangement so costly it structurally required Mysore to fight wars for indemnities just to break even — a striking case of a [[world-machines]]-style infrastructure (cavalry) whose maintenance cost outran the polity's ordinary revenue and demanded permanent extraction.

Britain won paramountcy with money, not horses. The British began the contest with a cavalry of five hundred against Maratha-Afghan-Sikh forces numbering in the hundreds of thousands, and closed the gap through bribery, mercenary recruitment (James Skinner's horse), and playing rival maharajas against each other rather than through equestrian superiority. This inversion — legibility and administrative-financial capacity substituting for the traditional steppe/settled cavalry advantage — is a genuine hinge point in the book's argument: the horse-power calculus that had determined Eurasian political order for millennia is here overridden by fiscal-military statecraft, foreshadowing the horse's coming obsolescence relative to industrial and bureaucratic power. Yet having inherited the Mughal position, the British discovered they still needed cavalry to defend it, and could never breed adequate horses domestically (their Pusa stud farm cost more per horse than market purchase), leaving them structurally dependent on the same Central Asian supply lines their predecessors had relied on.

The Great Game was born from Britain's horse shortage. Confronted by declining domestic supply as internal peace killed the profit motive for warhorse breeding (the once-thriving Kattra breeders faded once their "plundering excursions" were suppressed), the British were driven to seek remounts from Bukhara — a market the Mughals had exploited routinely but which no Englishman had visited since Elizabeth I. Moorcroft's 1819–25 expedition, undertaken against orders, is presented as the opening move of the rivalry with Russia for control of the steppe's horse-and-strategic resources, a rivalry later formalized in name by Perovsky's aphorism about needing to "act English-style." The passage on Moorcroft's Kabul observations is the chapter's clearest statement of why Central Asia so long out-produced settled empires in cavalry: cheap natural forage (clover, alfalfa, threshed chaff) let Afghan and Turkmen breeders raise powerful horses at negligible cost, a structural advantage no amount of English stud-farm investment could replicate.

Maritime power, not horse power, finally broke the steppe's political relevance — but only at its coastal edge. The Second Opium War (1860) pits the Qing's still-formidable thirty-thousand-strong Mongol/Manchu bannermen, arrayed in the old encirclement (battue) tactics, against European rifle volleys on marshy terrain that denied cavalry room to maneuver; the bannermen take heavy losses and withdraw, and the emperor concedes trade access. Yet the same Qing cavalry, deployed against Yakub Beg's Xinjiang revolt, crushed the rebellion with undiminished effectiveness — horse power still ruled the interior even as it failed at the coast. This split outcome nuances any simple "gunpowder ended cavalry" narrative: it was terrain and the maritime vector of attack, not horses' obsolescence per se, that doomed the bannermen at Tianjin.

The 1881 Treaty of St. Petersburg closed the open steppe forever. Russia and Qing China, mutually threatened by their own "rebellious steppe subjects," agreed to a formalized border near Balasagun — the first such border ever drawn across the steppe — burying, in Chaffetz's words, "the political independence of the steppe peoples." This is the book's clearest statement of a [[decay-sequence]]-like closure: an open, permeable, nomad-governed zone becomes two bordered, legible imperial possessions. The destruction of the Akhal-Teke-breeding Turkmen in Skobelev's brutal 1881 campaign — the last independent horse-breeding steppe society — is presented as the literal end of an age: raiding, capture-resistant, mobile political forms give way completely to settled/legible imperial control, exactly the kind of transition the [[capture-resistance]] and [[legibility-as-machine-core]] framework anticipates for nomadic peoples confronting encroaching states.

Notable passages

The Panipat chase (Scindia's Kathiawar mare vs. the pursuing Turkmen horse) dramatizes breed economics as personal fate: speed versus stamina, with stamina winning and costing Scindia his knee and his horse but, oddly, not his life. It is the chapter's most vivid illustration of horse quality as a determinant of individual as well as imperial survival.

Maharaja Ranjit Singh's 1831 boast — "All of my conquest had been done by the sword [i.e., cavalry]. I have never known the infantry or the artillery to be of any service" — paired with the description of his jewel-caparisoned stable horses (bridles later insured for tens of thousands of rupees at the 1851 Great Exhibition), captures the Sikh Empire as consciously, ceremonially the last cavalry-founded state "in India—and, indeed, the world."

Moorcroft's diary entry on Kabul's cheap winter forage is quietly the chapter's thesis statement: Chaffetz explicitly glosses it as "the secret to the superiority of the Central Asian cavalry over the Indian cavalry, and the key to the Afghan victory at Panipat." Structural feed economics, not martial spirit, explain centuries of steppe military advantage.

Moorcroft's incidental transcription of a Bukharan goat-and-rider game — "It was the first description in English of the game of buzkashi" — is a small gem: an ethnographic first buried inside a failed procurement mission, and a reminder that horse culture (sport, training, ritual) traveled alongside the trade in animals themselves.

The description of Turkmen political economy — a people who deliberately settled desert margins "to avoid paying taxes" and built their entire raiding-and-slaving economy around the Akhal-Teke's endurance — reads as a textbook case of tax-evading, capture-resistant nomadism organized around a specific technology (the horse breed itself). Their 1881 destruction by Skobelev, and General Kuropatkin's triumphant claim that Central Asian horses would become "the jewel in the crown of Russia," marks the closing of that possibility space for good.
