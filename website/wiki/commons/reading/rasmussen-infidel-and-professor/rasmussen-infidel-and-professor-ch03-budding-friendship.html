Separate Chairs, Converging Minds: Smith at Glasgow, Hume in Edinburgh (1751-1754)

Commons · The Infidel and the Professor: David Hume · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Adam Smith settles into the Chair of Moral Philosophy at Glasgow while David Hume, twice passed over for a university post, becomes Keeper of the Advocates' Library in Edinburgh, and between 1751 and 1754 the two men's intellectual partnership deepens through Hume's Second Enquiry and Political Discourses (which Smith visibly absorbs into his own lectures), the opening of their surviving correspondence, and the founding of Edinburgh's Select Society.

This unit tracks the institutional and intellectual consolidation of both men's careers in the early 1750s: Smith takes up first the Chair of Logic and then the more prestigious Chair of Moral Philosophy at Glasgow, provoking quiet controversy over his lax handling of the natural-theology portion of the curriculum; Hume, rebuffed a second time for a Scottish professorship (this time Glasgow's vacated Chair of Logic), instead becomes Keeper of the Advocates' Library in Edinburgh, a post that gives him the research base for The History of England. Meanwhile Hume publishes the Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals and the Political Discourses, both of which Smith visibly incorporates into his own jurisprudence lectures at Glasgow -- on the specie-flow mechanism, free trade, and the conventional (non-contractual) origins of political authority. The chapter closes by tracing the geography and social infrastructure of the friendship: Hume in Edinburgh, Smith in Glasgow, thirteen hours apart by coach; the opening of their surviving correspondence in 1752; and the 1754 founding of Edinburgh's Select Society, where both men were founding members.

Themes

Institutional respectability versus intellectual candor. Smith's tenure at Glasgow is shadowed throughout by the suspicion that his "principles were not sound," fed both by his minimal, naturalistic handling of natural theology and by his open association with Hume -- to the point that a Genevan physician warns his own son against absorbing "Hume's dangerous principles" while studying under Smith. The unit reads Smith's cautious 1751 letter to William Cullen ("I should prefer David Hume to any man for a colleague; but I am afraid the public would not be of my opinion") as the classic case scholars cite for Smith's supposed timidity toward Hume's notoriety, but complicates that reading: Smith had an institutional duty distinct from friendship, and the remark's frank admission of preference is itself early evidence of how highly he already rated Hume, a mere two years into their acquaintance.

Transmission of ideas from Hume's page to Smith's lectern. The unit documents, in granular detail, how directly Smith's Glasgow teaching drew on Hume's freshly published work -- the specie-flow mechanism from "Of the Balance of Trade" and "Of Money," and the systematic demolition of social-contract theory from "Of the Original Contract," reproduced by a student note-taker "blow by blow." This is less mere influence than active curation and amplification: Smith treats Hume's essays as required intellectual furniture for future lawyers and statesmen, embedding Political Discourses' free-trade cosmopolitanism into what would eventually become The Wealth of Nations.

Hume's brief for commercial modernity. In the Political Discourses, and especially the essay later retitled "Of Refinement in the Arts," Hume mounts a full-throated defense of commerce and luxury against the older civic-republican and Christian suspicion of wealth as corrupting. His claim that "ages of refinement are both the happiest and most virtuous," and that commerce creates the "middling rank of men" who underwrite free government, is a foundational brief for what the world-machines framework would recognize as the modernity-machine's optimism: a world organized around progress, productivity, and legible, orderly commercial exchange rather than static hierarchy or ascetic virtue.

The infrastructure of an intellectual friendship. Geography (opposite sides of Scotland), institutions (the Advocates' Library, the Literary Society of Glasgow, the Select Society), and habit (Hume's recurring, half-joking pleas for Smith to visit) together constitute the material scaffolding of the friendship long before its most dramatic tests. The 1754 Select Society -- founded with Hume as treasurer and Smith explaining its charter at the first meeting -- becomes a genuine hub of Scottish Enlightenment sociability, its debates reportedly drawing more "general curiosity" in Edinburgh than the House of Commons did in London.

Notable passages

The Cullen letter passage is the unit's most argued-over piece of evidence: taken alone it looks like Smith hedging on Hume's candidacy, but the surrounding discussion (Rae's conjecture that Smith "acted in concert" with Cullen in Hume's favor, and the letter's own unequivocal praise) reframes it as compatible with real warmth constrained by professional duty.

The testimony on Smith's teaching -- Millar's claim that no situation showed off "the abilities of Mr. Smith" better than the professorship, and Smith's own late-life verdict that his Glasgow years were "by far the happiest and most honourable period of my life" -- establishes how central this post was to Smith's self-conception, which sharpens the stakes of the parallel controversy over his religious teaching.

The long passage on Smith's jurisprudence lectures shows Hume's ideas being taught almost verbatim to the next generation of Scottish elites, down to the specific analogy of the impressed sailor who cannot be said to have tacitly consented to his own kidnapping -- a vivid instance of one thinker's argument becoming another's curriculum.

Hume's 1753 letter to Smith -- "Were you not my Friend, you wou'd envy my robust Constitution" -- is flagged in the text itself as the first explicit naming of their friendship in the surviving correspondence, making it the emotional hinge of the whole unit even amid all the professional and philosophical business surrounding it.
