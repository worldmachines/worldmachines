The Quarrel with the Wild Philosopher: Hume, Rousseau, and the Limits of Friendship

Commons · The Infidel and the Professor: David Hume · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Rasmussen narrates the collapse of David Hume's friendship with Jean-Jacques Rousseau in 1766-67, showing how Rousseau's persecution-fueled paranoia turned Hume's rescue of him into an imagined conspiracy, and how Adam Smith's counsel that Hume stay silent went unheeded when Hume published their correspondence anyway.

This chapter recounts the Hume-Rousseau affaire: Hume's rescue of the persecuted Rousseau, their brief cohabitation in Paris and London, Rousseau's spiraling conviction that Hume was the mastermind of an international conspiracy against him, and the public rupture that followed when Hume, against Adam Smith's explicit advice, published their correspondence to clear his name. The episode is presented less as gossip than as a foil: it dramatizes, through contrast, what made the Hume-Smith friendship durable, and it establishes a precedent — Smith counseling Hume against airing a controversy in print — that the book will invoke again for a far higher-stakes manuscript.

Themes

Temperamental and philosophical opposition as the real fault line. Rasmussen catalogs the contrasts between the two men point by point — physical bearing, sociability, and above all philosophy: "Hume's thoroughgoing defense of the modern, liberal, commercial order was matched by Rousseau's blistering attack on that order," with Hume crediting "civilization, refinement, and commerce" for "an indissoluble chain of industry, knowledge, and humanity" while Rousseau saw only "inequality, dependence, and corruption." This is close to a miniature staging of the tension the book elsewhere associates with a confident, legible, commercially-optimistic modernity against a radical critique of exactly that order — resonant with the modernity-machine's pull toward Progress versus a counter-current that treats civilizational "improvement" itself as corrosive. Yet the chapter insists it was not the intellectual gulf but incompatible temperaments — Hume's serenity against Rousseau's persecution-anxiety — that made rupture "all but inevitable."

Paranoia manufacturing evidence from kindness. The chapter's structural irony is that every act of Hume's generosity — securing him refuge, a pension, a house at Wootton — becomes, in Rousseau's retelling, proof of a plot to control and bury him. D'Holbach's early warning that Hume was "warming a viper in your bosom" is vindicated almost verbatim by Rousseau's thirty-eight-page indictment, built entirely on "gut feelings" rather than evidence.

Smith as the counselor of restraint, ignored. Smith's July 6 letter is the chapter's ethical center: a case against publicizing a private grievance regardless of who is in the right, because reputational combat has its own costs ("By endeavouring to unmask before the Public this hypocritical Pedant, you run the risk, of disturbing the tranquillity of your whole life"). Hume solicits this advice, receives it from multiple quarters (Smith, Boufflers, Millar), and overrides it anyway — publishing the Concise and Genuine Account while claiming reluctance. Rasmussen flags this as uncharacteristic of "le bon David": the paranoia about Rousseau's forthcoming memoirs and the "glee" at Rousseau's later humiliation in France sit uneasily with Hume's usual equanimity.

Publicity as self-defeating strategy. The chapter frames publication itself as the real casualty of Hume's judgment: contemporaries and Rasmussen alike conclude the pamphlet was "gratuitous and perhaps a bit spiteful," and Smith's distaste for intellectuals who "publish all their little gossiping stories in Newspapers" is set up explicitly as a preview — the same advice, the chapter notes, that Smith will later give about "a philosophical masterpiece" rather than "an airing of dirty laundry," foreshadowing the Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion.

Notable passages

D'Holbach's parting warning to Hume — that he was "warming a viper" in his bosom — functions as dramatic irony the chapter cashes in almost immediately; Hume's dismissal of it marks his blind spot toward Rousseau's instability. The Dover embrace, by contrast, captures Rousseau's genuine and disarming warmth, complicating any reading of him as simply villainous — Rasmussen wants both the paranoia and the pathos on record. Smith's letter of July 6 is quoted at length because it is the clearest statement in the book of his "deep, enduring antipathy to public controversy," a trait that will recur. Hume's private verdict — debating whether Rousseau was "an arrant Villain or an arrant Madman or both" — shows how far Hume's initial charm curdled into contempt. The chapter's closing assessment, that the affair's aftermath "did not show Hume at his best," is Rasmussen's own historiographical judgment, delivered evenhandedly against the grain of Hume's usual heroic billing in the book.
