The Theory of Moral Sentiments and Hume's Delighted Response (1759)

Commons · The Infidel and the Professor: David Hume · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
In 1759 Adam Smith publishes The Theory of Moral Sentiments as a sustained but unnamed dialogue with Hume's moral philosophy, diverging on sympathy, utility, justice, and religion even as he builds squarely on Hume's foundations, and Hume responds with a teasing letter, a laudatory anonymous review, and further private correspondence that together display the depth of their friendship.

This unit covers the publication of Adam Smith's first book, The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759), and the network of intellectual and personal exchange it set off with Hume. It first situates Smith as a slow, deliberate writer (in contrast to Hume's famous fluency), traces how the book quietly but pervasively engages Hume's moral philosophy without ever naming him, and then works through the four major points on which Smith revises Hume — sympathy, utility, justice, and religion — before turning to Hume's warm, witty private letter on the book's reception and his anonymous review in the Critical Review.

Themes

An unnamed but unmistakable dialogue with Hume. Smith never mentions Hume by name in The Theory of Moral Sentiments, but the questions, arguments, and even examples are saturated with Hume's influence, and Smith explicitly praises "an ingenious and agreeable philosopher, who joins the greatest depth of thought to the greatest elegance of expression" in terms no contemporary reader could fail to recognize as Hume. Smith's reticence about naming any philosopher (not just Hume) reflects a rhetorical strategy of appealing to everyday moral experience rather than technical debate; the book's basic architecture — morality as a human, sentiment-based, practical phenomenon, judged from a corrected disinterested standpoint — is presented as an extension and refinement of Hume's system rather than a rejection of it.

Sympathy: from contagion to projection. The most fundamental of Smith's four divergences concerns sympathy itself. Hume treats sympathy as a largely passive, mechanical "contagion" — emotions are transmitted from one mind to another the way vibrating strings convey motion. Smith instead argues for an active, imaginative "projection" into another's situation, one that depends on understanding the circumstances that produced a feeling, not just observing its outward signs. Smith marshals cases Hume's account cannot easily explain — blushing for another's unnoticed rudeness, pitying someone who has lost her reason, grieving for the dead — deliberately reworking an example from Hume's own Treatise to make the point. This richer notion of sympathy in turn grounds Smith's two-part moral judgment (propriety, from Part 1, plus merit/demerit, from Part 2), against Hume's single external, effects-based standard.

Utility, beauty, and the illusion of the machine. Smith accepts Hume's claim that perceived utility makes objects beautiful but adds what he calls his own original insight: that the appearance of "fitness" or contrivance is often valued more than the very end it serves — as when tidying askew chairs costs more effort than the convenience gained, or when a watch's precision is prized beyond its practical function of keeping one from being late. Smith extends this to the pursuit of wealth and power that "drives the economy and indeed the entire process of civilization," arguing people mistake the machinery of comfort for comfort itself. This confusion of means for the appearance of order over the end itself is a recognizably Smithian anticipation of ideas he will develop in The Wealth of Nations, and it resonates suggestively with legibility-as-machine-core's point that systems can be valued for their orderliness and contrivance independent of what they actually deliver. On the moral side, Smith denies that utility is the first source of our approval of virtue (a mountain of praise for a well-made chest of drawers is not the same in kind as praise for a virtuous person), arguing Hume — like philosophers generally — overrates abstract, society-wide effects and underrates the concrete, particular circumstances that actually move ordinary moral judgment.

Justice as a natural, not artificial, virtue. Both thinkers treat justice narrowly (refraining from harm to life, liberty, or property) and agree it alone among the virtues is essential to society's survival and best enforced by rigid rules. But where Hume calls justice an "artificial" virtue grounded in reflection on its social utility, Smith grounds it in the sentiment of resentment we feel on a victim's behalf, arising "antecedent to all reflection upon the utility of punishment." It is a natural, spontaneous sentiment, not a conclusion reasoned toward — another instance of Smith accusing Hume of being, in a memorable formulation, "not sufficiently Humean."

An ambivalent, not hostile, treatment of religion. In sharp contrast to Hume's view of religion as almost entirely pernicious, Smith treats religious belief sympathetically without making it foundational: God and providence are invoked evasively and hedged ("attended with circumlocutions... and frequent use of the verb 'to seem'"), and Smith's moral framework needs no divine premise. Still, Smith argues religious belief in an afterlife often springs from our noblest impulses and functions as a practical "buttress" reinforcing moral conduct, though his qualifications on this point are themselves distinctly Humean in spirit, and were further tempered in later editions.

The friendship confirmed in success. Hume's letter on the book's publication is a set piece of comic delay — piling up interruptions, gossip about other authors, and mock-philosophical stoicism before revealing that the book is already a hit, teasing Smith by pretending the news will be bad. Hume's anonymous review in the Critical Review is similarly generous, praising Smith's genius while subtly signaling their points of real disagreement (on the "projection" account of sympathy and on utility) with careful hedges. Their subsequent private exchange over whether "all kinds of Sympathy are necessarily Agreeable" — which Hume calls "the Hinge of your System" — shows the friendship operating as real philosophical collaboration, not mere flattery.

Notable passages

The impartial-spectator passage crystallizes Smith's central innovation: moral standards are set by the sentiments an impartial spectator, free of "particular connexion" to anyone involved, would feel — right and wrong reducing to what would earn such a spectator's approval or disapproval. The blushing example shows Smith reusing Hume's own case from the Treatise to argue that only an active "projection" into another's situation, not passive "contagion," can explain sympathizing with someone oblivious to his own rudeness. The chairs-and-watch passage is Smith's own proudest addition to Hume's utility theory, showing how the appearance of fitness eclipses the end it serves. The justice passage isolates Smith's reworking of Hume's "artificial virtue" account into one rooted in spontaneous resentment. Finally, Hume's letter — with its mock-tragic buildup to the "melancholy News" that the book is already a triumph — is included for what it shows about the emotional texture of the friendship itself, alongside the philosophical substance.
