Smith's Early Life and First Meeting with Hume

Commons · The Infidel and the Professor: David Hume · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
Adam Smith's biography from his 1723 birth in Kirkcaldy through Glasgow, a disillusioning stint at Oxford, the composition of his early manuscript on the history of astronomy, and his probable first meeting with Hume in autumn 1749 traces the formation of a mind already quietly Humean before the two men ever spoke.

This unit covers Adam Smith's life from his 1723 birth in Kirkcaldy to his probable first meeting with Hume in the autumn of 1749: his fatherless, only-child upbringing by a devout Presbyterian mother; his studies at Glasgow under Francis Hutcheson and the mathematicians Robert Simson and Robert Dick; a dispiriting six years at Oxford's Balliol College, capped by a nervous breakdown; the composition there of his earliest surviving work, The Principles Which Lead and Direct Philosophical Enquiries; his return to Kirkcaldy and subsequent public lectures in Edinburgh; and finally a portrait of the two men's contrasting temperaments as they likely crossed paths for the first time.

Themes

A biography built in parallel to Hume's, then diverging. Rasmussen stacks up the coincidences deliberately: like Hume, Smith grew up fatherless, was raised by a devoutly Presbyterian mother, remained an only child, and never married. But where Hume's post-university years were a restless sequence of odd jobs (merchant's clerk, tutor, secretary on military and diplomatic missions), Smith took “a much straighter path to the life of a man of letters” — Glasgow, Oxford, then lecturing his way toward an academic career. The parallel construction sets up the friendship to come by establishing just how much common ground (and divergence) preceded it.

Oxford as institutional decay, Scotland as institutional ascent. Smith's verdict on Balliol — that its professors had “given up altogether even the pretence of teaching,” and that richly endowed universities become “sanctuaries in which exploded systems and obsolete prejudices found shelter and protection, after they had been hunted out of every other corner of the world” — is one of the era's most quotable indictments of ossified institutions, and it stands in deliberate contrast to his praise of the fast-modernizing Scottish universities as “without exception the best seminaries of learning that are to be found any where in Europe.” It is also, in miniature, an origin story for the theory of institutional decline Smith later formalized in The Wealth of Nations.

A secretly Humean education. The chapter's centerpiece anecdote — Balliol dons confiscating Smith's copy of Hume's Treatise and reprimanding him for reading it — establishes the two men's first point of contact was purely textual, years before they met. Rasmussen then traces the debt in detail: Smith's Principles restates Hume's theory of the association of ideas and custom in causal inference nearly verbatim, only extending it from everyday beliefs (billiard balls) to the history of scientific theory itself.

Science and religion as inventions of a self-soothing imagination. The philosophical heart of the unit is Smith's argument, in the astronomy essay of the Principles, that both science and religion arise not from reason discovering truth but from the imagination's need to relieve the discomfort of unexplained phenomena — the “chaos of jarring and discordant appearances.” Frightening or awe-inspiring irregularities (storms, eclipses, rainbows) generate, in turn, polytheism, monotheism, and eventually scientific law, each superseding the last only by leaving fewer explanatory “gaps.” Order is imposed on nature by the mind, not discovered within it — a genealogy of certainty by way of successive imaginative constructions being displaced, which echoes (without being identical to) the way WMP's history-machine concept treats a paradigm's apparent coherence as something assembled and only retrospectively felt as necessary. Smith soft-pedaled the argument's obvious implications for a designing God, plausibly for reasons of “prudence,” and the essay went unpublished until after his death. Strikingly, it anticipates Hume's own Natural History of Religion (1757) by several years — evidence, Rasmussen argues, of two minds converging independently rather than one influencing the other.

Contrasting temperaments, converging friendship. The unit closes by setting the gregarious, physically imposing, universally charming Hume against the reserved, absent-minded, prematurely famous-for-mumbling-in-church Smith. Rasmussen pushes back gently on biographers who over-read this contrast (Hume too preferred small company and could stare vacantly into space; Smith too made friends easily and proved a capable administrator), setting up the friendship's improbable ease.

Notable passages

The Heilbroner epigraph on Smith's outsized real-world influence frames the whole book's premise that Smith, for all his personal dullness next to Hume, reshaped how the West sees itself. The Oxford passage is Smith's own voice at its driest and most damning, and doubles as biography and incipient social theory. The confiscated-Treatise anecdote is the unit's narrative hinge: proof, well before any personal acquaintance, that Smith had already found his way to Hume's thought. The two passages from the Principles — on science as imagination's self-soothing and religion as the offspring of ignorance and terror — carry the unit's most substantive philosophical content, showing a twenty-something Smith working out a naturalistic, deflationary account of belief-formation that he would keep private for the rest of his life.
