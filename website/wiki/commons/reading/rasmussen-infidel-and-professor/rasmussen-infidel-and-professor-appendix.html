Hume's My Own Life and Smith's Letter to Strahan

Commons · The Infidel and the Professor: David Hume · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
The appendix reprints, verbatim and without commentary, David Hume's autobiographical essay "My Own Life" and Adam Smith's letter to William Strahan describing Hume's cheerful, unreligious death, the two primary documents that first made Hume's serene skeptical death a public controversy.

This appendix reproduces, without added commentary, the two primary texts on which much of the surrounding book's argument rests: David Hume's short autobiographical essay "My Own Life," written in April 1776 a few months before his death, and Adam Smith's letter to the publisher William Strahan recounting Hume's final illness and death, first published together in 1777 as The Life of David Hume, Esq., Written by Himself.

Themes

Hume's self-portrait is an exercise in studied equanimity. Across the essay he narrates a career of repeated public failure — the Treatise's "dead-born" reception, the History of England's early volumes greeted with "reproach, disapprobation, and even detestation" — met each time not with despair but with recovery attributed to "natural temper." The essay's closing self-assessment, a man "of mild dispositions, of command of temper... capable of attachment, but little susceptible of enmity," reads less as neutral biography than as a deliberate literary performance of the philosophical ideal Hume spent his career describing: a character whose stability does not depend on external validation.

Financial independence as the precondition for intellectual independence. Hume repeatedly ties his freedom of thought to frugality and self-sufficiency — resolving "to make a very rigid frugality supply my deficiency of fortune, to maintain unimpaired my independency" — and takes evident satisfaction in never having "preferred a request to one great man." The essay narrates his life as a slow, hard-won conversion of literary reputation into material and psychological autonomy.

Smith's letter stages Hume's death as a deliberate rebuttal to religious anxiety about the "virtuous atheist." Where contemporary piety expected a skeptic's deathbed to be wracked with terror or last-minute repentance, Smith records instead relentless cheerfulness, joking conversation, and games of whist to the very end. The extended Charon dialogue — Hume inventing excuses to delay his own passage to the underworld, each dismissed by an impatient ferryman — turns mortality into material for wit, refusing the consolations (and the coercions) of "prevailing systems of superstition."

The letter converts personal grief into a public verdict on character. Smith's final paragraph, that Hume approached "as nearly to the idea of a perfectly wise and virtuous man, as perhaps the nature human frailty will permit," is a calculated claim rather than a mere epitaph; the essay's opening editorial note points readers elsewhere in the book (chapters 11–12) for the controversy this letter's publication provoked.

Notable passages

Hume opens by disclaiming the vanity of autobiography while immediately practicing a controlled version of it, in a passage that sets the essay's ironic, self-aware register.

The account of the Treatise of Human Nature's failure ("It fell dead-born from the press") is the essay's most quoted line, staging the "cheerful and sanguine temper" Hume claims let him absorb repeated professional disappointment without bitterness.

The essay's closing "funeral oration" for himself is Hume's most direct self-characterization, offered with explicit awareness that describing one's own virtues risks vanity — a risk he takes anyway, in the same spirit of composed self-possession the whole essay performs.

Smith's Charon dialogue is the letter's centerpiece: Hume, weeks from death, jokes his way through classical mythology's ferryman rather than reaching for religious comfort, and Smith records it as evidence of genuine, not performed, equanimity.

Smith's closing eulogy elevates the private friendship into a public claim about Hume's character, deliberately separating the "philosophical opinions" over which "men will, no doubt, judge variously" from the "character and conduct" Smith insists were beyond dispute.
