The Letter to Strahan and the Backlash Against Smith

Commons · The Infidel and the Professor: David Hume · updated 27 August 2026 · source on GitHub
After Hume's funeral, Adam Smith composes the Letter to Strahan eulogizing Hume as a model of wise and virtuous living and dying without religion, and the resulting public backlash from Horne, Johnson, Boswell, Wesley, and others brings Smith, by his own account, ten times more abuse than The Wealth of Nations had.

This unit narrates the aftermath of David Hume's death: his funeral and tomb, Smith's refusal of payment as literary executor, the composition and publication history of Smith's Letter to Strahan (appended to Hume's autobiographical My Own Life), a close reading of the Letter's content and rhetorical purposes, and the intense, sustained religious backlash it provoked against Smith — abuse that Smith himself said exceeded what The Wealth of Nations had earned him tenfold.

Themes

The Letter as eulogy, paean to friendship, and vindication. Rasmussen reads the short Letter to Strahan as doing several things at once: it is a moving personal tribute (Smith uses the word "friend" seventeen times in a few pages), an implicit model of what ideal Enlightenment friendship looks like (echoing Dugald Stewart's remark on their mutual "ambition... to record it to posterity"), and — most consequentially — an attempt to vindicate Hume's reputation as a skeptic who nonetheless lived and died well. The famous closing sentence, comparing Hume to "a perfectly wise and virtuous man," deliberately echoes Phaedo's epitaph for Socrates, associating Hume's death with one of the most iconic deaths in the Western tradition.

A quiet defense of a life without religion. The chapter argues that beneath its eulogistic surface, the Letter functions as an implicit brief for the possibility and morality of an irreligious life: Smith, who had argued in The Theory of Moral Sentiments that religious faith offers comfort in the face of death, here documents a friend who needed no such comfort and yet died with exemplary equanimity. Smith even softens Hume's more overtly antireligious jokes (the Charon dialogue) before publishing, which did not prevent the backlash but shows Smith calibrating exactly how much offense he was willing to risk.

The old religious order strikes back. The controversy that follows — George Horne's sarcastic open letter, an anonymous critic in the Weekly Magazine, Samuel Johnson's flat refusal to believe Hume died calmly ("He had a vanity in being thought easy"), Boswell's outraged private and published reactions, William Johnson Temple's moral censure, Edmund Burke's amused dismissal, and John Wesley's sermon addressed to Hume's ghost over a decade later — reads as a coordinated defense of providential Christian order against a public, prestige-backed assertion that virtue does not require faith. The controversy persisted for decades, eventually mutating into rumors (as late as 1852) that Hume secretly died in anguish and that Smith was either lying or duped — rumors traceable, tellingly, only to devout sources. This is a vivid instance of an old legitimating order lashing out at a symbolic figure (a celebrated "infidel" praised by a former professor of moral philosophy) as its cultural authority is publicly contested — a dynamic resonant with the gramsci-gap pattern of morbid symptoms accompanying a legitimacy transition, here playing out at the level of religious versus secular-humanist ethical authority rather than a full world-machine shift.

Smith's characteristic reticence. Despite the scale of the abuse, Smith never retracted his claims, never expressed regret, and never publicly responded to critics — consistent with the book's broader portrait of Smith's aversion to public controversy, here overridden just once by loyalty to Hume.

Notable passages

The unit's rhetorical center is Smith's "fateful sentence" likening Hume to a "perfectly wise and virtuous man," which Rasmussen identifies as the specific trigger for the scandal — not skepticism about religion in the abstract, but the claim that an avowed skeptic could be a moral exemplar. Smith's own retrospective assessment — that this "harmless Sheet of paper" earned him "ten times more abuse" than his attack on "the whole commercial system of Great Britain" — frames the scale of the reaction. George Horne's opening sally ("You have been lately employed in embalming a philosopher; his body, I believe I must say") exemplifies the sarcastic register of the pushback, while Samuel Johnson's retort to Boswell (turning Hume's own logic on miracles against him) shows the backlash reaching into Smith and Hume's own social circle. Finally, the passage on the Letter as an implicit "defense of the possibility and morality of a life without religion" is the analytical crux tying Hume's death-scene composure to Smith's own moral philosophy about religion's role in sustaining virtue and consolation.
