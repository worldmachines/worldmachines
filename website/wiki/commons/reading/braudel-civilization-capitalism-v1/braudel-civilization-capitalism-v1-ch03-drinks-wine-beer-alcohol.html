Drinks: Water, Wine, Beer, Cider, and the Rise of Alcohol

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Europe's drink hierarchy runs from water — scarce and often foul even in wealthy cities — through wine's climate-bounded belt from the Loire to the Crimea and the northern beer-and-cider zone, to the sixteenth-to-eighteenth-century rise of distilled brandy and grain spirits from apothecary medicine into a mass-consumed, state-taxed, exchange-quoted commodity.

Braudel surveys the hierarchy of European drinks across the fifteenth–eighteenth centuries — water as a scarce and often foul baseline even in wealthy cities, wine as the continent's defining commercial drink bounded by climate and carried to its colonial "New Europes," beer and cider as the northern and regional counterweights structured by class and economic cycle, and finally the "belated" but transformative rise of distilled brandy and grain alcohol, which migrates over three centuries from a monastic and apothecary medicine into a mass-consumed, state-taxed, and exchange-quoted commodity.

Themes

Water is the unglamorous baseline of material life, and even the richest towns rationed it. Venice's public cisterns were sand-filtered rain traps, not springs down to the lagoon's fresh-water table, and ran dry for weeks; Paris depended on 20,000 water-carriers hauling Seine water (itself fouled by dyers) until steam pumps arrived in 1782; eighteenth-century London's underground wooden pipes delivered water only a few times a week. Braudel's point is structural, not anecdotal: fresh water is the item civilizational wealth does not automatically solve — an illustration of what he elsewhere calls the limits of the possible operating beneath even prosperous market economies.

Wine's northern edge is a climatic and longue-durée fact, not a cultural preference, and its export maps European expansion. Jean Bodin's line fixing the vine's commercial limit at the forty-ninth parallel runs from the mouth of the Loire to the Crimea and Trans-Caucasia, essentially unchanged for centuries (vine stumps were buried against Ukrainian frost "in ancient times"). Wine's spread into Mexico, Peru, Chile, and the mid-Atlantic islands (Madeira, the Azores, the Canaries) is explicitly a re-enactment of the Old World "zealously remodelled" onto the New — world-scale comparison operating through a single commodity rather than an abstract civilizational claim.

Wine and beer map onto a rich/poor and north/south economy that inverts under hardship. Southern wine-drinkers mocked northern beer- and gin-drinkers as people who "did not know how to drink"; but wine itself split by price into an aristocratic vintage culture (Romanée, Tokay, Champagne — luxuries only codified as such from the eighteenth century) versus a mass urban drink so cheap it functioned as emergency calories when bread was dear. Le Grand d'Aussy's observation that hard times pushed drinkers from wine to beer, and prosperity pushed them back, makes drink choice a live economic indicator rather than a fixed taste.

Brandy's three-century climb from apothecary shelf to stock-exchange commodity restages Braudel's material-life/market-economy/capitalism ladder in miniature. For centuries distillation was "practised only by apothecaries," and brandy circulated as medicine (Arnaud de Villeneuve's aqua vitae, and the doctors whose brandy-soaked sheet killed Charles the Bad); guilds and towns then fought over the fiscal "privilege" to distil (Colmar, the Paris vinegar-makers and victuallers, the wet coopers); by the eighteenth century brandy had "the appearance of a national industry," graded for market by the empirical "Dutch test" for proof strength, and finally, carried by Dutch merchant networks, quoted on the Amsterdam Stock Exchange alongside rum. This restaging is close kin to what the World Machines project calls a history-machine — a paradigm (here, alcohol-as-commodity) that assembles silently through scattered, independent local practices (monastery recipes, apothecary trade, town guild disputes) before lurching into visibility as an industry and a listed financial asset; the fit is partial, since Braudel's units are a commodity and its guilds rather than an institution or an idea, but the silent-assembly-then-visibility shape is the same. The standardizing instruments along the way — the Dutch test's fixed alcohol grades, town licensing of the brewing season (Nuremberg's Michaelmas-to-Palm-Sunday rule), the Paris aides tax on wine passing the city gates — are small-scale acts of what the project calls legibility-as-machine-core: rendering a slippery, locally variable substance measurable and enumerable so it can be taxed and traded at scale, though operating here on a single commodity rather than on a civilizational order.

The unit's own frame — drink as drug — brackets everything that follows. Braudel opens by insisting drinks "have always served as drugs, a means of escape," placing wine's role for the Castilian peasant (a quita-penas, "drowner of sorrows") and the era's rising drunkenness on the same continuum as the era's other new stimulants, tea, coffee and tobacco. Alcohol's fiscal capture by "the watchful state" at the unit's close is the same substance moving from personal escape to public revenue.

Notable passages

The opening frame (c1) names drink as drug before any history is told, so the drunkenness and stimulant-seeking narrated later reads as continuous with that claim rather than as a separate moral complaint. The Venice water passage (c2) is the unit's starkest material-life detail: a maritime capital of famous wealth rationing rainwater through sand filters. The forty-ninth-parallel passage (c3) gives Braudel's climatic boundary for commercial wine and, in the same breath, frames colonial vine-planting as the Old World imprinting itself on the New. The three-stage periodization (c4, "the sixteenth century created it; the seventeenth consolidated it; the eighteenth popularized it") is Braudel's own longue-durée claim about when a drink becomes a civilizational fact rather than a curiosity, paired with the alembic's etymology as a reminder of distillation's older, non-European roots. The Amsterdam Exchange passage (c5) is the clearest single sentence in the unit where a drink becomes a capitalist financial instrument, ranked and quoted like any other traded good. The Weigert/Edouard Adam passage (c6) is the unit's explicit instance of the limits of the possible: brandy's spread was capped by primitive stills until specific technical inventions moved the ceiling, decades apart.
