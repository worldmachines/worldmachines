The Human Engine and the Animal Engine: Power Before Coal

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Between the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries pre-industrial societies ran on human muscle and an unevenly distributed menagerie of draft animals — llamas and mules in the Americas, camels under Islamic caravan trade, oxen, and horses whose medieval collar multiplied their pulling power fourfold — with Braudel arguing throughout that a technology's actual use, not its invention, is set by a society's readiness to adopt it.

Opening the book's chapter on technology and energy, Braudel first lays out a general theory of technological change — invention (inventio) is cheap and constant, but application (usurpatio) waits on a society's "receptivity," so centuries can separate a device's first appearance from its actual adoption — before turning to the concrete inventory of pre-industrial power sources available between 1450 and 1789: human muscle, then draft animals, compared systematically across the Americas, Islam, China, India, and Europe, with coal (and the mechanized future it would fuel) named only as the missing piece hindsight reveals.

Themes

Technology is not a self-propelled engine of history; society decides when to use what already exists. Braudel opens by attacking Lefebvre des Noettes's determinism (the claim that the horse-collar alone "progressively reduced man's slavery") and Lynn White's spectacles-caused-the-Renaissance thesis, insisting technology and society are mutually explanatory but neither is sufficient alone. His governing example is the scythe, known for centuries but confined to hay until grain-cutting needs and an acceptable wastage rate made its general harvesting use worthwhile only around 1800 — and, in the same vein, Pirenne's line that Viking America "was lost as soon as it was discovered, because Europe did not yet need it." Braudel generalizes this into a "ceiling" model: technology is sometimes an achievable possible that people are not yet ready to exploit, sometimes a hard ceiling that blocks them outright, and breakthroughs occur only when accumulated pressure — economic, social, psychological — finally cracks that ceiling. This is Braudel's limits of the possible argument, though the chapter does not use that exact phrase here.

The human engine is weak but flexible, and its market price runs ahead of its horsepower. Measured mechanically, a man delivers only three to four hundredths of a horsepower against a cart horse's twenty-seven to fifty-seven hundredths — Belidor reckoned seven men to one horse. Yet in Louis XIII's France a man's day-wage was half a horse's hire, not a seventh, because human labor brought tool-use, dexterity, and the capacity to run "human engines" (capstans, pedals, cranks, the loom) that raw strength alone could not.

Cheap human labor is itself an anti-mechanization force. Braudel's clearest thesis statement in this unit: wherever labor stayed abundant and cheap — ancient slave economies, China's coolies — the incentive to mechanize never appeared, because "there is never any progress unless a higher value is set on human labour." Rising labor cost, not invention, is what triggers substitution of machine for man.

Animal power is unevenly, almost arbitrarily, distributed across the world, and that distribution has its own economic and political logic. This is Braudel's world-scale comparative method at work. The New World's power base — llamas, then imported mules and oxen — was comparatively simple and, in Brazil's tropeiro mule trade, directly generative of merchant capital ("pioneers of an early form of capitalism"). The Old World inherited older, more segmented arrangements: camels and dromedaries split hot and cold deserts between them and gave Islam a near-monopoly on inland caravan transport; horses were comparatively rare and expensive in China, India, and Black Africa, abundant and militarized in Islamic lands, and increasingly bred, stabled, and congesting the streets of Europe's capitals by the eighteenth century.

The horse-collar is Braudel's concrete proof of his own "ceiling" theory. Roman yoke-harnessed horses, throttled at the neck, were worth less in draft work than four slaves; the twelfth-century collar multiplied a horse's effective power four- or fivefold and converted it from a war animal into a farm and transport animal — one invention, adopted only once Europe's agricultural and demographic expansion made the investment worthwhile.

A partial resonance with history-machine. Braudel's account of inventions accumulating quietly for generations — the steam engine "invented a long time before it launched the industrial revolution — or should one say before being launched by it?" — before a social threshold makes them suddenly consequential, rhymes with Lohse's history-machine, which assembles silently and distributedly until it lurches into visibility. The fit is partial: Braudel's threshold is economic/social readiness, Lohse's is paradigm visibility, and Braudel is explicit that "the force that overcomes the obstacle is never a simple internal development of technology or science."

A partial resonance with modernity-machine and legibility-as-machine-core. The chapter's recurring claim that mechanization only happens once labor is expensive enough to displace foreshadows the cost-driven, scale-seeking logic WMP assigns to the Modernity Machine's legibility project. The fit is loose: Braudel describes a pre-modern, materially-driven threshold well before that machine's construction phase (1200–1600) reaches this kind of energy infrastructure, and his register is economic rather than institutional or epistemic.

  • c2 sets the human engine's raw mechanical weakness against its wage premium and tool-enabled flexibility.
  • c3 is Poinsot's 1806 primary-source case for spade-tillage over the plow — Braudel's illustration of human labor's productive ceiling and society's resistance to it.
  • c4 states outright that cheap labor blocks mechanization and only a costlier human engine drives "progress."
  • c5 credits Islam's camel monopoly with building "a resourceful ancient capitalism" across the Old World's arid belt.
  • c6 shows the horse-collar quadrupling a horse's usable power — Braudel's own illustration of a technology waiting on social readiness before it is exploited.
