Stimulants and Their Global Circuits: Alcohol, Chocolate, Tea, Coffee and Tobacco

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Alcohol, chocolate, tea, coffee and tobacco each spread from non-European origins into worldwide use between roughly 1500 and 1800, moving from local material-life habits into commodities that colonial powers weaponized for dependency and that an organized capitalist sector eventually mass-produced for profit.

This unit surveys, civilization by civilization, the intoxicants and stimulants that were not European in origin but became European (and, in tobacco's case, near-universal) between the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries: fermented and distilled alcohol outside Europe, then chocolate, tea and coffee as the three new hot drinks of the seventeenth century, then tobacco as the fourth great stimulant. Braudel treats each as a case study in his three-tier distinction between material life (subsistence habits like manioc beer or maple sap), the market economy (the Parisian cafe, the tea fleet, the pedlar), and capitalism proper (plantation monopolies, the Tobacco Monopoly, an "active capitalist sector" organizing production and distribution). He is equally interested in the diffusion mechanics — who copied whom, and whether any civilization can claim priority — as in the substances themselves.

Themes

Every civilization solved the alcohol problem independently, and the still does not settle who led. Maple sap in Canada, pulque from agave in Mexico, fermented manioc among the Tupinambas, palm wine, Northern European mead and beer, Far Eastern rice wine — Braudel opens by cataloguing parallel, unconnected solutions to fermentation before asking whether the European still (dated roughly eleventh–twelfth century) gave Europe a real advantage in producing "super-alcoholic" spirits. He then works through Persian, Chinese and Arabic evidence on distillation's origins and refuses to award priority to any one civilization — a explicit instance of the world-scale, non-Eurocentric comparison this project values in Braudel.

Alcohol became a deliberate instrument of colonial dependency, not an accidental vice. Brandy, rum and aguardiente are named outright as "Europe's poisoned gifts to the civilizations of America," and the chapter's sharpest passage quotes the Viceroy of Mexico recommending that pulque-drinking be spread to the still-independent Apaches precisely because it would create "a new need" binding them in "obligatory dependence." This is a portrait of successful capture rather than resistance to it — worth reading against capture-resistance as the picture of what that concept stands against: a population's autonomy deliberately dissolved by manufacturing appetite.

Chocolate, tea and coffee each took a distinct route into Europe and struck a different depth of root. Chocolate moved from Mexico through Spain (c. 1520) and stayed most at home in Spain, only slowly and unevenly reaching France (Richelieu, the Regency) and England; a 1768 Paris observation — "the great take it sometimes, the old often, the people never" — marks its limits. Tea moved from China through Dutch, Portuguese and English carriers, becoming a genuine mass habit only in Holland and England after 1720–30 sea trade opened, while France, Germany and Spain largely passed it by; coffee moved out of the Islamic world (Aden, Mecca, Cairo, Istanbul) via travelers' accounts before conquering Paris after the Turkish ambassador Soliman Aga's 1669 reception.

Tea is presented as Braudel's paradigm "plant of civilization": a commodity that disciplines the society that grows it. The passage on tea cultivation — pruning, same-day processing, repeated drying — is offered explicitly as an analogue to European viticulture, both requiring "thousands of years of progress" by "a civilisation of sedentary peasants." Braudel extends this to a speculative rule that plants of civilization are mutually exclusive: tea triumphed precisely in wine-less zones (northern Europe, Russia, Islam).

Coffee's Paris career shows the market economy tipping into capitalism. What begins as an Ottoman social ritual (Della Valle's account of hours-long coffee sessions in Constantinople) becomes, by the 1780s, a street-level Parisian commodity sold by pedlar women to workmen, and simultaneously a plantation crop organized across Java, Bourbon, Martinique and Santo Domingo. Braudel states plainly that coffee had become "a 'national commodity' like tea, a means of making money," with "an active capitalist sector" behind its production and distribution — his own three-storey house (material life / market / capitalism) made visible in a single commodity's career.

Tobacco spread as a distributed, ungovernable practice rather than through any single authority's decision. Unlike pepper, tea or coffee, tobacco had "no producer market... no civilization behind it," arriving from "savage" America and diffusing purely on climatic adaptability. Government prohibitions "encircled the world" — England, Japan, the Ottoman Empire, the Mogul Empire, Sweden, Denmark, assorted German states — and were everywhere ignored, nowhere more strikingly than in China, where consumption became universal "down to toddlers two feet high" despite a ban in force until 1776. This pattern — a practice assembling itself silently and independently across dozens of unconnected polities until it is simply, irreversibly everywhere — rhymes with history-machine's account of paradigms that lurch into visibility from distributed components rather than from any "great man" or central decision; the fit is partial, since Braudel is narrating a commodity's diffusion rather than a conceptual paradigm shift, but the shape (no single origin, no single authority capable of stopping it) is the same.

Braudel steps back to name a general law of civilizational compensation. "Every civilization needs dietary luxuries and stimulants": pepper in the twelfth–thirteenth centuries, alcohol in the sixteenth, tea and coffee after, tobacco throughout, with the nineteenth and twentieth centuries due their "own good and evil drugs." He reads this as compensation for chronic dietary hardship (the shortage of meat noted alongside the "excesses of alcohol" and "the coming of coffee") rather than as simple fashion — and pointedly rejects Michelet's claim that coffee was the "drink of the Revolution" as exaggerated.

Notable passages

The distillation-origins passage (c2) is the unit's clearest piece of explicit world-scale method: Persia, China and the West are each weighed and none is crowned, ending on the etymological hint that a Chinese word for spirit derives from Arabic araq. The Galvez quotation (c1) is the unit's most politically charged passage, naming alcohol distribution as calculated policy for producing dependency. The tea-cultivation passage (c3) states Braudel's "plant of civilization" thesis in its most compact form. Della Valle's Constantinople account (c4) preserves coffee's original meaning as unhurried, hours-long male sociability before its reinvention as a Parisian fashion good. The "national commodity" passage (c5) is the unit's most direct statement of capitalism organizing a stimulant's production and distribution. The prohibitions passage (c6) shows tobacco's diffusion outrunning every government that tried to contain it.
