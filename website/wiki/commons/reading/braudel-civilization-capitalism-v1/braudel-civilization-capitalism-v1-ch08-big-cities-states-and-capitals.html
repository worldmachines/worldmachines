The Big Cities: States, Capitals, and the Political Arithmetic of Growth

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries a handful of European capitals overtook the historically larger cities of the East because capital status was created by political centralization rather than trade, leaving London, Paris, Naples and St Petersburg as economically top-heavy "unbalanced worlds" sustained by court, office and rent rather than commerce.

Braudel argues that the explosive growth of London, Paris, Naples, St Petersburg and their peers between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries was a political phenomenon, not a commercial one: these cities swelled because they were the seat of an increasingly centralized territorial state, and their outsized economies were sustained by court spending, officeholding, rents and government bonds rather than by trade. He opens with world-scale comparison — for centuries the biggest cities had been in the East and Far East (Istanbul, Peking, Delhi), and it took until the sixteenth century for the West to catch up and then overtake them with a new kind of state-made giant city. He contrasts Europe's capitals, which accreted durable market infrastructure, with India's "official towns," which wandered and decayed with the prince's whim, and briefly notes Japan's Yedo/Osaka/Kyoto trio as a partly convergent case. The unit closes with two extended case studies pushing the pattern to its extremes: Naples, split between a vast destitute underclass and a parasitic "super-society" of clergy, nobility and officials; and St Petersburg, a city willed into being on a flood-prone swamp by Peter the Great, with an artificially skewed, garrison-heavy, strikingly polyglot population.

Themes

Capitals grew because of the state, not the market. The chapter's organizing claim, under the heading "The function of capital cities," is a kind of law: the more centralized the state, the more populous its capital, and these towns "produced the modern states... produced the national markets, without which the modern state would be a pure fiction." This is political concentration presented as the engine of urban giantism, with commerce as an effect rather than a cause. This resonates with modernity-machine and legibility-as-machine-core — a capital manufacturing a unified national market out of scattered local economies is exactly the kind of scale-enabling infrastructure the Modernity Machine optimizes for — though the mechanism Braudel describes here is fiscal and political concentration (court, officials, bond markets) rather than measurement technology like cadastres or timetables, so the fit is partial: it is legibility's outcome (a single national market) arrived at by a different, more brute-force route (princely and administrative gravity) than the primer's canonical examples.

East led, then West overtook it. Istanbul, Peking and Delhi were enormous "not [as] a function of the density of population... but due to their role as powerful political concentrations" behind an empire. Braudel's explicit East/West/world comparison (a recurring method of his) sets up the rest of the chapter: European capitals from the sixteenth century on produced a new, more consequential kind of giant city, one bound up with the emerging territorial state rather than simply an ancient empire's seat.

India's wandering "official towns." Where Europe's capitals accumulated lasting institutional and market infrastructure across generations, Mughal India's capitals moved with the prince's whim — Bengal's capital alone shifted from Rajmahal to Dacca to Murshidabad — and "as soon as the prince abandoned it the town was jeopardized, deteriorated and occasionally died." This is a striking, almost literal case of "great man" urban history — a town's fate riding entirely on one ruler's presence — and it sharpens by contrast with history-machine's idea of infrastructure (here, a national market) assembling silently and distributedly rather than through a single figure's will; Europe's capitals in this chapter look more like that slower, distributed accumulation, India's like its opposite.

Capitals as economic parasites — "unbalanced worlds." London and Paris are shown running large structural deficits with the rest of the country: Werner Sombart's estimate that trade alone could support at most 100,000 Londoners, against a city actually sustained by the Crown, salaried officialdom, resident gentry and holders of government bonds; Lavoisier's balance sheet for Paris (250 million livres for human upkeep against a fraction of that in productive income); and contemporary economists — Cantillon, Quesnay, Mercier — already diagnosing the capital as a machine that concentrates and consumes wealth produced elsewhere rather than generating it.

Naples and St Petersburg as case studies in extremity. Naples juxtaposes ragged, familyless poor who "proliferate... having no relationship with the state except through the gallows" against a super-society of clergy and nobility that, through Church and noble landholding covering most of the kingdom, absorbs the kingdom's surplus and somehow keeps the system standing "at small cost." St Petersburg is the opposite kind of extremity: a capital built by sheer political will against a flood-prone, waterlogged site, its population artificially swollen and demographically skewed by garrison and court (148,529 males to 69,428 females in 1789) and famously polyglot — "there is no town in the world... where more or less every inhabitant speaks such a large number of languages."

A partly convergent outlier: Japan. The rise of Yedo (Tokyo) past Kyoto and Osaka — driven by the Shogun's forced daimyo residence system — is flagged as "more similar to European growth," suggesting the same state-driven logic of capital-city giantism operating well outside Europe, which fits Braudel's larger comparative ambition (Europe / China / India / Islam / the Americas) even as it sits slightly apart from the chapter's main West-versus-East frame.

Notable passages

  • c1 sets the world-scale baseline: the East held the biggest cities for centuries before the West caught up and "wiped out a deficiency," then began "tasting the luxuries, and the bitter-sweet pleasures of cities that were already becoming too big" — the chapter's governing comparison in miniature.
  • c2 is the India material: capitals as fragile extensions of a prince's person, wandering and dying without him, ending on the vivid image of Delhi's own site-shuffling as "a kind of whirling dance around itself."
  • c3 states the chapter's thesis directly — the "political arithmetic" linking state centralization to capital size, and the claim that capitals "produced the national markets, without which the modern state would be a pure fiction," closing on the double-edged "the price demanded was very high."
  • c4 is the London case for "unbalanced worlds": Sombart's trade-only ceiling of 100,000 inhabitants set against a city actually living off Crown, officialdom, gentry and bondholders, "making it into a unity and creating artificial needs."
  • c5 is the Naples underclass passage, moving from the physical geography of the poor quarter (Largo del Castello to the Mercato) to the famine of 1763-4, and includes the era's own diagnosis of a population with "no relationship with the state except through the gallows."
  • c6 is St Petersburg's polyglot character, an eyewitness (1765) noting a town where servants routinely speak Russian, German and Finnish and the educated speak "eight or nine languages... which they sometimes mix up to quite pleasing effect" — the human texture behind the city's artificial, state-willed demography.
