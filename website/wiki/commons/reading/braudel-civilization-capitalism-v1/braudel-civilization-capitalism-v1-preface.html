Preface: The Limits of the Possible

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel's preface justifies Volume I's method by defining "the limits of the possible" — the slow-moving, largely unchanging border between what pre-industrial societies could and could not do — and by arguing that the routine-bound "material civilization" beneath the market economy, together with the unremarked repetitions of everyday life, deserves history's attention as much as its unique events.

Braudel opens Volume I of Civilization and Capitalism by justifying its method: stitching together demography, food, costume, lodging, technology, money and towns — "parahistoric languages" that traditional history keeps apart — in order to map the slow-moving border between what pre-industrial societies could and could not do, to separate the routine-bound "material civilization" beneath the market economy from the market economy itself, and to defend attention to everyday life as the register at which repeated, unremarked routine hardens into structure, as opposed to the unique event that dominates capital-H History.

Themes

The limits of the possible. Braudel's organizing device is a "border which in every age ... separates the possible from the impossible, what can be done with a little effort from what cannot be done at all." Between the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries this border, set by food supply, population pressure, low labour productivity and slow control over nature, "hardly changed at all" — real change along it waited for the nineteenth century (he cites overland transport, not "commonplace, regular, well-developed" until roughly 1830, just before the railway). This is Braudel's own diagnostic term for the volume's subject and recurs as its guiding concept.

Material civilization as the substrate that resists exchange. Below the market economy sits "material civilization": "Everpresent, all-pervasive, repetitive," run by routine (sowing, planting, terracing, sailing "as they always have done"), accounting for 80–90% of the world's population through "all rural life." Braudel insists there is "no clear demarcation line as between oil and water" separating it from the market economy above — the two coexist in a "double register" whose relative weight shifts by era (he notes material life was swollen again by the recessions of the 1970s, just as in the seventeenth century). This slow-changing, all-pervading, barely-visible infrastructure that organizes life across centuries is close in shape to world-machines's core idea of deep infrastructure beneath the level of events — though Braudel's material civilization is inert and cyclical rather than a machine moving through a Dawn–Day–Dusk lifecycle; it is defined by resisting change, not by construction and decline. His remark that this substrate "refuses to enter fully into the movement of exchange" also brushes against capture-resistance, but the resemblance is loose: Braudel's material life is protected by "distance and isolation" and backwardness rather than by any active, self-organizing refusal — it is a residue, not a strategy.

A journey to another planet. The book's temporal reach is dramatized through Voltaire: an eighteenth-century mind is close enough to a modern one that a long conversation at Ferney would hold no surprises, but his material life — "lighting at night, heating, transport, food, illness, medicine" — would "greatly shock us." The whole volume is framed as this kind of imaginative journey: stripping away present-day surroundings to recover "the rules which for so long locked the world into a stability."

Structure versus event. Braudel's most explicit methodological move: reducing the scale of historical observation shifts attention from "History with a capital letter" (trade routes, national and urban economies, unique events) down into "the environment of material life," where a happening that is merely repeated becomes, through repetition, "a generality or rather a structure" that "pervades society at all levels" across "endless ages." This near-exactly anticipates history-machine's contrast between paradigms that assemble silently and distributedly from unremarked, repeated components and "great men"/event-centered historiography — Braudel is making the same move a lifetime earlier, from the side of economic and social history rather than institutional paradigm formation.

Anecdotes as compressed evidence. Braudel defends the use of small, telling details — Maximilian of Austria eating with his hands, Louis XIV forbidding his children the fork, a Japanese costume of the fifteenth century oddly close to an eighteenth-century one — as enough to "set up a signal which points to a way of life," letting "a society stands revealed" through dress, table manners and lodging. This anticipates the book's method more than its content: these are the kinds of granular observations later chapters will marshal at scale.

Notable passages

The opening statement of "the limits of the possible" (c1) is the volume's thesis in miniature — a border, nearly unmoving for three centuries, that the whole apparatus of demography, food and technology exists to trace. The material-civilization passage (c2) is the clearest statement of what sits below the market economy and why it resists being folded into economic history proper. The Voltaire-at-Ferney passage (c3) does rhetorical work as much as argumentative work — it is the image Braudel wants the reader to carry into the rest of the book. The structure-versus-event passage (c4) is the preface's real methodological payload, justifying the book's entire scale of attention. The anecdotes passage (c5) previews the texture of evidence — costume, table manners, travelers' notes — that recurs throughout the volume.
