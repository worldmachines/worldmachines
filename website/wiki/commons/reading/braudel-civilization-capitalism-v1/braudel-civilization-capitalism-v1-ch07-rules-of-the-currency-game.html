Some Rules of the Currency Game

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Europe's stratified gold-silver-copper currency, doubled by an abstract layer of 'moneys of account' and multiplied by its velocity of circulation, formed a single world-spanning monetary net whose bullion flowed east and whose hoarding drained it, while a barter and subsistence economy persisted largely outside its reach.

Braudel lays out the mechanics of early modern European money as a single, world-spanning system: barter and primitive money at the base, a tri-metallic coinage of gold, silver and copper in the middle, and credit (from pawnbroking to bills of exchange) at the top, all of it netted together with the currencies of Asia, Africa and the Americas through the bullion trade. He then works through the specific 'rules' governing that system — the competition between metals, Gresham's Law, the abstraction of money of account, the chronic drains of flight and hoarding, the velocity of circulation, and finally the barter and subsistence economy that the money economy never fully absorbed.

Themes

A single global monetary net. Europe's currency structure was not self-contained: American silver was carried "as far as the Far East" to be converted into local money, and Braudel explicitly rejects the mercantilist lament that this was a ruinous "permanent monetary haemorrhage" — "Europe did not die of it," any more than a besieging army dies of spending powder and shot. The world's currencies, he insists, were already "enmeshed in the same net" well before 1500, with movements in Italy, Egypt and the Far East mutually determining one another.

Tri-metallism as a social division of labor. Gold, silver and copper were not redundant but functionally stratified — gold for princes, great merchants and the Church; silver for ordinary transactions; copper, the '"black" money' of the poor. A country's dominant metal was itself diagnostic: Spain became a mere "channel" through which New World silver passed on to "foreign nations," while England's and Naples's monetary health showed in which metal circulated fastest.

Gresham's Law and the long oscillation between gold and silver. "Bad money drives out good" organized a centuries-long alternation: gold ascendant to roughly 1550, silver ascendant 1550–1680 (fed by American amalgam-mined silver), gold recovering after 1680 with Brazilian gold. Each metal's relative abundance stimulated new mining of the other, in what Braudel — quoting Marx — calls gold and silver's status as "hostile brothers."

Money of account as an abstraction layered over real coin. The livre, sou and denier (like the pound sterling, ducat or rupee elsewhere) were never coins anyone held — they were "units of measurement, like the hours, minutes and seconds on a clock," a notional common measure that let heterogeneous, fluctuating real coinages be priced, taxed and compared. Governments periodically re-anchored and then let these units drift from gold (France in 1577, 1602, 1726, 1785), each re-anchoring a deliberate political act of legibility-making.

Flight and hoarding as chronic structural drains. Braudel calls these Europe's "two incurable diseases": precious metal bled continuously toward Asia since Roman times to pay for silk, pepper and spices, while independently, saving and luxury hoarding (silver plate, chests of coin, treasuries like Sixtus V's or the Bank of Amsterdam's) immobilized metal that never entered circulation — both processes starving the same "engine" regardless of gross stock.

Velocity as the real multiplier. Stock size alone could not explain transaction volume: Naples's 18 million ducats in circulation settled some 144 million ducats of payments in a year, meaning "each coin must change hands eight times." Braudel reaches for Irving Fisher's equation (MV = PQ) to formalize this — a rare moment of explicit quantitative modeling laid directly over the historical narrative.

A barter and subsistence economy running alongside the market. Baratto (barter) was not archaic residue but an ongoing, even codified practice — taught via the "rule of three" in merchants' arithmetic textbooks — and it was constitutive, not marginal, for peasants and wage-in-kind laborers: "the peasants who form three-quarters of our people do not settle a tenth of their consumption in hard cash." Even in an "advanced" eighteenth-century economy, the market economy's reach was partial.

Notable passages

The opening frames Europe's currency as a layered system — barter, metal, credit — extended "like a vast net thrown over the wealth of the other continents," then Braudel pivots to a polemic against reading Asia-bound bullion flows as a wound: the besieger-of-a-town analogy ("he had to sacrifice powder, shot and time on it") makes the drain look like the cost of winning rather than a symptom of decline.

The Gresham's Law passage is Braudel at his most textbook-precise, stating the mechanism ("bad money drives out good") and its political trigger — governments "readjusting money, raising prices of gold or silver coins" — cleanly enough to carry the whole gold/silver oscillation argument.

The money of account definition is the conceptual keystone of the chapter: real coins and imaginary reckoning-units running on separate tracks, reconciled only at the moment of payment. This is Braudel's own clock analogy, and it resonates with the Modernity Machine's drive toward legibility-as-machine-core — a standardized measuring layer imposed over heterogeneous, fluctuating real value so that transactions, debts and prices become comparable and recordable. The fit is not exact: Braudel's money of account is a bookkeeping convention rather than a state cadastre or timetable, but the legibility-making function — collapsing variety into a common, auditable unit — is the same move. Braudel's own insistence that "Europe had no power to reshape for its own ends this world-wide monetary structure with its existing coherence" also rhymes, loosely, with history-machine: a coherent order that no single sovereign designed, assembled instead from the distributed, independent actions of merchants, mints and metal flows across continents — though unlike a Lohse-style paradigm shift, this coherence is closer to a structural constraint than a historiographic lurch into visibility.

The Naples velocity passage is the sharpest piece of quantitative history in the unit: a concrete stock (18 million ducats), a concrete flow (144 million in payments), and Irving Fisher's MV = PQ laid over both — a demonstration of exactly the kind of quantitative method WMP wants paired with qualitative historiography.

Finally, the barter/subsistence passage marks the boundary of the money economy itself — peasants, wage-in-kind laborers, and codified merchant barter (baratto) all sit outside or alongside monetary life. This is Braudel's own three-storey house showing through directly: material life persisting beneath and beside the market economy rather than being absorbed by it. It also touches capture-resistance only loosely — "the poor evaded" money not through deliberate resistance but through poverty and exclusion, a structural absence of capture rather than an active refusal of it.
