Costume and Fashion

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel argues that costume changes almost nowhere except where political rupture disturbs a stable social order, and that Europe's turn toward perpetual fashion — crystallizing as a concept around 1700 — makes dress a visible index of prosperity, social mobility, and a civilization's willingness to break with tradition, all riding on top of a much slower geography of textile production and trade.

Braudel treats costume not as anecdote but as a legible index of social position, tracing why most of the world's clothing stayed static for centuries — poverty, political stability, and imitation checked (unsuccessfully) by sumptuary law — before turning to how and why Europe, from roughly the fourteenth century and decisively from 1700, became the one civilization where dress changed constantly, then closes by asking what that acceleration actually measures: not frivolity but the "energies, possibilities, demands and joie de vivre" of a society, tied concretely to prosperity, social mobility, and the textile trade that supplied new fabrics.

Themes

Costume as a semiotic battlefield governments could not win. Sumptuary laws existed because dress instantly signals rank, and elites wanted that signal protected from imitation; but no law stopped "the passion to move up in the world," and displays of luxury by lords, new mothers, and mourners went on regardless (c1). The chapter's opening claim is that the history of clothing is a proxy for the whole structure of a society — production, cost, and hierarchy at once.

Changelessness is the default state, and only political rupture breaks it. Braudel surveys mandarin China, Japan, Mughal India, and the Ottoman and Safavid worlds to show that where the social order held, costume held with it for centuries; conquest (the Mughal conquest of India, the Ottoman advance into the Balkans and North Africa) is the one force that resets the wardrobe (c2). Poverty produces the same changelessness by a different route — the poor "the world over" had no fashion at all, only feast-day costume handed down unchanged and homespun that "varied even less."

Europe's break: 1350, then 1700. Within this generally static world picture, Europe is the outlier. The sudden shortening of men's costume around 1350 is where, in Braudel's words, "one could say that fashion began." But fashion in the full modern sense — clothes that must turn over constantly and mark "keeping up with the times" — only crystallizes as a concept around 1700 (c3); before that date, in Braudel's account, the pace of costume change had never been as swift. This is Braudel naming a hinge date at which what he elsewhere calls the limits of the possible visibly loosens.

Fashion is not frivolous — it indexes a civilization's energy and its willingness to break with tradition. Braudel stages this directly against Persian and Japanese self-descriptions of civilizational stability: a Shogun's secretary boasts his nation "had not changed its costume for over a thousand years," diagnosing European costume-inconstancy as mere "levity" (c4); Chardin reports the same self-satisfied changelessness from Safavid Persia, where people "are not anxious for new discoveries." Braudel's counter-reading is that changeable dress correlates with the societies that turned out to hold "the future" — restlessness in trifles like dress as a leading indicator of openness to deeper transformation.

Fashion as a manufactured, economically weaponized signal. Fashion accelerates because the privileged keep needing new "gilded costumes" once the bourgeoisie catches up and copies the old ones (c6) — a race Braudel ties explicitly to social mobility and "material progress." Trade exploits this directly: Lyon's silk merchants paid "silk illustrators" to change patterns every year specifically so that Italian copyists, who could reproduce a design but not originate one in time, always sold last season's pattern.

The material substrate: a slow geography of textiles. Behind the acceleration of fashion sits an extremely slow-moving geography of fiber production and trade — wool zones fixed from the fifteenth to eighteenth centuries, cotton's centuries-long spread from India through the Arab world into the Mediterranean, and silk's glacial migration from China to Byzantium under Justinian, centuries before it ever reached Sicily and Andalusia (c5). Braudel is explicit that fashion's caprice rides on top of a much slower substrate of production and diffusion — a longue-durée base beneath a short-cycle surface.

Fashion in the broad sense. Braudel closes by generalizing "fashion" beyond clothing to mealtimes, greetings, hairstyles, beards, and — most tellingly — bodily cleanliness, which regressed in the West between the fifteenth and seventeenth centuries as public baths closed under pressure from plague fear and clerical moralizing. Facial-hair fashions cycle on a roughly century timescale (beards banished under Louis XIV, returning only with Romanticism, vanishing after the First World War, returning again after 1968) — evidence, for Braudel, that even the most trivial-seeming customs obey slow, quasi-economic rhythms rather than pure caprice.

Notable passages

  • Sumptuary laws and the passion to rise (c1). States the chapter's thesis in miniature: dress as social signal, sumptuary law as elite defense of that signal, and the persistent failure of that defense against imitation and ambition.
  • Political conquest as the only agent of costume change (c2). Braudel's clearest statement that stable societies keep stable costume, and that only a rupture in the social order — here Mughal conquest — resets it; a direct instance of what he elsewhere calls the limits of the possible.
  • 1700: fashion becomes all-powerful (c3). Names the exact historical hinge at which "fashion" acquires its modern sense of perpetual, obligatory change, distinguishing it from mere abundance or display.
  • Fashion as index of civilizational energy (c4). Braudel's own interpretive verdict, staged against a Japanese secretary's and Chardin's proud reports of Persian and Japanese changelessness — Braudel reads Europe's costume-inconstancy as a symptom of the same openness that let it seize "the future."
  • Silk's centuries-long migration to Byzantium (c5). The textile-geography argument in miniature: silk's slow, guarded diffusion from China to Justinian's Byzantium exemplifies the glacial substrate underneath fashion's apparent caprice.
  • Fashion as a barrier the privileged keep rebuilding (c6). Ties the acceleration of fashion directly to social mobility and material prosperity — new elites copy old signals, forcing old elites to manufacture new ones.

World Machines resonances

Braudel's own framing categories are implicit here even where he doesn't name them: this is material life (clothing as one of the basic goods, alongside food and shelter, gathered in the chapter's closing "Conclusions") shading into the market economy once Lyon's silk trade starts engineering fashion cycles as a competitive weapon against Italian imitators — a small, concrete instance of Braudel's own three-tier distinction between subsistence, exchange, and capitalism playing out inside a single industry.

The unit's core claim — that costume changes only when "political upheavals... affected the whole social order," and otherwise sits still for centuries — is Braudel's longue-durée and "limits of the possible" argument applied to a single material practice, and reads as a small case study for world-machines: long stretches of institutional stasis (his China, Japan, Ottoman, and Mughal examples) punctuated by rapid, conquest- or threshold-driven reconfiguration (1350, 1700), rather than smooth continuous change.

The sumptuary-law project — governments trying to fix a legible, enforceable correspondence between social rank and permitted dress — half-resonates with legibility-as-machine-core: both are attempts to make a social order readable and controllable from above. The fit is partial, though: sumptuary law legislates a moral/status semiotics rather than the statistical, cadastral legibility the Modernity Machine describes, and Braudel's own point is that this particular legibility project failed repeatedly against imitation — closer to an ancien-régime precursor than to the modern administrative technique.

Ceremonial costume that outlives its original function — nuns "wear[ing] the costume of women in the middle ages," Benedictines and Franciscans keeping ancient habits, the English monarchy's ceremonial dress reaching back to the Wars of the Roses, Carthusians alone keeping beards after Louis XIV abolished them — rhymes with the later stages of the decay-sequence (Allegorical/Ornamental/Invisible): a form that once carried live meaning persists as fixed symbol long after the social conditions that generated it have moved on. The rhyme is loose — decay-sequence describes institutional infrastructure decaying, not costume — but the underlying pattern, form outliving function and becoming pure signal, is the same shape.

Silk and cotton's "silent advance" across centuries — mulberries and silk-worm rearing spreading tree by tree into Sicily, Tuscany, Venetia, and Savoy, invisible until an industry visibly exists — is a faint echo of the history-machine idea of a paradigm assembling silently and distributedly before it lurches into visibility; here the "paradigm" is a textile-and-craft infrastructure rather than a historiographic one, so the connection is suggestive rather than exact.
