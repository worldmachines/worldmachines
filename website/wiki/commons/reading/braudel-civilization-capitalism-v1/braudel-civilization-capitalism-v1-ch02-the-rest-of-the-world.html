The Rest of the World: The People of the Hoe and the Primitive Peoples

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Beyond the great grain civilizations lies a vast, remarkably homogeneous world of hoe- and digging-stick cultivators — and, below them, still more marginal gathering-hunting peoples — whose technological difference from plough agriculture Braudel treats not as a lower rung on one ladder but as a distinct layer of material life coexisting, in the very same fifteenth-to-eighteenth centuries, with grain civilizations and advancing European capitalism.

Having spent the chapter on wheat, rice, maize and potatoes, Braudel turns to everything outside that story: the belt of hoe- and digging-stick cultivators that girdles Oceania, pre-Columbian America, Black Africa and much of South and Southeast Asia, and, below them again, the still more marginal gatherer-hunter-fisher peoples. His aim is a 'synchronic' world picture — placing these populations correctly in the fifteenth-to-eighteenth-century frame rather than reading them, as European travellers did, as earlier stages on a single ladder every society must climb.

Themes

Plants are made by cultures as much as cultures are made by plants. Braudel opens by undercutting the phrase 'dominant food plants': wheat, rice, maize and potatoes did not simply spread because they were superior, they were bred, selected and transformed by the societies that adopted them (a thousand cultivated potato varieties out of Pre-Columbian America's five or six). Manioc is his control case — nutritionally sound, a genuine 'bulwark against famine' in parts of Africa, yet it never climbed the social scale the way maize and potatoes did, because the cultures that carried it stayed marginal. Success or failure runs through the plant's social carrier — what Braudel elsewhere calls material life — not through the plant's intrinsic merit alone — a distinctly longue durée claim: technique and culture co-select each other over centuries.

The hoe-cultivation belt is ancient and nearly world-spanning. Braudel dates hoe/digging-stick agriculture possibly earlier than plough agriculture with harnessed animals (fourth millennium BC), tentatively originating in India or Mesopotamia. He borrows Ester Boserup's demographic argument for why tools change at all: shrinking fallow periods under population pressure force the shift from digging stick to hoe to spade and swingplough — 'the tool, according to this theory, is the result and no longer the cause.' This is Braudel's version of the 'limits of the possible': not soil or climate but 'technical parameters' (Pierre Gourou's term) — inherited habits and land availability — that quietly constrain what a society can become.

A single, homogeneous material culture spans an enormous belt, with local exceptions. Rectangular one-storey houses, coarse pottery, hand looms, fermented (non-alcoholic) drinks, small domestic animals (goats, sheep, pigs, dogs, chickens, bees — never cattle), a vegetal diet of bananas, breadfruit, oil palms, taro and yams: this is Braudel's inventory of the hoe-world's shared material base. Exceptions prove the rule's local sensitivity — cattle appear in African steppes via ancient Abyssinian contact; the banana, otherwise universal, fails in the Sudan and New Zealand. Pre-Columbian America is the largest exception of all: populated by Asian migrants across the Bering Strait from perhaps 48,000 BC, who fragmented into isolated micro-cultures with 'islands' of unrelated languages, building agriculture on manioc, potatoes and maize essentially from scratch.

New crops layer onto old ones rather than replacing them. Portuguese maritime trade brought manioc, maize, sweet potatoes, groundnuts and European vegetables into the Congo, but these 'grew only moderately well among the established plants' — millet and manioc, native peas and beans persisted alongside the newcomers. Absorption, not substitution, is the pattern; the palm tree economy (oil, wine, vinegar, fibre, even currency) remains the most distinctively African fixture throughout.

A corrected hierarchy of 'primitiveness.' Braudel is explicit that hoe cultivators are not the lowest rung — that place belongs to peoples who live by gathering, hunting and fishing without agriculture at all (his source's 'predator' categories on Gordon Hewes's map), who do not control their environment but merely 'slip in between the obstacles and constraints it offers.' He then uses this single criterion — cultivation or not — to correct the European voyage literature (Magellan to Cook), which is otherwise vague and self-projecting. Tahiti, described by Bougainville and Cook as generous, disciplined traders who cultivated gourds, sweet potatoes, yams and sugar cane, is emphatically not primitive by this test; the sealskin-clad people of the Le Maire Strait and the Australian Aborigines Cook met, who neither planted nor stored, are. The same test resolves the vast, heterogeneous population of North America surveyed via Abbé Prévost's compilation: the presence of maize, hoe or digging stick marks a 'settled or semi-settled peasant community' regardless of how 'unsophisticated,' against which hunting-fishing tribes are a genuinely different category — one increasingly squeezed as Basque whale-hunters turned fur-trappers and 'European capitalism laid hands on the great stocks of furs and skins of America.'

Synchrony and diachrony join hands. Braudel's closing move is the chapter's real argument: the 'agricultural revolution' did not happen once, everywhere, but spread unevenly over millennia, so that the fifteenth-to-eighteenth-century world contains, simultaneously, grain civilizations, hoe cultivators and pure gatherer-hunters — different diachronic stages of the same human story, present at once in synchronic cross-section. This resonates loosely with world-machines's picture of multiple infrastructures coexisting in friction — though Braudel's layers are multi-millennial subsistence-technology strata that persist unevenly rather than ~1,000-year institutional machines cycling through Dawn/Day/Dusk; nothing here supersedes anything else, it simply accumulates side by side. Boserup's account of tool change as demographic result rather than inventive cause also rhymes, more loosely still, with history-machine's idea of paradigms assembling from distributed pressure rather than being authored by 'great men' — though Boserup's mechanism is material and Malthusian where the history-machine concept is institutional-paradigmatic; the resonance is in the shared refusal of a heroic-invention story, not in mechanism.

Notable passages

The manioc/plant-culture passage states the chapter's governing claim: apparent plant 'success' is inseparable from the 'development techniques' of the society that carries it — the frame that licenses everything that follows about hoe cultivators not being backward by nature. The Boserup passage is the unit's one explicit theoretical citation and its clearest longue durée mechanism: population pressure shortens fallows, and tool change follows as consequence, not cause. The homogeneity passage is Braudel doing comparative material-life description at its most concrete — a checklist of house form, craft, drink, animals and diet meant to be read as one coherent, geographically vast pattern. The 'users of the hoe are not on the lowest rung' passage does the classificatory work the whole unit turns on, naming cultivation as the dividing line and relocating gatherer-hunter-fisher peoples below it. The Tahiti passage shows Braudel applying that test against eyewitness travel narrative, converting Bougainville's and Cook's ships'-log color into an argument that welcome-with-barter is itself evidence of 'social discipline.' The Basque/fur-trade passage closes the unit by showing the hoe-and-hunting world already being reorganized by the market economy proper — Indian hunters displaced by fur-trapping networks answering to Hudson Bay and Saint-Lawrence trading posts, with Braudel naming the agent plainly as 'European capitalism.'
