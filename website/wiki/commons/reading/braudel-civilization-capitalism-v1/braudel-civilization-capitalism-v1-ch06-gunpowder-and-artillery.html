Artillery as a 'Revolution' in Slow Motion

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Artillery and firearms spread across Eurasia over three centuries as a slow, contested, and ultimately non-monopolizable technology whose diffusion — through trade, itinerant gunners, and capitalist arms production — reorganized state finance and siege warfare without redrawing the frontiers of the world's great civilizations.

This unit opens Braudel's chapter on technology by naming artillery, printing, and ocean navigation as the era's three great 'revolutions,' then immediately undercuts the word: none of them happened fast, and only ocean navigation produced a lasting global asymmetry. The rest of the unit is a sustained case study of the other one — gunpowder and artillery — tracing its disputed Chinese origins, its slow mechanical and tactical maturation in Europe (from siege bombard to mobile field gun, from shipboard cannon to arquebus, musket, and rifle), the capitalist arms industry and state arsenals that grew up to supply it, and its curiously non-decisive effects when measured on a world scale.

Themes

'Revolution' is a figure of speech. Braudel's frame for the whole chapter is that technological change in this period diffuses gradually and is never held for long by one group: Arabic numerals, gunpowder, the compass, paper, silkworms, and printing presses all spread "so slowly in their place of origin that neighbouring groups had time to learn about them." Artillery illustrates the point on its own timeline — from its battlefield debut at Crécy and Calais in the 1340s to becoming a major factor in European war only with Charles VIII's Italian campaign 150 years later, in 1494.

Chinese priority, and a corrective against Eurocentric historiography. Braudel is explicit that "a kind of 'Western nationalism'" has led historians to deny or minimize Europe's debt to China, insisting instead that gunpowder was manufactured in China from the ninth century AD and that the unevidenced attribution to Roger Bacon should be discarded. He tracks the same pattern worldwide — saltpetre known in Egypt as 'Chinese snow' by 1248, cannon in Cairo by 1366 — and only concedes, following Cipolla, that European artillery pulled decisively ahead of Chinese artillery by the end of the fifteenth century, after which Chinese cannon-founders were said to have "neither the experience nor the skill to handle" their own guns.

Artillery becomes an industry, and the industry favors rich states. As cannon grew mobile (Charles VIII's gun-carriages), naval (armed merchantmen, calibre-to-tonnage ratios), and personal (arquebus, musket, rifle, bayonet — a changeover Braudel says "had taken two centuries to come about"), the capital and organizational demands of supplying it grew with it. Production stayed dispersed — near rivers and forests, in workyards rather than rationalized factories — but only "rich states" could sustain the costs, and they used that advantage to eliminate the independent cities that had managed to preserve their autonomy. The result, in Braudel's own words, is that artillery and firearms "quite transformed inter-state warfare, economic life and the capitalist organization of arms production" — his market-economy and capitalism storeys visibly reorganizing around a technology of state violence.

War's fiscal weight, quantified even in peacetime. True to his account-book instincts, Braudel pauses on Venice's gunpowder stores in the 1580s — enough powder to represent security worth 1,800,000 ducats, more than the city's annual receipts, with a further calculated cost to convert coarse powder to the more efficient fine grade. The point, as he says explicitly, is to show "the huge scale of war expenditure, even when there was no war," a material-life fact hiding inside a market-economy ledger.

Diffusion without domination. On a world scale, Braudel's conclusion is deliberately anticlimactic: technology, not just objects but expertise, moved everywhere — European gunners served as far afield as Turkey, Persia, Siam, and Mughal India (the Great Mogul's gunners were European mercenaries until Aurangzeb's death in 1707) — yet "artillery did not create any permanent imbalance of power in favour of one or another prince." Ottoman gunners used captured Christian artillery to devastating tactical effect at Mohács, Malta, and Famagusta, and Portuguese cannon hauled onto the Great Wall bought the Ming dynasty roughly a decade more of life against the Manchus — but these are punctual events, not structural shifts. "Islam stayed where it was. The Far East was not fundamentally touched." Artillery is the diffusion case that proves Braudel's opening claim: unlike ocean navigation, it spread everywhere and settled nothing.

Notable passages

The chapter's methodological thesis — that innovations diffuse rather than stay captured — is stated before artillery is even introduced, then the whole unit tests it against one technology in detail. The China-priority passage shows Braudel doing explicit historiographical correction rather than narrating past it. The capitalist-arms-production and Venice-bookkeeping passages are the two places the unit's argument visibly touches his three-storey model of material life, market economy, and capitalism. The mercenary-gunners and 'no permanent imbalance' passages close the unit by extending the diffusion thesis to expertise and then to grand strategic outcome, setting up the explicit contrast with ocean navigation's asymmetry that opened the chapter.

This diffusion-without-capture pattern — a technology assembling itself worldwide through many independent, uncoordinated transmissions (traders, mercenary gunners, captured guns) before its consequences become visible in aggregate — has a loose kinship with history-machine, though Braudel's subject is a technology's spread rather than a historiographical paradigm's; the rhyme is in the silent, distributed assembly, not the object being assembled. The state's drive to standardize calibres (Charles V's seven sizes cut to Henry II's six) and rationalize arsenals also brushes against legibility-as-machine-core — measurement and orderability applied to ordnance rather than to population or land — though Braudel is careful to show this legibility staying incomplete, since European arsenals remained "workyards" rather than rationalized factories until the industrial revolution.
