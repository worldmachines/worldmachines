From Paper to the Printing Press, and Why Only Europe Took to the High Seas

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Paper's mere cheapness (not quality) made movable-type printing economically viable and turned it into a capitalist enterprise whose deepest effects were unplanned, while Europe's parallel monopoly on ocean navigation is explained, against a purely technical account, by the post-thirteenth-century rise in Europe's material life and the driving hunger of its capitalist towns.

This unit runs two of Braudel's linked case studies in Europe's technological "explosion" between the thirteenth and eighteenth centuries: the rise of paper and the printing press, and Europe's eventual monopoly on the open ocean. In both cases Braudel is less interested in crediting an "inventor" than in asking why a technology that was available, or nearly available, elsewhere in the Old World took hold and scaled specifically in Europe — and in both cases his answer turns out to be economic and psychological rather than narrowly technical.

Themes

Paper was a necessary, not sufficient, precondition. Papermaking migrated from China via the Islamic world to Spain in the twelfth century and to Italy by the early fourteenth, riding an expanding rag-collection economy (flax and hemp cultivation, old rope, even law-suits over rag supply). Paper never matched parchment's strength or beauty; its whole advantage was price, and it was exactly that cheapness — plus a uniform, flexible surface — that made it "the only solution to the problem of the printing press."

Movable type is treated as a contested, possibly global invention. Braudel refuses to settle who "invented" the European press — Gutenberg, Waldfogel, Coster, or someone unknown — and insists the real question is whether it was a rediscovery. China had woodblock printing from the ninth century, Pi Cheng devised movable pottery type around 1040–50, movable wooden type spread as far as Turkestan by the fourteenth century, and perfected metal type was in wide use in China or Korea in the decades just before Gutenberg. Braudel gives real weight to travellers' reports that the technique reached Germany "by way of Tartary and Muscovy," without declaring the case proved. Read this way, the press looks less like a single European breakthrough than a technique assembling itself across separate, non-communicating Old World locales over four centuries before it "lurches into visibility" in 1440s Mainz — a pattern that resonates with the history-machine idea of a paradigm accreting silently from distributed components rather than springing from one inventor's insight.

Printing was a capitalist enterprise from its first day, not a neutral vessel for ideas. Books were a luxury commodity, subject to "the strict laws of profit, supply and demand"; heavy capital costs (materials, labour, and above all paper, over double the other costs) put printing houses in the hands of financier-publishers Braudel calls "the Fuggers on a small scale" — Buyer, Verard, the Giunta family, Koberger, Aldo Manutio, Plantin — who controlled distribution through the new fair circuit (Lyon, Frankfurt, later Leipzig). Content followed the market more than any single ideology: Latin devotional literature dominated the fifteenth-century incunabula, classical humanist texts only became profitable in the early sixteenth century, and the Reformation and Counter-Reformation put the same infrastructure to opposite uses.

Printing's most consequential effect was unplanned. Braudel credits print, via Spengler, with an indirect but real role in the seventeenth-century "mathematical revolution": only once printers began reproducing scientific texts did Euclid, Apollonius, and — crucially — Archimedes become widely available, recovering concepts of the infinitesimal and the limit that had sat almost unread (even Leonardo da Vinci had to hunt for an Archimedes manuscript) since antiquity.

The Old World's maritime civilizations already knew each other, yet stayed inside rigid maritime "frontiers." Mediterranean, Indian Ocean, and Pacific shipping formed a continuous, long-used route to the Indies; Vasco da Gama's 1498 voyage rerouted this ancient traffic rather than discovering it. Even ship technology cross-pollinated — the lateen sail moved from the Indian Ocean into the Mediterranean via Islam, while the northern centreline rudder and clinker hull moved south, fusing in the Portuguese caravel around 1430 — yet each civilization's ships stopped at boundaries as firm as land borders: Chinese junks never passed the Gulf of Tonkin, Indonesian/Arab/Indian ships never went beyond Tourane.

The central puzzle, and Braudel's explicitly non-technical answer. Given Chinese junks technically superior to European ships (Zheng He's seven state expeditions, 1405–1433, reaching Arabia and the Abyssinian coast) and Islam's proven navigational skill, why did only Europe convert Old World maritime knowledge into command of the high seas? Braudel rejects a technical explanation outright — Japanese-crewed junks later crossed the Pacific to Acapulco, proving the hardware was adequate — and instead locates the cause in political will plus economic "need": Ming China turned inward after 1433 and transferred its capital to Peking; Portugal's own king had a pilot silenced and a returning ship burned rather than let good news about the ocean spread; and only Europe underwent the long, multi-century (a genuinely longue durée) post-thirteenth-century rise in its material life and the growth of its capitalist towns, which Braudel names outright as "the driving force. Without them technology would have been impotent." This is a striking instance of Braudel's own three-storey framing (material life / market economy / capitalism) doing explanatory work directly in the prose, and it resonates with the world-machines premise that a technology's uptake is never explained by the hardware alone but by the whole interlocking infrastructure of institutions, economics, and inherited assumptions around it — though Braudel's register here is squarely economic-historical rather than civilizational-cyclical.

Notable passages

  • Paper's only virtue was price. Braudel is blunt that paper's triumph over parchment was economic, not qualitative — the passage beginning "Paper had neither the strength nor the beauty of parchment" states plainly that cost alone "bound [it] to mark it out as the only solution to the problem of the printing press."
  • The case for a non-European origin of movable type. The Loys Le Roy passage, reporting Portuguese sailors' claims that printed Chinese books had "long been in use over there" and speculating on transmission "by way of Tartary and Muscovy to Germany," is Braudel's clearest statement that the European "invention" may be a borrowing — he calls the evidence unproved but the doubts "serious."
  • Printing's delayed payoff for mathematics. The passage on the recovery of Archimedes, Euclid, and Apollonius of Perga through the print trade ties a commercial technology directly to the conceptual roots (the infinitesimal, the limit) of the seventeenth-century "mathematical revolution."
  • Framing the ocean-navigation puzzle. The section-opening passage — "The conquest of the high seas gave Europe a world supremacy that lasted for centuries" through "Europe remained alone in the race" — states the comparative puzzle this half of the unit exists to answer.
  • Zheng He's voyages and their abrupt end. The passage on the admiral "Cheng Huo" (Zheng He) narrates seven Ming naval expeditions reaching as far as Arabia before China's turn inward, ending flatly: "Then, as far as we know, everything stopped" — Braudel's key counter-example against a purely technical account of European exceptionalism.
  • The non-technical answer. The unit's closing passage places the burden of explanation on "the growth of the capitalist towns of the West" and the long postthirteenth-century rise in material life and psychology, explicitly naming the hunger for gold, conquest, and spices as the engine behind Europe's technological "accumulation of practical discoveries."
