Peking and London: Parasitical Capitals and the Verdict on Urbanization

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel closes his chapter on the great cities of the ancien régime — and the whole volume — by pushing the parasitical-capital thesis to its extremes in Peking and London, then arguing that cities are a yardstick of their society rather than an autonomous force, and that the real pivot toward industrial modernity bypassed the old capitals entirely.

This unit closes Braudel's chapter on the great cities of the ancien régime — and, as the text says explicitly, closes the whole volume — with two case studies (Peking under the Manchu emperors, London from Elizabeth I to George III) followed by a summarizing essay, 'Urbanization, the sign of modern man'. Peking is offered as the extreme instance of a thesis stated at the unit's opening: no capital sustains itself; all live off the labour of others. London gets fuller treatment — its river-shaped growth, the Crown's repeated and always-defeated attempts to cap its size, the rival poles of the City and Westminster, a westward drift of wealth against an eastward drift of poverty, and its filth, crime and immigrant quarters (Irish, and Jews fleeing Bohemia and Poland). The unit ends by refusing to either romanticize or condemn urban growth: a city is a yardstick of the economy and polity that produced it, and — against Rousseau's fear, voiced in Emile, that Paris was draining France to death — Braudel notes that the real pivot of the next century came not from the old capitals at all but from the mill towns that the capitals could only watch from the sidelines.

Themes

No capital is self-sufficient — every one of them is a parasite. The unit opens by generalizing the book's running claim across a roll call of capitals (Rome under Sixtus V, Paris, Madrid, Lisbon, Venice, Vienna, Mexico City, Lima, Rio, Delhi, Batavia): none 'could have existed from the work of its own hands'. Even Sixtus V's attempt to industrialize Rome, and Sebastien Mercier's dream of a seaport Paris, are cited as proof the pattern can't be engineered away — a capital remade in London's own image 'would still have remained a parasitical town'.

Peking is pushed to the edge of the thesis: population and climate at the limit of the survivable. Two to three million people are kept alive through six-month Siberian winters only by imported rock coal and a fur trade running from imperial sable down to the poor's sheepskin. Braudel pairs this with the invisible economy underneath the palace — a thousand families in Peking living solely off selling matches, others off washing and reselling rags gleaned from street sweepings ('Nothing is thrown away in the kingdom of China') — the same parasitism the opening thesis describes, restated as bare subsistence rather than luxury.

Legibility efforts collide with a proliferation they can't contain. London's Crown tried four times (1580, 1593, 1607, 1625) to prohibit new building and cap the city's growth. Each prohibition only pushed construction into subdivided houses and secret courtyard building, producing 'a whole clandestine proliferation of hovels and shanties on land of doubtful ownership' — a labyrinth the state could no longer read. This is close to a clean instance of legibility-as-machine-core: the attempt to render the city governable (halt it, license it, count it) generates exactly the proliferation that divergence-machine treats as native terrain — though the period (Tudor–Stuart London) predates WMP's dating of that machine's construction, so it's the tension between ordering and escaping order that rhymes, not the periodization. Peking runs a parallel but inverted version: the state does achieve legibility for its own purposes (a published directory 'gives the names and positions of the streets', used by the mandarins' own attendants), while Braudel's own sources reproduce that same asymmetry — richly documenting the palace, almost silent on the fish market and the game market. 'In the sources, the unusual conceals the everyday.'

A city is a yardstick, not an agent to be praised or blamed. Braudel's closing methodological move rejects both the nostalgic and the alarmist readings of urban growth: cities 'are what society, the economy and politics allow or oblige them to be... a means of measurement,' and their study is a way into 'the whole history of material life' — Braudel's own term for the book's deepest layer, beneath the market economy and capitalism proper.

The real transition happens off-stage, while the visible capitals only watch. Against Rousseau's Emile warning that Paris was draining France ('France would be much more powerful if Paris were annihilated'), Braudel counters that nothing of the sort happened — and that when the actual rupture came, it bypassed the old capitals entirely: 'It was often as spectators rather than participants that the capital cities watched the coming Industrial Revolution. Not London, but Manchester, Birmingham, Leeds, Glasgow' launched it. This reads close to Lohse's history-machine: a new configuration assembling silently in provincial mill towns until it 'lurches into visibility' and displaces the previously dominant urban form — though Braudel's frame is an urban-economic pivot within a single Modernity-Machine epoch, not a full machine transition.

Braudel's own 'limits of the possible' shows up as a failure of imagination. Sebastien Mercier, imagining the world of 2440, 'made the mistake of assuming that this world of the future would not change its scale' — he could only extrapolate from the France of Louis XVI in front of him, and so missed 'the immense possibilities still opening up for the monstrous agglomerations of his time'.

Notable passages

The unit's opening paragraph states the parasitical-capital thesis directly, using Sixtus V's failed attempt to industrialize Rome and Mercier's dream of a seaport Paris as proof cases before generalizing to the whole roll call of European and colonial capitals.

Peking's climate passage shows Braudel at his most material: population, cold, coal and furs, laid out almost as an engineering problem in collective survival, before the same population reappears one page later as a mass of match-sellers and rag-pickers subsisting beneath the visible palace economy.

London's building-prohibition passage is the unit's clearest legibility-vs-proliferation moment: four royal bans between 1580 and 1625, each one answered by a fresh 'clandestine proliferation of hovels and shanties on land of doubtful ownership'.

The 'palace scenes' vs 'fish market' aside is a rare moment where Braudel comments on his own evidentiary bias — the wealth of detail on the emperor's storehouses and the great bell set against near-silence on the everyday markets.

The concluding 'yardstick' passage is the unit's — and arguably the chapter's — methodological thesis statement: cities are not to be judged in themselves but read as an index of the whole economic and political order that produced them.

The closing Industrial Revolution passage is the unit's pivot beyond the ancien régime: London and Paris, the capitals just anatomized at exhausting length, turn out to be bystanders to the actual next chapter of history, which starts in Manchester and Birmingham instead.
