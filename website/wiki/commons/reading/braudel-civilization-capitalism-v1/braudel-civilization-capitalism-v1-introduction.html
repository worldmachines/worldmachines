Introduction: Three Economies, Not One

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Braudel recounts how a 1952 commission from Lucien Febvre to summarize pre-industrial European economic history grew into a rejection of the classical single-economy narrative and a proposal instead of a tripartite schema of material life, the market economy, and capitalism as distinct, coexisting strata that structures all three volumes of the book.

Braudel opens the trilogy by narrating how a 1952 commission from Lucien Febvre — meant to be a simple summary of pre-industrial European economic history — grew into an "interminable venture" once direct research made him doubt the classical account (Sombart, Kulischer, and economists generally) of a single, statistics-ready "economy" evolving smoothly toward the market, the firm, and capitalist investment until the Industrial Revolution split history in two; in its place he proposes a tripartite schema — material life, the market economy, and capitalism as three distinct, partly antagonistic strata of the same social formation — that structures the three volumes of Civilization and Capitalism, and he closes by staking out the book's comparative, world-scale ambition and acknowledging its collaborators.

Themes

The break with classical economic history. Braudel frames the whole project as a reaction against economics that treats "the economy" as a single homogenous reality that can be lifted out of context and reduced to statistics, and against the standard textbook narrative in which pre-industrial Europe (studied "quite exclusively of the rest of the world") marches toward the market, the firm, and capitalist investment until the Industrial Revolution "neatly divides human history in two." Direct observation of the fifteenth- to eighteenth-century evidence disconcerted him because it didn't fit.

The three-storey schema. Braudel's core move is to split "the economy" into three layers rather than one. Underneath the market lies material life — the "infra-economy," self-sufficiency and barter within a very small radius, a substrate too diffuse and undocumented for ordinary economic history to see. The market economy itself is the visible middle layer: production, exchange, shops, fairs, banks — the "transparent" visible realities that economic science was built to observe, and to which it therefore confined itself. Above the market sits capitalism, a "shadowy zone" of privileged actors, monopoly, and action from a distance (the Fuggers, the Welsers, the great Indies companies) — a domain Braudel insists is not synonymous with the market economy, even though the two are conventionally collapsed into one. He expects this term-by-term contrast to be the most controversial aspect of the book.

Comparative history across time and the whole world. Method, not just content: Braudel wants comparison through the long term and the dialectic of past/present, and comparison through as wide a space as possible — explicitly refusing the Eurocentric frame of the textbooks, and naming his debts for Islam (a decade in Algiers), the Americas (four years in Brazil), Japan, China, and India.

The present validates the schema. Braudel reaches for a strikingly modern proof of material life's persistence: after the 1973–4 crisis, barter, "moonlighting," home work and odd-jobs resurfaced at a scale some economists put at 30–40% of GNP, entirely outside official accounting — for Braudel, evidence that the infra-economy is not a pre-industrial relic but a permanent substrate that markets never fully absorb.

Structure and title. The schema is mapped directly onto the trilogy: Volume I, The Structures of Everyday Life: the limits of the possible, weighs the place occupied by material life; Volume II, The Wheels of Commerce, distinguishes the market economy from capitalism; Volume III, The Perspective of the World, is the chronological history built on both.

Notable passages

The origin story (c1) sets up everything that follows: Braudel's dissatisfaction with a single, statistics-ready notion of "the economy" is the negative space his tripartite schema fills. The material-life passage (c2) and the capitalism passage (c3) are the schema's two poles — one below the market's visibility, one above it, both defined by their distance from the "transparent" visible realities that economic science normally studies. c4 states the comparative method explicitly (long term plus world scale), and c5 ties the schema to the trilogy's actual titles, anchoring "the limits of the possible" as this volume's organizing question. c6's 1973–4 aside is the passage most likely to be missed on a first read, but it is doing real work: it is Braudel's evidence that material life is a structural feature of any market economy, not a period costume for the pre-industrial one.

This resonates with legibility-as-machine-core, though inverted: where WMP's legibility names a state/modernity project of making the world visible and measurable, here it is the market economy that is legible ("transparent," statistics-ready) while capitalism specifically thrives by operating in a zone that stays illegible to ordinary participants and to economic science itself — legibility and its evasion as two sides of the same boundary. The three co-present, mutually antagonistic strata (material life / market / capitalism) also loosely echoes world-machines's three-machines-always-in-friction structure, though the resonance is partial: Braudel's layers are synchronic strata within one social formation, not sequential thousand-year eras.
