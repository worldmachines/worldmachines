Money as Indicator: The World's Imperfect Currencies and Persistent Barter

Commons · Civilization and Capitalism · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Opening Chapter 7, Braudel argues money is best read not through received metaphors but as a function of an economy's own complexity, then catalogues the world's commodity and "imperfect" currencies — cowries, manillas, wampum, coral olivette, salt, slave-priced cloth — before showing that barter itself never disappeared, surviving as pockets inside supposedly monetized Europe and its colonies.

The unit opens Chapter 7 ("Money") with Braudel's case for treating money as a diagnostic instrument rather than a self-contained topic, traces the social shock of monetization across very different societies, dismisses older economists' metaphors for money in favor of his own functional definition ("currencies are languages"), surveys world-scale differences in monetary sophistication (China, Islam, Europe, Japan), and then moves into the section actually titled "Imperfect currencies and economies," cataloguing commodity moneys — salt, cowries, manillas, coral, wampum, furs, tobacco — from Senegal to Siberia to colonial America, before closing with "Barter within monetary economies," which shows barter surviving inside Europe itself well into the twentieth century.

Themes

Money is proposed as an indicator, not a subject in itself. Braudel justifies devoting a whole chapter to money by arguing it "inserts itself into all economic and social relationships," so that watching its circulation, scarcity, or channels yields "a fairly accurate assessment... of all human activity, even the most humble." This frames the whole unit: everything that follows (primitive currencies, barter survivals) is offered as evidence read through money's behavior, not as monetary history for its own sake.

Monetization is a universal, repeatable, and irreversible drama. Whatever the society — sixteenth-century Turkey replacing timars with private property, Tokugawa Japan's urban-bourgeois crisis, or contemporary Africa where 60–70% of transactions still bypass money — "any society based on an ancient structure which opens its doors to money sooner or later loses its acquired equilibria and liberates forces that can never afterwards be adequately controlled." Societies outside the market economy are not exempt, only postponed: each is "a condemned man on temporary reprieve."

Money is a function of an economy's complexity, not a mystical substance — and currencies are languages. Braudel walks through and discards a lineage of metaphors (money as "the blood of the social body," Petty's "fat of the Body-politick," oil greasing a machine, Locke's conflation of money with capital) to arrive at his own definition: money "only becomes established where men need it and can bear the cost," and its forms multiply with the economy's own complexity. This is the pivot on which the chapter's actual title-section turns: because money is relative to need rather than absolute, "imperfect" currencies and credit are not deficient money but money doing the same job at a different level — a claim he seals with the line "currencies are languages... they only exist when the dialogue itself exists."

A civilization's monetary sophistication tracks its position in a world system, with trade as the prime mover. China's simple money reflects a position of dominance over tributary neighbors who needed no elaborate settlement system; Islamic dinars and dirhems were "the instruments of its power" precisely because no rival (bar Byzantium) could match them; medieval Europe perfected its coinage under the pressure of competing with the "hostile Muslim world"; Japan's post-1638 closure still let money in through the crack left open to Chinese junks and Dutch ships. Braudel's summary judgment — "Trade steered the economies" — subordinates money to long-distance commerce as the real engine.

"Imperfect" currencies are money in every functional sense; the perfect/imperfect line is an illusion of hindsight. The catalogue that follows — salt cubes eaten as both money and food in Abyssinia, cotton cloth and cowries on the Guinea coast, copper manillas, coral "olivette" manufactured in Tuscany and worn on a belt in Nigeria, dried fish in Iceland, furs ("soft gold") in Russia and Alaska, tobacco and tlacos in the Americas, wampum among North American Indians and colonists until the 1670s–1720s — all display "all the appearances and properties of money," including denomination, scale, and multiples. Braudel's starkest instance is the West African slave trade's "piece of India": a unit of cotton cloth whose value came to designate, and then simply mean, a male slave between fifteen and forty — commodity-money pricing a human being directly. European contact is consistently catastrophic for these systems: cowries, wampum, and zimbos all suffer "monstrous and catastrophic inflation" once flooded with European reserves, and counterfeiting (glass-paste wampum manufactured in nineteenth-century Europe) finishes off what inflation started.

Barter is not a primitive stage money supersedes; it persists inside monetized economies as "internal colonies." The unit's second subsection turns the argument on "civilized" Europe itself: Adam Smith's Scottish village paying the baker in nails, Catalan Pyrenees villagers paying in sacks of grain, Corsica unmonetized until after WWI, parts of French Algeria until WWII, Novgorod and Russia slow to mint until German coin arrived, and the American "Truck system" (Solingen cutlers, Pforzheim miners, Black Forest clockmakers all paid in kind) recurring across Holland, England, France, and Germany. Even the newly independent United States is caught in the same pattern — Clavière and Brissot's admiring account of barter among "country people" — and Genoese merchants at the Besançon/Piacenza fairs settled millions of ducats in bills of exchange down to "a few handfuls of ecus... real money," barter and high finance meeting at the same table.

A resonance worth naming, held loosely. Money's role here — as the thing that makes disparate economic activity legible, comparable, and extractable ("the modern state was the great provider," via taxes, mercenary pay, and salaries, "and recipient of these transfers") — rhymes with legibility-as-machine-core: money is a standardizing instrument that renders local, incommensurable arrangements readable and taxable, much as cadastres and double-entry bookkeeping do elsewhere in that framework. The fit is partial, though: Braudel's money is bottom-up and needs-driven ("as many types of money... as there are economic rhythms"), closer to organic accretion than to a deliberately engineered legibility apparatus, and the state is only one of several beneficiaries he names. Separately, the recurring pattern of an "ancient structure" losing its "acquired equilibria" once a new medium of exchange penetrates it has a family resemblance to world-machines's picture of one infrastructure's logic displacing another's — but Braudel's drama plays out locally and repeatedly across centuries rather than as a single dated Dawn/Day/Dusk succession, so it should be read as a structural analogy, not an instance of the framework's own cycle.

Notable passages

The opening definition of money as "an excellent indicator" (c1) sets the chapter's method before any content arrives — everything that follows is offered as data read off money's behavior.

The "condemned man" passage (c2) is the unit's clearest general thesis: monetization is not a local event but a recurring, inescapable, and irreversible drama, illustrated with deliberately disparate cases (Ottoman land tenure, Tokugawa cities, contemporary Africa) to prove its universality.

The definitional passage running from Braudel's dismissal of prior metaphors through to "currencies are languages" (c3, combining what are really two moves — first discrediting blood/fat/oil/capital metaphors, then proposing his own function-of-complexity definition) is the load-bearing pivot: it is the argument that licenses everything the chapter calls "imperfect" currency, since imperfection is being redefined out of existence.

The China/Islam/Europe/Japan comparison (c4) is the unit's most explicit world-scale argument, exactly the kind of Europe/China/India/Islam comparison this project tracks, and it ends on the summary claim that trade, not money itself, drives these differences.

The "piece of India" passage (c5) is the darkest and most concrete illustration of "imperfect currency" in the unit — a cloth-money whose exchange value literally becomes a slave's price and then the word for the slave himself.

The barter-as-internal-colonies passage (c6) closes the loop by showing that Braudel's imperfect/primitive currencies are not confined to distant continents: the same rudimentary exchange he documents in Africa and the Americas survived inside Europe's own "monetary" economies for centuries.
