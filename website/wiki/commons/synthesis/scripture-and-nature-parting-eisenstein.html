Scripture and Nature: Print's Parting of the Ways (Eisenstein)

Commons · Synthesis · updated 27 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Read together, Eisenstein's Reformation and Scientific Revolution notes show print trading access against convergence along the axis of language: Scripture was opened to everyman by vernacular translation and fractured for it, while the Book of Nature converged by escaping into notation and closed itself to everyman in the process.

Kind: synthesis across the batch's Reformation, Scientific Revolution and conclusion notes. elizabeth-eisenstein states that the two traditions, which took a “like course” in the age of scribes, came to “a parting of the ways” under print (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-conclusion-2). Her own formula for the split is “image to word” for Protestant reform and “word to image” for science. The notes also contain a second pattern that no single one states. Along the axis of language, the two books traded access against convergence in opposite directions.

The shared start

Copernicus emended Ptolemy's Almagest and Erasmus emended Jerome's Vulgate. Both pursued traditional restorative goals by untraditional, print-enabled means, and “broke new paths in the very act of seeking to achieve old goals” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-conclusion-1). Bishop Sprat could describe both the Anglican church and the Royal Society as “passing by the corrupt copies and referring themselves to the perfect originals” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-conclusion-2).

Scripture: access bought with fracture

  • The drive to bring “glad tidings” to everyman required nationalizing Scripture into mutually unintelligible vernaculars. “It is no accident that nationalism and mass literacy have developed together”, while Latin had kept Catholic Europe linguistically unified (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch06-christendom-disrupted-2).
  • Print's amplification was “channeled by the fixing of literary linguistic frontiers”, so each vernacular accumulated its own stereotypes. Bible passages circulated far more densely in Protestant literary cultures (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s6-amplification-reinforcement).
  • The open book did not deliver one reading. Sola scriptura led both to Erasmian higher criticism and to rigid literalism: “Open books, in some instances, led to closed minds” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch06-christendom-disrupted-3). Vernacular Bibles never delivered the “pure original words of God” they promised (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-conclusion-2).

Nature: convergence bought with closure

  • The Book of Nature could only be read through the “little books of men”: printed maps, illustrations and cross-referenced compendia, with a great library “just as much a scientific instrument” as a telescope (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s1-great-book-of-nature).
  • Print let three complete rival cosmologies sit side by side on Riccioli's frontispiece. Just as conflicting commentaries sent readers back to the Good Book, conflicting “little books” sent them to check against the “great book of Nature”. By Newton's day the Rudolphine Tables were driving out competitors, “at least in regions where there was a free trade in ideas” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s2-copernican-revolution).
  • Standardized diagrams beneath diverse vernacular names, uniform maps, tables and notation were “silent instructors” that emancipated “nature's language” from “the old confusion of tongues” and enabled a “large-scale pooling of talents” and consensus (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-conclusion-2). An international technical literature of mathematical and pictorial statements needing no translation already worked against vernacular divergence, though more weakly (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s6-amplification-reinforcement).
  • The price surfaces in the Galileo notes. The Protestant promise that everyman could read for himself extended to science (Palissy's “it is given to everyman to know and read this beautiful book”). It then broke on Galileo's insistence that the book is written “in the language of mathematics”, so that Newton became “as incomprehensible to the average mechanic as Thomas Aquinas” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s3-galileos-trial-1).

The connection

Set side by side, the notes show one trade-off running in opposite directions. Scripture swapped a Latin credential for vernacular access, and the vernaculars are what split it along linguistic and confessional lines. Nature swapped vernacular words for language-free notation, and the notation is what let scattered observers converge, while raising a new mathematical credential in place of Latin. In both cases the multiplication of rival authorities sent readers back to an original. The scriptural original fractured further under many translations; the natural one, checked through shared tables, converged. This is this page's reading, not a formula Eisenstein offers. She warns that efforts to compress the communications shift into “any single statement or neat formula are likely to lead us astray” (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch02-defining-initial-shift-2). It also sits beside, not in place of, her other variables. Confessional control of the presses decided where Copernican work could be printed at all (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s3-galileos-trial-1).

World Machines resonances, with their hedges

  • legibility-as-precondition-not-cause. The same fixity produced opposite outcomes depending on what it was coupled to, a language-bound sacred text or a language-free notation. Eisenstein's own closing line, that the press “laid the basis both for literal fundamentalism and for modern science”, carries her usual hedge: an agent, not a sole cause (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-conclusion-2).
  • legibility-as-machine-core. The Galileo note flags an inversion. The press did not make nature newly legible to a lay public so much as fence legibility off behind a technical code. The rhyme is partial, since Eisenstein's specialists are credentialed by training rather than by a centralizing state apparatus (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s3-galileos-trial-1).
  • nationalism and benedict-anderson. The scriptural half anticipates print-vernacular nationalism, but through confessional fragmentation, not secular print capitalism (eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch06-christendom-disrupted-2).
