Prohibition and price gradients make the market

Commons · Synthesis · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Read side by side, the Deviant Globalization chapters locate illicit markets on many different gradients (moral, fiscal, monetary, regulatory, scarcity, enforcement), each named by its own author in its own terms, and together suggest something none states alone: tightening or harmonizing rules on one side of a gradient tends to steepen it and move the trade into the nearest category or jurisdiction gap rather than end it.

The editors of gilman-deviant-globalization state one mechanism: “we” create deviant markets by codifying moral outrage, so that deviant-globalization is “the ultimate arbitrage activity, growing at the intersection of ethical difference and regulatory inefficiency” (gilman-deviant-globalization-intro-1-globalization, gilman-deviant-globalization-intro-2-deviant-globalization). The chapter authors did not write with that idea in mind. Read together, though, their chapters show several distinct gradients rather than one, and a second pattern the editors only gesture at. This page is a reader's arrangement, and each mechanism stays with its author.

The gradients, author by author

  • Tax (Guevara, Rehnfeldt & Soares). Paraguay's cigarette taxes are among the world's lowest while Brazil's and Argentina's are much higher. The authors report that the multinationals first ran the round trip in the 1990s, and that when Brazil raised export taxes in 1999 dozens of Paraguayan factories opened. One manufacturer's reply: “We are replacing that market they abandoned” (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch06-guevara-paraguay-tobacco).
  • Currency (Steinberg). The rand's slide made a dollar-priced snail whose costs were paid in rands “a proverbial gold mine”. Contraband traders barter, gaining on the way out and skirting the weak currency on the way in. Steinberg's evidence for the barter is inferential, and he says nobody knows how much of the catch is bartered (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch09-steinberg-abalone).
  • Disposal cost and regulation (Clapp). Hazardous waste flows from high-cost, strict jurisdictions to low-cost, weak ones, and the trade persists as long as disposal rules differ (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch10-clapp-toxic-exports).
  • Scarcity and price (Ambagtsheer). Medical success raised demand for organs while legal supply stayed far below it. She explains the black market through Nikos Passas's three asymmetries of demand, motive and enforcement. Her 2003 case shows an up-to-ten-fold markup between seller and buyer captured by intermediaries (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch04-ambagtsheer-organs).
  • Morality as the basis of the ban (Ambagtsheer, again). Organs are not inherently illegal; the paid exchange is, on the ethical ground that payment exploits the poor. This is the case closest to the editors' moral arbitrage, though she does not use the term.
  • Enforcement capacity (Keefe, Robb). Keefe argues that a control regime is only as good as its least vigilant member (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch01-keefe-snakeheads-2). Robb describes smuggling networks living “in the spaces between states”, profiting by arbitrage across legal systems (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch17-robb-brave-new-war).
  • Cost to pursue (Berinato). Distributed pain and concentrated gain: skimming small sums from thousands of victims yields a gain no single victim or bank will chase, a gradient between the cost of attacking and the cost of policing (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch13-berinato-hacker-economy-2).

The connection: rules steepen gradients and push trade into the gaps

The editors put the general case in the Conclusion: moralizing prohibition creates arbitrage, like a partly effective antibiotic selecting for resistant organisms (gilman-deviant-globalization-conclusion-2). The chapters supply mechanisms the editors do not spell out, and they differ.

  1. Harmonization raises the premium on the defector (Keefe). The more harmonized the rest of the system, the higher the payoff to the one country willing to cheat. It offers “an illicit back door into the licit international system”, as with Honduran passports sold for cash. On this logic, tightening rules everywhere but one place steepens the gradient at that place.
  2. Rules move the trade into category boundaries (Clapp). After the Basel-era bans, exporters shifted from waste for disposal to waste for recycling. Ships are not declared waste until they arrive, and broken e-waste is exported as reusable. The trade did not stop; it changed its label.
  3. Protection at home exports the harvest (Khatchadourian). China's logging ban after the 1998 Yangtze floods made it the world's largest log importer, “often from ‘high risk’ countries”, with much of the wood from Russia. He compares this to developed countries protecting their remaining forests by importing indiscriminately (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch11-khatchadourian-forests-1). A reported estimate that illicit timber depresses world wood prices shows the gradient feeding back into mainstream consumer markets (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch11-khatchadourian-forests-2).
  4. The rule is rewritten to protect licit commerce (Reding). A DEA official's plan to control ephedrine at a chokepoint was, Reding reports from one newspaper investigation, rewritten under pharmaceutical lobbying so that pill-form ephedrine stayed unregulated. It became “both a mandate and a road map for meth's expansion” (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch08-reding-inland-empire).
  5. The trade follows the route, not the commodity (Steinberg). “Contraband traders specialize in trade routes rather than commodities.” If he is right, closing one commodity's gradient leaves the route open for the next. He concedes the counterfactual cannot be tested.

No single chapter says this. Taken together, the five suggest that what the editors call regulatory inefficiency is often a product of regulation working unevenly: across jurisdictions (Keefe, Khatchadourian), across legal categories (Clapp), or under the influence of licit interests (Reding). That is a sharper claim than “prohibition creates the market”. Prohibition is one gradient among several, and attempts to flatten a gradient in one place can steepen it next door.

What the comparison does not show

  • The mechanisms are not interchangeable. Guevara and co-authors' tax gap and Steinberg's currency gap involve no moral ban at all. Ambagtsheer's ban is explicitly moral. Clapp's regime is environmental. Folding them all into “moral arbitrage” is the editors' move, and these authors do not make it.
  • The authors disagree about remedies. Clapp wants universal Basel ratification and binding rules on investment, which means more harmonization. Keefe's weakest-link logic warns that harmonization raises the premium on defectors. The editors want the rules managed, not abolished. These positions are left unreconciled here.
  • The evidence is mostly journalism and official estimates. Most chapters hedge their own numbers, and several of the causal stories (Reding's lobbying episode, Steinberg's barter) rest on single sources or inference.
