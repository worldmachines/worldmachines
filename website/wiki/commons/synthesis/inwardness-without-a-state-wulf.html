Inwardness without a state (Wulf)

Commons · Synthesis · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Read in sequence, the Magnificent Rebels notes trace a line from freedom of mind in politically fragmented, despotic German states, through a Kulturnation 'independent of state lines', to Fichte's 'Ich of a nation', with Madame de Staël naming the inward turn as compensation from outside and cosmopolitan and pro-French currents running against it.

Wulf's book, wulf-magnificent-rebels, gives the endpoint of this line a single paragraph: Fichte's 1807 'Ich of a nation', 'a dangerous idea' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1). Scattered through the notes are the earlier steps. Placed in order, they make a connection none of them states.

Freedom of mind where political freedom was absent

  • Fragmentation into more than fifteen hundred states made censorship hard to enforce, and Jena's four dukes left no one in charge (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-2, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch03-finest-minds-2). Saxe-Weimar's 'freedom from censorship' meant self-censorship by professors as representatives of the state (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch12-atheism-dispute).
  • Wulf argues students were electrified by Fichte because their rulers regulated marriage and private theatricals; the note reads her as implying that philosophical freedom substituted for, or ran parallel to, political freedom (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-1).
  • The circle met the Revolution mostly through print and admired it from afar (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch05-novalis-in-love, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch06-splendid-circle-1); the Schiller-Goethe-Humboldt circle discussed everything but politics (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch03-finest-minds-2).
  • Madame de Staël, finding that Goethe and Schiller did not read political newspapers, mused that Germans live in the ideal because their real world is restricted (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch19-current-exodus). After the French victory of 1806, 'The people turned inwards' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch20-french-in-town-2).

A nation of language

  • Horen was an organ of the German Kulturnation, 'a cultural nation independent of state lines but bound by a shared language', resting on johann-gottfried-herder's idea that a people is whoever was raised in a language. The note calls it an early, non-political seed (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch03-finest-minds-1).
  • The verse Shakespeare led Germans to adopt him 'as a national poet', which the note reads as only a hint (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch04-intellectual-friction-2).
  • Friedrich Schlegel linked the fragmentation of German poetry to the absence of a unified nation (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch06-splendid-circle-2) and, disillusioned in Napoleonic Paris, became 'stubbornly and idiotically German' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch19-current-exodus).
  • Fichte's Addresses held that the Germans form a nation by shared, living language, and Wulf reads them as extending the Ich-philosophy to 'a bigger Ich' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1). Wilhelm von Humboldt, developing Herder and Fichte, wrote that 'each nation is a mental whole' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-2).

The connection

The structural condition Wulf credits for Jena's freedom (no state strong enough to police thought) is also the condition under which a German nation had to be imagined without a state, as language and culture. The early notes present the self's freedom as flourishing in that gap. The Horen note plants a nation that lives in the same inward, cultural space. Fichte's Addresses then fuse the two by giving the language-nation the self-positing structure of the Ich. On this reading the national Ich is not a betrayal of the inward turn but one of its possible completions under political powerlessness and foreign defeat. That is this page's inference from the sequence, not Wulf's argument; she draws only the final link, and Staël's compensation thesis is an outsider's hypothesis (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch19-current-exodus, wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1).

Currents against it

The notes also record the other direction. Novalis's Europe imagined a community that 'paid no attention to national borders' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch15-new-confederation); Friedrich's later journal Europe asked whether the continent could be seen as one whole, unity in diversity (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-2); Caroline, Schelling and Hegel welcomed the French victory at Jena, a pro-French rather than nationalist response (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch20-french-in-town-1, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch20-french-in-town-2); and Goethe disliked the emotional nationalism after 1806 (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-2).

Relation to Berlin

romanticism-and-political-danger and counter-enlightenment-and-nationalism trace Berlin's migration from individual to collective self-assertion, running through Herder's pluralism into nationalism. Wulf's material supplies a concrete biographical case (Fichte) and a concrete institutional seed (the Kulturnation of Horen), but also the cosmopolitan and pro-French counter-evidence Berlin's account can pass over. The fit is partial and the hedge travels with it. See also johann-gottlieb-fichte and art-of-being-selfish-wulf.
