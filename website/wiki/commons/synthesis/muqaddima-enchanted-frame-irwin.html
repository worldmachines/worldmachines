The Enchanted Frame of the Muqaddima (Irwin)

Commons · Synthesis · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Read across Irwin's chapters, the Muqaddima's apparently secular social mechanism sits inside an enchanted, providential frame that also bounds it: God works through natural causes, sorcery is real but historically inert, and the laws give no forecast, so the future passes to divination and the Last Days. The secularizing readers took the bounded zone for the whole.

robert-irwin's biography (irwin-ibn-khaldun) returns chapter after chapter to the supernatural in ibn-khaldun: God's decree behind ruins, occasionalist theology, Sufi miracles, sorcery, alchemy, letter magic, astrology, the Mahdi. Each reading note treats one part of this. The connection argued here is that, taken together, these passages show how the enchanted frame both contains the social mechanism of the Muqaddima and bounds it, and why the mechanism could later be mistaken for secular social science. Irwin does not put it this way himself, and he stresses Ibn Khaldun's inconsistency throughout, so this is a reading across his evidence, not his thesis.

1. God works through natural causes

On ruins and dynastic fall Irwin finds an “apparent ambivalence”. Most causes are natural or social (plague, bad government, softness, nomad destructiveness), but at times the cause is God's decree, as in Qur’an 17:16 on the luxurious made to act wickedly. Irwin's resolution is that Ibn Khaldun “conceived of God working through natural causes”, which gives a dynastic fall two sufficient causes (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch01-masudi-iram-weird-science). In theology Ibn Khaldun “seems to have agreed” with Ash‘arite occasionalism, in which cotton burns because God makes it burn, though he was not “entirely consistent”, and he warned that the Lawgiver forbade the study of causes (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch04-aristotle-theology-maliki-law). The regularities of social life are thus God's habitual way of acting. They can be described, but inquiring behind them is closed off.

2. The occult is real but historically inert

Sorcery, the evil eye and dream-divination are real, attested by the Qur’an and by Ibn Khaldun's own experience, yet forbidden. According to the Muqaddima they cannot interfere with the processes of human history (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-supernatural-occult). Miracles are a different matter: the early conquests and Sufi karamat do act in the world, and differ from sorcery morally rather than phenomenally (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch06-sufi-mystic, irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-supernatural-occult). The result is a map on which ordinary history runs on social causes (group feeling, luxury, taxation) under God's general governance. Sorcerers cannot bend it, and only God's direct acts can.

3. The laws give no forecast, so the future passes to divination

Irwin notes that “the laws of history, as formulated in the Muqaddima, provided no exact guidance to what would happen next”. Ibn Khaldun held that the future resembles the past “more than one drop of water resembles another”, had no idea of progress and expected the End of Time. Irwin believes the pull of sorcery lay in its overlap with “the power to know the future” (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-future-last-days). The ranked epistemology of the unseen (prophet, kahin, ‘arraf), the za’iraja, the malahim and astrology all address the question the laws leave open. At Damascus Ibn Khaldun relayed conjunction prophecies to Timur, which Irwin thinks he probably did not believe but used to put the conqueror in a good humour (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch05-office-holding-timur).

4. Where the two frames touch

The social mechanism and the supernatural meet at defined points:

  • The Mahdi. Ibn Khaldun accepts the expectation but insists the Mahdi “would need the support of ‘asabiyya”. Politics and occultism are “two sides of a strange coin”, and Mahdi claimants actually founded dynasties (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-future-last-days).
  • Economics. Hoarders are persecuted by the “combined psychic powers” of those they dispossess, and a Fez qadi chooses the wine tax because it is not haunted by the payers' souls. Irwin notes twice that economic theory here “slides into occult science” (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch08-economics).
  • Public interest. Alchemy, astrology and prophecy are judged by istislah. Easy gold would destabilize economies, and prophecies incite rebellion. The reasoning is legal and moral, but its object is social order (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-supernatural-occult, irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-future-last-days).

5. The frame is the purpose

The outer boundary is the purpose of writing history at all. Ruins are ‘ibra, warnings (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch01-ruins-black-death). Qur’anic invocations close every section as “an extended form of punctuation” (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch09-teaching-writing). The Egyptian recension grew partly through occult additions (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch05-arrival-in-egypt). Irwin's tentative conclusion is that the book was meant to prepare Muslims for the Last Judgment (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch11-ending-up).

The connection

The bounded zone of social causation is what the secularizing readers took for the whole Muqaddima. Because God acts through natural causes and sorcery cannot move history, the chapters on ‘asabiyya, luxury and taxation can be read without the supernatural and look like sociology. Irwin's charge that modern readers “exaggerated Ibn Khaldun's rationality” by discarding the occultism and futurology (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch11-ending-up) is on this reading not only a complaint about selective quotation. The frame itself made the selection easy. See terminal-medievals-vs-early-moderns-irwin.

World Machines resonances, with the hedges

  • For a forecasting project (psychohistory-and-world-machines), occasionalism is a foil: psychohistory presupposes regularities that Ash‘arite theology treats as merely God's habit, though Irwin says Ibn Khaldun was not consistent on this (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch04-aristotle-theology-maliki-law). The reflexivity of fourteenth-century prophecy, where promises of foreordained victory ignited revolts, is a premodern case of forecasts feeding back on what they forecast (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-future-last-days). See psychohistory-and-world-machines-irwin.
  • The cosmos of correspondences and the chain of being that Ibn Khaldun took, perhaps, from the Ikhwan al-Safa’ is a very different legibility regime from the one the modernity-machine built. It invites comparison with the Hermetic material in hermetic-magus-yates and frances-yates (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-supernatural-occult). The comparison is loose: Irwin does not claim the occult content had structural effects on history.
