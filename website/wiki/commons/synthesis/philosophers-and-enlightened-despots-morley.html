Philosophers and enlightened despots (Morley)

Commons · Synthesis · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Read across the Volume II notes, Morley's philosophers met sovereigns in three ways (as prestige to be harvested, as advisers whose paper schemes failed on contact with power, and as ministers compromised by service) and in each the exchange ran mostly in the ruler's favour, leaving open, for Morley, whether the old order could have been reformed from the throne at all.

This synthesis draws on Volume II of john-morley's diderot-and-the-encyclopaedists (morley-diderot-v2). Morley treats the philosophers' dealings with sovereigns chapter by chapter; the pattern across chapters is the claim of this page, not a thesis Morley states in one place.

The local arguments

  • Prestige harvested. Catherine II befriended the philosophers for their vogue, not their philosophy, in Morley's words, and the Duchesse de Choiseul called them flattered dupes who covered her crimes. Toleration on her banners silenced them on the Partition of Poland and her husband's death, Rousseau excepted (morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-1; see catherine-the-great). Frederick the Great admired Raynal's History until it rebuked him, then said of its author “I don't know them” (morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-1). When the System of Nature appeared, voltaire made sure the King heard of his refutation, and Frederick, offended that the book spared political superstitions no more than theological ones, wrote a defence of theism (morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-1).
  • Advice that fails on contact. Catherine pillaged Montesquieu for her Instruction, though his own method would have shown that absolute power cannot impose a free-labour civil system on serfdom (morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-1). She judged Diderot's principles fine books but very bad business, made “on paper” rather than “on the human skin” (morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-2). His University plan deferred on religion to “her majesty's political religion”, and in one anecdote she caught him flattering her (morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-2). Raynal looked to kings for the redress of slavery, as Turgot and the Economists did (morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-2).
  • The philosopher as minister. Diderot's late Essay on Claudius and Nero defends Seneca, Nero's minister, as a case where morality's general rules condemn but common sense acquits. Morley partly agrees that a statesman may be applauded for clinging to the helm, but holds that “the contrast between lofty writing and slippery policy” harmed Seneca's fame. The note on this chapter observes that Morley makes the link to Diderot's own courting of Catherine only implicitly (morley-diderot-v2-ch08-closing-years-1).
  • The reformer's hope. Turgot argued that calm, solid treatment of the public good serves all governments, and that even despotisms contain abuses rulers have no interest in keeping (morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1).

The connection

Taken together, these episodes show the exchange between philosophers and sovereigns running mostly one way. Rulers took prestige, fame, cover for their crimes and a reformist vocabulary. Philosophers took patronage, honour denied them at home, and a toleration slogan that blinded them. When advice was actually tested, it met the obstacle Catherine named and Morley's Montesquieu point implies: institutions built on serfdom or privilege do not take reform written on paper. The Seneca essay is the same problem seen from inside, a philosopher justifying service to a bad ruler, written by a man just back from a despot's court. Grimm, the most successful courtier, became an informal channel between courts until 1792 (morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-2), and Catherine that same year banished Voltaire's busts to the cellars, which the notes read as Russian enlightenment cooling under revolutionary pressure.

Morley does not draw a single moral from this. He leaves open whether the French monarchy could have reformed itself even in 1758, while excusing Turgot's hope of avoiding violence (morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1), and he credits Catherine with rating Diderot at his true worth as a great talker (morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-2). The synthesis should be read with that openness: the pattern is of asymmetric exchange, not of a proven impossibility of reform from above.

World Machines resonances, with the notes' hedges

The question of whether ideas reform systems through rulers or through revolution sits near intellectual-vs-revolutionary, with the notes' caveat that Turgot's argument is a contemporary's policy advice, not a theory of intellectuals (morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1). The diplomats who assumed Diderot's access to Catherine must be political, and Grimm's letters as an informal channel between courts, show a network of ideas being read as, and partly becoming, an instrument of states (morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-2). Catherine's forced modernisation rhymes loosely with the modernity-machine, within Morley's linear frame (morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-1).
