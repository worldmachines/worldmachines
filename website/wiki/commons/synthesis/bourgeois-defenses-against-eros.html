Bourgeois Defenses Against Eros

Commons · Synthesis · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Read across Gay's chapters, the bourgeois defenses against eros sort by what each displaces — distance moves the object, learned ignorance the knower, privatization the audience, hypocrisy the rule — and share a paradox (each licenses what it conceals), characteristic failure points, and a cost borne disproportionately by women and children.

Education of the Senses (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1) names several defenses in separate chapters — learned ignorance, the doctrine of distance, hypocrisy as fortification, privatization, reaction formation — and each note treats its own as a local instance of Gay's general claim that bourgeois sexuality was managed rather than repressed. Read side by side, they do something no single chapter states: they sort into distinct mechanisms by what each one displaces, and they share a common failure pattern and a common distribution of cost. This page makes that cross-chapter argument; it is an interpretation assembled from the notes, not Gay's own typology.

Four displacements

  1. Distance displaces the object. The nude is licensed in proportion to how far myth, history or exoticism remove it from the viewer's own time and place; a serpent added to a Salon nude, or a Christian captive's backstory for Powers's Greek Slave, is enough (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-carnal-knowledge-4-doctrine-distance). The same rule redeems Rabelais and the Decameron for a proud Ohio reader — “their coarseness was redeemed by their distance” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-carnal-knowledge-3-readers-conflict) — and Amiel's “Platonic libertinism” keeps urban erotic spectacle at the distance of eye and ear (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-carnal-knowledge-1-platonic-libertinism). What is being looked at is not denied; its location is moved.
  2. Learned ignorance displaces the knower. Here the knowledge exists but the subject declines to hold it: “unconsciously desired, informally imparted, and assiduously fostered” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-learned-ignorance-1-age-factitious-innocence), “the desire not to know... armored by defensiveness against correctives” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-learned-ignorance-2-profession-anxiety). Its purest domestic form is Millicent Todd's refusal to wonder why her father whistled on coming home — “to conjecture would have been to know” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-erotic-record-3-mabel-loomis-dickinson).
  3. Privatization displaces the audience. Candor is kept but routed to a channel with no public: the diary addressed as confidant, the letter to be burned, the cipher in Todd's “little book” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-fortifications-4-private-experience, gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-pressures-1-facts-life-death, gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-appendix-sexual-symbols-todd-diaries). The erotic life is fully spoken; “the repression, if any, is a matter of audience, not of desire itself” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-erotic-record-2-mabel-loomis-todd). Mosher's respondents show the same move in vocabulary rather than genre: pleasure voiced as “spiritual union” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-communions-2-dubious-certainty-numbers).
  4. Hypocrisy displaces the rule. Rather than moving the object, the knower or the audience, the moral code itself is kept vague: “wide, often ambiguous bands of prohibitions” that carve out “space for the passions — within reason” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-erotic-record-3-mabel-loomis-dickinson), an internalized parental voice projected onto “your next-door neighbor” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-fortifications-1-search-safety). The engagement period, officially chaste and tacitly a time for “exploring and being explored,” is this mechanism at class scale (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-erotic-record-1-mabel-loomis).

Reaction formation — denying women desire “to safeguard man's sexual adequacy,” and fearing feminism as maternal retaliation (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-offensive-women-2-manhood-danger) — sits apart from the four: it displaces the affect itself onto its opposite, and the notes show it producing not quiet management but loud discourse (ridicule, femme-fatale art, the physiology of “vital force”) (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-offensive-women-3-castrating-sisterhood, gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-offensive-women-4-sex-mind-body).

A shared paradox: each defense licenses what it conceals

Gay says this explicitly only of distance, which made art “simultaneously less and more erotic” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-carnal-knowledge-4-doctrine-distance). But the other chapters show the same structure. Privacy is what preserved Todd's and the Hawthornes' candor for a time (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-fortifications-4-private-experience); the vague rule is what let Amherst absorb an affair rather than expel it (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-erotic-record-3-mabel-loomis-dickinson); and the censors' war on obscenity generated an obsessive discourse about obscenity, the crusaders' fantasies “slightly sublimated mirror images” of what they hunted (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-carnal-knowledge-3-readers-conflict). Read together, this is the notes' strongest case that “defense” and “repression” are not synonyms in Gay: a defense is a compromise that keeps desire in circulation on terms the self can afford.

A shared failure pattern

Each mechanism fails at a characteristic point, and the points differ in instructive ways:

  • Distance breaks when the object is made contemporary: Manet's recognizably French, recognizably acquainted nudes, not any greater explicitness, broke “the treaty” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-carnal-knowledge-4-doctrine-distance).
  • Learned ignorance survives even its own critics: the turn-of-century enlighteners, “condemning euphemisms, use little else,” so that “ignorance claimed its allies... even among the crusaders for knowledge” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-learned-ignorance-3-flight-knowledge); its defeat in the masturbation panic produced only “a new, equally anxious” campaign, “a symptom far more than a solution” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-learned-ignorance-2-profession-anxiety).
  • Privatization fails at the moment of transmission to the public or to posterity: Sophia Hawthorne's scissors, the Carlyle and Dickens heirs' bowdlerizing, Alcott destroying her journals (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-fortifications-4-private-experience, gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-fortifications-1-search-safety).
  • Official suppression backfires into diffusion: the Bradlaugh–Besant prosecution turned “a few hundred purchasers” into nearly 200,000 sales (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-pressures-3-struggle-malthus).

A shared distribution of cost

Across chapters the price of these defenses falls disproportionately on women and children — those denied the words to interpret what the culture displayed. Edith Wharton's mother answered her pre-wedding questions by pointing at statues (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-carnal-knowledge-4-doctrine-distance); Annie Besant married with “no more idea of the marriage relation than if I had been four years old” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-learned-ignorance-1-age-factitious-innocence); Carey Thomas, reading a physiology manual alone, felt she had “eaten of the fruit of the tree of knowledge” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-carnal-knowledge-2-lessons-body); Millicent Todd bore a household's disorder as “witness and sacrifice” (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-erotic-record-3-mabel-loomis-dickinson); the seven-year-old in Cleveland was cut (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-learned-ignorance-2-profession-anxiety). The notes also hold the counterweight Gay insists on: men too entered marriage misinformed and prone to fiasco (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-learned-ignorance-1-age-factitious-innocence), and women were often more practically candid about the body than the men who called them dissimulating (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-carnal-knowledge-2-lessons-body). The asymmetry is in who bore the cost of the defenses, not in who was ignorant.

Resonances (hedged)

  • The notes place this whole apparatus against michel-foucault: both refuse a story of simple silence, but Gay's defenses are psychological compromises inside persons and households, not effects of a discursive dispositif.
  • Learned ignorance invites comparison with not-knowing-as-capture-resistance, which reads institutions as “machines for the production of ignorance” and prizes an open not-knowing. Gay's learned ignorance is institutionally fostered in a similar way, but it is the opposite stance: defended rather than open, a way of not attending — so the rhyme runs through manufactured ignorance, not through capture-resistance.
  • Distance and privatization both keep a legible surface while routing the real traffic elsewhere; the notes read the diary as a “countertechnology” to modernity-machine legibility, built from the same materials (gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-fortifications-4-private-experience). The fit is partial: these are domestic and psychological mechanisms, not administrative infrastructure.

Vol. II: defenses that reroute desire rather than manage knowledge

The four displacements above were read from Education of the Senses. The Tender Passion (gay-bourgeois-experience-v2) adds mechanisms that act on desire's aim rather than on its object's location, the knower or the audience. Displacement proper moves erotic energy onto a substitute that is not an erotic object at all, such as a symphony, a landscape, God, a department store or a locomotive (gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch5-1-food-love, gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch5-2-budget-displacements, gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch5-3-glandular-christianity-part-1, gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch5-4-sexualizing-modernity; see stratagems-of-sensuality-gay). This differs from distance, which keeps the nude but removes it in time or myth. Gay calls religious displacement "a dubious blessing" that tightened repression for some while licensing forbidden longings by lending them religious dignity (gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch5-3-glandular-christianity-part-1). Denial as cultural policy keeps a whole zone of practice unnamed: "most bourgeois chose the spurious safety of ignorance over the risky benefits of knowledge," and that choice protected homosexual lovers for decades (gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-1-dividends-denial; see dividends-of-denial-gay). Reaction formation reappears in new places: lust turned into prudishness (gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch5-1-food-love), and fear and buried wishes turned into inflated counts of prostitutes (gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch6-2-hysteria-remorse-part-1). Fiction becomes a rehearsal space where defenses are taught and the alternative is safely tried, a safari that turns out to be a trip to the zoo (gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch3-2-hearts-undisciplined-others).

The Vol. II material confirms the shared paradox that each defense licenses what it conceals, and states it in Gay's own words. The "convention of the unspeakable," which protests that nothing can be said and then says it, gave the century "space for an impressive measure of frankness while hinting that frankness was out of reach" (gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch6-2-hysteria-remorse-part-1). Railroad, music, nature and God "expand—and at the same time ... police" sensual love (gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch5-4-sexualizing-modernity).

Vol. II: new failure points and new bearers of cost

Vol. II adds failure points to the list above. Denial fails at naming. Once newspapers, trials and clinical categories named homoerotic love from the 1880s, the protection ended, and exposure "invited blackmail, ruined careers, drove some into suicide" (gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-1-dividends-denial). Legal defense fails as compromise formation. Statute law and its capricious enforcement show "the return of the repressed in culture" (gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-2-return-repressed). Readers' defenses fail by misreading. Eliot's inaccurate summary of Henry Esmond and Forster's disbelief count as defenses against oedipal material (gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch3-3-beyond-good-evil-part-2).

The distribution of cost widens as well. Beyond women and children, Vol. II's heaviest bearers are homosexual men, for whom the end of reticence was the danger (gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-1-dividends-denial), and poor women exploited by employers, whose sexual education came "from above" (gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-epilogue-1-between-condescension-envy). Gay leaves Albert Dodd's cost open: "we shall never know how expensive this victory must have been to him" (gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-1-dividends-denial). The volume's closing judgment qualifies the whole apparatus. The defensiveness was a tribute to passion, and middle-class people "were generally ill at ease not so much with their amorous emotions as with giving them voice" (gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-epilogue-2-lion-love-last-thoughts).
