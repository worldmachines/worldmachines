Calibrated Candor: Heterodoxy as a Publication Problem

Commons · Synthesis · updated 23 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Across Rasmussen's narrative the operative constraint on Enlightenment heterodoxy was not belief or even circulation but attributed publication at scale — so Hume and Smith differed less in conviction than in where each set a single dial, and the enforcement mechanism was reputational cost administered by an authority that could no longer compel belief.

Claim: Across Rasmussen's narrative, the variable governing Enlightenment heterodoxy is not what a thinker believed, nor even to whom he said it, but whether he attached his name to it in print. Hume and Smith — whose private views the book argues were broadly convergent — differed almost entirely in where they set that one dial, and both adjusted it constantly, in both directions, over forty years.

No single note in this batch makes this argument. Each records a local decision about exposure; assembled, they describe one mechanism.

The Evidence, Note by Note

  • rasmussen-infidel-and-professor-ch01-cheerful-skeptic — Hume "castrat[es]" the Treatise of its material on miracles and the immortality of the soul, and later calls this "a Piece of Cowardice." Then he publishes the First Enquiry under his own name with the miracles and design essays intact. In 1764 he counsels a wavering young clergyman that "innocent Dissimulation" is simply the price of passing through the world. The dial moves; the beliefs do not.
  • rasmussen-infidel-and-professor-ch02-encountering-hume — the young Smith works out a fully naturalistic account of science and religion as devices by which the imagination soothes itself, soft-pedals its obvious implications for a designing God for reasons of prudence, and leaves the essay unpublished for the rest of his life.
  • rasmussen-infidel-and-professor-ch03-budding-friendship — Smith teaches Hume's Political Discourses to Glasgow students almost verbatim while handling the natural-theology curriculum so minimally that his "principles" fall under suspicion, and writes to Cullen that he "should prefer David Hume to any man for a colleague; but I am afraid the public would not be of my opinion."
  • rasmussen-infidel-and-professor-ch04-historian-and-kirk — the Kirk's excommunication campaign; Hume answering it by publishing the Natural History of Religion, his most hostile treatment of religion yet; and, in the same period, proposing to Hugh Blair that they simply stop discussing religion so the friendship can continue.
  • rasmussen-infidel-and-professor-ch07-wild-philosopher — Smith's July 6 letter urging Hume not to publish his side of the Rousseau quarrel makes an argument entirely about the costs of publicity, independent of who is right: "By endeavouring to unmask before the Public this hypocritical Pedant, you run the risk, of disturbing the tranquillity of your whole life."
  • rasmussen-infidel-and-professor-ch10-natural-religion — Smith refuses to publish the Dialogues while calling it "finely written" and circulating the manuscript privately, including to the duc de La Rochefoucauld; he tells Strahan he wishes it "had remained in Manuscript to be communicated only to a few people." Nothing in the record objects to its content. And Hume had sat on that manuscript for twenty-five years for exactly Smith's reason — a wish to "live quietly, and keep remote from all Clamour."
  • rasmussen-infidel-and-professor-ch11-philosophers-death and rasmussen-infidel-and-professor-ch12-ten-times-more-abuse — My Own Life omits every professional cost of Hume's irreligion; Smith preserves a harsher Charon dialogue in a private letter to Wedderburn and softens it for print. Even at maximum candor, the dial is being turned.
  • rasmussen-infidel-and-professor-epilogue — the 1790 sixth edition of The Theory of Moral Sentiments replaces the doctrine of atonement with the Tartarus/Elysium passage. Heterodoxy enters the canon as a revision rather than a declaration; the Archbishop of Dublin read it correctly as "the infection of David Hume's society."

The Connection

Put together, these show that the same content moved almost without friction through private channels — letters, hand-passed manuscripts, dinner tables, a Glasgow lecture room, a Paris salon — while being throttled at exactly one point: attributed publication at scale. The constraint was not on thought, and not even on circulation. It was on the public act of standing behind an idea in one's own name.

Three consequences follow that no individual note states.

1. The infidel/professor distinction is a difference of setting, not of doctrine. rasmussen-infidel-and-professor-introduction dismantles the atheist-versus-believer caricature directly, reframing it as temperament and public strategy. What this batch adds is the mechanism: both men were running the same calculation on the same dial, and the book's title names two positions on it rather than two convictions.

2. Enforcement was reputational, not legal — which is what a receding authority looks like. Excommunication carried no civil penalty; the Kirk's campaign endangered Hume's friends, not Hume. An order that can still impose social costs but can no longer compel belief, and whose critics are professors and library keepers rather than fugitives, is an authority in the condition receding-containers describes — thinning rather than breaking. The morbid symptoms show at the moment of contest: the backlash to the Letter to Strahan answers not an argument but an exemplar, and Smith's own accounting is exact — a "harmless Sheet of paper" earned "ten times more abuse" than an attack on "the whole commercial system of Great Britain." A legitimating order absorbs metaphysical dispute far more easily than a rival model of how to live and die well, which is the register in which the gramsci-gap describes a legitimacy transition.

3. The dial had jurisdictions, and the republic of letters arbitraged them. rasmussen-infidel-and-professor-ch06-feted-in-france shows the same corpus drawing excommunication motions in Edinburgh and adulation in Paris, where the d'Holbach circle found Hume insufficiently irreligious. How much candor is affordable was a local price, and the informal network of clubs, salons, and correspondence documented in scottish-enlightenment functioned as a channel between markets with different prices.

Why This Matters for the Frame

The standard picture of the Enlightenment is of ideas winning arguments in public. What this batch documents is closer to the history-machine pattern: heterodox content accumulating silently and privately across decades, distributed through a network no one designed for that purpose, and then lurching into public legibility all at once — in 1777–79, with My Own Life, the Letter to Strahan, and the Dialogues arriving within three years of each other. The first two were written in 1776, but the Dialogues had sat in manuscript for a quarter-century, and the convictions all three expressed were decades old. The visible controversy is not the moment the thinking happened; it is the moment the accumulated stock was finally attributed.

It also puts a useful correction on venkatesh-rao-the-divergence-machine-ii's reading of Hume and Smith as allies expanding the argument-of-progress on adjacent frontiers. They were — but the frontier they advanced on was as much publishing strategy under an incumbent orthodoxy as it was epistemology. The move from Hume castrating his own Treatise in 1737–38 to Smith calling a dead skeptic "a perfectly wise and virtuous man" in 1777 is a measurable change in what could be said out loud with a name attached, and it cost Smith something to make.
