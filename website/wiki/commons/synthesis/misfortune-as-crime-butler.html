Misfortune as crime in Erewhon

Commons · Synthesis · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Across Erewhon's courts, birth formulae, myth of the unborn, motive-blind gods and money-ranking, Butler builds one closed moral economy in which outcome is desert, each layer warranting the next, so that the doctrine resists argument and finally serves as a pretext.

Several reading notes describe one piece of Erewhon's inverted morality each: the trials, the straighteners, the gods, the birth formulae, the unborn. This page connects them. Read together, they form a single, layered moral economy built on one principle: what happens to you is what you deserve. Law, theology, myth and money each carry a part of it, and the parts justify one another. Every voice below is Erewhonian or the narrator's. Butler's own 1872 preface rejects the reading that the book denies moral responsibility (butler-erewhon-prefaces), and the likely target, Victorian moralising about poverty and sickness, is a reading, not something the text says.

Layer 1 — the court: outcome without cause

Bodily illness before seventy is tried and punished, as is ill luck generally. Forgery, arson and robbery go to the "straighteners", and bad conduct counts as the result of "pre-natal or post-natal misfortune" (butler-erewhon-ch10-current-opinions). The Personal Bereavement Court fines a widower for losing his wife and flogs a youth for being swindled. The judge in the consumption trial refuses every causal explanation (unhealthy parents, an accident in childhood) as "curious metaphysical questions", reduces the case to "are you wicked or not?", and concludes "it is your crime to be unfortunate". Even acquittal would convict the prisoner of "having been maligned unjustly" (butler-erewhon-ch11-some-erewhonian-trials). Luck is "the only fit object of human veneration", and who counts as lucky is settled "by a kind of higgling and haggling of the market, and ultimately by brute force". The note on that chapter flags that the judge's regress is also a real problem about responsibility, and the text does not settle it.

Layer 2 — the myth: you chose to be born

The court's refusal to look behind the outcome gets its metaphysical warrant from the mythology of the unborn. The unborn choose birth, which their wiser fellows call "a felony", and pester their allotted parents into it (butler-erewhon-ch19-the-world-of-the-unborn). Each child therefore signs, or has signed for it, a birth formula confessing that it plagued two contented people "with malice aforethought" (butler-erewhon-ch18-birth-formulae). The narrator states the link outright: the birth-formula theory exists to justify punishing the sick, "it is all his own doing" (butler-erewhon-ch20-what-they-mean-by-it). The judge too had called the unborn "our natural enemies" (butler-erewhon-ch11-some-erewhonian-trials). The contract is manufactured after the fact. The infant's cry counts as consciousness of guilt, and the fourteen-year-old is bribed and intimidated into re-signing at an age when the law would let no one bind him to anything smaller. This is a loose rhyme with the problem of tacit consent in thomas-hobbes and jean-jacques-rousseau, but Butler's mode is mockery, not theory. The arrangement also moves responsibility away from those who acted, which loosely echoes bureaucratic-diffusion-of-responsibility.

Layer 3 — the gods: the deed, not the motive

The Erewhonians' real gods of physical law punish the act regardless of intent. The air-god drowns a man whether he fell in or went in to save a child, "even as the English law, which assumes itself to be known to every one" (butler-erewhon-ch16-arowhena). A sect carries the principle beyond death, rewarding the healthy, handsome and strong and torturing the sickly whatever their conduct. The majority reject it, interestingly, because it would cheapen this life (butler-erewhon-ch17-ydgrun-and-the-ydgrunites).

Layer 4 — money and the body: fortune as rank

The reverse side of punishing misfortune is honouring good fortune. Men are classed by horse-power, the foot-pounds their money could raise (butler-erewhon-ch09-to-the-metropolis). Money is "the sacrament of having done for mankind that which mankind wanted", and the narrator, changed by Erewhon, endorses the idea (butler-erewhon-ch20-what-they-mean-by-it). The body is checked first. The narrator is stripped and examined before anything else is asked of him (butler-erewhon-ch07-first-impressions), and the Government's conditions for sending him on to the capital include light hair, blue eyes and "no suspicion of ill health" (butler-erewhon-ch08-in-prison).

Why the layers matter together

No single note says this. The inversion is not one satirical joke repeated but a closed system: the court needs the myth, the myth is backed by the gods, and wealth and the body supply the visible tally. That closure is what makes it hard to argue with. The narrator, carried along by the court's confidence, admits that judge, jury and prisoner alike cannot "emancipate" themselves from "the bondage of the ideas in which he had been born and bred", and concludes that justice "is relative" (butler-erewhon-ch12-malcontents). The note ties this to normative-self-blindness. The system's internal critics, the malcontents, argue mostly from "men's pockets", and they would still flog and hang, "but pitifully".

The system is also usable as a tool. When the authorities want the narrator gone over his watch, they charge him with measles, a disease that "could be sufficiently punished in a person of my age" (butler-erewhon-ch28-escape). By that point the doctrine of desert is plainly a pretext available for any purpose.

What it costs

Punishing illness does not abolish it; it hides it. People paint their faces, families keep secret medicine-chests, and it becomes "impossible to say whether any one was well or ill" (butler-erewhon-ch14-mahaina). Childbirth is concealed as a disreputable illness (butler-erewhon-ch13-views-concerning-death). That dynamic, which Erewhon shares with its other prohibitions, is traced in prohibition-breeds-evasion-butler.

World Machines note (hedged)

The reading notes repeatedly offer a loose rhyme with Legibility: the body and its failures are classified into offences whose categories override causes and individuals. Every note hedges it. Butler is satirising moral jurisprudence and respectability, not building a model of state measurement. See butler-erewhon for the book as a whole.
