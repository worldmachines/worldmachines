Censorship, connivance and insinuated doctrine (Morley)

Commons · Synthesis · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Read across the Morley notes, the old regime's censorship was neither tyranny nor efficient control but a porous, inconsistent mixture of surveillance and connivance, and that specific mixture shaped the Encyclopædia's form (insinuation, cross-reference, public form against private mind) and, on Morley's own evidence, bent the philosophers' habits of truth-telling.

This synthesis draws on Volume I (Preface, Chapters I–VIII) of john-morley's study diderot-and-the-encyclopaedists; Volume II material may extend it in later sections. Each note listed below makes a local point about censorship, police, the book trade or the Encyclopædia's text. None of them states the connection argued here, which is the synthesis's own and is offered as an interpretation of Morley's evidence, not as Morley's thesis.

The local arguments

  • A readable city. Morley explains how a police agent could seize the unpublished Sceptic's Walk: mid-century Paris was “more like a very large provincial town”, divided into definite worlds, and censorship itself bred whispering that “let the police into as many secrets as they choose to know”. The copyist Glenat turned out to be a spy, and Sartine laughed at Diderot's remonstrance (morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-3).
  • Arbitrary repression with patronage exits. Diderot's 1749 imprisonment came by lettre de cachet, which Morley sets against English prosecution by law; yet a kinswoman's letter moved him from dungeon to château, and the booksellers' interest secured his release (morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-3).
  • An incoherent government. The authorities suppressed the first two volumes in 1752 and then asked the editors to resume; they withdrew the privilege in 1759 while fifty compositors kept setting the book. Morley calls this the incoherence of a government acting by momentary calculation, from lack of tenacity and conviction rather than mere arbitrariness (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-3).
  • Connivance in the machinery itself. Sophie Voland's letters to Diderot were routed through Damilaville's revenue office, which Morley reads as connivance between authority and its foes (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-1).
  • A leaking market. Hawkers turned pirate publishers; prohibition raised prices and demand (“Good, another edition!”); the government, seeing foreign printing as an economic leak, chose to print Bayle at home (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-4).
  • Exemplary cruelty at the edges. An apprentice and a colporteur were pilloried, branded and sent to the galleys over copies of a banned book, which Diderot ties to anger at Voltaire's article Tyrant (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-2).
  • Censorship from inside the trade. Le Breton, anticipating the state, secretly cut the final volumes and burned the proofs (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-4).
  • The text's techniques. D'Alembert conceded the bad theology articles and blamed the censors; Diderot's method was to state a national prejudice respectfully and overturn it through cross-references to articles with solid principles (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-2). The Aius-Locutius article proposes that writing against government and religion be forbidden in the vulgar tongue but allowed in a learned one; the theology articles keep “formal respect” with “veiled and secret sarcasms”, and Diderot's true mind on Christianity appears only in a private letter (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-1).
  • The cost to the writer. The Liberty article contradicts Diderot's 1756 letter to Landois, and Morley's only explanation is that he wrote what authority required. Its demand that denial of liberty be punished by the magistrates is, Morley notes, exactly the Jesuits' reasoning about attacks on God and the Church (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-3).

The connection

Taken one at a time, these notes describe either a repressive state or clever writers. Taken together they describe a specific regime of control: one that could see a great deal (informers, a small and legible city, a privileged guild of booksellers) but could not or would not hold a line, because its own officers, revenue offices, patrons, publishers and ministers' economic calculations kept opening exits. Surveillance was strong; enforcement was intermittent and negotiable; punishment, when it came, fell exemplarily on the weakest links, the colporteur rather than the editor.

That combination, more than censorship as such, explains the Encyclopædia's characteristic form. A regime that read everything but enforced inconsistently rewarded a text that could pass a reading: articles of formal respect, dissent placed in the cross-reference apparatus, heterodoxy confined to a learned register or a digression on a Roman minor god, the real views kept for private letters. The insinuated doctrine Morley describes is the shape of writing for a censor who will read but can be outwaited. It also explains why the enterprise survived suppression: the same porousness that made dissimulation necessary made continuation possible.

Morley's own evidence adds a further, less comfortable step. The dissimulation did not stay on the surface of the text. In the Liberty article the habit of writing what authority required merges with Diderot's habit of measuring conclusions by their social effect, and the philosopher ends up reproducing the incumbent's own argument for punishing opinion. Morley condemns the mystifications as odious while, with Condorcet, putting the shame on the ecclesiastics and ministers whose tyranny made them necessary (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-2, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-3). Read together, the notes suggest the regime shaped not only how the philosophers wrote but, in places, how they reasoned.

World Machines resonances, with the notes' hedges

The police passage loosely rhymes with Legibility: a state whose surveillance depends on the city being readable, though Morley describes a police practice and does not theorise state legibility (morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-3). The cross-reference technique reads as a legibility game between a censoring incumbent and writers who encode dissent, and it touches illegibility-as-elite-defense only from the opposite side, since here the authors, not the elite, work by indirection (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-2). The leaking book market shows an incumbent control apparatus failing to seal the market rather than an elite hiding things, and is partly a story about the printing trade (compare eisenstein-printing-revolution) (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-4). Morley's verdict on Louis XV's government rhymes with the decay of an incumbent order in gramsci-gap terms, but he describes a monarchy's confusion, not an interregnum (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-3).

Volume II: anonymity, flight and the bishops' memorials

This section draws on Volume II (morley-diderot-v2), which adds evidence on both sides of the mixture. Surveillance still bit. When L'Esprit was prosecuted in 1758, the censor who passed it was cashiered and Helvétius lost a post in the Queen's household; yet the persecution brought him vogue and the Encyclopædic circle's sympathy (morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1). The System of Nature appeared under the name of a dead Academy secretary, and Diderot, rumoured to be its author, fled toward the frontier (morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-1). Morley generalises that the French insurgents suppressed authorial vanity, as the Jesuits did, because of their constant need for secrecy: the System of Nature, Voltaire's denied pieces, Turgot's anonymity in the Encyclopædia and Diderot's unpublished masterpieces (morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-1). Some works never faced the censor at all: The Nun circulated among a few friends (morley-diderot-v2-ch02-romance-1), and Diderot thought he had burned the only copy of D'Alembert's Dream (morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-2). By the 1780s the repressive side looked spent. The bishops' memorials against Voltaire's Kehl edition and Raynal's History asked for the 1757 death penalty and settled for imprisonment, fines and spying, and Morley's answer is that “the only remedy for a moral disease is a moral remedy” and that the old methods were “impossible for ever” (morley-diderot-v2-ch08-closing-years-2). Read with Volume I's evidence, this extends the page's claim: the porous regime produced not only insinuation within published texts but a large literature of anonymity, flight and private circulation.
