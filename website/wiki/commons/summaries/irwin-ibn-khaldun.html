Ibn Khaldun: An Intellectual Biography — Robert Irwin (2018)

Commons · Summaries · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Robert Irwin's Ibn Khaldun: An Intellectual Biography (2018) argues that the author of the Muqaddima was a man of his Maghribi, Maliki, Sufi, occult-believing and Mamluk-court world rather than a proto-modern sociologist, and treats the long reception history as a sequence of readers remaking him in their own image.

Author: robert-irwin · Subject: ibn-khaldun · Princeton University Press, 2018

Irwin's biography opens by collecting the superlatives (Toynbee, Trevor-Roper, Hodgson, Gellner's “superb inductive sociologist”) and Ibn Khaldun's own claim to have founded “an entirely original science”, and then declares the conventional account “at best only partially true” (irwin-ibn-khaldun-preface). His programme is to resist the nineteenth- and twentieth-century habit of recasting Ibn Khaldun as a precursor of Machiavelli, Hobbes, Montesquieu, Vico, Marx, Weber and Durkheim. In Irwin's view the Muqaddima's world had more in common with the Qur’an and The Thousand and One Nights than with modern sociology: causation rests on God's will, social organization aims at salvation, and angels and demons affect battles and prophecy. Irwin refuses to make Ibn Khaldun relevant; his strangeness is the point, reading him is like meeting “a visitor from another planet”, and “the contrasts matter more than the comparisons”.

The book is therefore two books braided together: a life and thought of a fourteenth-century Maghribi scholar-politician read inside his own frame, and a reception history in which almost every reader finds an Ibn Khaldun in their own image. Irwin's closing verdict is that previous accounts “have exaggerated Ibn Khaldun's rationality and posited an essentially secular frame of mind” by lopping off his Maliki jurisprudence, occultism, futurology and bizarre scientific ideas (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch11-ending-up).

Structure, chapter by chapter

Preface. The chorus of praise, a brisk life (courts of Fez, Tunis, Tlemcen and Granada; a castle retreat in western Algeria and the first draft of the Muqaddima; Mamluk Egypt as Maliki chief qadi; the meeting with Timur outside Damascus), the Muqaddima's diagnosis of why historians err (partisanship, gullibility, ignorance of what is intrinsically possible; the zahir of events against their batin), a catalogue of the unresolved modern questions, and the anti-modernization charge. Patricia Crone's warning that one must think away modernity to understand premodern societies frames the method (irwin-ibn-khaldun-preface).

Chapter 1: ruins and al-Mas‘udi. The tale of the City of Brass introduces Ibn Khaldun's central preoccupation, the ruins of North Africa, read as ‘ibra (warning, admonition), the Qur’anic concept behind the title Kitab al-‘Ibar. Ibn Khaldun “took moral messages” from ruins rather than approaching them as an archaeologist. The Black Death of 1348, which killed his parents and teachers, is framed as the shock that made him reflect on how the world had changed (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch01-ruins-black-death). Al-Mas‘udi is the admired predecessor he had to attack: Ibn Khaldun dismisses the marvels with common sense and a feel for inherent probability, but Irwin defends mirabilia as literature and catalogues Ibn Khaldun's own “weird science” (the sun neither hot nor cold, spontaneous generation, contradictory plague theories). Ruin and dynastic fall have a double causation, natural development and God's decree, which Irwin likens to overdetermination (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch01-masudi-iram-weird-science).

Chapter 2: the Maghribi political world. Post-Almohad struggle among Hafsids, Merinids and ‘Abd al-Wadids, unreliable tribal levies, no primogeniture; a geography largely borrowed from al-Idrisi that reads “more like a comprehensive encyclopedia than a closely focused thesis”; and an early career as a “bureaucratic condottiere” who changed sides, plotted and was imprisoned (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch02-topography-early-career). Ibn al-Khatib, the Granadan vizier-poet-Sufi, is “perhaps the single most influential person in Ibn Khaldun's life”; his murder and that of Ibn Khaldun's brother Yahya close a chapter whose “nightmarish turbulence” the Muqaddima barely mentions. Irwin allows, hedged, that the Muqaddima may have been written to understand political failure (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch02-ibn-al-khatib-spain-return).

Chapter 3: nomads, ‘asabiyya, wisdom literature. The Muqaddima was drafted at Qal‘at Banu Salama among the Awlad ‘Arif by a man who had bribed and led tribesmen. ‘Asabiyya, the group feeling that drives toward royal authority and is “one of God's tools”, powers a three-to-four-generation dynastic cycle that works for the Almoravids and Almohads but not for the long-lived Merinids, Hafsids or ‘Abd al-Wadids, and whose importance Irwin thinks modern readings have overstressed (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch03-nomads-asabiyya). Irwin locates the moral engine in an inherited trope of austere early Arab conquerors corrupted by luxury: “this kind of moralizing, more than a newly invented sociology”. He also shows the key terms (badawi, ‘Arab) to be slippery, the Banu Hilal catastrophe to be partly legend, and genealogy to be a socially useful fiction (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch03-bedouin-berbers-genealogy). The Muqaddima partly belongs to mirrors-for-princes and pseudo-Aristotelian wisdom literature (the Sirr al-asrar, the Circle of Justice), while the ‘Ibar is conventional, weakly chronological and only implicitly illustrates the laws; set against Froissart, Ibn Khaldun has “the perhaps delusive appearance of modernity” (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch03-wisdom-literature-ibar).

Chapter 4: philosophy, theology, law, and cycles. The Muqaddima's “philosophy” is hikma, wisdom and advice literature, not falsafa; Ibn Khaldun disowned the philosophers, preferred al-Ghazali to Averroes, and took from Averroes a mood (pessimism) rather than a doctrine. Irwin then sets the Khaldunian cycle in a long family of pessimistic cyclical histories: the Qur’anic term for every nation, dawla as a “turn”, al-Kindi and the Ikhwan al-Safa’, Crone's Confucian contrast, Polybius, giambattista-vico, Spengler, Mann's Buddenbrooks, Mahfouz's Harafish and Valéry (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch04-philosophy-cyclical-history). His Aristotle was largely pseudo-Aristotle; theology is Ash‘arite and occasionalist, if inconsistently; and Maliki jurisprudence is his home discipline and the model for the principles of history. Irwin endorses Gibb (“It is hard to contest”) that the cyclical pessimism is religious and moral, not sociological (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch04-aristotle-theology-maliki-law).

Chapter 5: Egypt, Timur, the Ta‘rif. In Mamluk Cairo Ibn Khaldun rose under Barquq through professorships, Maliki judgeships and Sufi posts, signed a fatwa against his patron in 1389, and kept expanding the Muqaddima, including with occult material. He praised the mamluk institution as an artificially reinforced ‘asabiyya, yet described Egypt as free of tribes, which Irwin calls “nonsense” given the tribes in the 1389 civil war (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch05-arrival-in-egypt). The survey of offices shows posts that grow or shrink with holder and regime; the thirty-five days with Timur mix curiosity, astrological prophecy, flattery and self-preservation, and Irwin keeps Ibn Khaldun's claims apart from Ibn ‘Arabshah's hostile embroidery (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch05-office-holding-timur). The Ta‘rif is a reticent curriculum vitae, not a confession; in Cairo he was an unpopular outsider, praised by al-Maqrizi, who kept the moral and missed the method, and slandered by rivals whose charges Irwin weighs sceptically (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch05-autobiography-outsider).

Chapter 6: Sufism. Circumstantial evidence “overwhelmingly” indicates that Ibn Khaldun was a Sufi, but a jurist's Sufi who wanted mysticism under quasi-legal supervision and who later called for the books of Ibn al-‘Arabi and Ibn Sab‘in to be burned. Irwin rejects Fromherz's claim that the Muqaddima's philosophy of history is a Sufi historiography: in Ibn Khaldun a perished dynasty is replaced, not reborn (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch06-sufi-mystic).

Chapter 7: the occult and the future. Sorcery, the evil eye and dreams are real but forbidden, alchemy and treasure hunting are frauds harmful to society, and letter magic is credited but obscure; sorcery, however, cannot interfere with the processes of history (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-supernatural-occult). Irwin's claim is that the interest in sorcery was really an interest in knowing the future. The Muqaddima's laws “provided no exact guidance to what would happen next”; Ibn Khaldun saw no progress and expected the End of Time; even the Mahdi “would need the support of ‘asabiyya”. The chapter ends with a faint Toynbee-mediated line into Asimov's psychohistory (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-future-last-days).

Chapter 8: economics. Against Reagan's Laffer-curve citation and Marxist appropriations, Irwin argues that Ibn Khaldun “was not an economist in the modern sense”: moral judgment outweighed the observed movement of money and goods, economic theory “slides into occult science”, and the tax spiral follows, rather than causes, political and moral decline. What is distinctive is that only he, among Mamluk-era writers on taxation and coinage, set such moralizing inside a science of society, ‘ilm al-‘umran (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch08-economics).

Chapter 9: teaching and style. Ibn Khaldun spent more of his life teaching than anything else, in a culture where “originality was at a discount”. He preferred oral transmission, distrusted commentaries, valued memorization as soul-shaping, and wrote the plain, repetitive, abstract prose of a lecturer, with Qur’anic invocations as “an extended form of punctuation” (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch09-teaching-writing).

Chapter 10: the afterlife. No school of Khaldunian historians followed. Seventeenth-century Ottomans read him as a diagnostic of imperial ageing but hoped decline could be arrested; Hammer-Purgstall's “Arab Montesquieu” stuck; De Slane's translation, commissioned by the French Ministry of War, rendered ‘asabiyya as “esprit de corps”; Gautier turned the nomad-settled contrast into a colonial verdict; the Germans read him through Hegel (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-afterlife-french-germans). Toynbee made him known in English as a “long-lost relative”; Gibb, Hourani and Hodgson inherited or reworked the reading; Rosenthal's translation is defended as the best substitute for a missing critical edition (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-toynbee-rosenthal). Lacoste's Marxist, Mahdi's Straussian and Gellner's secular sociologist are each judged anachronistic, with Crone's Confucian contrast as a sharper foil and the Nahda's rediscovery as a re-export from European editions (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-colonialist-mahdi-gellner). Arab scholars, Fromherz and Dale, and the novels of Himmich and Herbert's Dune follow, and Irwin argues against Bruce Lawrence that Khaldunian scholarship is a product of many agendas, not simply of colonial Orientalism (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-arab-american-novels-orientalism).

Chapter 11: ending up. Against H. A. L. Fisher's plotless history, Ibn Khaldun drew laws from bloody contingency. Irwin admits Ibn Khaldun's inconsistencies and his own failures to understand parts of him, suspects the Muqaddima's ideal audience was its author, grants that the models can be compared to those of Darwin, Marx and Durkheim though they “cannot apply to modern societies”, and suggests, tentatively, that the book's ultimate purpose was to prepare Muslims for the Last Judgment (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch11-ending-up).

Throughlines

Religious frame over proto-sociology. The purpose of history is ‘ibra; the principles of history were modeled on the principles of jurisprudence; the cycle is driven by infractions of the Shari‘a through pride, luxury and greed. Irwin keeps this against every secularizing reading, while granting one measured concession: Ibn Khaldun's capacity to reason abstractly and generalize about society makes him “almost unique among medieval historians” (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch04-aristotle-theology-maliki-law). See muqaddima-enchanted-frame-irwin.

‘Asabiyya and the dynastic cycle, bounded. Irwin presents the theory seriously and then limits it: induced from the Caliphate, Almoravids and Almohads, exempting the eastern lands and the Mamluks, inconsistently applied, and overstressed by modern readers. See asabiyya-irwin.

Reception as remaking. From al-Maqrizi through the Ottomans, De Slane, Gautier, Von Kremer, Toynbee, Gibb, Hodgson, Mahdi, Lacoste, Gellner, Reagan and Engels, each reader keeps what suits and drops the rest. Irwin names the mechanism (disciplines' hunger for an ancestor, the self-perpetuating gravity of a heavily studied subject) and includes himself among possible offenders. See terminal-medievals-vs-early-moderns-irwin.

Three voices kept apart. Irwin distinguishes Ibn Khaldun's claims, his own judgments (often hedged: “perhaps”, “it seems to me”, “I believe”) and the claims of the scholars he reports, whether he endorses them (Gibb, Crone) or disputes them (Gellner, Mahdi, Fromherz, Lawrence). See robert-irwin.

World Machines resonances, with Irwin's hedges

  • The dynastic cycle (conquest, luxury, loss of group feeling, fiscal extortion, fall) rhymes with civilization-undone-by-its-own-success, secular-cycles and linear-vs-cyclical-paths. But it runs over three or four generations on a tribal-military mechanism, not a roughly thousand-year infrastructure lifecycle of the kind in world-machines, and Irwin insists its engine is moral and theological (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch03-nomads-asabiyya, irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch04-aristotle-theology-maliki-law).
  • Ruins that survive as warnings loosely echo the ornamental and allegorical stages of the decay-sequence, though Ibn Khaldun's ruins are theological messages, not a stage in an infrastructural typology (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch01-ruins-black-death).
  • The reception history is a sustained case for the retrofitting problem in terminal-medievals-vs-early-moderns and a caution for WMP's own use of ibn-khaldun: resonances are resonances, not anticipations (irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch11-ending-up).
  • For psychohistory-and-world-machines, Irwin's Ibn Khaldun offers laws without forecasts, a future handed to divination, and a “ghostly” influence on Asimov through Toynbee. See psychohistory-and-world-machines-irwin.
