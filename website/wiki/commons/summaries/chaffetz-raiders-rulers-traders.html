Raiders, Rulers, and Traders — David Chaffetz (2024)

Commons · Summaries · updated 23 September 2026 · source on GitHub
David Chaffetz's Raiders, Rulers, and Traders (2024) argues that the breeding, riding, and trading of the horse — a strategic resource on the order of petroleum — is the hidden mechanism behind the rise and eclipse of Eurasia's land empires from the Bronze Age to 1919.

Source: David Chaffetz, Raiders, Rulers, and Traders: The Horse and the Rise of Empires (W. W. Norton, 2024). Ingested as fourteen L1 reading notes, one per chapter plus prologue and epilogue.

A single-cause history of Eurasia, argued at full length: that the domestication, breeding, riding, and trading of the horse is the hidden mechanism behind the rise, spread, and eclipse of the continent's great land empires, from the Bronze Age to the First World War.

The thesis

Chaffetz's governing analogy is stated in the first pages of chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-prologue and never abandoned: riding converted the horse from livestock into "a strategic asset" on the order of petroleum in the twentieth century. Two consequences follow, and between them they organize the book.

The first is an asymmetry. Steppe peoples were a demographic fraction of settled Eurasia but "controlled half of the world's horses," because only the steppe had pasture at the scale horse-breeding requires. Settled civilizations — China, Iran, India — were therefore structurally short of the one resource that decided land warfare, and had to buy it, hire its handlers, or plant client steppe nations on their frontiers.

The second is a dependency loop. Because the deficit could not be closed by will, settled empires repeatedly reached for steppe horse power to solve internal problems and then found they had imported a force they could not dismiss. Zhou courtiers hire the Dog Rong to sack their own capital and cannot get them to leave (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch04-heavenly-horses); Assyria draws its cavalry from Median breeders on its frontier and is destroyed by them in 612 BCE (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch03-engines-of-empire); the Tang staff their stud farms with Turkish grooms on a massive scale (more than a million Turks are thought to have immigrated into China as grooms or cavalrymen) and are broken by An Lushan, himself a half-Turk groom risen to command (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch06-equine-mania). The pattern is stated as doctrine in chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch03-engines-of-empire: cavalry and empire are "like the chicken and the egg."

Two paired quotations, curated by Chaffetz precisely because they converge across sixteen centuries and thousands of miles, do the work of a thesis statement — the Han general Ma Yuan's "horses are the foundation of military power, the great resources of the state," and the Mughal general Firuz Jang's "Without him, no sovereignty could be erected."

The arc

Origins (prologue, ch. 1–2). The horse survives the Ice Age extinctions only by an accident of habitat, clinging on in the "drier, colder Eurasian steppe" after being hunted out in North America and Europe. Domestication proceeds through hunting, corralling, milking, and only very late riding — and remains, in Chaffetz's repeated formulation, "only skin-deep." Mare's milk, not meat or war, is the hinge that ratchets human dependence upward. Riding itself is a technology the written record never noticed: it has to be reconstructed from arthritic lumbar vertebrae in horses and elongated thigh bones in riders, because no contemporary Sumerian or Akkadian text even distinguishes horse from donkey (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch01-domesticated-for-milk). Then two military revolutions in sequence — the chariot, invented on the steppe and adopted by settled elites as an aristocratic dueling instrument, and its eclipse by the gelded, bow-armed mounted archer, which was cheap, scalable, and plebeian (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch02-horses-for-heroes).

The imperial horse economies (ch. 3–6). Cyrus, having watched horse-dependency kill both Assyria and Media, makes Persian self-sufficiency in horses "an ideological imperative" and builds the first state stud-farm bureaucracy: three royal farms, uniform-colored herds, an imperial brand, a post-horse relay. The Mauryas replicate the achievement against monsoon India's ecology and against a genuine rival technology, the elephant. Han China never solves the problem at all — its very word for warhorse, rong ma, means "western foreigners' horse" — and its attempt at legible market control, a border monopsony, hands Modun the blueprint for history's first steppe empire. chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch05-silk-road-or-horse-road then argues the book's sharpest historiographic correction: the trade route was a horse road, not a silk road. The Tang build the largest cavalry establishment ever attempted — seven hundred thousand horses, ninety thousand remounts a year, fifty-eight stud farms — and simultaneously aestheticize the horse into dancing spectacle, a soft power that "could not save his reign."

Conquest (ch. 7–9). The mass encirclement hunt (nerge, or battue) is the steppe's war college, and skilled chiefs convert its hospitality and cohesion into followings and then into states — Khitans, Seljuks, Ghaznavids (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch07-hunting-for-supremacy). Genghis Khan is presented not as an innovator from nothing but as a synthesist who studied his predecessors' failures and built an apparatus to prevent them, with pasture allocation as the medium of control. His empire's limits are literally ecological: Hülegü is stopped at Syria because it "lacked sufficient grass and water for the Mongols' one hundred thousand horses" (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch08-genghis-khan-empire). By Timur's era the steppe army has "lost its low-cost status," and horse power begins to compel conquest rather than merely enable it — Timur's troops paid in ulufat, named from the word for alfalfa; Aurangzeb's Deccan campaigns as a "perpetual-motion machine"; Nader Shah sacking Delhi and still going bankrupt, having "done nothing more than ride the whirlwind" (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch09-riding-the-whirlwind).

Closure (ch. 10–12, epilogue). Qing China and Muscovite Russia finally do what no settled power had managed: subordinate the horse-breeders permanently — the Qing by combining famine relief for compliant Mongols with the annihilation of the Oirats, the Russians by absorbing steppe method wholesale through the Cossacks and outwaiting steppe fragmentation (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch10-empires-strike-back). In India, Britain wins paramountcy with money rather than horseflesh, beginning with five hundred cavalry against forces in the hundreds of thousands — then discovers it has inherited the Mughal horse-supply problem along with the Mughal position, and the Great Game follows from that shortage (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch11-the-great-game). The 1881 Treaty of St Petersburg draws the first border ever across the steppe. Colonial breeding programs then fail on both sides (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch12-last-horse-powers), imperial cavalry pageantry peaks at the 1911 Delhi durbar, and within a decade the RAF over Kabul settles a war that Skinner's Horse had ridden out to fight. The chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-epilogue closes at a royal buzkashi match in mid-century Kabul, with Afghanistan as the last refuge of a horse culture that Soviet collectivization and Iranian sedentarization had extinguished everywhere else.

Throughlines worth tracking

  • The steppe as a place, not a people. Its scale (3.1 million square miles) and ecology set the terms for everything on its shores, the way the Mediterranean did for its own. See the-eurasian-steppe.
  • The horse trade as the actual continental infrastructure. Caravans functioning as itinerant states, fairs seeding pilgrimage sites, silk serving as currency rather than cargo. See the-horse-road.
  • Legibility applied to horseflesh, and its limits. Every settled power converges on the same bureaucratic instrument and every one of them keeps failing at the same point. See stud-farms-and-the-limits-of-legibility.
  • Contingent loyalty as the standing weakness of steppe polities. "If they so chose, horsemen could simply saddle up and abandon their chiefs" — the same autonomy that made steppe peoples unconquerable made their khanates impermanent, and Russia built two centuries of policy on the difference (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch10-empires-strike-back).
  • The self-consuming conquest cycle. Steppe elites take settled land, sinicize or Persianize within a few generations, lose touch with the breeders who put them there, and are displaced by a fresher wave — Khitans by Jurchens, Jurchens by Mongols (chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch07-hunting-for-supremacy).

Method and evidence

Chaffetz is explicit that the book is possible now because of an evidentiary convergence: post-Soviet opening of Russian, Ukrainian, Kazakh and Mongol scholarship, improved carbon dating (the Sintashta burials were dated conclusively only after 1992), and paleogenomic analysis of horse and human skeletons. Three kinds of evidence recur — osteopathic trauma patterns, DNA (the Kushan-era collapse in equine Y-chromosome diversity is traced to buyers wanting foals that resembled famous sires), and forage arithmetic (an elephant's 330 pounds a day against a horse's 20).

He also uses two deliberate negative cases as controls. Western Europe, where a knight class was one to two percent of the population and sea power drove empire, and the North American plains, where the horse arrived late and had two centuries to work with against Eurasia's four millennia. Both are there to isolate what was distinctive about the steppe's horse ecology rather than to treat horse culture as inevitable.

Where this touches the World Machines frame

The book supplies an unusually complete worked example of infrastructure-first history (infrastructure-as-political) running on a non-fossil energy base — grass converted to muscle, with pasture as the hard ceiling on imperial reach. See machines-as-energy-regimes-chaffetz, which extends machines-as-energy-regimes with the book's horse-power material.

It also supplies a two-and-a-half-millennium series of legibility projects aimed at a single resource, with a consistent failure mode, which bears directly on legibility-as-machine-core and on the sharper claim of legibility-as-precondition-not-cause.

Its final chapters bear on the modernity-machine page's dating of the steppe's eclipse (see modernity-machine-chaffetz), and its imperial-pageantry material supplies fresh candidate instances for decay-sequence (see decay-sequence-chaffetz). The steppe peoples themselves are one of the corpus's cleanest historical cases of capture-resistance — Turkmen settling desert margins specifically "to avoid paying taxes," Yomuts fleeing wholesale into Iran rather than surrender horses to a requisition, Kazakh herders slow-walking Russian covering stations by sending pregnant or immature mares.

And the horse itself, in chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch01-domesticated-for-milk's account, is a small Conatus-shaped case in its own right: an animal whose domestication was never deep enough to complete, demonstrated by Przewalski foals rewilded in 2008 who "quickly lost their willingness to socialize with humans" once their herd reached two thousand.
