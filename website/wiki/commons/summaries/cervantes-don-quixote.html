Don Quixote

Commons · Summaries · updated 23 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Cervantes's two-part novel (1605/1615) in John Ormsby's 1885 translation: a Manchegan hidalgo reads himself into a dead institution and rides out into a world that no longer runs on it, until Part II turns his own printed history into a thing other people stage against him.

Work: Miguel de Cervantes Saavedra, Don Quixote of La Mancha — Part I (1605), Part II (1615).
Edition read: John Ormsby's 1885 English translation, with Ormsby's own prefatory apparatus.
Author page: miguel-de-cervantes. Narrator-device page: cide-hamete-benengeli. Squire page: sancho-panza.

An elderly La Mancha hidalgo reads chivalric romances until "his brains got so dry that he lost his wits," renames himself Don Quixote, and rides out to practise a profession that no longer exists. The book that follows is at once the funniest and the most systematic account in early modern literature of what happens when a person keeps operating an institution's rules after the institution has stopped running — and then, in Part II, of what happens when that person's own printed history becomes a thing other people can read, stage, and weaponise against him.

What this edition puts in front of the novel

Ormsby's volume withholds the story behind four pieces of apparatus, and each does real work.

  • A survey of the English translations from Shelton (1612) through Motteux, Jervas and Smollett, judged by one standard: does the version keep Cervantes's "unsmiling gravity" or substitute the translator's own comic register? Ormsby's fiercest charge — that Motteux's "Franco-cockney" comic inflation is "an absolute falsification of the spirit of the book" — is also his statement of method, and his defence of the maligned, wooden Jervas is a defence of deadpan as fidelity (cervantes-don-quixote-front-introduction).
  • A biography in two parts: ancestry, Lepanto and five years' captivity in Algiers (cervantes-don-quixote-front-cervantes-life-1); then the failed dramatist, the tax-collector's road, the accidental gathering of the novel's material, Avellaneda's forged sequel, and death in 1616 (cervantes-don-quixote-front-cervantes-life-2).
  • Ormsby's critical essay on the book's reception, composition and humour (cervantes-don-quixote-front-essay-don-quixote).
  • Cervantes's own Preface — a staged dialogue in which a friend supplies a recipe for faking scholarly authority — followed by mock commendatory verses spoken by the very romance characters the book will demolish, and a straight-faced dedication to the Duke of Béjar (cervantes-don-quixote-front-preface-commendatory-dedication).

Ormsby's essay supplies the historical premise the whole novel stands on, and it is sharper than the usual story that Cervantes killed chivalry. Chivalry, he argues, was already dead: "essentially republican in its nature," it could not survive the fall of Granada and Ferdinand's stripping of "the free institutions of mediaeval Spain." What Cervantes swept away was its costume afterlife — cavaliers "prancing and curvetting before the windows of their mistresses" — and the evidence he cites is Don Felix Pacheco's testimony that after the book appeared, any man still in that drapery was mocked as a Don Quixote himself.

Structure

Part I (1605) runs three movements. A first solo sally ends in a mock dubbing at an inn and a beating on the road home; the household then puts the library on trial and burns most of it. A second sally, now with Sancho Panza, produces the canonical set pieces — windmills, sheep-armies, the funeral procession, the fulling mills, Mambrino's basin, the freed galley slaves — before retreat into the Sierra Morena, where the novel changes shape: Cardenio's and Dorothea's interlocking betrayals, a rescue plot staged in costume, and a return to the inn that becomes the book's great convergence chamber. There the interpolated novella "The Ill-Advised Curiosity" is read aloud, four lovers are reconciled, a ransomed Captive tells a tale modelled on Cervantes's own Algiers years, and a Canon of Toledo delivers the novel's realist poetics ("fiction is all the better the more it looks like truth") while Don Quixote is carted home in a cage.

Part II (1615), written under the shadow of a promised, deliberate ending (cervantes-don-quixote-transition-part2-dedication-preface), inverts the premise. Don Quixote's history is now in print, and everyone he meets has read it. The third sally therefore runs less on his imposition of a world than on other people's productions staged for him: Samson Carrasco's two disguised duels, the Duke and Duchess's month-long theatrical residency, Sancho's fictional governorship of Barataria, Barcelona's staged civic welcome and its mechanical oracle. It ends with a defeat honoured under the code that produced it, a homeward road, the recovery of reason, and death.

Throughlines

A frame that no evidence can reach. The novel's engine is not misperception but absorption. When the sheep turn out to be sheep and the teeth are gone, Don Quixote does not revise — he adds an enchanter (cervantes-don-quixote-p1-18-20). The barber's basin is a helmet by a theory of "contraries" (cervantes-don-quixote-p1-25-26); the friends walking beside the cage only wear the likeness of his friends (cervantes-don-quixote-p1-46-48); Samson Carrasco's own face under a lifted visor is enchanters' work (cervantes-don-quixote-p2-13-16); a lion that yawns and turns away is a victory, certified by affidavit (cervantes-don-quixote-p2-17-19). Sancho keeps offering the falsification test — follow the sheep, enchanted men do not eat or sleep — and it is never honoured. The cost of this closure is not only comic: Andres, "rescued" from a flogging, returns to report that the flogging resumed the moment the knight rode off, and curses "all the knights-errant that have ever been born" (cervantes-don-quixote-p1-29-32).

Two orders sharing one field. Again and again the comedy is a collision of legibility regimes over the same material world: castle versus inn, settled by the innkeeper's "all I care about is to get my money" (cervantes-don-quixote-p1-14-17); knightly exemption versus the Holy Brotherhood's written warrant, description compared against face (cervantes-don-quixote-p1-43-45); a chivalric abduction versus a village rain-procession, resolved by a forked stick (cervantes-don-quixote-p1-52). The sharpest instance is technological: Don Quixote's lament against artillery, which lets "a base and cowardly arm" fell a nobleman from a distance he never sees, is a knight naming the exact machine that dissolved the precondition of his own honour code (cervantes-don-quixote-p1-38-40).

The double act as a single mechanism. The curate's aside that knight and squire "had both been cast in the same mould" (cervantes-don-quixote-p2-01-04) is the book's own verdict on its central pairing. Sancho is not a foil but a counter-epistemology — hunger, wages, harvest-time, proverbs — and by Part II he is also the fiction's co-author and its principal casualty. See sancho-panza.

A book made of other books. Cervantes packs the novel with genres it then criticises from inside: the pastoral (Marcela and Chrysostom, cervantes-don-quixote-p1-10-13, cervantes-don-quixote-p1-14-17; Leandra's valley of ritualised mourners, cervantes-don-quixote-p1-49-51); the Italianate novella ("The Ill-Advised Curiosity," cervantes-don-quixote-p1-33-34, cervantes-don-quixote-p1-35-37; Claudia Jeronima, cervantes-don-quixote-p2-60-62); the captivity narrative (cervantes-don-quixote-p1-38-40, cervantes-don-quixote-p1-41-42); the romance of recognition (cervantes-don-quixote-p1-27-28, cervantes-don-quixote-p1-23-24); and, in Part II, documentary testimony about the 1609 expulsion of the Moriscos, delivered first by Ricote and then by his daughter Ana Félix (cervantes-don-quixote-p2-53-56, cervantes-don-quixote-p2-63-66). The literary-critical scenes are equally in-text: the curate's inquisition of the library (cervantes-don-quixote-p1-06-09), the innkeeper's insistence that the romances are licensed history (cervantes-don-quixote-p1-29-32), the Canon of Toledo's Aristotelian brief (cervantes-don-quixote-p1-46-48, cervantes-don-quixote-p1-49-51).

The book inside the book. Part II's real innovation is that its hosts are readers. The Duke and Duchess have read Part I and script a full chivalric reception around it (cervantes-don-quixote-p2-30-32); Barcelona welcomes "the true, the legitimate, the real one" against Avellaneda's counterfeit (cervantes-don-quixote-p2-60-62); Don Quixote reroutes his actual itinerary from Saragossa to Barcelona expressly to falsify a rival book's account of him (cervantes-don-quixote-p2-57-59); and a character lifted out of Avellaneda is made to swear before a village magistrate that this Quixote is not the one he knew (cervantes-don-quixote-p2-71-74). The device this all runs through is the fictitious Moorish chronicler — see cide-hamete-benengeli.

Composition by accretion, closure by force. Ormsby reconstructs the novel as opportunistic rather than architected: Sancho enters only after a landlord's offhand remark that knights travel with squires, and the chapter divisions and found-manuscript frame were bolted on partway through (cervantes-don-quixote-front-essay-don-quixote). The ending is equally contingent. Avellaneda's 1614 forgery, abusive about Cervantes's age, poverty and maimed hand, is what broke the habit of dawdling; Cervantes announces in the Part II preface that he will show Don Quixote "dead and buried, so that no one may dare to bring forward any further evidence against him" (cervantes-don-quixote-transition-part2-dedication-preface). A canonical text is finished, and its hero killed, as an act of property enforcement against unauthorised copies.

Sanity as termination. The death chapters make the book's structure explicit: the friends read lucidity as the sign that he is dying, Sancho — now the rationalist — begs him to keep believing, and the will disinherits any niece who marries a reader of chivalric romance (cervantes-don-quixote-p2-71-74). Recovering his reason is not a cure; it is the end of the person the reason belonged to.

Where this sits in the World Machines frame

Part I appears in 1605, at the exact hinge in the canonical table between the Medieval machine's collapse and the modernity-machine's switch-on. The novel is unusually useful evidence for three project concerns, and the notes flag it repeatedly:

  • as the fullest literary case of the decay-sequence — an institution whose forms are performed as costume, spectacle and epitaph long after their function is gone;
  • as the primary literary exhibit in terminal-medievals-vs-early-moderns, with the qualification that Cervantes himself is not one;
  • and as a running comedy of Legibility regimes in contact — chivalric self-certification against warrants, registers, affidavits, notaries and expulsion edicts (legibility-as-machine-core).

Two cautions belong on this page. First, the book is a comic novel, not a treatise: the notes are careful to mark where a resonance is genuine and where it would be forced. Second, Ormsby explicitly rejects the German allegorical reading of the book as "the eternal struggle between the ideal and the real," and warns that admirers who grew "ashamed of laughing over it" invented most of its philosophy. Any WMP use of the novel has to survive that warning.
