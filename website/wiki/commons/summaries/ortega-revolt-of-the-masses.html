The Revolt of the Masses

Commons · Summaries · updated 24 September 2026 · source on GitHub
José Ortega y Gasset's 1930 diagnosis of Europe's crisis as the rise of the self-satisfied “mass-man” — a psychological type bred by the nineteenth century's own success — extended into critiques of specialist science, the interventionist State, Bolshevism and Fascism, and ending in a case for a united Europe.

Author: José Ortega y Gasset — see jose-ortega-y-gasset
Published: 1930 (Spanish original); read here in a 2012 English edition translated by Marciano Guerrero, prefaced by two essays of Guerrero's own (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-essays-introduction)

What the Book Is

The book diagnoses one phenomenon: “the coming of the masses to full social power,” which Ortega names “the rebellion of the masses” and calls “the greatest crisis that peoples, nations, and civilization can be afflicted with.” He insists from the first page that “rebellion,” “masses,” and “social power” are not narrowly political terms — the crisis is intellectual, moral, economic, and religious, reaching down to “our manner of dressing and our manner of enjoyment” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch01-the-reality-of-the-masses).

Its central figure is the mass-man, defined against the “select minority” as a psychological type rather than a social class: the mass-man “feels himself ‘just like everybody’” and is content with it, while the select man “demands more of himself than the rest” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch01-the-reality-of-the-masses). Everything after is a dissection of how this type was produced, what he does, and what he endangers.

Throughlines

The mass-man as a psychological type

Ortega's pivotal move is to strip “mass” and “elite” of any necessary tie to class: both types occur in every class, including the working class and the nobility (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch01-the-reality-of-the-masses). He later sharpens it — a man is “mass” “not so much because of his multitude as because of his inertia” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch07-noble-life-and-vulgar-life) — and the endnotes reduce it to a single mental act: the mass-man “is satisfied with thinking the first thing he finds in his head” (n. 16, ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-endnotes). See mass-man.

The century that bred him

Ortega grounds the mass-man's rise in a demographic fact borrowed from Werner Sombart — Europe's population never exceeded 180 million for twelve centuries, then rose to 460 million between 1800 and 1914 — and argues this flood outran traditional culture's capacity to form it: the schools “instructed the masses in the technique of modern life” but could not educate them (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch05-a-statistical-datum). Liberal democracy, experimental science, and industrialism freed the average man so completely that he took the result “not as an organized, but as a natural system,” yielding the spoilt child's “free expansion of his vital desires” and “radical ingratitude” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch06-the-dissection-of-the-mass-man-begins). The ideal of the rights of man, once realized, hardened into “unconscious assumption” — the mass-man simply feels himself sovereign (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch02-the-rising-of-the-historic-level).

Civilization as artifice

“It is artificial and requires the artist or the artisan”; neglect it and “in a trice you find yourself left without civilization” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch10-primitivism-and-history). The mass-man uses his motor-car as “the spontaneous fruit of an Edenic tree” — a primitive “who has slipped through the wings on to the age-old stage of civilization” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch09-primitivism-and-the-technical). Against Spengler, Ortega argues technique is “not causae sui” but a precipitate of pure science, which lives only on cultural enthusiasm; kill that and technicism survives only “for the duration of the inertia of the cultural impulse which started it.” Experimental science itself is a historical freak confined to the quadrilateral of London, Berlin, Vienna, and Paris in one century (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch09-primitivism-and-the-technical). The endnotes push this to its acute limit with Hermann Weyl's remark that the death of “ten or twelve specified individuals” would lose contemporary physics (n. 14, ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-endnotes).

From hermetism to direct action

The mass-man “regards himself as perfect” and so never submits his opinions to an external standard; since culture just is “a series of standards to which it is possible to appeal,” its absence is “barbarism,” and the mass's only political mode is “direct action” — violence as prima ratio, “the Magna Carta of barbarism,” embodied by Syndicalism and Fascism (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch08-why-the-masses-intervene-in-everything). The same structure is restated as a three-step psychology (ease → closure → direct action) and generalized into a genus of “heir-man” — the degenerate hereditary aristocrat, the spoiled child, and the mass-man as its fullest case (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch11-the-age-of-the-self-satisfied-dandy). The last chapter names the outcome as a moral vacuum rather than a transition: refusing all rules is itself a rule, “a negative moral that preserves from the other its form — an empty hole” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch15-arriving-at-the-real-issue).

Two case studies: the specialist and the State

The most prestigious modern figure, the man of science, turns out to be “the prototype of the mass-man”: mechanized, specialized research lets mediocre men produce results “as with a machine,” yielding the “learned ignoramus” who carries a specialist's petulance into every other field (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch12-the-barbarism-of-specialization). The State, built by the bourgeoisie into “a formidable machine,” tempts the mass-man with the “possibility of obtaining everything … merely by touching a button”; State intervention absorbs spontaneous social effort until “the skeleton eats up the flesh around it,” as in late Rome — with Mussolini's “All for the State” as the contemporary specimen and England's willingness to “let disorder alone” as the counter-ideal (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch13-the-greatest-danger-the-state).

Who commands in the world

Rule, for Ortega, rests on public opinion, not force — “You can do everything with bayonets, Sire, except sit on them” — and opinion is “the universal law of gravitation in political history.” Europe no longer believes it rules, smaller nations behave like children once the schoolmaster leaves, and Moscow and New York are European forms worn as camouflage rather than successor civilizations (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch14a-who-commands-in-the-world-1-3). Fashionable “decadence” is recast as capacity trapped in nation-states too small for it, and the State itself as “absolutely a work of imagination” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch14b-who-commands-in-the-world-4-6); the State is “the will to do something in common,” not blood, language, or territory (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch14c-who-commands-in-the-world-7). The book closes on Renan's “daily plebiscite” read as a future program — “we are defending our tomorrows, not our yesterdays” — a “background-Europe” assembling unnoticed beneath national rivalries, and the claim that only building Europe into “a great national State” can answer the fervor of a successful Soviet five-year plan (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch14d-who-commands-in-the-world-8-9).

Ortega's ambivalence

The book is not a simple lament. Ortega's “aristocratic” reading of history is, he insists, a claim about what a society is, not a program for what it ought to be (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch02-the-rising-of-the-historic-level). He calls liberal democracy plus technical knowledge “the highest type of public life known so far” and the mass-man “pure potency for the greatest good and for the greatest evil” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch05-a-statistical-datum); liberalism is “the noblest cry that has ever resounded in this planet” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch08-why-the-masses-intervene-in-everything); history is undetermined — “reality hesitates, walks up and down” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch09-primitivism-and-the-technical). His own age, by the only test he accepts, is not decadent: it would trade places with no past one, being “superior to other times, inferior to itself” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch03-the-altitude-of-the-times), a Regent “who had all the talents except the talent to make use of them” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch04-the-growth-of-life).

Reading Cautions

  • Guerrero is not Ortega. The influence triad (Schopenhauer, Nietzsche, Mill), the portrait of Ortega's prose as clear rather than hermetic, the claim that Ortega's aristocracy is “of the mind,” the claim that Ortega failed to foresee the United States filling Europe's vacuum, and the claim that nothing was lost in translation are all Guerrero's — the last is translator's advocacy, not evidence (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-essays-introduction).
  • Dated material. Ortega's aside that “inferior races — the pygmies, for example —” were pushed toward the tropics reflects period racial-hierarchy science and should be read as such (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch11-the-age-of-the-self-satisfied-dandy).
  • Anti-Fascist and anti-Bolshevik at once. Fascism and Syndicalism are his examples of direct action (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch08-why-the-masses-intervene-in-everything), Fascism and Bolshevism “two false dawns” of “mere primitivism” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch10-primitivism-and-history), and Mussolini's State one “admirably built up — not by him, but … by liberal democracy” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch13-the-greatest-danger-the-state).
  • A contested category. The book's “mass” is exactly the category carey-intellectuals-and-masses treats as a fiction (the-mass-as-fiction). The two readings are both held in this wiki; neither settles the other.

In World Machines Terms

  • gramsci-gap — the resonance the notes reach for most, and disagree on. The strongest statement is Ortega's own: “life today is the fruit of an interregnum, of an empty space between two organizations of historical rule” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch14d-who-commands-in-the-world-8-9); the vacuum of opinion filled provisionally by force is read as the same European interregnum Gramsci named, in idealist rather than materialist vocabulary (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch14a-who-commands-in-the-world-1-3). But the hedges matter: Ortega reads the groundlessness as exhilarating, “kids let loose from school,” not morbid (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch03-the-altitude-of-the-times); he is more voluntarist than Gramsci, thinking the interregnum resolvable by continental will (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch14d-who-commands-in-the-world-8-9); and in the moral register he explicitly denies there is any dawning order at all — the mass-man “simply lacks moral ground,” so the chapter-15 note reads his condition as pure absence rather than an interregnum (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch15-arriving-at-the-real-issue).
  • decay-sequence — shape shared, tempo and trigger differ. Technicism surviving on the inertia of a dead cultural impulse is form outliving function (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch09-primitivism-and-the-technical); but Ortega's infrastructure goes invisible at the height of its function, “naturalization as a feature of success, not decay” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch06-the-dissection-of-the-mass-man-begins), and his loss is abrupt intergenerational forgetting rather than slow drift (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch05-a-statistical-datum). See civilization-undone-by-its-own-success.
  • history-machine — both for and against. History's sea-level rising after long invisible preparation (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch02-the-rising-of-the-historic-level) and “background-Europe” assembling unnoticed through war and trade (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch14d-who-commands-in-the-world-8-9) match silent distributed assembly; but Ortega's crediting of Hegel, Comte, and Nietzsche as prophets (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch06-the-dissection-of-the-mass-man-begins) and of a “great company-promoter” for the State (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch14b-who-commands-in-the-world-4-6) is the great-men reading the concept defines itself against.
  • world-machines — partial. “Who commands in the world” is Ortega's route to something like the question of which order dominates, but his model is a single locus of command passing between blocs, not three machines in coexisting friction (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch14a-who-commands-in-the-world-1-3).
  • modernity-machine and fiat-progress. The shared faith of progressive liberalism and Marxist socialism that history guarantees a good outcome, which “cast away the rudder of history,” is diagnosed from inside the collapse of the Progress telos — as a failure of “directing minorities,” not an infrastructural regime running down (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch04-the-growth-of-life).
  • Conatus — inverted. The mass-man declines the striving to distinguish himself and is content in sameness (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch01-the-reality-of-the-masses); “all life is the struggle, the effort to be itself” (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch11-the-age-of-the-self-satisfied-dandy); and the mass-man craves capture by the State rather than resisting it (ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch13-the-greatest-danger-the-state) — Ortega's frame throughout being moral-psychological rather than Spinozist.
