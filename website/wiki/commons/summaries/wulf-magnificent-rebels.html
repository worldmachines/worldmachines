Magnificent Rebels (Andrea Wulf, 2022)

Commons · Summaries · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
L2 summary of Andrea Wulf's Magnificent Rebels (2022): a narrative group biography of the 'Jena Set' (Fichte, the Schlegels, Caroline, Novalis, Schelling, Goethe, Schiller and others, 1794-1806), arguing that they placed the free, self-determining Ich at the centre of thought and bequeathed us both its liberation and its selfishness.

Andrea Wulf's Magnificent Rebels: The First Romantics and the Invention of the Self (Knopf, 2022) is a narrative group biography of the writers, philosophers and scientists she calls the 'Jena Set', gathered in the small Saxon university town of Jena and in neighbouring Weimar between 1794 and the Battle of Jena in October 1806. Its argument, stated in the Prologue and the Epilogue, is that this group, 'intoxicated by the French Revolution, placed the self at the centre stage of their thinking', and that 'we still think with the minds of these visionary thinkers' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-1). The reading notes treat this as Wulf's thesis-setting assertion. The chapters in between are mostly scene, letter and gossip, and they show the group's life far more than they demonstrate its later influence. It is the second Wulf book in the corpus, after wulf-invention-of-nature; for the author see andrea-wulf.

The book's question and thesis

Wulf opens with her own life to pose the question: when did we come to expect the right to determine our own lives, and to take what we want? She presents modern life as a thin line between free will and selfishness, self-determination and narcissism, empathy and righteousness, and her guiding questions are 'Who am I as an individual? And who am I as a member of a group and society?' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-1). Her answer is Jena. Fichte's Ich, 'lit by the fire of the French Revolution', turned the liberation of the individual into a rebellion against the despotism of the state, and 'We are this Ich': the liberation of the self from a divinely organised universe is, on her account, 'the foundation of our thinking today' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-2). The Epilogue returns to the tension and resolves it sympathetically as 'The Art of being Selfish' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-influence-selfish; see art-of-being-selfish-wulf).

Why Jena

Wulf's answer to 'Why Jena? Indeed, why Germany?' is structural and contingent. More than fifteen hundred German states made censorship 'much more difficult to enforce'; there were about fifty universities, very high literacy and an 'Age of Paper'; and Jena's university answered to four Saxon dukes, so that 'no one was truly in charge' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-2, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch03-finest-minds-2). She sets this against a despotic ancien régime and an Enlightenment she pictures as clockwork, measurement and utility, whose downside the friends felt: 'The friends began to look inwards.' The notes read this as an account of a scene arising from institutional accident, not a systematic model (see jena-set-wulf).

Structure and arc

The notes cover a Prologue, twenty chapters and a three-part Epilogue, told chronologically. The notes record that Part II opens with Chapter 7 (its epigraph is Caroline's 1799 image of the group as a 'magic cauldron') and Part IV, 'Fragmentation', with Chapter 16. The period labels below are this page's, not Wulf's.

  • Gathering, 1794-96. Goethe rides to Jena and the Goethe-Schiller friendship begins in a disagreement over the primordial plant (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch01-happy-event-1, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch01-happy-event-2). Fichte arrives and makes the self-positing Ich the talk of the taverns (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-1, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-2). Schiller's Horen and his Letters on the Aesthetic Education of Man turn the Jena Set into a group for the first time (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch03-finest-minds-1, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch03-finest-minds-2). August Wilhelm Schlegel marries Caroline Böhmer and they arrive in Jena, followed by Friedrich Schlegel, while Novalis rewrites Fichte through love (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch04-intellectual-friction-1, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch04-intellectual-friction-2, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch05-novalis-in-love, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch06-splendid-circle-1, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch06-splendid-circle-2).
  • The high years, 1797-99. Goethe and Alexander von Humboldt's galvanic experiments and the 'little academy', then the Horen quarrel (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch07-little-academy-1, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch07-little-academy-2); Novalis's grief, death wish and 'Magical Idealism' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch08-handful-of-darkness); the Athenaeum, the fragment and 'symphilosophy' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch09-sublime-impertinence-1, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch09-sublime-impertinence-2); the Dresden summer and Schelling's arrival, then his lectures on living nature (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch10-symphilosophy, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch11-naturphilosophie); the atheism dispute and Fichte's dismissal (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch12-atheism-dispute); Dorothea Veit's divorce and Lucinde (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch13-divorce-women-sex); the crowded Leutragasse household and Schelling's philosophy of art (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch14-schlegel-clique); and four days in November 1799 around Novalis's essay Europe (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch15-new-confederation).
  • Fragmentation, 1799-1803. Estrangement and feuds (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-1); Dorothea's 'republic of despots', Caroline's illness and her daughter Auguste's death in July 1800 (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-2); grief, blame, Goethe's fever and Novalis's death in March 1801 (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-1, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-2); the Fichte-Schelling split, the Schlegel divorce and Caroline's marriage to Schelling (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-1, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-2).
  • Dispersal and fall, 1803-06. The exodus from Jena, Madame de Staël's visit and Schiller's death in 1805 (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch19-current-exodus); Napoleon's approach, Hegel's 'world soul', the Battle of Jena and the ruin of the town (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch20-french-in-town-1, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch20-french-in-town-2).
  • Epilogue. 'After Jena' follows the survivors: Fichte's national Ich, Caroline's death in 1809, the Schlegels' conversions, the Humboldts, Hegel, the Novalis myth, Schiller and Goethe (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1, wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-2). 'Influence' and 'The Art of being Selfish' trace the reception and restate the thesis (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-influence-selfish).

Throughlines

The self, as doctrine and as lived experiment. Kant's self as lawgiver, Fichte's self-positing Ich, Novalis's revision through love and feeling, Schelling's identity of self and nature, and Hegel's Phenomenology form the intellectual line; Caroline's defiance, Dorothea's divorce and the Humboldts' open marriage form the lived one. See invention-of-the-self-wulf and johann-gottlieb-fichte.

Collective creation. Horen, the Xenien, the Schlegel-Caroline Shakespeare, the Athenaeum fragments, symphilosophy and the Leutragasse household, with a recurring pattern of women's uncredited work. See symphilosophie-wulf, caroline-schelling and august-wilhelm-schlegel.

Nature as a living whole. Goethe's morphology, Humboldt's galvanism and Schelling's Naturphilosophie, carried outward by Humboldt, Coleridge and the Transcendentalists. See naturphilosophie-wulf and friedrich-wilhelm-joseph-schelling.

Revolution and Napoleon as backdrop. The circle met the Revolution mostly through print and admired it from afar (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch05-novalis-in-love, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch06-splendid-circle-1); Caroline, Schelling and Hegel still welcomed the French victory as their own town was ruined (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch20-french-in-town-2). The notes repeatedly flag Wulf's juxtapositions of the political and the personal as ironic rather than causal (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-2, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-2).

Freedom and its dark side. Contemporary charges of egotism, Schiller's verdict on a self-centred generation, Novalis's plan to die by willpower, the 'republic of despots', the gap between professed equality and conduct, and Fichte's 'Ich of a nation'. See art-of-being-selfish-wulf and inwardness-without-a-state-wulf.

The scene's rise and fall. Conditions, infrastructure, hubs and the vicious circle of dispersal are gathered in jena-set-wulf.

Method and voice

Wulf writes as a narrative historian. The notes consistently separate three voices: her own arguments (concentrated in the Prologue and Epilogue), the historical actors' words in letters and lectures, and gossip she reports as gossip. She hedges motives ('may have played a part'), reconstructs some scenes conditionally ('Caroline might have') and says when a cause is unclear (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch01-happy-event-1, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-2, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-2). Her strongest claims are first-person judgements: that the Jena Set is 'intellectually speaking, the most important' of comparable groups (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-2), that Schelling's philosophy 'became the heartbeat of Romanticism' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch11-naturphilosophie), and that the Jena Set, not Freud, opened the dark regions of the mind (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch08-handful-of-darkness). One note judges her Romanticism-versus-Enlightenment portrait 'somewhat one-sided' (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch11-naturphilosophie).

World Machines resonances, and their hedges

  • Enlightenment as machine. Novalis's 'gigantic millwheel' and Prussia's 'mechanical administration' rhyme with modernity-machine and Legibility. The notes insist Wulf's machine is a metaphor, not a machine-theory of eras, and that her Romantics wanted a poetic synthesis with science rather than a rejection of it (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-2, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch08-handful-of-darkness). See poeticising-the-mechanical-world-wulf.
  • Scene as network. Jena's accidental, distributed governance and the many-hand fragment form half-rhyme with history-machine, though the notes stress that Wulf gives a local explanation, not a theory, and that the Athenaeum was deliberate and authored (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch03-finest-minds-2, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch09-sublime-impertinence-1).
  • Great men and collectives. Hegel's Napoleon as 'world soul' sits at one pole, Goethe's late claim that his life's work 'is that of a collective' at the other (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch20-french-in-town-1, wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-2).
  • Dusk. Caroline's 'what can last no longer must perish' has a faint echo of Dusk logic in world-machines terms, but the note calls it a Romantic-era political remark, not a cyclical theory (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch20-french-in-town-2).
  • Inwardness. Against Peter Gay's infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness, Wulf treats the self as a discovery and liberation rather than an infrastructural construct (wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-2, wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-influence-selfish).
  • Counter-Enlightenment. Her Novalis and Schelling extend reason with imagination, which sits uneasily with isaiah-berlin's anti-rationalists (wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch08-handful-of-darkness, wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch09-sublime-impertinence-2); the political dangers Berlin traces (romanticism-and-political-danger) appear in Wulf mainly in a single Epilogue paragraph on Fichte (wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1).
