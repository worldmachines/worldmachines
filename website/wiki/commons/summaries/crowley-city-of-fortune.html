City of Fortune

Commons · Summaries · updated 23 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Roger Crowley's narrative history of the Venetian seaborne empire, tracing the Stato da Mar from the year-1000 Adriatic campaign through the hijacked Fourth Crusade and the long Genoese wars to the double shock of Ottoman conquest and the Portuguese Cape route, and finally to a two-century attritional unwinding.

Source: Roger Crowley, City of Fortune: How Venice Ruled the Seas (2011; 2013). Twenty-three L1 reading notes cover the prologue, twenty-one chapters, and the epilogue.

City of Fortune is a narrative history of the stato-da-mar — Venice's seaborne empire — from the year-1000 campaign that made the Adriatic a Venetian sea to the 1669 surrender of Crete and Napoleon's entry into St Mark's Square in 1797. Crowley's controlling claim is that Venice was a polity with "no land holdings, no natural resources, no agricultural production or large population," and that this absence, not any abundance, is what produced everything else: a mercantile nobility instead of a feudal one, an empire of ports and chokepoints instead of provinces, and a civic life organised so completely around trade that the city's own 1343 appeal to the pope could state flatly that "we cannot live otherwise and know not how except by trade."

The frame

The book opens on a private moment rather than a battle. On the night of 9 April 1363 Petrarch is woken in his waterfront house by a merchant galley bound for the Don casting off into a storm, and his awe curdles into moral unease at "this insatiable thirst for wealth" (crowley-city-of-fortune-prologue). Crowley's point is that what struck Petrarch as sublime was, to Venetians, routine — departure was "almost as unconscious as crossing the threshold of your house," ritualised in the Sposalizio del Mare, in which the sea's gender is feminised so that it can be wed and thereby possessed. The prologue then runs time backwards to 1201 and the arrival of six French knights "about a crusade." The epilogue answers the frame with an arrival rather than a departure: Felix Fabri coming home by night in 1480 to hear all the bells of Venice ringing (crowley-city-of-fortune-epilogue).

The arc

Seizure (1000–1250). Geography comes first: the lagoon's channels and mud flats admit trade and repel conquest, producing a city with no feudalism and no fixed loyalty to pope or emperor — "neither to the Church nor to the emperor, nor to the sea nor to the land" (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch01-lords-of-dalmatia). Doge Pietro Orseolo II's year-1000 expedition against the Narentine pirates establishes both the template of Venetian statecraft ("shrewd diplomacy and the precise application of force") and the axiom that the Adriatic must be a Venetian sea; the Byzantine Golden Bull of 1082 converts a client into a duty-free monopolist, and the resentment that follows sets the charge that the Fourth Crusade detonates.

That crusade occupies the book's most sustained sequence. A holy war is priced to the mark and the month — 4,500 horses, 20,000 foot, 94,000 marks, half of all future conquest — with a destination (Egypt) concealed from the men who took the cross (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch02-the-blind-doge). Only about twelve thousand of thirty-three thousand contracted crusaders arrive, leaving a shortfall of thirty-four thousand marks that Doge enrico-dandolo converts into leverage: debt deferred against future conquests, and the fleet redirected first onto the Christian city of Zara (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch03-thirty-four-thousand-marks) and then, via the pretender Alexius Angelus, onto Constantinople itself, while Innocent III's excommunications are suppressed, forged around, or simply outrun (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch04-dog-returning-to-its-vomit). What the crusaders find behind the eight-hundred-year-old triple walls is an empire whose navy has sold off its own anchors and rigging (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch05-at-the-walls); the interregnum that follows leaves four rival emperors in one city (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch06-four-emperors); and in April 1204 the second assault produces three days of sack in which Frankish soldiers melt statuary for coin while Venetians loot "like connoisseurs," carrying home the bronze horses, relics, and marble that will build St Mark's Square (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch07-the-works-of-hell). Venice emerges with "a Quarter and Half a Quarter of the Empire of Romania" — three eighths, reckoned like merchandise — and turns it not into provinces but into a network: Modon and Coron as "the Eyes of the Republic," Crete, Negroponte, the Cyclades farmed out to freebooters like Marco Sanudo (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch08-a-quarter-and-half-a-quarter).

The system and its rival (1250–1400). The middle of the book is dominated by Genoa: near-twins in maritime necessity, opposites in political temperament — communal, state-directed Venice against individualist, privatised Genoa. The War of Saint Sabas and the 1258 Battle of Acre are followed by Genoa's diplomatic counter-stroke, the 1261 Treaty of Nymphaion, which helps restore Byzantium and permanently mortgages it to two foreign rivals (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch09-demand-and-supply). Beneath the war runs the economic engine: urban populations outgrowing their hinterland, an obsessively regulated grain office, and the spice trade as "the first manifestation of a world trade." When Acre falls in 1291 the contest moves north into the Black Sea, where the Pax Mongolica has opened a corridor of safe transit — and where, in 1345–46, the siege of Caffa sends the plague back down Venice's own trade routes to kill perhaps two-thirds of the city (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch10-in-the-jaws-of-our-enemies; compare the Florentine view in the-decameron). The aftershocks are structural: thinner fleets, a class rift between commanders and oarsmen, and in 1363 a revolt on Crete led not by Greeks but by Venetian feudatories who had intermarried, spoken Greek, and drifted toward Orthodoxy — "Crete was starting to conquer the conquerors" (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch11-the-flag-of-st-titus). The rivalry ends in the War of Chioggia: a quarrel over precedence at a Cypriot coronation escalates until the Genoese hold the mouth of the lagoon and demand to "put a bridle on those horses of yours on the portico of St Mark's" (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch12-bridling-st-mark). Venice survives by universal mobilisation, by a popular revolt that frees Vettor Pisani from prison, and finally by patient encirclement — sinking hulks across Chioggia's three exits and starving the besiegers into surrender on 24 June 1380. Crowley's verdict is deliberately anti-heroic: Venice outlasted Genoa "less through military supremacy than through the durability of her institutions, the social cohesion of her people" (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch13-fight-to-the-finish).

Day and Dusk (1381–1503, with a coda to 1797). The recovery after Chioggia doubles Venetian territory in forty years and produces the mature imperial machine: colonies built as miniature Venices with their own Piazza San Marco and clock tower, three separately held keys on the Candia counting house, forty-five miles of archive shelving, colonial officials rotated every two years to prevent "local contamination" (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch14-stato-da-mar). The commercial system reaches the same pitch: state-chartered galley convoys on fixed dates and fixed stopovers, meshing with the Indian monsoon and the German winter fairs into "interconnecting trading cycles, like the meshing cogs in an enormous mechanism, which moved goods and gold all the way from China to the North Sea" (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch15-like-water-in-a-fountain). Where Genoa sent armed galleys, "Venice sent diplomats – again and again and again." The peak self-portrait is Jacopo de' Barbari's 1500 woodcut of a dolphin-shaped city blessed by Neptune; Crowley's turn is to note that "even as Neptune's trident was raised triumphantly aloft, the Stato da Mar was simultaneously in hidden decline" (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch16-city-of-neptune).

Part III is the Ottoman half-century. Venice manages Mehmet II's rise by the method its own diplomats compared to juggling a glass ball — neither throwing it back with force nor letting it drop — and spends more than any European power on understanding the sultan's court, then sends ambassadors to congratulate the conqueror within days of Constantinople's fall on 29 May 1453 (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch17-the-glass-ball). As the permanent front-line state it runs refortification ledgers, a bailo, and at least fourteen authorised attempts to poison Mehmet, while Pius II's crusade collapses at Ancona in 1464 before an Europe "too nationalistic, too divided, too materialistic, too secular" to reconstitute (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch18-the-shield-of-christendom). The long war that follows turns on a single failure of nerve — the lawyer-admiral Nicolo da Canal withdrawing twice within an hour of severing Mehmet's retreat, and Negroponte falling in 1470, after which "the greatness of Venice has been humbled" becomes contemporaries' own reading (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch19-if-negroponte-is-lost). Zonchio in 1499 is worse because it is not even a defeat by destruction: a vanguard simply refuses to engage while its own crews scream "Attack!", and no one is ever punished — "systemic," in Crowley's word (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch20-pyramid-of-fire). Then the double blow: Modon and Coron gone in 1500, a peace of practical vassalage in 1503, and — arriving by a Lisbon ambassador's report in 1501 — the news of Vasco da Gama's Cape route, which the diarist Girolamo Priuli reasons out in real time as the doom of the whole Levantine relay (crowley-city-of-fortune-ch21-hands-on-the-throat-of-venice). The epilogue refuses a single terminus: Cyprus 1570, the muda system decaying from the 1520s, Crete 1669, Tinos 1715, and Napoleon in 1797 — attrition, not collapse.

Throughlines

  • The virtual economy. Crowley's recurring formulation, stated in the prologue and returned to in the final chapter: Venice as "the first virtual economy" and "the first virtual city," an empire of cash with no land base, whose hero-type is "the man of business" rather than the knight. Wealth entirely relational, and therefore existentially precarious — sever the routes and "the whole magnificent edifice might simply collapse."
  • Empire as network, not territory. The Stato da Mar "never comprised the occupation of substantial blocks of land"; Crowley's own comparison is to the British way-station system — "Venice created its own Gibraltars, Maltas and Adens."
  • Quantification as instinct. A crusade priced by the horse and the month, an empire reckoned as three eighths, rations counted to the kilo, galleys timetabled for two centuries, bobbins marked to trace a spinner's work. This is the book's most WMP-legible pattern, and it is treated at length in venetian-legibility-and-its-blind-spot.
  • Contract as both discipline and instrument. The same literalism that makes Venice inflexible about repayment is what lets Dandolo satisfy a contract's letter while redirecting its substance; the March Pact divides the spoils of Constantinople before a stone is thrown, and is broken within hours of the breach.
  • Contingency told as destiny. Crowley repeatedly stages near-run things — Dandolo's galley grounding under the banner of St Mark, a monk crawling through a bricked-up gate, a hundred galleys fighting blind in a winter strait where "outcomes were decided by luck" — and then shows them calcifying into Venetian legend, most literally in Tintoretto's re-enactment four centuries later.
  • The sea as protection, opportunity, and fate. The "manic-depressive streak in the Venetian soul": the lagoon that repels invaders is also the medium that drowns sailors, carries the plague home from Caffa, and finally simply stops being where the money is.

In the World Machines frame

The notes read Venice mostly as a smaller-scale, faster-running analogue rather than a world-machines proper: a single city-state's empire with its own construction, dominance, and decline. Several resonances recur across the batch and are worth holding loosely rather than pressing. The sack of 1204 is a literal handoff of institutional material — bronze horses, relics, marble, and eventually Byzantine ceremony itself — from a machine deep in its Dusk into the foundations of one at its Dawn, which is infrastructure-inheritance in its most physical form. Constantinople in 1203–4, with four simultaneous emperors and a mob-crowned nonentity in Hagia Sophia, and Crete in 1363, with the flag raised upside down, are both textbook gramsci-gap interregnums. The 1500 woodcut celebrating a maritime identity the nobility had already begun to disinvest from, and the Senza performed long after its "tough, energetic realities" had hollowed out — Crowley's own word is "ornamental" — track the decay-sequence closely. And the Portuguese coda, two disguised agents mapping monsoon routes while Venice fights its terminal wars in the wrong sea, is history-machine logic in its purest form: a successor order assembling, distributed and unnoticed, outside the frame anyone is watching. Note also one genuine friction: crowley-city-of-fortune-ch06-four-emperors is filed as contradicting the decay sequence, since Byzantine authority there does not drain through stages at all but disintegrates in hours.
