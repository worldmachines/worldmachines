The Courtier and the Heretic

Commons · Summaries · updated 23 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Matthew Stewart's 2006 double biography reads the secret November 1676 meeting between Leibniz and Spinoza in The Hague as the hinge of modern philosophy, casting Spinoza as the active response to modernity and Leibniz as the reactive one whose system collapses back into the philosophy it was built to refute.

Matthew Stewart, The Courtier and the Heretic: Leibniz, Spinoza, and the Fate of God in the Modern World (W. W. Norton, 2006) — a double biography of gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz and baruch-spinoza built entirely around the several days in November 1676 when Leibniz called on Spinoza at his lodgings on the Paviljoensgracht in The Hague, about three months before Spinoza's death.

The Central Conceit

Stewart's structural gambit is to treat that hidden meeting as the hinge of both lives: "Everything before points toward it for resolution; and everything after points back for explanation." The evidentiary basis is almost nothing. From days of conversation, one document survives — a page in Leibniz's hand titled That a Most Perfect Being Exists, carrying Leibniz's own marginal claim that Spinoza, after first contradicting it, "thought it to be sound." Stewart performs the reconstruction openly and hedges it as he goes, and he closes the meeting chapter by turning his skepticism on the annotation itself: perhaps the note "was less a statement of fact than an expression of its author's needs."

The evidence that the meeting mattered is therefore indirect and mostly Leibniz's: forty years of minimizing it (a chance visit, a few hours, "in passing"), against a documented three-day stay, correspondence he later denied, fifteen pages of savage private marginalia on the Ethics, and a late, unpublished half-confession that he "once went a little too far, and began to lean to the side of the Spinozists."

The Argument

Stewart states his thesis almost programmatically in the first chapter and again in the last. Spinoza is "the first and archetypal instance of the active response to modernity": a philosopher who derives God, mind, ethics, and politics from a universe of pure natural causation "without purpose or design," and arrives at a liberal, democratic order fit for an irreducibly plural society. Leibniz is "the paradigm for the reactive response" — a strategy for recovering something of the old God and the old man "by means of an analysis of the limits of reason."

The closing chapter presses this into a claim about intellectual history: Spinoza's line supplied modern science and the liberal political order, while Leibniz's reactive line — anxious about purposelessness, unwilling to assume personal responsibility for happiness — became, paradoxically, the dominant lineage of academic philosophy, restated in Kant's noumenal self, Hegel's teleology, Bergson's élan vital, Heidegger's Being, and the postmodern critique. Stewart's verdict is deliberately asymmetric: Leibniz's greed, vanity and "insatiable, all too human neediness" make him "the philosopher of the common man," while Spinoza remains "a rara avis" whose teaching is "a philosophy for philosophers."

Structure

The book alternates strictly. Odd chapters after the first belong to Leibniz (3, 5, 7, 9, 11); even chapters belong to Spinoza (2, 4, 6, 8, 10). The two lines converge in chapter 12, the meeting itself; chapters 13–17 follow Leibniz alone through forty years of aftermath; chapter 18 is reception history and thesis.

A second scaffolding runs underneath: the triptych God / Mind / Salvation, used to expound Spinoza's Ethics whole, then to show Leibniz's mature system built point-by-point against it, then to argue that Leibniz's system collapses back into it on all three heads.

Throughlines

Disavowed intimacy. The book's engine is the documented gap between what Leibniz said in public and what he did in private. In 1671–72 he calls the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus "intolerably impudent" to Thomasius and urges Spitzel to savage it, while writing to "Mr. Spinoza, celebrated doctor and profound philosopher" under an optics pretext routed through an intermediary. He warns Spinoza that his own patron Huet is preparing a refutation — a paragraph Leibniz's later editors quietly suppressed.

Character as philosophy. Stewart insists "there are no two finer examples of the dictum that character is philosophy." Spinoza's excommunication produces withdrawal and self-sufficiency; Leibniz's rejected Leipzig doctorate produces the opposite, a life thrown "back at the mercy of society." The monomaniac compresses a lifetime into one adamantine volume; the omnimaniac scatters himself across hundreds of unfinished projects. Stewart even prices the difference: "1 Leibniz Unit = 11 Spinoza Units."

A philosophy defined by its enemy. Stewart argues Leibniz's system is easier to state negatively than positively, and that all its enemies — mechanism, modern philosophy, atheism — eventually collapse into one proper name. The excised word "Spinozists" in an early draft of the Discourse on Metaphysics, changed to "the latest innovators," is offered as material proof that the treatise was conceived as a counter-text.

The return of the repressed. The book's philosophical climax runs Leibniz's own commitments against him. Who built the pyramid of possible worlds among which God chooses? If God did, there is only one possible world and Spinozism follows; if it stands independent of God, God is "no longer God, but just a logical operator." Is God a monad? If not, monads are modes of the one substance; if so, everything God does follows with logical necessity from his own concept. Pre-established harmony turns out to be empirically indistinguishable from Spinoza's parallelism — a charge contemporaries were making by 1712. Even Leibniz's ethics, with each monad possessing "a conatus of sorts," reads to Russell as though it could be cribbed from the Ethics.

Posthumous distortion. Both men were reshaped by later needs. Lessing's 1765 encounter with the Opera Posthuma and Novalis's "God-intoxicated man" turned the political revolutionary into a mystical pantheist. Kant's rationalist/empiricist scheme filed Spinoza and Leibniz together as fellow rationalists, obscuring the deeper opposition between them — a classification, Stewart notes, that survived because it was convenient to enough constituencies at once rather than because it was true.

Where This Sits in the World Machines Frame

Stewart's seventeenth century is explicitly an interregnum: an age in which "the theocratic order of the medieval era ceded to the secular order of modernity," with the old legitimacy dead and the new one unbuilt — the condition the gramsci-gap names. What makes the book unusually useful to the world-machines frame is that it supplies both a macro instance (two rival bids for what follows the theocratic order, still contending three centuries later) and a micro one: the same gap running inside a single mind, in Leibniz's 1676 notebooks, where a Spinozist argument assembles itself across scattered entries and then breaks off mid-word — Essentia est pr… — because its author will not let it finish.

The book also documents, at close range, the ambitions the framework tracks as legibility-as-machine-core: Leibniz's universal characteristic, a symbolic language meant to reduce all philosophical dispute to "Let's calculate!"; Colbert's administrators turning 1670s Paris into a countable object of sanitation, lighting and circulation; and Spinoza's own claim, from the other side, that "there is nothing that cannot be known." Whichever metaphysics is draped over it, both philosophers end up describing a lawful, all-determining cosmos fit for the modernity-machine to organize.

Cautions

Stewart is a popular historian writing an interpretive thesis, and his method leans on psychological reconstruction — vanity read as starved neediness, the monadology read as "a kind of ontological hologram of the character of its creator". He flags his own speculation where the record is thin, but the biographical-causal claims (that the meeting was "the defining event of Leibniz's life," that Leibniz's rivalries with Locke and Newton were displaced battles with Spinoza) are arguments, not findings. The documentary spine — the letters, the notebooks, the December 1676 note, the marginalia — is where the book's evidentiary weight actually sits.
