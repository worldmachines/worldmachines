Civilization and Capitalism, 15th–18th Century, Vol. I: The Structures of Everyday Life (Fernand Braudel)

Commons · Summaries · updated 23 September 2026 · source on GitHub
The first volume of Fernand Braudel's Civilization and Capitalism, which maps 'the limits of the possible' of pre-industrial material life worldwide from 1400 to 1800 (population, food, drink, housing, dress, energy, technology, money and towns) and proposes the three-storey model of material life, market economy and capitalism that organizes the whole trilogy.

The Structures of Everyday Life is the first volume of fernand-braudel's Civilization and Capitalism trilogy. Its subtitle is also its organizing question: the limits of the possible, meaning the slow-moving border between what pre-industrial societies could and could not do. The project began in 1952 as a commission from Lucien Febvre for a short summary of pre-industrial European economic history. It grew into an "interminable venture" once Braudel's direct research made him doubt the classical account (Sombart, Kulischer and the economists generally), in which a single, statistics-ready "economy" moves smoothly toward the market, the firm and capitalist investment until the Industrial Revolution "neatly divides human history in two" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-introduction).

The three-storey model

Braudel replaces that single economy with three coexisting, partly antagonistic layers. At the bottom is material life: an "infra-economy" of self-sufficiency, routine and barter within a small radius. It is "everpresent, all-pervasive, repetitive" and covers 80–90% of the world's population through "all rural life." In the middle is the market economy, made up of production, exchange, shops, fairs and banks. These are the "transparent" realities that economic science was built to observe, and economic science confined itself to them. At the top is capitalism, a "shadowy zone" of privileged actors, monopoly and action at a distance (the Fuggers, the Welsers, the great Indies companies). Braudel insists it is not the same thing as the market economy (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-preface, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-introduction). The Conclusion restates the model and finds capitalism's power in its "comparative freedom of movement" compared with the "inflexible" floors beneath it (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion). This volume weighs the ground floor, Volume II (The Wheels of Commerce) separates the market economy from capitalism, and Volume III (The Perspective of the World) builds the chronological history on both. See braudel-three-storey-house.

The limits of the possible

From the fifteenth to the eighteenth century the border between the possible and the impossible, set by food supply, population pressure, low labour productivity and weak control over nature, "hardly changed at all." Real change along it waited for the nineteenth century (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-preface). Almost every chapter traces this border in its own register: the arithmetic of births and deaths, wheat yields, the price of grain counted in hours of labour, the horse-power budget of wood, water and animals, and the speed of transport. See limits-of-the-possible.

Method

  • Structure over event. Reducing the scale of observation moves attention from "History with a capital letter" down into material life. There, a happening that is merely repeated becomes "a generality or rather a structure" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-preface). The Conclusion calls anecdotes "the dust of history." Each one is trivial alone, but "by indefinite repetition" they form "linked chains" and long-term "series" that behave as near-constants (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion).
  • World scale, against Eurocentrism. The stated aim is "if not to see everything, at least to locate everything, and on the requisite world scale" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion). The volume repeatedly corrects "Western nationalism." Gunpowder was made in China from the ninth century (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch06-gunpowder-and-artillery). Credit instruments run from Babylon to ninth-century Chinese bank notes (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch07-paper-money-and-credit). "Europe therefore neither discovered America and Africa" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch01-scale-of-reference).
  • Order-of-magnitude quantification. Braudel builds a world population from the rough Europe–China equivalence (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch01-guessing-world-population). He converts grain prices into labour-hours (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-wheat-trade-price-and-bread), applies Fisher's MV = PQ to Naples (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch07-rules-of-the-currency-game) and ranks Europe's power sources by horse-power (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch05-wind-water-wood-coal).
  • Unusual archives. He uses pollen and cave strata for maize (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-maize-and-dietary-revolutions), Last Supper paintings as a dataset on forks (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-table-manners-and-everyday-foods), inventories after death (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch04-interiors) and a childhood memory of a Lorraine village (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion).

The chapter arc

1. The weight of numbers. World population roughly doubled between 1300 and 1800. Because the growth was synchronous in China, Russia and the Americas, European explanations cannot account for it, and Braudel tentatively proposes shared climatic fluctuation (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch01-guessing-world-population). A "scale of reference" shows that small pre-industrial towns, armies and fleets weighed far more than their raw numbers suggest. Overpopulation is a ratio to economic capacity, not a density. The sites of civilization in 1500 are "even today" the same sites (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch01-scale-of-reference). Under the biological ancien régime, births and deaths both hovered near 40 per 1000, so catastrophe was answered by compensation, until the eighteenth century partly and reversibly broke the pattern (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch01-eighteenth-century-biological-watershed). Numbers also decided contact between peoples. Barbarian conquerors were absorbed, gunpowder states closed the nomads' open frontier "once and for all," and colonization ran differently against empty space than against resistant "cultures" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch01-many-against-the-few).

2. Daily bread. Wheat, rice and maize are "plants of civilization," early and almost irreversible choices. Wheat was locked into a rotation-livestock-manure system and yielded about 5:1 for three centuries (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-wheat-cultivation-and-yields). Local supply dominated. The maritime grain trade came to roughly 1% of Mediterranean consumption, and grain priced in labour-hours stayed above the "serious" 100-hour line from the 1540s to the 1880s (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-wheat-trade-price-and-bread). Rice yields about five times wheat's calories per hectare, but it is "an even more tyrannical and enslaving crop" that demanded state supervision and left the highlands unused (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-rice). Maize and the potato took two centuries of scattered local adoption to become an eighteenth-century dietary revolution (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-maize-and-dietary-revolutions). Beyond the grain civilizations lay a world of hoe cultivators and gatherer-hunters, whom Braudel treats as coexisting at the same time, not as earlier rungs (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-the-rest-of-the-world).

3. Superfluity and sufficiency: food and drink. Luxury is a moving, relational category. Europe was carnivorous from 1350 to 1550, and meat then retreated for three centuries as population pressed on the land (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-luxury-and-meat). The fork took two centuries to become customary. Salt, fish, pepper and above all sugar tie everyday food to long-distance trade and, in sugar's case, to plantation slavery (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-table-manners-and-everyday-foods). The history of drink runs from rationed water to brandy quoted on the Amsterdam exchange (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-drinks-wine-beer-alcohol). Chocolate, tea, coffee and tobacco trace a route from local habit to colonial dependency to "an active capitalist sector" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-stimulants-chocolate-tea-coffee-tobacco).

4. Houses, clothes and fashion. Housing changes "only very slowly, if at all," within limits set by materials and rank (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch04-houses-throughout-the-world). Interiors stayed nearly frozen everywhere except the West, which also invented domestic privacy around 1720–25 (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch04-interiors). Costume stays still unless political rupture shakes the social order. European fashion begins around 1350 and becomes perpetual and obligatory around 1700, an index of a society's "energies, possibilities, demands and joie de vivre" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch04-costume-and-fashion).

5–6. Energy and technology. Invention is cheap and adoption is rare. "There is never any progress unless a higher value is set on human labour" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch05-human-and-animal-energy). "Civilizations before the eighteenth century were civilizations of wood and charcoal," and the lack of energy that could be transported was "the major handicap of ancien regime economies" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch05-wind-water-wood-coal). Iron remained a "poor relation," outside the speculative capital that copper and silver drew (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch05-iron-a-poor-relation). Of the era's three "revolutions," artillery spread everywhere and settled nothing (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch06-gunpowder-and-artillery). Printing was a capitalist enterprise from its first day, and only Europe took to the high seas, for reasons Braudel explicitly calls non-technical (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch06-printing-and-ocean-navigation). Transport barely moved: "Napoleon moved no faster than Julius Caesar" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch06-transport-and-history-of-technology).

7. Money. Money is an indicator, and "currencies are languages." Any ancient structure that opens to money "loses its acquired equilibria," and barter survives inside monetized Europe as "internal colonies" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch07-imperfect-currencies). Europe's gold-silver-copper coinage, overlaid by abstract moneys of account, formed one world-spanning net (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch07-rules-of-the-currency-game). Outside Europe, coin layered onto older moneys rather than replacing them (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch07-outside-europe-metallic-money). Credit is an ancient, near-universal technique that unified the world only as "the unity of injustice" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch07-paper-money-and-credit).

8. Towns and cities. A town is a bundle of functions, not a size. Town and country create each other, and towns survived only through steady immigration (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch08-towns-problems-of-definition). Walls, grids and hierarchies express each civilization's handling of space (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch08-self-consciousness-and-hierarchies). Only in the West did "the city win hands down" against the territorial state, and capitalism grew there like a "cuckoo's egg" in the town's nest (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch08-originality-of-western-towns). Capitals grew because of the state, not trade, and were "unbalanced worlds" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch08-big-cities-states-and-capitals). Peking and London push the parasitical-capital thesis to its extremes. When the Industrial Revolution came, the capitals watched it as "spectators": "Not London, but Manchester, Birmingham, Leeds, Glasgow" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch08-big-cities-peking-and-london).

Throughlines

  • Ceilings, not trends. Across demography, diet, energy, transport and technique, the volume describes long plateaus under a hard ceiling. Change comes in short bursts ("first the accelerator, then the brake") when accumulated pressure forces society past it (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch06-transport-and-history-of-technology, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch05-wind-water-wood-coal).
  • No technology "in itself." The scythe, the horse-collar, coal, Newcomen's engine and the printing press all waited on social readiness, labour costs or "need," not on invention (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch05-human-and-animal-energy, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch06-transport-and-history-of-technology, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch06-printing-and-ocean-navigation).
  • Slow, distributed diffusion. Maize, Champa rice, tobacco, silk, blast-furnace technique and movable type each spread through many independent local adoptions long before anyone saw a single "revolution" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-maize-and-dietary-revolutions, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-rice, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-stimulants-chocolate-tea-coffee-tobacco, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch05-iron-a-poor-relation).
  • Inequality as the engine. Plague "only attacks poor people." The peasant "never ate his best produce." Capitals live off others' labour, and money unifies the world into favoured and handicapped economies. In the Conclusion, "the inequalities, the injustices, the contradictions" are what "made the world go round" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch01-eighteenth-century-biological-watershed, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-luxury-and-meat, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch08-big-cities-peking-and-london, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch07-paper-money-and-credit, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion).
  • The China question. China's technical precocity, followed by a plateau, recurs in chapter after chapter without a settled answer. See braudel-china-precocity-puzzle.

Where it meets the World Machines frame

The notes flag several resonances. Each one is partial, and the hedges are part of the claim.

  • world-machines. Material civilization is a slow-changing, all-pervading, barely visible infrastructure beneath events, which is close in shape to a world machine. But it is "inert and cyclical rather than a machine moving through a Dawn–Day–Dusk lifecycle." The three floors also loosely echo three machines in friction, but they are "synchronic strata within one social formation, not sequential thousand-year eras" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-preface, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-introduction, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion).
  • history-machine. Braudel's structure-versus-event move "near-exactly anticipates" the contrast between paradigms that assemble silently and event-centred history. The diffusion stories of maize, brandy, tobacco and the "evolution, then revolution" of energy repeat the same shape, although his subject is commodities and techniques, not historiographical paradigms (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-preface, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-drinks-wine-beer-alcohol, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch05-wind-water-wood-coal).
  • legibility-as-machine-core, inverted. In Braudel's schema the market economy is the legible floor, and capitalism "thrives by operating in a zone that stays illegible." Peasants hid maize off the main roads to escape dues (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-introduction, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-maize-and-dietary-revolutions). See legibility-as-precondition-not-cause.
  • divergence-machine. Civilizations are "different planets" whose "elementary answers" "never combined," which is pluralism without convergence. The notes add that Braudel's civilizations are millennia old, not a machine dated 1600–2000 (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-luxury-and-meat).
  • secular-cycles and machines-as-energy-regimes. The biological ancien régime's arithmetic of compensation, and the civilizations of wood and charcoal before those of coal, supply the demographic and energetic substrate those pages model (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch01-eighteenth-century-biological-watershed, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch05-wind-water-wood-coal).
  • coevalness. The "synchronic" world picture, with grain civilizations, hoe cultivators and gatherer-hunters present at the same time and not as stages on one ladder, sits close to the refusal of the time queue (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-the-rest-of-the-world).
  • capture-resistance, mostly by contrast. Material life "refuses to enter fully into the movement of exchange," but through "distance and isolation": it is "a residue, not a strategy." The Viceroy of Mexico's plan to bind the Apaches through pulque is a picture of deliberate capture (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-preface, braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-stimulants-chocolate-tea-coffee-tobacco).

Against the Modernity Machine's 1200–1600 window

Braudel does not engage Venkatesh Rao's essay on the modernity-machine (venkatesh-rao-the-modernity-machine). But several of this book's reading notes test the essay's 1200–1600 construction window against Braudel's material. The rhymes below are the notes' own unless marked as this section's, and each is partial.

  • Towns built the instruments first. Braudel dates the Western urban renaissance to the eleventh century, carried by rural revival and a returning monetary economy ("And money meant towns"). The free towns of Italy, Flanders and Germany invented public loans, bills of exchange, accountancy and trading companies, along with a calculating "time is money" mentality. The territorial state that later absorbed them inherited those institutions wholesale. The note reads this as the Modernity Machine's legibility infrastructure being "built by towns before it was ever built by states" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch08-originality-of-western-towns). On Braudel's dating, some of those foundations predate 1200.
  • The Black Death and labour's bargaining power. Braudel argues that 1350–1550 was unusually favourable for ordinary living standards. Black Death labour scarcity drove up real wages, and meat eating on a large scale was "not ... reserved to the very rich." After 1550 the trend reversed for three centuries as population pressed on fixed land (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-luxury-and-meat). This section, not the note, observes that the first half parallels the essay's Oligarchy–Workers shift, and that the second half shows the material gain did not last.
  • Fashion as a dated hinge. In Braudel's words, around 1350, with the sudden shortening of men's costume, "one could say that fashion began." Fashion becomes perpetual and obligatory only around 1700 (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch04-costume-and-fashion).
  • Crécy from the gunpowder side. Artillery made its battlefield debut at Crécy and Calais in the 1340s, but became a major factor in European war only with Charles VIII's Italian campaign of 1494 (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch06-gunpowder-and-artillery).
  • Parallel revivals that the state reclaimed. Braudel finds urban revivals in classical Greece, Rome, Islam from the ninth century and Sung China. In each case the state eventually re-subordinated the city. Only in the medieval West did "the city win hands down," for causes he calls "still inadequately explained" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch08-originality-of-western-towns). This bears on the essay's claim of regional early modernities without settling it.
  • Precedents outside the window. Other notes place Braudel's material before or beside the window, not inside it. The Chinese hydraulic state is "a much older, non-capitalist precedent, not the modernity-machine proper" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-rice). Chequerboard grid towns "predate and run parallel to that machine's construction window" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch08-self-consciousness-and-hierarchies). The sugar plantation's accounting apparatus assembles from the 1520s, "well before the Modernity Machine's stated 1600 'Day'" (braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-table-manners-and-everyday-foods).
