Robin Douglass, *Rousseau and Hobbes: Nature, Free Will, and the Passions* (2015)

Commons · Summaries · updated 28 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Robin Douglass's intellectual history of Rousseau's engagement with Hobbes and with French and Genevan 'Hobbism', arguing that Rousseau shared Hobbes's diagnosis of politics (disordered passions, the need for unity) but opposed his solutions on the grounds of nature as a normative standard, free will, and natural goodness, and that his anti-Hobbesian polemic targeted Hobbes's natural-law critics and the doux commerce theorists as much as Hobbes.

Robin Douglass's study (Oxford University Press, 2015) is an intellectual history of jean-jacques-rousseau's engagement with thomas-hobbes, and with "Hobbism", the image of Hobbes that circulated in eighteenth-century France and Geneva. Douglass insists these were two different targets. His thesis is that Rousseau's critique of Hobbes, above all in the Discours sur l'inégalité, was polemical. It was aimed as much at Hobbes's critics in the modern natural-law tradition and at the doux commerce theorists as at Hobbes himself, because Rousseau took all of them to rest their justifications of the social order on Hobbesian premises. Douglass calls this the principal historical contribution of the study (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-hobbes-and-rousseau). He also refuses the opposite error of an unequivocally anti-Hobbesian Rousseau. His verdict is that Rousseau shared Hobbes's diagnosis of politics and opposed his solutions (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-oppositions-and-affinities).

The framing problem: law above man

The book opens from Rousseau's 1767 letter to the Marquis de Mirabeau, a reply to the Physiocrats' "legal despotism". Rousseau poses the problem of politics as finding a form of government that places law above man, and sees no tolerable middle between "the most austere democracy" and "the most perfect Hobbism", where a mortal-God sovereign stands above the law. The Physiocrats, he says, calculated the despot's true interest but ignored the "play of the passions", so their system suits "the people of Utopia" and not "the children of Adam" (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-mirabeau-letter). The same problem runs through the Discours sur l'économie politique, Du contrat social and the Poland Considérations. The book returns to the letter at its close (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-republics-naturally-good).

Method

Douglass writes as a historian of reception, not of derivation (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-methodological-problems):

  • Coherence without a "mythology of coherence". Free will and natural goodness are fully formed by the Discours sur l'inégalité. The theory of the passions keeps changing up to Emile.
  • Reception, not influence. He asks what Rousseau thought was at stake in targeting Hobbes, not what he borrowed. Resemblance is not to be read as derivation.
  • What Rousseau read. There is no definitive proof Rousseau read Hobbes. Douglass makes a probabilistic case for De cive, most likely in Sorbière's French translation, plus the commonplaces of the day. His best clue is the phrase "un Enfant robuste", which he reports is unique to the French De cive before the Discours. The burden of proof lies on anyone claiming Rousseau read Leviathan (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-engagement-in-context).
  • Against the Hobbesian Rousseau. He disputes the Strauss, Derathé, Davy and Jouvenel readings and reverses Richard Tuck's method: he uses Hobbes to understand Rousseau better, not Rousseau to defend Hobbes (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-hobbes-and-rousseau).

The argument is organised by three themes whose relation "often appears contradictory": nature as a normative standard, free will, and the ordering of the passions. On the first two Rousseau is read against Hobbes. On the third he shows notable affinities with him (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-nature-free-will-passions).

Chapter 1: the French and Genevan Hobbes

Douglass reconstructs the Hobbes Rousseau met, selectively and without a grand narrative (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-french-reception-opening). The earlier French welcome, in the Mersenne circle, was for Hobbes's mechanistic science. It gave way to a moral-political reputation in which his name, paired with Spinoza's, served as a rhetorical weapon.

  • Nicole and Bayle. Nicole's neo-Augustinian moralism took over Hobbes's state of war as a picture of fallen man. Bayle's Dictionnaire article became the main eighteenth-century source on Hobbes, and his civil-law case for a society of atheists carried Hobbesian themes into the debate over church and state (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-nicole-bayle).
  • Malebranche attacked Hobbes's materialism and his supposed grounding of justice in amour-propre and will, and set dualism against him as the guarantee of freedom. Rousseau later revived these criticisms (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-malebranche).
  • Barbeyrac and Burlamaqui built a French natural-law tradition that defined itself against Hobbes. Barbeyrac's four charges (Epicurean self-interest, the state of war, unlimited royal power over state and religion, justice made by the sovereign) became the standard indictment. Their conflation of Hobbes's definition of justice with his laws of nature made Pufendorf look less Hobbesian than he was (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-barbeyrac-burlamaqui-natural-law).
  • Montesquieu attacked Hobbes on the state of nature and on justice reduced to the sovereign's will. Douglass calls him a limited but real forerunner of Rousseau (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-montesquieu-against-hobbes).
  • The Encyclopédie mostly repeated Barbeyrac. Diderot's "Droit naturel" is the exception: it introduced a universal general will against a Hobbesian "violent interlocutor", and Rousseau set out to revise it (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-diderot-encyclopedie).

The Hobbes Rousseau inherited was, in Douglass's word, "much diminished". See hobbism-douglass.

Chapter 2: the Discours sur l'inégalité

Rousseau's critique of the state of nature is read as a move inside modern natural law. It rests on three arguments: against Pufendorf as much as Hobbes, from free will, and from natural goodness. The Discours is a hypothetical history whose conclusions are meant to be real, because its aim is to separate the original from the artificial in man (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-state-of-nature-opening).

  • Two principles. Self-preservation, which is Hobbesian, and pity, which is set against Hobbes. Rousseau's physical account of natural man targets Pufendorf's natural weakness, which ends in a condition virtually identical to Hobbes's. L'Etat de guerre denies a natural state of war so that the rights of war (and with them slavery and sovereignty) cannot be derived from nature (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-state-of-nature-state-of-war).
  • Free will enters in language closer to Pufendorf and Burlamaqui than to a quarrel with materialist philosophes. It grounds the inalienability of freedom. Where Hobbes has one principle (self-preservation), Rousseau has two inalienable gifts of nature, life and freedom (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-free-will-moral-nature).
  • Natural goodness took shape where the luxury debate (Melon and the doux commerce theorists) met natural law. Rousseau separates amour de soi-même from amour-propre in direct response to Sorbière's French Hobbes, lets pity temper amour-propre, and so recovers a golden age as a genealogical stage (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-natural-goodness-golden-age).
  • Harmony and the "Hobbesian moment". Natural goodness means the absence of contradiction. The golden age is a normative model, not a destination. The war of all against all appears late, as a product of inflamed amour-propre and inequality, and is used to expose Hobbesian justifications of society as a trick of the rich (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-harmony-contradiction-hobbesian-moment).
  • The critique reappraised. Against the "apples and oranges" consensus and Pettit's defence of Hobbes, Douglass argues the critique bites. The state of nature is the standard against which sovereignty is justified. Hobbes's natural man is historically contingent, which threatens his claim to a science of politics. And the golden age raises the bar Hobbes had lowered (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-rousseaus-critique-reappraised).

Chapter 3: Du contrat social

Rousseau tries to combine modern sovereignty theory (Bodin, Hobbes, Pufendorf) with the classical republican demand that law rule over men (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-sovereignty-and-law-opening).

  • Free will against slavery. The opening chapters rest on the moral/physical distinction that Pufendorf-style critics had already used against Hobbes. Rousseau then treats those critics' two-pact theories as no better than Hobbes, because the people still alienates its sovereignty (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-free-will-slavery-obligation).
  • A Hobbesian problematic. The Manuscrit de Genève, written against Diderot's "Droit naturel", accepts Hobbes's problem of showing the "independent man" his interest in being just. Douglass calls the project "fundamentally Hobbesian" and admits this sits uneasily with the Discours (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-state-of-nature-to-political-society).
  • Sovereignty inverted. Rousseau keeps absolute, rights-free sovereignty and the body-politic vocabulary, but lodges sovereignty permanently in the people's general will: inalienable, unrepresentable and indivisible. See sovereignty-inverted-douglass and douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-sovereignty-inverted.
  • Freedom preserved. Equal legislative right and a general will that is each citizen's own will make obedience to law a dependence on things, not on men. Douglass argues against the "pluralistic reading" that the conflict of private and general wills is healthy (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-freedom-preserved).
  • Nature and denaturing. "Law before justice" is read on a Ciceronian pattern, not as Hobbesian positivism, whose target Douglass calls "largely a straw Hobbes". The legislator's denaturing is compatible with nature because independence was only a descriptive feature of the state of nature (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-law-nature-denaturing).
  • Civil religion. Rousseau praises Hobbes as the only Christian author to see both "the evil and the remedy", reuniting "the two heads of the eagle". Hobbes's aim is negative unity; Rousseau's is a cultivated love of fatherland (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-unity-civil-religion).

Chapter 4: ordering the passions

The republic's second half is "making men what one needs them to be", by ordering the passions so that love of fatherland becomes the dominant one (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-ordering-the-passions-opening).

  • The Augustinian background. A French tradition from Augustine to Nicole treated amour-propre as the vicious root of fallen man. Malebranche and later moralists neutralized it. Rousseau's claim that it is "naturally neutral" was aimed at a shared Augustinian-Hobbesian picture of man (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-neutralizing-amour-propre; see amour-propre-douglass).
  • Reason and passion. Reason for Rousseau is judgement. It orders the passions and does not master them, so politics must speak to the heart. This sets him against Stoic and proto-Kantian readings and against the Abbé de Saint-Pierre's "perfected reason" (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-reason-and-the-passions).
  • Free will and virtue. Freedom as conscious choice among inclinations, free of dependence on other wills, is thin enough to coexist with heavy will-formation. In a well-ordered republic virtue is love of fatherland, where duty and inclination coincide (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-free-will-and-virtue).
  • Love of fatherland. Public education generalizes amour de soi-même into love of fatherland and turns the positive branch of amour-propre (pride, not vanity) toward civic glory (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-love-of-fatherland).
  • Hobbes and fear. Hobbes too held that reason cannot overcome the passions, only direct them. His ordering rests on fear of death and of the state of nature, redirected away from "Spirits Invisible" (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-hobbes-and-fear).
  • Of love and fear. Natural goodness licenses a politics of love where Hobbes, read through Nicole's Augustinianism, could offer only a second-best politics of fear (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-of-love-and-fear; see love-and-fear-douglass).

Conclusion

Rousseau is neither Hobbesian nor anti-Hobbesian but "paradoxical" in his own sense: opposed to prevailing opinion. His invocations of Hobbes were polemical moves that exposed contemporaries as more Hobbesian than they would admit. The deepest disagreements concern human nature and free will (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-engagement-in-context). The two thinkers agreed on the problems (disordered passions, the need for a unified will, the civil subordination of religion) and differed on the solutions, a difference Douglass traces to competing metaphysics of free will (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-oppositions-and-affinities). The book ends with a limit. Rousseau's republic suits only naturally good peoples not yet corrupted by commerce, luxury and inequality, so it was never a live option for large commercial states. But his critique of commercial society stayed sharp enough to demand adam-smith's reply (douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-republics-naturally-good).

Throughlines

  • Hobbes versus Hobbism. The book's governing distinction. See hobbism-douglass.
  • Shared problems, opposed solutions. Affinity on unity, absolute sovereignty and civil religion. Opposition on the state of nature, the alienation of freedom and the passion politics should engage.
  • Free will as the hinge. The moral/physical distinction is unavailable to a materialist. It explains why force cannot oblige and why sovereignty cannot be alienated.
  • Nature as a regulative standard. The polarity that matters is accordance versus contradiction with nature, not nature versus society.
  • A secularized Fall. Original Sin is recast as entrenched inequality and luxury, which is also why the republic is fragile.

World Machines resonances (hedged)

Douglass is an intellectual historian, not a machine theorist, and makes no machine-theoretic claims. The notes repeatedly flag one loose rhyme. Rousseau's general will, legislator and civil religion are a design for making a people one, comparable at a distance to the legibility programmes of the modernity-machine and the-legible-commonwealth. But Rousseau's means are affective and civic rather than administrative or measurement-based, and he is hostile to the commercial modernity that machine describes. See making-a-people-one-douglass for that argument, stated with its limits. The Hobbism caricature, a received image assembled by many hands and stronger than any one text, loosely echoes the diffuse assembly of paradigms in history-machine, though Douglass frames it only as reception history. Rousseau's natural goodness is not the noble-savage trope: his golden age is a genealogical stage and a normative model, not a place to return to. The golden-age standard and the secular-fall narrative sit near kulturkritik, a link Douglass does not make.
