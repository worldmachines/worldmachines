Montaigne Complete Essays

Commons · Summaries · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Michel de Montaigne's Essays (To the Reader plus Books I to III, in Cotton's translation as edited by Hazlitt): a private self-portrait that grows from short exempla-driven essays into long Book III trials, arguing that custom rules belief and law, that reason cannot ground certainty, that judgment matters more than stored learning, and that death and the body are to be lived with rather than rehearsed.

Michel de Montaigne's Essays, “The Author to the Reader” followed by three books of chapters, read here in Charles Cotton's English translation (1685–86) as edited by W. Carew Hazlitt (1877). For the author see michel-de-montaigne.

What the book says it is

The preface presents the book as a private, domestic memorial, written for kinsfolk and friends who, “having lost me”, may recover “some traits of my conditions and humours”, with no aim at the reader's service or the author's glory. Its subject is Montaigne himself, shown “in mine own genuine, simple, and ordinary manner, without study and artifice”, so far as “public reverence hath permitted”; among peoples still under “nature's primitive laws” he would have painted himself “quite naked” (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch01-02-to-the-reader-various-ways). The rest of the book keeps testing this modest claim and enlarging it.

Shape

  • Book I is mostly short essays: a thesis is proposed, exempla from Plutarch, Seneca, Herodotus and the recent French wars are stacked against it, and often the essay then turns on its own thesis. The first chapter already does this and ends by calling man “a marvellous vain, fickle, and unstable subject” (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch01-02-to-the-reader-various-ways). Its longer pieces are on learning to die (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch19-study-philosophy-learn-die-1), custom (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch22-custom-should-not-easily-1), pedantry and the education of children (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch24-pedantry, montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch25-education-children-1), friendship (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch27-28-friendship) and the cannibals (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch30-cannibals).
  • Book II opens on the inconstancy of our actions (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch01-inconstancy-actions). Its centre is the Apology for Raimond Sebond, so long that it is read here in fifteen slices, from montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-1 to montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-15. Around it sit Of Books, Of Cruelty, Of Presumption and Of Giving the Lie, and the book closes on the stone and the distrust of medicine (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch37-resemblance-children-fathers-1).
  • Book III has fewer and much longer essays, several read in three to five slices: Of Repentance, Of Diversion, Upon Some Verses of Virgil, Of Coaches, Of the Art of Conference, Of Vanity, Of Managing the Will, Of Cripples, Of Physiognomy, and Of Experience, whose last pages close the whole book (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-5).

Method

  • The self as subject. “Others form man; I only report him”; “I do not paint its being, I paint its passage” (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch02-repentance). He reports that writing began when retirement left his mind running like a horse that has broken from its rider, and that he set its chimaeras down “hoping in time to make it ashamed of itself” (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch06-10-hour-parley-dangerous). See essay-as-self-study-montaigne.
  • The essay as trial. Judgment sounds a subject from the shore, gives “a stab, not so wide but as deep as I can”, and takes ignorance and doubt as its governing method (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch50-55-democritus-heraclitus). Opinions come “by disjointed articles”, not as a finished building (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-2).
  • Quotation and exempla. The book is built from borrowings that it also criticises. Pedants carry grain home without tasting it, and Montaigne admits his own book does the same (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch24-pedantry); he asks to be judged on how well he chooses and weighs what he borrows (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch10-books). Exempla are usually set against one another to be tested, and the reading notes keep his own voice apart from the Stoic, Pyrrhonist, Platonic and Plutarchan material he reports.
  • Contradiction and revision. “I add, but I correct not”, because “I now, and I anon, are two several persons” (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch09-vanity-2); pieces stay “as it came from the forge” so that his “mutations” show (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch37-resemblance-children-fathers-1). He may contradict himself but, in a saying he takes from Demades, he will “never contradict the truth” (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch02-repentance).

Main arguments

  • Custom rules belief, conscience and law. Custom works from infancy, dulls the senses and makes any practice seem rational; “The laws of conscience, which we pretend to be derived from nature, proceed from custom” (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch22-custom-should-not-easily-1). The same insight makes “barbarism” whatever is not in use in one's own country (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch30-cannibals) and drives the indictment of the conquest of Mexico and Peru (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch06-coaches-2). See custom-as-second-nature-montaigne.
  • Reason cannot ground certainty. The Apology strips man of his claimed superiority, sorts philosophy into dogmatists, Academics and Pyrrhonians, finds the senses unverifiable and judgment caught in an infinite regress, and leaves the emptied mind ready for faith (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-6, montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-15). See apology-for-raimond-sebond.
  • Judgment over learning. We should ask who is “better learned”, not who is “more learned”; learning must be digested, as bees make honey that is their own (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch24-pedantry, montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch25-education-children-1); conversation and contradiction exercise the mind more than books (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch08-art-conference-1). See judgment-over-learning-montaigne.
  • Death and the body. From the Ciceronian claim that to study philosophy is to learn to die (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch19-study-philosophy-learn-die-1), through his near-fatal fall from a horse (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch06-use-makes-perfect) and his kidney stone (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-3), to the judgment that philosophy's rehearsal of death torments more than death does (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch12-physiognomy-2) and the closing defence of enjoying one's own being (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-5). See learning-to-die-montaigne.
  • Public life in the civil wars. The Wars of Religion run through the book: each party draws religion “like wax into so many contrary figures” (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-1); innovation “gives form to injustice and tyranny” (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch09-vanity-2); as mayor of Bordeaux he kept the Mayor and Montaigne apart (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch10-managing-will-1); he refuses lying and betrayal even while granting that states need vicious services (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch01-profit-honesty); and he names cruelty “the very extreme of all vices” (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch11-cruelty). The essay on custom states the political rule: judge freely within, conform in conduct, and do not alter received laws (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch22-custom-should-not-easily-2).
  • Friendship. His friendship with Étienne de La Boétie is the one perfect instance he claims: “because it was he, because it was I” (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch27-28-friendship).

World Machines resonances, hedged

The reading notes repeatedly gloss custom as the invisible machinery that makes local arrangements feel like nature (habit-and-identity), and the essay as a technology of self-study (self-construction-through-writing, personal-essay-as-form), with the caution that self-construction is often only implicit in the text. The image of the state as a structure in which moving one brick is felt by the whole body is compared with the infrastructural view of a world-machines era, though Montaigne's frame is a single monarchy rather than an era (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch22-custom-should-not-easily-2). The Apology's “municipal law” of nature rhymes with the habit of treating one era's machinery as nature itself, though his frame is theological and sceptical rather than historical (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-8). The Rouen visitors' questions about obedience and inequality invite a Legibility reading that Montaigne does not draw himself (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch30-cannibals). Elsewhere in the wiki Montaigne appears through looking-forward-to-montaigne and eisenstein-printing-revolution.

Public life, fortune and war

Many of the shorter chapters concern the public world. On glory, honours and rank: Of Glory, Of Recompenses of Honour, the I.41-43 group on honour, inequality and sumptuary law, A Consideration upon Cicero, and Of the Inconvenience of Greatness (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch16-glory, montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch07-recompenses-honour, montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch41-43-not-communicate-mans, montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch39-consideration-cicero, montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch07-inconvenience-greatness); see glory-and-greatness-montaigne. On fortune and the limits of counsel: Of Prognostications and its companions, Various Events from the Same Counsel, the I.31-35 group and Of the Uncertainty of Our Judgment (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch11-15-prognostications, montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch23-various-events-from-counsel, montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch31-35-man-soberly-judge, montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch47-uncertainty-judgment); see outcomes-are-not-verdicts-montaigne. On heroic virtue: Of Cato the Younger, Of Virtue, the Defence of Seneca and Plutarch and Of the Most Excellent Men (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch36-37-cato-younger, montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch29-30-virtue, montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch32-defence-seneca-plutarch, montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch36-most-excellent-men); see heroic-virtue-montaigne.

Three chapters are about the craft of war. Of War Horses prefers fighting on foot with weapons one commands and distrusts the pistol (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch48-49-war-horses-destriers). Of the Arms of the Parthians blames soft modern soldiers who find armour heavy only for want of use (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch09-arms-parthians). The Observation on the Means to Carry On a War gathers Caesar's generalship from memory and endorses Marshal Strozzi's choice of Caesar as the soldier's breviary (montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch34-observation-means-carry-war). The I.34 essay records his father's proposal for a public office where wants and offers could be registered, a small design for matching needs that the note compares, loosely, to an information clearing-house (montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch31-35-man-soberly-judge).

Of Coaches opens far from the conquest material above. Montaigne starts from authors' habit of crowding plausible causes together, tests Plutarch's explanation of seasickness against his own experience, and argues that royal liberality is a weak virtue next to justice and that lavish spectacle is magnificence misspent (montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch06-coaches-1).
