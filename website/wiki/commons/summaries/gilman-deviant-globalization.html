Deviant Globalization (Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber, eds., 2011)

Commons · Summaries · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
Nils Gilman, Jesse Goldhammer and Steven Weber's edited volume Deviant Globalization (2011) frames the cross-border trade in illicit, informal and repugnant goods as the conjoined twin of mainstream globalization, central and permanent rather than a sideshow, and illustrates it with seventeen chapters of reprinted journalism and policy reports by other authors that were not written with the concept in mind.

Deviant Globalization: Black Market Economy in the 21st Century (Continuum, 2011) is an edited volume. Its argument belongs to the three editors, Nils Gilman, Jesse Goldhammer and Steven Weber, and is carried by their Introduction and Conclusion. Everything in between is by other authors: mostly reprinted magazine journalism, scholarly articles and policy reports from the 2000s, plus one previously unpublished chapter. The editors say none of these chapters was written with “deviant globalization” in mind, and that the selection is a lens, not a scientific sample. This page keeps the two layers apart: where a claim is the editors', it says so; where it comes from a chapter, it names that chapter's author.

The editors' thesis

The editors define deviant-globalization as cross-border economic activity that produces, moves and consumes illegal, informal or morally repugnant goods and services: narcotics, wildlife, dirty money, toxic waste, trafficked people, organs (gilman-deviant-globalization-intro-1-globalization). Their claims, in their own order:

  1. Entanglement. Deviant and mainstream globalization are one system: both are market-driven, both run on the same financial, communication and transport infrastructure, and that infrastructure is “dual use and value neutral”. As the systems grow more efficient they grow the formal economy and “its conjoined, deviant twin”. Every industry, they say, has a “deviant doppelgänger” (gilman-deviant-globalization-intro-2-deviant-globalization).
  2. Centrality. It is no sideshow, and, more boldly and conditional on the first two claims holding, it is changing the distribution of power in the world economy about as profoundly as any visible trend since 1989. The editors frame this as a claim that must survive scrutiny, and the Introduction offers few numbers for it (one UN figure and an unsourced estimate of Mexico's drug-trade workforce).
  3. Permanence and development. Their central normative claim is that deviant globalization is “a massive, permanent phenomenon” which, for many of the poor, is a route to development. On balance, they say, it has improved more lives than mainstream development projects. The note on the Introduction flags that this comparison is asserted there, not demonstrated. In the Conclusion they call it “radical experiments in actual development”, while refusing a Robin Hood reading: deviant entrepreneurs are “opportunistic parasites” running an ultra-libertarian development without durable public goods (gilman-deviant-globalization-conclusion-1).
  4. The state. Deviant entrepreneurs become political actors through money, violence and private provision of services. Most, the editors argue, want autonomous zones rather than the state, so deviant globalization thrives in weak, well-connected states rather than in truly failed ones. They make the neoliberal hollowing-out of Southern states (the Washington Consensus, structural adjustment) “a critical (perhaps even essential) prerequisite” (gilman-deviant-globalization-conclusion-1).

The editors define the activity by norm violation, not by illegality: it profits from knowing exploitation of moral and regulatory differences between places, which they call moral and regulatory arbitrage. They test and reject two common lenses, the affective “yuck factor” and the contractual “coercion factor”, and propose two alternatives: a psychodynamic “return of the repressed” account of demand and a regulatory-economics account of supply, in which prohibitions are control systems that shape illicit markets rather than instruments of a winnable war (gilman-deviant-globalization-intro-2-deviant-globalization).

Their policy conclusion is to manage rather than eradicate. Prohibitionist policy fails, they argue, through moralizing, the politician's need for visible progress, and denial that the deviant economy will be outgrown. Moralizing works like a partly effective antibiotic that selects for resistant organisms. They offer five principles: regulate rather than eliminate; let differing moral standards bargain through institutions; require transparency; err on the side of permissiveness while protecting the most vulnerable; and crowd out deviant innovation with legitimate innovation. They add two cautions, against turning back the clock and against doing nothing. Their last word is that there are “analytic solutions” but no policy solutions (gilman-deviant-globalization-conclusion-2).

Structure

The Introduction (two notes) defines the concept, sketches what globalization is and how old it is, and lays out the lenses and the plan. The editors present the chapters in four sections: human flows, drugs, resource flows, and new organizational forms, ending with insurgency and networked guerrillas. Because the activity is hidden by design, they prefer ethnographic closeness to formal statistics. The Conclusion (two notes) argues deviant globalization as development, as a challenge to the state, as a problem for liberal views of globalization, and as a policy problem.

Throughlines across the chapters

These are recurring features of the chapters' evidence. The chapters were not written to show them, and seeing them as one pattern is the editors' frame or this page's reading.

  • Shared infrastructure. Keefe says the snakehead boom was “no accident”, exploiting trade volume, communications and “friction-free” capital (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch01-keefe-snakeheads-2). Ambagtsheer shows the organ trade cannot exist without surgeons, nephrologists, ambulance drivers and morticians (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch04-ambagtsheer-organs). Black's arms ride the same dhows as cement and food (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch15-black-arms-gulf-of-aden). Berinato's criminals sell subscriptions and customer service on ordinary browsers, banks and ISPs (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch13-berinato-hacker-economy-1). Hari calls Dubai the global supply chain made visible, “Market Fundamentalist Globalization in One City” (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch05-hari-dubai-2). The editors' frame reads all of these as dual use.
  • Gradients make markets. Tax gaps (Guevara and co-authors), currency collapse and barter (Steinberg), disposal-cost gaps (Clapp), scarcity and price gaps (Ambagtsheer), and one country's logging ban exporting the harvest (Khatchadourian). See prohibition-and-price-gradients-make-the-market.
  • States as accomplices and hosts. In the chapters the state is rarely simply absent. Flynn's diplomat says stopping the bar trade would mean arresting half the senate (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch02-flynn-sex-trade-1). Ellis's “pliable sovereign” is the trafficker's ideal cover (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch07-ellis-west-africa-drugs-2). Junger's bunkering runs on rented soldiers (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch12-junger-blood-oil-2). Black cites a UN estimate that most Somali domestic-market weapons leak from the forces fighting the insurgency. See deviant-entrepreneurs-and-the-hollowed-state.
  • Illegibility and uncountable markets. Nearly every author says the numbers are guesses. Flynn quotes a State Department ambassador calling trafficking estimates an “intelligent guesstimate” (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch02-flynn-sex-trade-3); Keefe says official migration figures extrapolate from apprehensions (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch01-keefe-snakeheads-1); Baker and Joly concede their figures are loosely reliable (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch14-baker-joly-illicit-money). This is why the editors prefer ethnography to statistics.
  • Enforcement displaces rather than ends. Guevara and co-authors' customs chief: close one pier and two more appear (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch06-guevara-paraguay-tobacco). Berinato: takedowns produce new variants and servers (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch13-berinato-hacker-economy-2). Flynn: arrested girls are replaced (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch02-flynn-sex-trade-2); Moldova's antitrafficking unit reaches only the low-hanging fruit (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch02-flynn-sex-trade-4).

The chapters do not all share the editors' stance. Sullivan and Robb treat deviant actors as security threats to be countered, not as a development route (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch16-sullivan-criminal-insurgencies, gilman-deviant-globalization-ch17-robb-brave-new-war). Baker and Joly want illicit finance curtailed. Flynn condemns the trade he describes. Khatchadourian's Russian policeman remarks that nobody locally profits from the forest's disappearance, which sits in tension with the development thesis (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch11-khatchadourian-forests-2). Reding reads meth as a symptom of globalization's “fault lines” in a hollowed-out Iowa town, an interpretation built on one town's story (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch08-reding-inland-empire). Seager's short text frame stresses coercion and disparity (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch03-seager-sex-trafficking). Ellis reports, without endorsing, a Nigerian view of the drug trade as legitimate commerce and redistribution (gilman-deviant-globalization-ch07-ellis-west-africa-drugs-1).

Where it touches other pages

  • The editors' dual-use infrastructure is the same set of networks that farrell-underground-empire and weaponized-interdependence read from the state's chokepoint. The editors' interest is markets, norms and development, not chokepoint power, and their “strategic interdependence” policy game is related to Farrell and Newman's argument but is not the same.
  • The editors insist globalization is old and “anything but inevitable”. That resonates with globalisation-as-inevitability-rhetoric, though they are criticising a specific liberal claim, not the rhetoric of inevitability as such. Compare archaic-globalization-bayly on older globalizations, and aspatial-globalisation on their insistence that space still matters.
  • Several chapter notes glance at braudel-three-storey-house's shadowy lower and upper floors; none of the authors is a Braudelian.
  • Legibility: legibility-cuts-both-ways gathers the chapters' cases of forged or bought documents.

World Machines resonances (hedged)

The Conclusion notes read the collapse of the Cold War consensus on state-led development as rhyming with the late modernity-machine. They read the proliferation of non-state actors in weak-state zones, and prohibition selecting for adaptive actors, as close to the divergence-machine (gilman-deviant-globalization-conclusion-1, gilman-deviant-globalization-conclusion-2). The fit is loose. The editors are policy analysts writing about one roughly thirty-year neoliberal episode, not a thousand-year infrastructure cycle; they use no machine language and still give states a regulatory role. The chapter authors are journalists and policy analysts, and any world-machines reading of their material is the reader's, not theirs.
