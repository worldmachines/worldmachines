The Intellectuals and the Masses

Commons · Summaries · updated 23 September 2026 · source on GitHub
John Carey's 1992 study arguing that literary modernism was substantially a defensive reaction by the English literary intelligentsia against the newly literate mass public created by educational reform, and that 'the mass' itself is an invented category whose theological lineage runs from Augustine's massa damnata to the death camps.

Author: John Carey
Full title: The Intellectuals and the Masses: Pride and Prejudice among the Literary Intelligentsia, 1880–1939
Published: 1992; grew out of the 1989 T. S. Eliot Memorial Lectures at the University of Kent, extended with additional case-study chapters (carey-intellectuals-and-masses-preface)

The Argument

Carey's thesis is stated with unusual bluntness in his preface: modernist literature and art are best understood not as autonomous aesthetic developments but as a hostile reaction by the literary intelligentsia to the sudden emergence of a mass reading public. The 1870/1871 Education Acts produced, within a generation, a huge literate population that bypassed the traditional literary gatekeepers; Northcliffe's Daily Mail and the human-interest story then took over fiction's cultural function without needing novelists at all (carey-intellectuals-and-masses-ch1-revolt-of-the-masses).

The intellectual response, Carey argues, was functional and almost mechanical: modernist difficulty, obscurity and hostility to popular taste were not incidental stylistic features but purpose-built devices whose purpose was "to exclude these newly educated (or 'semi-educated') readers, and so to preserve the intellectual's seclusion from the 'mass'." A century of formal innovation — fragmentation, allusion, difficulty — is reframed as a defensive maneuver by an elite reacting to a literacy expansion it could not control.

The Semantic Move: "The Mass" Is a Fiction

Carey's methodological pivot is to refuse to treat "the mass" as a real social object at all. "The 'mass' is, of course, a fiction" — a term whose function is to strip the majority of people of human status, or at least of whatever qualities its users claim as their own distinguishing superiority. Rather than staging mass culture and its critics as two equally real social facts in conflict, Carey treats the category itself as a rhetorical instrument to be unmasked.

He then supplies its genealogy: Augustine's massa damnata / massa perditionis — the condemned mass of humanity minus the elect few — traced forward, deliberately shockingly, to Hitler's death camps and "this well-established Christian precedent for disposing of the surplus 'mass' by combustion." See the-mass-as-fiction for the concept and its recurring images.

Throughlines

Modernism as engineered exclusion

Having failed to prevent literacy itself, intellectuals set about making literature unreadable: Eliot's dictum that poets "must be difficult," Grigson's New Verse founded explicitly against mass values, Scrutiny capped at 750 copies as a badge of purity, and Ortega's The Dehumanization of Art, which openly welcomes modern art's function of segregating the public into "illustrious" and "vulgar" castes. Carey names this the theory of the avant-garde: nominally progressive, functionally reactionary, since its purpose is to claw back what universal literacy had granted. Treated as a WMP problem in illegibility-as-elite-defense.

Two rewritings, one operation

The book's subtlest chapter argues that flattering images of the mass do the same work as hostile ones. Pound's crowd-as-cherry-blossoms, Forster's divinized punkah-wallah, Woolf's begging old woman transfigured into an eternal earth-mother — each erases the actual social and economic reality of the person observed (poverty, caste, the fact of begging for coppers) behind an aesthetic screen. Description functions as elimination just as reliably as Le Bon's crowd-as-bacillus does.

The natural-aristocracy repair

Once birth, ecclesiastical sanction and a monopoly on literacy had stopped conferring distinction automatically, intellectuals needed "new proof of their distinction" and improvised it: esoteric knowledge (Lawrence, Yeats), transcendence of commercial time (Eliot), the sacredness of aesthetic rapture (Bell), outright self-election (Pound), and — when argument failed — divine sanction (Huxley, Greene, Steiner). Carey treats these less as philosophy than as symptom. Clive Bell's Civilization is the case that gives the game away: a leisured "civilizing élite" supported by public funds requires, Bell says explicitly, a class of slaves and an efficient police force to manage their discontent. This is a legitimacy crisis with the shape of a gramsci-gap — an expired warrant for authority and no organic successor — worked out in aesthetics rather than politics proper. It also sits directly across from the case for canonical selection made in western-canon.

From metaphor to policy

The book's most severe move is to refuse the distinction between rhetoric and program. Lawrence's 1908 "lethal chamber" letter, Yeats's engagement with sterilization science, Shaw's proposal that "Extermination must be put on a scientific basis if it is ever to be carried out humanely," Wells's New Republic killing the unfit "with an opiate," Lewis's inbred examination-entry caste: these are shown as operational, not ornamental. The line is completed in carey-intellectuals-and-masses-ch9b-hitlers-intellectual-programme, where Carey contests Trevor-Roper's dismissal of Hitler's cultural views as "trivial, half-baked and disgusting" and shows each element — state arts subsidy, veneration of Shakespeare and Goethe, the "divine spark" of genius, contempt for gutter journalism and cinema, the aristocratic principle against democracy — with a direct counterpart among respected English intellectuals. "The tragedy of Mein Kampf is that it was not, in many respects, a deviant work but one firmly rooted in European intellectual orthodoxy."

The counter-tradition

Carey is not writing a prosecution brief only. A minority resisted: Chesterton, Shan Bullock (whose Robert Thorne dissolves into "thousands like him... Twopenny clerks" and yet "has a soul, this figure that I see in the crowd"), Conan Doyle, Kipling, and later Betjeman and Stevie Smith, who inhabit the suburb rather than judging it. Above all carey-intellectuals-and-masses-ch8-narrowing-the-abyss-bennett installs Arnold Bennett as "the hero of this book": a pawnbroker's son and night-school matriculant whose fiction locates delicacy and "aristocratic instinct" in drapers' apprentices and housemaids, whose criticism championed Dostoevsky, Chekhov, Conrad, Joyce, Proust and the unknown Faulkner, and who took domestic technology — plumbing, electricity, labour-saving design that "enriched the lives of millions of women" — as seriously as art.

Tensions Inside the Book

Carey's strongest chapters are the ones where the indictment does not close cleanly. He concedes that the suburban grievance began in a real ecological loss — documented in housing statistics and Times editorials — before it "fused with contempt for those who lived in them." He argues that Gissing's granular, compassionate knowledge of individual poverty is precisely what makes the novels valuable even as his generalizing doctrine makes his legacy pernicious. And his verdict on Wells is that the irresolution, not the doctrine, is what makes him worth reading: "despite his contempt for failures, failures are his only successes." See h-g-wells.

The carey-intellectuals-and-masses-postscript extends the story past 1939. Television, Carey grants, gave ordinary audiences unprecedented access to traditional culture, and poets after Eliot (Larkin, Hughes, Heaney) abandoned the demand for difficulty. But post-structuralist "theory" — deliberate unintelligibility, the doctrine that biography and "human interest" are irrelevant to a text — is read as the same exclusion strategy reinvented once democratized media closed off modernism's avenue, with Barthes's "aristocratic readers" and his contempt for plastic as the old snobbery in new dress.

In World Machines Terms

Universal education is a textbook modernity-machine legibility project: a population standardized, schooled and scaled (legibility-as-machine-core). Carey's material records what happened when that project succeeded and the incumbent literary culture found itself outnumbered by readers it had not authorized. The response was not to reassert control over the newly legible mass but to flee upward into deliberate obscurity — the argument developed in illegibility-as-elite-defense. The category the intellectuals built to justify that flight is treated in the-mass-as-fiction, and its administrative terminus connects to necropolitics.
