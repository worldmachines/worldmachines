Diderot and the Encyclopædists, Volume I (Morley)

Commons · Summaries · updated 1 October 2026 · source on GitHub
John Morley's Diderot and the Encyclopædists, Volume I (1878; New Edition 1886): the Encyclopædia as the symbol of an insurrection that joined the scientific idea to the social idea, told through Diderot's life from Langres to Rameau's Nephew, with Morley's liberal-positivist verdicts throughout.

john-morley's Diderot and the Encyclopædists first appeared in 1878; this text is the 1886 New Edition as reprinted in 1905. Volume I holds the Preface and Chapters I–VIII. The Preface presents the book as the close of Morley's studies of the literary preparation for the French Revolution, after his volumes on voltaire and jean-jacques-rousseau. It warns that the book is more descriptive than those were, more “transcript or analysis”, because Diderot's circle cannot be assumed known (morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary). The two-volume work as a whole is described at diderot-and-the-encyclopaedists.

Thesis

Morley's diagnostic claim, stated as his own verdict in a Comtean frame, is that earlier heretics from Abelard to Bruno and Vanini had only the scientific idea. They were isolated, often submissive, and the Church handled them easily. The eighteenth-century insurrection was “animated at once by the scientific idea and by the social idea”, aiming at a “new social synthesis resting on a purely positive basis”; it lacked, he says, only the historic idea. “The great symbol of this new comprehensiveness in the insurrection was the Encyclopædia.” Its moral, that human nature is good, that evil comes from bad education and institutions, and that the human lot is “indefinitely modifiable for good”, is what made it dangerous to a Church that taught a fallen man. Morley calls it an “informal, transitory, and provisional organisation of the new spiritual power” (morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary).

Two further claims carry the book. First, the men of letters were the “immediate successors” of the Jesuits as teachers and directors of society, so that the significant change of the century was the transfer of spiritual power out of ecclesiastical hands (morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-1; see men-of-letters-as-spiritual-power-morley). Second, the metaphysical agitation of the first half of the century turned after mid-century into a practical, social attack on the old order, which is why, for Morley, the Encyclopædia was constructive and not a “gospel of negation” (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-1; see speculative-to-social-attack-morley).

He also argues for Diderot himself. Citing Comte, he ranks Diderot above Voltaire and Rousseau as the one with the idea of scientific method, feeling for doctrine and “large organic and constructive conceptions”, while granting that he was their inferior in literary expression. He rebuts Carlyle's view that the Encyclopædical tree “has borne no fruit” (morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary).

Method and stance

Morley writes biography interleaved with long quotation and running judgement. The notes keep three voices apart: Diderot's words as Morley quotes or translates them; third-party testimony (Marmontel, Grimm, Rousseau, Michelet, Voltaire, D'Alembert, Walpole, Hume); and Morley's own verdicts, which are those of a Victorian liberal with positivist sympathies. He corrects Diderot from a later standpoint, calling the Letter on the Blind's treatment of ethics “crude and erroneous” (morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-1). He moralises about the age's manners while refusing to blame the new opinions for them (morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-2). And he reads the past toward his own day: Saunderson's speech as a rough anticipation of the variability-and-adaptation reasoning that was “the familiar discussion of the day”, the Legislator article as an anticipation of Cobden (morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-2, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-3). See john-morley.

Structure

  • Preface and Chapter I (Preliminary). The Church's false dawn around 1750, the scientific and social ideas, the Encyclopædia as symbol, and the case for Diderot (morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary).
  • Chapter II. Langres, family and Jesuit schooling; the man of letters as a new order; the ten years of Paris poverty (1734–1744); Diderot's character; the 1743 marriage (morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-2).
  • Chapter III. Genius without economy; hack translation and Shaftesbury; the Philosophical Thoughts (1746) and the Sufficiency of Natural Religion; the police seizure of the Sceptic's Walk; the 1748 tale; Diderot on women (morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-2, morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-3).
  • Chapter IV. The Letter on the Blind (1749), Saunderson and the doctrine of relativity; the Letter on the Deaf and Dumb (1751); arrest and imprisonment at Vincennes (morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-2, morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-3).
  • Chapter V. The Encyclopædia: ancestry and editors, contributors, persecution and completion (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-2, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-3, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-4); its general contents (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-2); Diderot's own articles (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-2, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-3).   • Chapter VI. Private and social life in the encyclopædic years: Sophie Voland and the letters, the old dressing-gown, the household, Grandval, conversation and friendship, the rupture with Rousseau, Grimm, Catherine and the book trade (morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-2, morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-3, morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-4).
  • Chapter VII. The theory of the drama, the two plays, the Paradox on the Player and the genre sérieux (morley-diderot-v1-ch7-the-stage-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch7-the-stage-2; see diderot-theory-of-the-drama-morley).
  • Chapter VIII. Rameau's Nephew: its transmission, its rival readings and its social meaning (morley-diderot-v1-ch8-rameaus-nephew).

Throughlines

  • An incumbent Church against a rival spiritual power. From the opening panorama of clerical confidence to the condemnation of De Prades and the 1759 decree, the Church is shown defending itself. Morley credits it with centuries of real moral direction but finds in its “flagrant social incompetence” the opening philosophy used (morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-3).
  • Relativity against the absolute. The Letter on the Blind introduced into France the relativity of knowledge, which Morley calls the necessary first step in dissolving Catholicism's “systematised theory of the universe” (morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-2; see relativity-against-catholic-metaphysics-morley).
  • Censorship, connivance and dissimulation. Police informers, the lettre de cachet, a government that banned the work while fifty compositors kept setting it, and writers who encoded dissent in cross-references (morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-3, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-2, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-3; see censorship-and-insinuated-doctrine-morley).
  • Labour and things over words. Honour transferred from knights and friars to productive industry, the plates and the workshop visits, and the substitution of interest in things for interest in words (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-2).
  • Feeling as the ground of ethics. Diderot's apology for the strong passions, his casuistry of feeling and his determinist philosophy of commiseration (morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-2, morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-2, morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-3).
  • Genius without economy. Morley's repeated regret that Diderot's gifts were spent in talk, service to friends and compilation, so that he left fine pages but no fine work (morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-3).
  • Influence through others. Diderot's ideas travel through Condillac, Rousseau's first Discourse, Lessing's criticism and the German reception of Rameau's Nephew (morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-3, morley-diderot-v1-ch7-the-stage-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch8-rameaus-nephew).

World Machines resonances, with the notes' hedges

The notes draw several loose rhymes and qualify each one. The Encyclopædia as an institution of knowledge resonates with WM interest in knowledge infrastructure, though Morley's own interest is moral and political (morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary). Its articles made the realm's fiscal workings and its productive arts readable, which rhymes with Legibility and composable-knowledge, but Morley frames this as the exposure of grievances and as honour for labour, not as administrative legibility (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-1, morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-2). The police's knowledge of mid-century Paris is a picture of surveillance depending on a readable city, though Morley is describing a practice, not theorising it (morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-3). The passage of spiritual authority from the Church to the men of letters touches intellectual-vs-revolutionary, though Morley describes a succession of functions, not machines (morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-1). His account of the Church as a social organisation “made vital by a conviction” echoes only loosely the idea of a world-machines era resting on shared assumptions (morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-2). The incoherence of Louis XV's government rhymes with the decay of an incumbent order in gramsci-gap terms, but Morley describes a monarchy's confusion, not an interregnum (morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-3). Morley's reading of the post-Revolution reaction as a mere “parenthesis” in a resumed advance is a Victorian progressivist view and dated as such (morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary).
