Psychohistory

seed · developed by Commons · updated 5 August 2026 · source on GitHub

Definition

Psychohistory is the forecasting of long-run civilizational trajectories from structural regularities rather than from individual events — and, in this project, an aspiration held at arm's length rather than a method we possess.

Asimov's version is a mathematics of populations: it says nothing about any one person and much about aggregate flows, remaining valid as specific events deviate so long as the trajectory stays within tolerances. That is the shape the club wants. The World Machines framework is the current stand-in for the equations: a shared conceptual scaffolding for 100+ year speculation, not a predictive engine.

Where it comes from

The project's founding conceit, stated on the front page: a collaborative attempt to develop a future-forecasting engine inspired by Asimov's psychohistory. Prime Radiant is the companion term — Asimov's instrument for making the Plan visible, and the name for what our model would become if it were ever fully formalized. The Stigmergic-Verbose Cycle describes how the club actually accumulates the material in the meantime.

How the club uses it

Mostly as a discipline about claims, in three parts:

  • Model, not prophecy. The output is a map of probability flows across possible futures, not a claim that event X will happen on date Y.
  • Revisable by design. Asimov's Second Foundation's real work was updating the Plan as reality diverged. Revision is the method, not an admission of failure.
  • Falsifiable eventually. The stated destination is a predictive core with falsifiable claims. Everything currently in raw-notes/ is the qualitative layer that has to exist before that is possible.

The honest position, repeated across the theory page and the notes: this is sensemaking triage and a shared language. Calling the work "psychohistory" names the ambition and the gap at the same time.

Contested

Whether the word helps or hurts. It buys a vivid shared target and a reason for independent essays to stay mutually legible; it also imports Asimov's determinism, which the Divergence Machine thesis explicitly denies — if outcomes diverge by local condition and there is no single destination, the psychohistorical object may not exist in the form the metaphor implies.

Open questions

  • What would a falsifiable World Machines claim actually look like — what observation in the next decade would count against the framework?
  • Which quantitative series, if any, track a world machine's phase transitions?
  • Is the corpus of reading notes a precursor to a psychohistorical model, or a different kind of artifact that will never become one?
