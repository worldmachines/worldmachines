When the Prophecy Becomes a Player

Brandon · Essays · updated 6 September 2026 · source on GitHub

A note on epistemic exposure and social prediction

In a recent SIGPSY study group, we read Robert Merton's 1948 essay, "The Self-Fulfilling Prophecy". Merton begins by describing a bank run: a scenario in which a solvent bank collapses after its depositors become convinced that it is insolvent. Their belief produces a run, the run destroys the bank, and the bank's eventual collapse appears to validate the belief that caused it. A false definition of the situation becomes part of the situation and creates the conditions that make it true.

Merton's point extends beyond self-fulfilling prophecies. He mentions the inverse case as well: a prophecy can provoke behavior that prevents the predicted outcome from occurring. In both cases, the prediction is not an inert description. Once people become aware of it, they may begin to act in relation to it. Their actions can alter the system the prediction was attempting to describe.

Shortly afterward, I read Venkatesh Rao's "The Curiously Playable Universe". Rao is interested in what allows a domain of knowledge to become playable. A playable domain has something resembling a state, a reasonably stable collection of possible moves, repeatable interactions, and feedback that allows experience to accumulate. Mathematics, programming, and increasingly physics have been turned into environments where humans and machines can learn through repeated play.

Reading Rao after Merton clarified one of the basic problems facing psychohistory. The people inside a social system can encounter a description of their own state. They can interpret it, organize around it, resist it, exploit it, or attempt to prevent the outcome it predicts. The description then becomes another move within the domain. The prophecy becomes a player.

I think we need a term for the point at which this happens. I propose epistemic exposure.

Epistemic exposure is the extent and manner in which actors within a social system become aware of a claim or prediction about that system, creating the possibility that their response will alter the conditions or outcome being predicted.

Epistemic exposure is not the same thing as publicity. A prediction published online and ignored may have very little epistemic exposure. A private prediction delivered to one person with the power to act may have a great deal. Who encounters the prophecy matters, as do its credibility, timing, framing, and usefulness as a guide to action.

Epistemic exposure is also distinct from social reflexivity. Exposure creates the possibility of a reflexive response: a prophecy becomes cognitively available to actors positioned to respond. Social reflexivity is the process by which those actors interpret knowledge about their own system and alter their behavior in response. A reflexive effect occurs only when that response changes the system's state or trajectory. Exposure can therefore occur without producing a reflexive effect. People may ignore the prophecy, lack the influence to change the system, or respond in ways that cancel one another out. We might be able to observe that a prophecy reached certain people while remaining uncertain about what influence it had. Exposure is part of the history of the prophecy. A reflexive effect is a causal inference we might draw from that history.

This distinction matters for SIGPSY because our venture is not merely to explain the past. We are interested in whether models of historical change can support conditional claims about the future. But the success or failure of such a claim could become difficult to interpret once the claim circulates. A revolution predicted in advance might be accelerated by people who welcome it, delayed by people who fear it, or prevented by institutions that respond to the warning. A prophecy that fails because people successfully avert it is different from a prophecy that fails because its model was wrong. Likewise, a prophecy that helps bring about its own fulfillment cannot be treated as straightforward evidence that the original model accurately described an inevitable process.

Asimov's Foundation offers one response to this problem. The Seldon Plan works only while the population it predicts remains unaware of the Plan's details. The Prime Radiant is controlled by the hidden Second Foundation, whose members can inspect and adjust the model while most of the galaxy cannot. Controlled epistemic exposure is built into Asimov's psychohistory.

That solution sits uneasily with the emerging ethos of the World Machines project. World Machines has developed through public essays, attributed working notes, shared reading, and inspectable artifacts. A hidden group protecting the validity of its prophecies by keeping everyone else ignorant would reproduce a priesthood model that the project has so far moved away from.

But while adopting secrecy is not an option for our psychohistory project, failing to account for epistemic exposure also seems unwise. Epistemic exposure is something an open psychohistorical project will probably eventually need to understand. Every prophecy has an exposure history: where it traveled, who encountered it, how it was interpreted, and what opportunities those people had to act upon it. That history may be as relevant to evaluating the prophecy as its original conditions and confidence.

I don't yet have a proposal for what to do with the concept of epistemic exposure. Perhaps it should become a metric. Perhaps it could be documented as part of a prophecy record. Perhaps different kinds of prophecies will require different postures toward circulation (tactical prophecy making). Perhaps exposure itself will prove too difficult to separate from the broader causal processes being modeled. These are questions for further discussion and study.

For now, I think it's enough to bring the concept into the SIGPSY lexicon. Prophecy-making in a social domain introduces a variable that is largely absent from prediction in the natural sciences: the possibility that the predicted system will learn about the prediction. If SIGPSY intends to make prophecies, we need language for describing what happens when those prophecies enter the systems they describe. Epistemic exposure is my proposed starting point.
