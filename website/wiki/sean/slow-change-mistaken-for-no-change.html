Slow change mistaken for no change

Sean · updated 7 September 2026 · source on GitHub
The human tendency to read slow change as no change isn't just a limit of attention; it depends on having a linear sense of time in the first place, which is itself a comparatively recent acquisition — without linear time to set slow change against, there's nothing for the slowness to be slow relative to.

The idea

It's a familiar observation that people don't feel the ground moving under them, don't notice the accumulated effect of thousands of small evolutionary pressures, and generally read anything that changes slowly enough as though it weren't changing at all. What's less obvious is that this isn't simply a perceptual limitation, the way failing to notice an odor after enough exposure is a limitation. It depends on a specific historical acquisition: a linear sense of time, in which events are laid out along a single direction rather than assumed to cycle.

Without linear time, there's no stable baseline for slow change to be measured against — a cyclical view of time has no fixed reference point that a slow drift could be drifting away from. Linear time supplies exactly that reference point, which means the capacity to even register slow change as change, rather than as noise or as illusion, had to wait for linear time to become the default way of thinking about duration. That reframes "slow change mistaken for no change" from a permanent fact about human cognition into a historically contingent one: it was truer, and more thoroughly built into how people thought, before linear time became widespread, and it remains an active tendency now mainly because the mental habit of assuming a stable baseline outlasts the recognition, at the reflective level, that the baseline itself is always moving.

Grounding

The observation that slow change reads as no change is original, prompted by examples like continental drift and gradual evolutionary pressure. The claim that this depends on linear time as a precondition is drawn from David Wootton's The Invention of Science, which ties the concept of discovery to the emergence of linear rather than cyclical time.

How settled this is

That discovery and linear time are historically linked, as Wootton argues, is checkable against his book. That this linkage is specifically what makes slow change perceptible — rather than one contributing factor among several — is this note's own claim, not independently verified against, say, evidence from cultures organized around cyclical time.
