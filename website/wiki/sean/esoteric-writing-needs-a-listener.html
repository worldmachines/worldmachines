Esoteric writing needs a listener

Sean · updated 11 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Philosophy taught as conversation reads differently than philosophy taught as a manual: esoteric writing relies on context, tone, and a present interlocutor the way a technical manual does not. The contrast raises a real question about which register a given tradition's texts are actually written in.

The idea

One account of esoteric philosophical writing holds that philosophy is properly taught through conversation — a claim that explains why Plato reads as alive in a way a philosophical treatise laid out step by step does not. Aristotle's surviving works read more like lecture notes; Plato's dialogues read like transcripts of an exchange, where meaning depends on who is speaking, to whom, and what's being withheld or implied. On this account, genuinely esoteric writing behaves like a conversation even on the page: it leans on context, tone, and the presence of an interlocutor who can be addressed differently than a general reader.

That contrast sharpens by comparison with texts written in a different register entirely — closer to a technical manual, laying out steps plainly because the goal is replicable practice rather than a relationship with a reader. A text like that can still be difficult, even initiatory, without being esoteric in the conversational sense: it isn't withholding through implication, it's just dense with technical claims. Whether a given tradition's central texts are esoteric-as-conversation or esoteric-as- manual is a real question about how that tradition expects to be transmitted, not just a stylistic observation.

Grounding

The esoteric-writing-as-conversation argument is drawn from Arthur Melzer's Philosophy Between the Lines, by way of a review of that book. The contrast with manual-style technical writing, and the suggestion that a tradition's central texts can be sorted by which register they're actually written in, is original to this note.

How settled this is

That Melzer's book argues philosophy has historically often been written esoterically, and that this connects to a claim about conversation as philosophy's proper medium, is checkable against Philosophy Between the Lines directly. Sorting any particular tradition's texts into "conversational" versus "manual" registers is this note's own reading, not a claim from Melzer, and hasn't been tested against much beyond the two comparisons made here.
