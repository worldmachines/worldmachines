Is an AI's causal story fictive or factive?

Sean · updated 13 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Kant's account of causation as necessarily supplied by the mind rather than read straight off the world, and historical narrative's own habit of adding causation to a sequence of events, raise a genuine question about an AI's explanation of why something happened: is it more like a scientific claim about the world's structure, or more like a historian's necessarily-constructed causal story?

The idea

Kant's account of causation holds that cause-and-effect isn't simply read off the world as it's encountered; it's a category the mind necessarily supplies in order to have coherent experience at all — necessary, not arbitrary, but supplied rather than discovered. Historical writing does something similar at a higher, more visible level: a historical narrative is not identical with what happened, it's a story about what happened, with causation added by the historian's account of why one event led to another — an account that is doing real interpretive work, not merely transcribing a causal chain the events already carried.

That raises a specific question about an AI system's causal explanations. When a model explains why something happened — why a decision produced a given outcome, why a trend moved the way it did — is that explanation more like a factive claim, a report on the world's actual causal structure, or more like a historian's necessarily-constructed narrative: organized, plausible, useful for understanding, but not simply read off a reality that already carried the causal story pre-attached? The two possibilities call for different kinds of scrutiny. A factive claim can be checked against the world directly. A fictive one — fictive in the sense that a historian's narrative is fictive, not in the sense of being false — has to be evaluated by different standards: coherence, plausibility, whose interests the particular causal emphasis serves.

Grounding

Kant's account of causation as a category necessarily supplied by the understanding is drawn from the Critique of Pure Reason. The historical-narrative comparison and the extension to AI-generated causal explanations are original to this note.

How settled this is

Kant's claim about causation is a real, extensively discussed philosophical position, checkable directly against the text (and against the large secondary literature interpreting it). The extension to whether AI's causal explanations are fictive or factive is this note's own open question — it isn't answered here, and it isn't clear what kind of evidence would settle it.

Open

Is there any way to actually test whether an AI's causal explanations function more like factive claims or more like historical narrative, or is the distinction itself too coarse to apply cleanly to how these systems generate explanations in the first place?
