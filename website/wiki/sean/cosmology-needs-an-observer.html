Cosmology needs an observer

Sean · updated 11 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Cosmology, cosmography, and cosmogony aren't three words for the same thing: on one distinction, cosmology specifically requires a human observer, which is part of why \"nature\" and \"the environment\" aren't quite interchangeable either.

The idea

Three related words get used almost interchangeably and shouldn't be: cosmogony is an account of how the cosmos came to be; cosmography is a description or mapping of the cosmos as it stands; cosmology, on a distinction worth keeping, is neither of those by itself — it's an account of the cosmos as ordered for someone, which means it requires a human observer in a way the other two don't. A cosmogony can be told with no one watching. A cosmology, on this reading, is only a cosmology once there's a vantage from which the order matters.

That distinction has a quiet consequence for a nearby pair of words: "nature" and "the environment." "The environment" names what surrounds an observer functionally — the set of conditions a life has to operate within, closer to cosmography's mapping-without-a-stake. "Nature," in the older, richer sense the Romantic and pre-Romantic traditions meant by it, carries something closer to cosmology's requirement of an ordered relationship to an observer — not just what's around you, but what your being here is answerable to. Collapsing the two words into synonyms loses exactly the distinction that made "nature" worth having a separate word for in the first place.

Grounding

The cosmology/cosmography/cosmogony distinction, and the claim that cosmology specifically requires a human observer, is drawn from Rémi Brague's writing on the history of cosmology. The extension to distinguish "nature" from "the environment" along the same axis is original to this note.

How settled this is

Brague's tripartite distinction is a scholarly argument about the history of the concept of cosmology, checkable against his work directly. The application to "nature" versus "the environment" as everyday words is this note's own extension — a plausible mapping, not a claim Brague makes about ordinary usage.
