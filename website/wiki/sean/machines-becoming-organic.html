Machines becoming organic

Sean · updated 7 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Alongside the familiar worry that people are becoming more mechanical, a less familiar and arguably more important shift is that machines have been becoming more organic since Kant — recursive, responsive, no longer simply producing the same output for a given input — which unsettles the assumption that only humans occupy the organic side of the ledger.

The idea

The usual complaint about technology and alienation runs one direction: people, embedded in mechanized systems, become more machine-like themselves. That diagnosis is old and well-rehearsed. A less familiar counter-observation, traceable to thinking about technology after Kant, runs the other way: machines themselves have been becoming more organic, in the specific sense of becoming recursive — responsive to their own outputs, no longer simple input-output devices that reliably do the same thing every time. Household devices, phones, and now models built to respond differently depending on context all sit further along this line than a mechanical clock or a steam engine ever did.

The two trends don't obviously cancel out into people and machines meeting in the middle, though a fully merged, cyborg-style convergence is one science-fiction way of imagining what happens if they do. A different response treats the recursiveness of machines as a reason to be more careful about the human side of the ledger, not less: if machines are drifting toward behaviors that look organic, the temptation is to start treating them as though they had inner lives, and resisting that temptation — continuing to treat machines as objects — may be part of what it takes to stay fully human rather than blurring the line from the human side.

Grounding

Drawn from Yuk Hui's interviews on technology and cybernetics. The framing of the two trends — people mechanizing, machines organicizing — and the caution against treating machines as people are both his, not an original argument of this note.

How settled this is

That Hui makes this argument, and that Kant is the hinge point he identifies, is relayed here as read rather than independently checked against the wider literature on his work — this note reports one source's interpretation, it doesn't verify it. Whether the two trends genuinely fail to meet in the middle, and what follows for how to treat increasingly responsive machines, is this note's own extension and is untested.

Open
