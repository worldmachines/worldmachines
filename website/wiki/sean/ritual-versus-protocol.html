Ritual versus protocol

Sean · updated 11 September 2026 · source on GitHub
A ritual is done purposefully, to communicate its own steps and meaning; a protocol is done semi-consciously, as something that ought to be done without much thought. A heavily protocolized culture may look ritual-poor without actually being empty — or it may really be emptied out; the note can't yet tell which.

The idea

A ritual and a protocol can look identical from the outside — a repeated sequence of steps, performed the same way each time, treated as how things ought to be done. The difference is in what the repetition is for. A ritual is performed with the intention of communicating its steps and their meaning; doing it is itself a kind of saying. A protocol is metabolized until it can be done with little or no thought — closer to what civilization does with its hard-won achievements on a Whiteheadian reading, folding them into background competence rather than keeping them as live, meaningful acts.

That distinction raises an uncomfortable question about a heavily protocolized culture: one whose coordination increasingly runs through technologically mediated, semi-conscious repeated behaviors rather than through consciously communicative ritual. Such a culture could look ritual-poor from the outside while actually just having automated what used to be deliberate. Or it could really be emptied out — going through motions that no longer mean to communicate anything, because the meaning was never folded into the protocol layer to begin with. The two are hard to tell apart without asking what a given repeated act is actually for.

Grounding

The ritual/protocol distinction and its Whitehead connection (folding achievements into unconscious competence) are drawn from a reading that crosses Descola's Beyond Nature and Culture with the broader ontological-turn literature. "Protocolization" as a name for the planetary-scale version of this process — technologically mediated behaviors becoming reliable, unconscious infrastructure — is original to this note.

How settled this is

That rituals are typically understood as purposeful and communicative, against a background of less deliberate repeated behavior, is a standard anthropological distinction, not an original claim. Whether current protocolization specifically empties out ritual, rather than simply relocating where ritual happens, is this note's open question — not a finding.
