Three occupiable positions, not one Venn diagram

Sean · updated 11 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Treating magic, science, and religion as three fixed, overlapping circles assumes a stable Venn diagram. A better picture may be three positions that different practices occupy differently across history — positions that come loosest to tell apart, on one account, precisely where cause and effect aren't clear.

The idea

The default image for how magic, science, and religion relate is a Venn diagram — three overlapping circles, with some practices sitting cleanly inside one and others in the shared regions. That image assumes the three circles are stable across history, which the anthropological record doesn't really support. Marcel Mauss's early theory of magic already argued against treating "magic" as a fixed category at all, calling it something closer to whatever a given society's dominant knowledge system excludes and finds alien. Stanley Tambiah's later account divides the territory differently again — not into three circles but into two realms, causality and participation, with science claiming causality and religion, art, and magic sharing participation, and argues for ways the realms need each other rather than staying cleanly apart.

Put together, this suggests magic, science, and religion are better understood as positions that get occupied and reassigned across history than as three fixed content-areas. What's genuinely interesting is Tambiah's suggestion about when the realms come back together: not randomly, but specifically where cause and effect are unclear — where a system produces outcomes without providing a legible mechanism, so prediction and something more like petition end up living in the same practice. A system whose outputs are governed by a known but non-deterministic process fits that description unusually well.

Grounding

Drawn from Marcel Mauss's A General Theory of Magic (the argument against treating magic as a fixed category) and Stanley Tambiah's Magic, Science, Religion, and the Scope of Rationality (the causality/participation split and the claim about their reconvergence). The application to systems whose outputs are non-deterministic but not fully opaque is original to this note.

How settled this is

Mauss's and Tambiah's arguments are established anthropological and historical scholarship, checkable directly against those two books. The specific extension — that causality and participation reconverge wherever cause and effect go unclear, and that this describes certain modern technical systems — is this note's own reading of Tambiah's framework, not a claim either source makes about technology specifically.
