Blumenberg and ELIZA

Sean · updated 5 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Blumenberg's little-known reading of Weizenbaum's ELIZA argues that intelligence, human included, is intermittent - only unreliably connected to a world outside itself - which de-natures the human rather than anthropomorphizing the machine, and reframes AI critique as a question of rhetoric rather than whether machines really understand.

The idea

Long before current large language models, Blumenberg wrote a little-known text on Joseph Weizenbaum's ELIZA, one of the first AI systems to interact successfully with humans. Weatherby's reading of that text (in the 2022 New German Critique special issue on Blumenberg) finds a phenomenological critique of interactive AI that reads as strikingly current.

The core move is not to ask whether the machine really understands. Blumenberg's claim is that intelligence of any kind - human included - is "intermittent": only unreliably and periodically connected to a world outside itself, constituting and continually re-constituting itself, patching its own interruptions. That claim does real work because it runs in both directions at once. It lets Blumenberg compare human consciousness to a computer without anthropomorphizing the machine, because the comparison isn't "the machine is like a mind" - it's "the mind was already structurally like a machine's alternation of data and instructions." The comparison de-natures the human rather than elevating the machine.

ELIZA, in this reading, is of the "rhetorical" type: it puts a user in a space of artificial meaning, or better, it shows how artificial meaning was in the first place. The program doesn't successfully fake human semantics by accident; its success reveals that human meaning-making was always a rhetorical production rather than a transparent window onto genuine understanding. The polemical payoff is a way to sidestep the usual argument entirely - instead of litigating whether AI has "real" semantics (a functional or anthropological question that never gets settled), critique the artifact on the plane of rhetoric and semiotics, where its success or failure can actually be evaluated.

Where it comes from

Nicholas Weatherby, "Intermittent Legitimacy: Hans Blumenberg and Artificial Intelligence," in Hans Blumenberg at 101, special issue of New German Critique 145, Vol. 49, No. 1 (February 2022), ed. Hannes Bajohr (Duke University Press). Reading notes captured 2026-07-17 in the vault; primary paper text is in-copyright (New German Critique / Duke UP) and kept for personal research, not redistribution - this note paraphrases the secondary reading-notes summary rather than the paper itself.

How settled this is

This is a fair, well-sourced summary of a specific scholarly argument (Weatherby's reading of a real Blumenberg text), not an independent claim. Its extension to current LLMs is an implication the reading notes draw but that Weatherby's paper (published 2022, about ELIZA specifically) does not itself make about present-day systems - worth flagging that the jump from ELIZA to now is an inference, however natural.

Connections

  • substrate-and-criterion-split - "intermittent" intelligence undercuts any account of AI's substrate that assumes it needs continuous, stable interiority to count as a genuine occupant of the position
  • chimera-of-mismatched-parts - the "rhetorical" reading of ELIZA and the chimera's mismatched-parts structure are two different vocabularies for the same refusal to let AI be sorted as either pure mechanism or pure mind
  • de-dramatizing-the-digital - a further instance of AI revealing rather than creating a pre-existing artificiality, this time about meaning rather than about digital friction

Open

Weatherby's paper reads Blumenberg's ELIZA text as "surprisingly relevant" to current, much more powerful systems, but the paper itself predates the current generation of LLMs by several years - whether "intermittent" and "rhetorical" hold up as descriptions of a model that maintains coherent long-context state is worth testing directly rather than assuming.
