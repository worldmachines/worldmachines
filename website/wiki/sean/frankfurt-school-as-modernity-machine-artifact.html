The Frankfurt School as a Modernity Machine artifact

Sean · updated 7 September 2026 · source on GitHub
The Frankfurt School's habit of reading everything as secretly the same underlying thing — commodification, instrumental reason — is itself a totalizing, convergent move, which makes their critique of modernity a Modernity Machine artifact rather than an escape from it; a purely negative critique that never proposes what to think instead leaves a positive alternative, closer to a Buddhist 'you are not your thoughts,' needed as a supplement.

The idea

The Frankfurt School's characteristic diagnostic move is to show that a wide range of cultural phenomena are secretly one thing — expressions of commodification, instrumental reason, or the culture industry working beneath the surface of apparent variety. That unifying, totalizing gesture is worth noticing for what it is structurally, independent of whether any particular diagnosis is correct: it's the same kind of move that builds a single frame and folds everything into it, which is the native posture of the thing being critiqued. A critique of modernity that inherits modernity's own totalizing form can describe the system convincingly and still be unable to step outside it, because the critique and its object share a grammar.

There's a second problem with the tradition that's more personal than structural: its critiques are almost entirely negative. They're good at showing what's wrong with a thought or a cultural artifact and bad at leaving the reader with anything to think instead — the effect is closer to being made unhappy with your own thoughts than to being handed a better one. A more workable alternative posture, worth naming as its own thing, treats the world in a "yes-and" register — building on what's offered rather than negating it — and treats the unhappiness produced by relentless negation as a symptom to be set down rather than a truth to be accepted. The Buddhist move of recognizing that you are not your thoughts supplies exactly the practical exit that pure negation doesn't: a way to notice a Frankfurt-School-style diagnosis is correct without being obligated to live inside the misery it describes.

Grounding

Prompted by a review of Martin Jay's The Dialectical Imagination, a history of the Frankfurt School. The "secretly the same thing" observation is drawn from that review's account of the School's method. The yes-and-versus-negation framing and the Buddhist "you are not your thoughts" counter are original, not part of the review's argument.

How settled this is

That the Frankfurt School's method reads cultural phenomena as expressions of a small number of underlying forces is a documented characterization, checkable against the review and against Jay's book directly. That this method is itself a Modernity Machine artifact — a totalizing move borrowed from the thing it critiques — is this note's own interpretive claim, untested against a closer reading of the tradition's more constructive or later figures. The yes-and/negation contrast and the Buddhist alternative are working opinions, not factual claims of any kind.
