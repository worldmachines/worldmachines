Fixity as a working cosmology

Sean · updated 7 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Believing that species and the heavens were fixed wasn't a failure of nerve or imagination but a working cosmology, load-bearing in its own right — nature had no corresponding art to change it, and multiple independent authorities agreed there was no such thing as new knowledge, so a stable, unchanging platform was a precondition for doing science at all, not an obstacle to it.

The idea

It's easy to read pre-modern claims that species or the heavens don't change as simple errors, corrected once evidence piled up. A more useful reading treats fixity as a working cosmology that did real intellectual work, not a mistake waiting to be swept away. Several independent authorities — religious teaching, inherited classical literature, and Aristotelian philosophy — converged on the same claim: there is no such thing as genuinely new knowledge, and history itself moves in circles rather than in a line. That convergence gave fixity real institutional weight; it wasn't one tradition's isolated blind spot.

There's also a structural reason fixity held on so long: nature, as an object of study, had no corresponding art attached to it. Without a practice for actively changing nature, there was nothing to work on, and nothing to work on meant no occasion to discover that nature could be worked on at all. Before anyone could notice slow change happening around them, they needed a stable platform to notice it from — a settled sense of what things are, against which a deviation could register as a deviation rather than as noise. Mutability, in this light, isn't a truth fixity was hiding; it's a second-order discovery that could only be made once the first-order platform of fixity had done its work.

Grounding

Drawn from two works in the history of science: an account of why nature lacked a corresponding "art" before the scientific revolution, and an account of the three authorities — religious teaching, classical literature, Aristotelian philosophy — that converged on fixity. The alchemy-versus-species observation that opens the note, that people once had the mutability question backwards, is original, not drawn from either source.

How settled this is

The three-authorities claim and the "no art of nature" argument are both factual claims about the history of science, made by named historians and checkable against their books — this note is reporting them, not originating them. The broader reading — that fixity was a working cosmology rather than a failure of nerve, and that mutability had to wait on it as a precondition — is this note's own synthesis of those two facts, and is an interpretive claim rather than a factual one: it's a way of connecting what the historians documented, not itself something either historian asserts.

Open

If fixity was a working cosmology rather than a mistake, was the shift to universal mutability actually an improvement, or just a different working cosmology with its own blind spots and its own costs — and is there a way to tell which without already assuming the answer?
