Ordinary time as a third tempo

Sean · updated 7 September 2026 · source on GitHub
Between kairos's decisive moment and chronos's counted sequence sits a family of named tempos — ordinary time, island time, flow, vacation time — that are neither pure decision nor pure measurement, and naming them clarifies that kairos doesn't oppose chronos so much as transfigure the rhythmic chronos underneath it, which is why kairos needs chronos's rhythms in order to have anything to transfigure.

The idea

Kairos and chronos are usually paired as opposites: the decisive moment against the counted sequence, quality against quantity. But a number of ordinary, familiar tempos don't fit cleanly into either category. The liturgical calendar's "ordinary time" — the stretches between the major feasts, counted but not organized around event-points — is perhaps the cleanest case: it's neither the qualitative decisive moment of kairos nor the flat, undifferentiated counting of pure chronos, but something that behaves like a third tempo altogether. Island time, flow, vacation time, and the shared, relational sense of quality time with other people all sit in similar territory — tempos with their own character that resist being filed under either heading.

Naming this third category clarifies something about kairos itself: it isn't simply anti-chronos. Rhythmic, predictable chronos — getting paid on a fixed schedule, a recurring weekly class — is exactly the material kairos needs in order to do its transfiguring work. Without an underlying rhythm to transfigure, there's nothing for a decisive moment to stand out against. Kairos transfigures chronos rather than replacing it, and the family of named tempos in between — ordinary time chief among them — are what that transfiguration looks like when it's partial, sustained, or built into a shared calendar rather than concentrated into a single decisive instant.

Grounding

Original — not drawn from a specific book or article, though "ordinary time" itself is a standing feature of the liturgical calendar rather than a term coined here. Developed out of a list of named tempos — island time, flow, vacation time, quality time, theatrical presence — assembled as worked examples alongside the rhythmic counter-examples (a pay schedule, a weekly class) that motivate the note's second claim.

How settled this is

That the liturgical calendar names a stretch of time "ordinary time" between major feasts is a checkable fact about the calendar itself. That island time, flow, and the others belong in the same third category, and that kairos structurally needs rhythmic chronos as raw material rather than merely coexisting with it, are this note's own claims and are interpretive — offered as a synthesis that fits the examples, not tested against a case where a decisive moment seems to arise with no evident underlying rhythm at all.

Open
