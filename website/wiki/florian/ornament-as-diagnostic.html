It takes connoisseurship for ornamental baggage to identify dead frameworks

Florian · updated 14 September 2026 · source on GitHub
You can tell a genuinely dead framework from a live one by counting its functional flaws — decorative, non-working features are a diagnostic for zombie institutions, not just an aesthetic quirk.

The idea

You can assess whether a framework is alive or dead by examining it for functional flaws: the more decorative, non-working features it carries, the more likely you're looking at a dead facade wearing the shape of a working structure rather than an actual working structure. This turns Rao's four-stage decay filter — the sequence by which a canonical structure passes from functional to performative to allegorical to ornamental — from a description into a usable instrument. Given some structure in front of you, right now, how do you tell a late-modern zombie or a postmodern ghost from something genuinely still alive, still doing real work under divergence? Check the moats. Count the parts that don't do anything. The ratio is the diagnosis.

The historical grounding is the "licence to crenellate": in England between 1194 and 1589, roughly 550 licences were granted allowing manor owners to add battlements to their houses. Almost none of these were built for siege. They were what the historian Charles Coulson called "passports to respectability" — battlements as a status marker borrowed from a genuinely defensive vocabulary, applied to buildings that would never see a siege. A crenellated manor house is architecturally a castle and functionally nothing like one. If you didn't know the history, you'd read the battlements as evidence of a defensive structure. Once you know it, the battlements become evidence of the opposite: a structure performing defensibility precisely because it no longer needs to be defensible.

That's the move this note operationalizes. Apply the same read to anything claiming to be canonical or load-bearing: an institution, a philosophical framework, a corporate strategy. Look for its crenellations — the parts that exist to signal rather than to function — and weight your read of its aliveness accordingly.

Where it comes from

Published in Shitty Castles (Notefields, March 2026, postscript section), where it operationalizes the decay sequence from that essay into a checkable procedure. Adolf Loos's Ornament und Verbrechen ("Ornament and Crime") is probably relevant background on the general question of what ornament signals, though it isn't the source of the licence-to-crenellate material.

How settled this is

The licence-to-crenellate history is well-documented and I take it as solid ground. The diagnostic itself — "count the ornament, infer the deadness" — is my own extension of Coulson's reading, not something the historical record establishes on its own. It hasn't been tested against a clear counterexample yet: a structure that is unambiguously alive and unambiguously ornamental at the same time. I haven't found one, which is doing some of the work of the claim, but the absence of a counterexample isn't the same as proof there isn't one.

Open

Decoration and function aren't mutually exclusive in living systems generally — a peacock's tail is both real handicap and real signal. Does this diagnostic over-read ornament as evidence of death in cases where the ornament is doing costly, and therefore real, signaling work? I haven't found a counterexample among the cases I've checked, but I also haven't gone looking hard enough to trust that absence.
