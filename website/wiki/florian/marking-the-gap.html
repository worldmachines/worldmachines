Marking the gap is more insightful than forcing closure

Florian · updated 14 September 2026 · source on GitHub
When a theory cannot reach closure, honestly marking what is not known — including by changing register entirely — is more productive than faking completeness, because it points at what might come later instead of papering over the hole.

The idea

When a theory can't reach closure — when there's a real hole in the argument and no honest way to fill it — marking the gap is a more productive move than forcing an artificial resolution. Registering, plainly, what isn't known is worth more than manufacturing a conclusion that only looks complete. A marked gap points forward, toward what might get figured out later; a forced closure just hides the fact that there's still something to figure out.

The clearest model for this is Alexander von Humboldt inserting a poem about the Lebenskraft — the "life force," a concept he couldn't make scientifically rigorous but also couldn't honestly drop — into a text that otherwise proceeds by careful empirical argument. He didn't argue the concept into coherence, and he didn't quietly cut it. He let the poem stand in for the gap: a different register entirely, marking the exact place where the prose argument ran out, without pretending the switch in register was itself an answer.

This claim was split out from a broader note about embedded, embodied inquiry under divergence, because it's separately falsifiable from that note's other components. You could believe that embedding and embodying inquiry is the right approach and still think gap-marking specifically is a bad habit — or vice versa. Keeping them apart makes each easier to actually test.

It's also a candidate answer to an open question raised elsewhere in this cluster: whether there's an honest way to hold open the gap between a framework being advantageous and a framework being true, without collapsing into either false modesty or false confidence. Gap-marking is offered as one such way — visibly showing where you don't know, rather than either overclaiming or hiding the hole with hedges.

But this is held as a live tension, deliberately not reconciled, with the very question it's answering: gap-marking can itself become a performative move — a way of looking honest and rigorous while never actually committing to anything checkable. Marking a gap and refusing to close it can shade into permanent, comfortable irresolution dressed up as intellectual honesty.

Where it comes from

From Wrestling With Eels, drawing on Humboldt's own practice — specifically the moment described there as "a visible marker that makes evident not what is known but what is not... a visible gap."

How settled this is

The Humboldt case is well-documented and I trust it as an accurate description of what he actually did. The generalization — that this specific move (change register, don't force closure) is broadly good practice rather than something that worked for Humboldt in that specific text — is mine, and it's the part still open. The live tension with performative gap-marking is not a flaw I'm hiding; it's the honest state of the claim, and resolving it prematurely would be exactly the forced closure this note argues against.

Open

Are there real cases where intellectual inquiry benefits from plastering over a gap — from a confident placeholder that turns out to be more useful than an honest admission of not-knowing? Also open: is there a version of "fake it till you make it" in theory-building that produces something genuinely interesting and useful, rather than merely performative? I haven't found a clean case of either, in favor of this note's claim or against it.
