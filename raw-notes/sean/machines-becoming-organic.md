# Machines becoming organic

## The idea

The usual complaint about technology and alienation runs one direction:
people, embedded in mechanized systems, become more machine-like themselves.
That diagnosis is old and well-rehearsed. A less familiar counter-observation,
traceable to thinking about technology after Kant, runs the other way:
machines themselves have been becoming more organic, in the specific sense of
becoming recursive — responsive to their own outputs, no longer simple
input-output devices that reliably do the same thing every time. Household
devices, phones, and now models built to respond differently depending on
context all sit further along this line than a mechanical clock or a steam
engine ever did.

The two trends don't obviously cancel out into people and machines meeting in
the middle, though a fully merged, cyborg-style convergence is one
science-fiction way of imagining what happens if they do. A different response
treats the recursiveness of machines as a reason to be more careful about the
human side of the ledger, not less: if machines are drifting toward behaviors
that look organic, the temptation is to start treating them as though they had
inner lives, and resisting that temptation — continuing to treat machines as
objects — may be part of what it takes to stay fully human rather than
blurring the line from the human side.

## How settled this is

This is a reading of someone else's argument that Sean found genuinely
surprising rather than a position he's independently verified — the claim
that Kant is the right hinge point, and the details of what "organic" means
for a philosopher of technology, are taken on the strength of the source
rather than checked against the wider literature.

## Where it comes from

Drawn from Yuk Hui's interviews on technology and cybernetics, with the
framing of the two trends (people mechanizing, machines organicizing) and the
caution against treating machines as people both attributed to that reading.
