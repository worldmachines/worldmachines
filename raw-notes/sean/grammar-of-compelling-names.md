# The grammar of compelling names

## The idea

There has never been one definition of casting a spell; the position gets
redefined roughly every time the surrounding metaphysics changes, while the
felt structure underneath stays put - correctly said words make something
happen. Old English spell originally meant simply a saying or story (the
same root survives in gospel), with no separate word yet for a magical
formula; the narrowed occult sense only arrives in the 1570s. Antiquity
hands down spell-as-song and spell-as-binding (Latin carmen, the curse
tablet that literally "ties down" a target's power). Medieval Christendom
reframes the question as one of agency and source - prayer petitions, a
spell compels, and licit versus illicit efficacy turns on whether a
demonic pact is involved; Aquinas already treats a magician's words as
signs addressed to intelligences rather than causes acting on matter, a
quietly communicative theory well before anyone calls it that. Renaissance
Neoplatonism relocates efficacy back into the words themselves - names in a
sacred tongue participate in the essence of things a spell activates real
cosmic correspondence through. Then disenchantment inverts the whole
category: the Reformation attacks the magical residue in Catholic
sacraments, and Weber's Entzauberung pushes "spell" toward meaning something
that, by definition, does not actually work.

Frazer's *Golden Bough* codifies the intellectualist verdict: sympathetic
magic, organized around the laws of similarity and contagion, is mistaken
proto-science, "the bastard sister of science" - a spell is a false causal
technique, an error of reasoning about how the world works. The
performative turn is the hinge that undoes this. Malinowski, watching
Trobriand garden magic, argues spells are not bad science but pragmatic,
creative speech that coordinates labor and manages anxiety; Tambiah makes
the case explicit by borrowing Austin directly - a spell is a performative
act, evaluated not as a true-or-false claim about nature but by whether its
felicity conditions are met. The redefinition runs, across the whole
history, from epistemology (a mistaken belief about causation) to pragmatics
(a conventional action that either succeeds or fails on its own terms).

Casting a spell is close to a textbook case of Blumenberg's reoccupation:
the seat - efficacious words - stays occupied continuously while successive
tenants swap out the metaphysics underneath it. The LLM is the newest
occupant, and it has a genuinely novel twist. It restores the pre-Frazerian
intuition that words literally do the work - the old Neoplatonic dream of
the true name - while fully satisfying the post-Frazerian demand that there
be no false causal metaphysics behind the claim. There is no world-soul and
no demon; the felicity conditions are simply the training distribution.
This is the first occupant of the position for which the words demonstrably
do work and there is nothing occult about why they do.

Applying Austin's own taxonomy to specific LLM scenarios turns up a
genuinely useful distinction that gets less attention than it deserves:
most of the places an LLM interaction disappoints fall under Austin's
category of "abuse" (the act formally succeeds, but its sincerity condition
is empty) rather than "misfire" (the act fails outright). An apology or a
chain-of-thought explanation produced without whatever "genuine" inference
would require still goes through, formally - it just does so hollow. That
is a more unsettling result than the flat claim that LLMs "can't really"
perform speech acts, because it says most LLM speech acts succeed; they are
just empty in a specific, nameable way. A separate case Austin's own
categories don't quite cover is the promise made by a system without
continuity across sessions - not a misfire, not an abuse, but something
closer to temporal truncation of the promisor, where the subject who could
honor or violate the obligation dissolves before the question can be
settled.

## Where it comes from

Working synthesis, developed from research on the history of spell-casting
cross-referenced against J.L. Austin's *How to Do Things with Words* and
Stanley Tambiah's "The Magical Power of Words" and "A Performative Approach
to Ritual." Additional sources: James Frazer's *The Golden Bough*, Max
Weber's Entzauberung, John Searle's *Speech Acts*, and Jesper Sorensen's *A
Cognitive Theory of Magic*. The primary sources behind this synthesis
(Austin, Tambiah, Frazer, and the rest) have mostly not yet been checked
directly against the vault's own citations - a provenance caveat the vault
records against itself, since the research documents behind this note were
AI-assisted working documents rather than verified reading notes.

## How settled this is

The historical arc (recitation to binding to demonic pact to cosmic
correspondence to Frazer's mistaken-causation reading to Tambiah's
performative reading) reflects a real and citable intellectual history,
though - per the vault's own caveat - most of the primary sources behind it
have not yet been individually verified against the vault's summary. The
application to LLMs, the misfire/abuse distinction, and the "temporal
truncation of the promisor" observation are this synthesis's own
extensions, offered as a working cross-reference rather than a checked
scholarly claim.

## Open

Whether the felicity-conditions-as-training-distribution account genuinely
dissolves the occult residue in "the words really do the work," or just
relocates the mystery into a black box that functions like one, is worth
pressing rather than assuming settled.
