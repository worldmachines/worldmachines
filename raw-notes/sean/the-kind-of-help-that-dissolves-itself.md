# The kind of help that dissolves itself

## The idea

Depth psychology once supplied a shared vocabulary strong enough that
public figures got read through it reflexively — a Freudian slip, an
obvious complex, a diagnosis offered in casual conversation. That habit has
mostly disappeared. One explanation is a straightforward decline in
status: the tradition offered diagnostic depth without ever quite
delivering a picture of the good life, a totalizing system in the way its
own scale of ambition seemed to promise.

A sharper account comes from a structural distinction between two kinds of
help. One kind — call it the minister's kind — takes you on indefinitely;
its success is measured by how well it holds you over time. The other kind
has a different success criterion entirely: it's working precisely when
you no longer need it. Depth psychology, on this reading, was built and
marketed more like the first kind of help than the second, even where its
actual insights pointed toward self-sufficiency. A practice that measures
its own success by whether it can be outgrown asks something different of
both the helper and the person being helped than one that measures success
by continued engagement — and a culture that has gotten more suspicious of
open-ended dependency might simply be less patient with help of the first
kind, whatever its diagnostic power.

## Grounding

The two-kinds-of-help distinction — one that keeps its subject, one whose
job is to become unnecessary — is drawn from Alan Watts's writing and
talks on spiritual teachers. The application of that distinction to explain
depth psychology's loss of cultural status is original to this note.

## How settled this is

That depth-psychological readings of public figures were once common and
are now comparatively rare is an observable cultural shift, not a strict
empirical finding — no count was run, this is impression rather than
measurement. The explanation offered — that the two-kinds-of-help
distinction accounts for the shift better than a simple decline narrative —
is this note's own interpretive claim, not something drawn from a source
that makes the argument directly.
