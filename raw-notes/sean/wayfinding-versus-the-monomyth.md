# Wayfinding versus the monomyth: two metaphors of motion

## The idea

Two different templates exist for what purposeful motion through unknown
territory looks like. Joseph Campbell's monomyth is built around a call to
a recognizable kind of trial, a threshold crossed, ordeals undergone, and a
boon carried back to a home that stays put while the hero is away — a
structure that assumes a canonical world, coded by conquest-and-exploration
adventure narratives, in which the journey resolves. Polynesian wayfinding
is a different template. Traditional open-ocean navigation is done without
instruments, by continuous, attentive reading of stars, swells, cloud
formations, and the behavior of birds near land, with course corrected
constantly as conditions change — there is no fixed bearing locked in at
the outset the way a compass heading would lock one in; there is only
sustained orientation under changing signs.

Applied to a reality that keeps proliferating new forms and signals faster
than any settled canon can contain them — a "divergence machine," in Rao's
contrast between convergent canonicity and expanding non-canonicity —
wayfinding is the sharper metaphor because it names the actual task
correctly: not reaching a known destination through a known world, but
staying oriented while the terrain and the very standards for orientation
keep changing at the same time. The monomyth's template assumes a canonical
world with an achievable resolution waiting at the end of the trial.
Wayfinding assumes there is no single settled world left to be canonical
about, only a practice of staying oriented inside one that won't hold
still.

## Grounding

Documented Polynesian wayfinding practice (dead reckoning by stars, swell
patterns, and wildlife signs, without instruments); Joseph Campbell's
monomyth structure in *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*; Venkatesh Rao's
contrast between convergent canonicity and expanding non-canonicity. The
application of wayfinding specifically as the better metaphor for the
divergence machine is original to this note.

## How settled this is

The wayfinding literature's description of the actual navigational
practice is well documented and not in dispute. Campbell's monomyth
structure is a standard reference point, easily checked against the text.
That wayfinding is specifically the better metaphor for the divergence
machine — rather than simply an appealing parallel — is this note's own
interpretive argument, not tested against alternative candidate metaphors.

## Open

Wayfinding as practiced is a skill built for one navigator or a small crew
reading shared signs together. Does it actually scale as a metaphor for an
entire civilization's motion, or does collective wayfinding at that scale
need something that wayfinding-for-a-few doesn't have to solve?
