# Mutability as agency, not drift

## The idea

The scientific consensus rightly rejected Lamarck's specific mechanism, but
rejecting the mechanism came bundled with rejecting a distinction worth
keeping: the difference between change driven by an intrinsic capacity to
act — wants producing new abilities, an organism doing something rather than
merely having something done to it — and change as pure, directionless drift,
where an organism is just one more thing being reshuffled by forces outside
itself. Lamarck's picture, whatever its scientific fate, insisted that
organisms are agents; the picture that replaced it treats change as something
that happens to organisms rather than something organisms do.

The present has swung hard toward the agentless picture and generalized it
far past biology: everything is now assumed to be in constant flux, all the
time, at every scale. That swing has an affective cost that doesn't get named
often enough. In a world where most things were assumed fixed, an unwanted
change to your own life could at least be read as something singular — fate,
fortune, a mark of being singled out. In a world where everything is assumed
to be constantly changing anyway, an unwanted change loses even that meaning;
you're just part of the general menagerie of things being reshuffled, with no
more standing as an agent in your own change than anything else. The anxiety
of universal flux may be, in part, the anxiety of having quietly lost the
vocabulary for agency inside change — the missing vocabulary being something
closer to the distinction between flexibility (bending under force) and
plasticity (giving and receiving form).

## Grounding

The agency definition — an intrinsic capacity to act, neither predetermined
nor random — and the historical account of Lamarck's rejected mechanism are
drawn from Jessica Riskin's work on the history of evolutionary theory (*The
Power of Life*, *The Restless Clock*). The claim about what the present's
swing toward agentless flux costs affectively, and the flexibility/plasticity
distinction offered as the missing vocabulary, are original, not part of
Riskin's argument.

## How settled this is

The historical claim — that Lamarck's picture treated organisms as agents
and that the successor picture treats change as agentless — is grounded in
Riskin's documented history and checkable against her books. The affective
claim — that losing this distinction explains some of the present's anxiety
about universal flux — is original and untested; it would weaken if that
anxiety traced more plausibly to something else, such as the sheer pace of
change rather than its perceived agentlessness.

## Open

Is there a way to reintroduce agency into how change is understood at scales
larger than an individual organism — an institution, a culture, a world
machine — or does agency-in-change only ever make sense at the scale of
something small enough to have wants in the first place?
