# Blumenberg's absolutism of reality and why Dark Forest is doing myth's job

## The idea

In *Work on Myth*, a different book from the one behind this corpus's
reoccupation notes, Blumenberg argues that myth's function isn't to
describe the world accurately but to manage something he calls the
"absolutism of reality" — raw exposure to a reality that offers no
guarantees and sets no limit on what can happen to a person, prior to any
story that names, bounds, or puts distance between a person and that
threat. Myth makes the world survivable by giving an unbounded threat a
shape and a name, even when the shape it gives is frightening.

Liu Cixin's *The Dark Forest* — the universe as a silent, maximally hostile
arena where any civilization that reveals itself gets annihilated by an
unseen, more advanced one — reads as exactly this kind of myth for an
audience that can no longer take older religious cosmologies as a live
source of comfort but is otherwise exposed to the same underlying
absolutism of reality. The dark forest doesn't offer safety, and it doesn't
pretend to. What it offers is a shape: a legible, nameable rule ("stay
hidden") for an anxiety that would otherwise have no rule at all, only
total, arbitrary exposure. A terrifying rule is still a rule, and having
one to hold is what myth was always doing, on Blumenberg's account, even
in versions that promised comfort rather than dread.

## Grounding

Hans Blumenberg's *Work on Myth* (*Arbeit am Mythos*, 1979) — distinct from
*The Legitimacy of the Modern Age*, which grounds the reoccupation notes
elsewhere in this corpus. The application to Liu Cixin's *The Dark Forest*
is original to this note.

## How settled this is

Blumenberg's absolutism-of-reality thesis in *Work on Myth* is established,
citable scholarship, though it is a distinct argument from his reoccupation
schema and shouldn't be conflated with it just because both are
Blumenberg's. Whether *Dark Forest* actually performs this function for
readers, rather than simply depicting a frightening universe, is this
note's own interpretive claim — it hasn't been checked against how readers
report experiencing the book.

## Open

Does a myth that offers no comfort at all, only a legible threat, still
count as doing myth's traditional job on Blumenberg's own terms, or does
his account require some measure of actual relief that *Dark Forest*
withholds?
