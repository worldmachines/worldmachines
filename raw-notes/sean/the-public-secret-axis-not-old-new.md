# The public/secret axis, not old/new

## The idea

It's tempting to read the rise of modern science and the persistence of
hermetic and alchemical traditions in the same period as a contest between
old superstition and new knowledge, with science eventually winning.
David Wootton's account of the period complicates that story by pointing
to a different axis entirely: before around 1600, the operative
distinction in how knowledge was organized wasn't old versus new, it was
what was generally known versus what was known only to a privileged few
holding secret wisdom. Being new didn't automatically put a claim on the
public side of that line, and being ancient didn't automatically put it on
the secret side.

That reframing does real work. It explains why figures now sorted neatly
into "scientist" and "occultist" could hold both roles without apparent
contradiction — they weren't straddling a science/superstition divide that
didn't yet exist for them, they were working within a shared secrecy/
publicity axis that cut across both. It also explains why the new
science's eventual commitment to publication and replicability was itself
a change of axis, not just a change of content: moving a body of knowledge
from the secret side to the public side was, in this framing, as
significant an event as any specific discovery it later enabled.

## Grounding

Drawn from David Wootton's *The Invention of Science*, specifically his
account of the pre-1600 public/secret distinction as prior to and separate
from an old/new distinction. The application to explain hermeticism's
coexistence with early modern science, and to reframe the scientific
method's commitment to publication as an axis change, is original to this
note.

## How settled this is

Wootton's characterization of the pre-1600 public/secret distinction is a
historical argument, checkable against *The Invention of Science* directly.
The further claim — that the shift to publication and replicability is
best understood as an axis change rather than a content change — is this
note's own extension of his point, not a claim Wootton makes in those
terms.
