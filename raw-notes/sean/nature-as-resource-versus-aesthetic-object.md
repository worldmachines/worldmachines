# Nature as resource versus nature as aesthetic object

## The idea

Encountering an animal in a field admits at least two structurally
different relations to it. One treats it as something usable — a horse
that could be caught, ridden, claimed, put to work; nature as a resource
in James Scott's sense, something the state and the market render legible
precisely by stripping away everything not relevant to use. The other
treats the same animal as one instance of a larger whole, appreciated
without any interest in using it — Kant's disinterested aesthetic
judgment, the free play of understanding and imagination that constitutes
the feeling of the sublime.

These aren't two moods layered over one underlying perception; they seem
to structurally exclude each other. Seeing the horse with an eye to its use
pulls it out of the aesthetic whole and into a category of things
available for a purpose — Scott's account of legibility as
pre-commodification is exactly this move, made at the scale of a state
rather than an individual glance. Seeing it aesthetically, disinterestedly,
requires setting aside the use-relation entirely, which produces its own
kind of alienation: you can appreciate nature as a totality only by
declining to be part of what it's for. Neither relation is obviously truer
than the other, but they don't blend — noticing which one you're in turns
out to be harder than it sounds.

## Grounding

The disinterested-aesthetic-judgment account is drawn from Kant's
*Critique of Judgment*, specifically the treatment of the sublime and the
free play of the faculties. The legibility-as-pre-commodification reading
is drawn from James C. Scott's *Seeing Like a State*. The claim that these
are structurally exclusive rather than complementary moods is original to
this note.

## How settled this is

Kant's account of disinterested aesthetic judgment and Scott's account of
legibility are both established readings of their respective texts,
checkable directly against them. That the two relations to nature are
structurally exclusive — rather than simply two available framings a
single observer can hold loosely together — is this note's own claim,
offered from a specific anecdote (a horse in a field) rather than tested
against a wider range of cases.
