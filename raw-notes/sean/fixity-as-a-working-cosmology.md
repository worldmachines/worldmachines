# Fixity as a working cosmology

## The idea

It's easy to read pre-modern claims that species or the heavens don't change
as simple errors, corrected once evidence piled up. A more useful reading
treats fixity as a working cosmology that did real intellectual work, not a
mistake waiting to be swept away. Several independent authorities — religious
teaching, inherited classical literature, and Aristotelian philosophy —
converged on the same claim: there is no such thing as genuinely new
knowledge, and history itself moves in circles rather than in a line. That
convergence gave fixity real institutional weight; it wasn't one tradition's
isolated blind spot.

There's also a structural reason fixity held on so long: nature, as an object
of study, had no corresponding art attached to it. Without a practice for
actively changing nature, there was nothing to work on, and nothing to work on
meant no occasion to discover that nature could be worked on at all. Before
anyone could notice slow change happening around them, they needed a stable
platform to notice it from — a settled sense of what things are, against which
a deviation could register as a deviation rather than as noise. Mutability, in
this light, isn't a truth fixity was hiding; it's a second-order discovery
that could only be made once the first-order platform of fixity had done its
work.

## How settled this is

This reading draws heavily on specific historians of science and is offered
as a considered synthesis of their arguments rather than an independent
thesis — the "art" argument and the three-authorities point both come from
named sources rather than from Sean's own research into the primary material.

## Where it comes from

## Open

If fixity was a working cosmology rather than a mistake, was the shift to
universal mutability actually an improvement, or just a different working

tell which without already assuming the answer?
