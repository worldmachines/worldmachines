# Counting up versus counting down on a new era

## The idea

Talk about a new era tends to come in one of two moods. There's the
end-of-history mood: something has finished, nothing definite has replaced
it, and the felt direction is a countdown toward an indefinite terminus —
the vocabulary of the Gramsci gap, the interregnum, the long twilight of
post-modernism. And there's a second mood, much rarer in practice, where the
same transition is heard as a countdown's opposite: a countup from a fresh
starting point, not yet fully named, with the affect of discovery rather
than depletion.

The interesting claim is not that one mood is more accurate than the other —
the underlying facts about the transition don't change — but that the two
moods are genuinely different time-ontologies laid over the same
chronological situation, and each carries its own emotional signature almost
automatically. Counting down toward an unspecified end produces anxiety
because the terminus is coming but its content is unknown. Counting up from
a starting point produces something closer to excitement, because what's
ahead is open rather than foreclosed, even though it's just as unknown as
the countdown's terminus.

This bears directly on how a reoccupation argument should be pitched. The
same underlying claim — that inherited answers no longer fit the questions
they were built for, and are being reoccupied by different, provisional
answers — can be told as another entry in the decline literature, or as an
invitation to notice a beginning. The choice of framing isn't cosmetic. It
determines whether the argument reads as one more diagnosis of collapse (the
register most cultural criticism inherits) or as something with room for
curiosity, even enjoyment, in it.

## Where it comes from

Sean's own observation, developed across several passes at a seed file on
reoccupation and new eras, drawing on a remark from Venkatesh Rao's
Contraptions circle that people are not only counting time forward but also,
in end-of-history moods, implicitly counting down toward some indefinite
terminus. The extension — that reoccupation vocabulary is the count-up
mirror image of that same countdown — is Sean's own.

## How settled this is

This is a personal diagnostic, not an empirical finding — an observed
difference in affect that Sean has found useful for his own writing, not a
claim that's been tested against how readers actually respond to the two
framings. It would weaken if the count-up mood turned out to be mostly a
function of novelty, fading once "the new era" stopped being new and started
accumulating its own disappointments.

## Open

or just the temporary lift of novelty — and if it's the latter, does a
reoccupation argument need a second act for once the new era stops feeling
new?
