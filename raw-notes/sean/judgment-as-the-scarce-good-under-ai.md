# Judgment as the scarce good under AI

## The idea

As routine knowledge work — reformatting, reconciling, and moving information
between forms — gets absorbed by a completing technology, the things that stay
scarce are the things a model can't cheaply substitute: judgment, a
distinctive point of view, and something close to esoteric knowledge, meaning
knowledge that isn't simply available for the taking once you ask for it.

The interesting move is in what point of view is actually made of. It's
tempting to think a point of view is just accumulated experience — that having
done or seen more gives you more to say. But the experience itself isn't the
valuable part; most people have comparable experiences without acquiring a
distinctive way of looking. What's valuable is the harder-to-transfer step of
turning an experience into a stance — a way of seeing that can be applied to
situations the original experience never touched. That step is closer to
judgment than to memory, and it's exactly the step a model trained on other
people's already-processed experience has the hardest time performing for you.

This gives a specific, actionable shape to "humans stay valuable" that's more
useful than a general appeal to creativity or emotional intelligence. It isn't
that people are valuable because they've lived through things; it's that some
people have done the further work of converting what they lived through into a
transferable way of seeing, and that conversion is the scarce good.

## Grounding

Original — not drawn from a specific book or article. Developed as part of
a broader argument that AI completes rather than expands the current
technology stack; the point-of-view-versus-experience distinction is this
note's own extension of that argument.

## How settled this is

This is a hunch about what becomes economically scarce, not a claim tested
against how knowledge work is actually changing — it names a plausible
mechanism, not an observed shift. It would weaken if models turned out to
get just as good at producing distinctive-seeming points of view as they are
at reformatting information, which is an open empirical question this note
doesn't resolve.

## Open

Can the conversion from experience to point of view actually be taught or
trained, or is it closer to a talent some people have and others don't — and
if it can be trained, what would that training look like once the raw
material (lived experience) is no longer scarce either?
