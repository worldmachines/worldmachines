# Western civilization as reoccupation

## The idea

"Western civilization" is often treated as a descriptive label — a rough
geographic and historical shorthand for a set of institutions, texts, and
habits that happen to have a common lineage. Read through reoccupation, it
looks like something more specific: a particular answer filling a slot that two
earlier universal answers had vacated.

The first vacated slot is the universalist church's claim to speak for
humanity as such. The second is the Enlightenment's related claim — that reason
discloses a plan or a trajectory for all of humanity, not just for one people.
Both claims failed or receded, and in the vacuum left by the French Revolution
and the Napoleonic wars, a narrower substitute stepped into the same shaped
hole: a particular tradition's inheritance dressed in the old universal's
language, so that "civilization" quietly comes to mean one civilization's own
unique genius rather than a claim available to everyone.

Tocqueville's distinction sharpens what's at stake in taking the reoccupied
version passively versus actively. A civilization can be inherited as
something you passively belong to, defined mainly by equality among its
members, or it can be practiced as something you actively make through
deliberation and participation — a distinction closer to freedom than to
equality. The reoccupation is easier to accept when civilization is treated as
a passive inheritance; treating it as an active practice raises the harder
question of whether a tradition organized around democratic equality can also
sustain the deliberate, participatory civilization-building Tocqueville has in
mind.

## How settled this is

The reoccupation reading of "Western civilization" is borrowed directly from a
specific piece of writing Sean encountered and found persuasive, not an
independent discovery — it's included here because it's an unusually clean
worked example of the reoccupation mechanism, not because the underlying
historical claim has been checked against the wider literature on the
concept's history.

## Where it comes from

An argument Sean read making the case that the idea of Western civilization
arose in the vacuum left by the French Revolution and the Napoleonic wars,
reoccupying both the universalist church and the universalist Enlightenment;
the Tocqueville extension (passive/equality versus active/participatory
civilization) is Sean's own addition to that argument.
