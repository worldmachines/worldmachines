# Appeal-foreclosure

## The idea

Rao's definition of [[new-nature]] says what it is: a regime governed by technological laws nearly as inviolable as physical ones. It does not say how to tell when something has crossed over. Slowness is the obvious proxy, but slowness fails in both directions — glaciers are slow and petitionable (you can lobby a legislature), and a hash function is fast and not.

The proposed criterion is whether a lever exists. The folk version is a single question: *can you still get someone on the phone to grant an exception?*

This separates two conditions that usually get lumped together.

**Ossification.** There is someone to ask, but asking does nothing. The standards body exists, the regulator exists, the company has a support line, and none of them will move. The lever is jammed, or captured by someone who won't pull it. This is the ordinary condition of large institutions, and it is frustrating precisely because petition remains conceptually possible.

**New Nature.** There is no one to ask. Not a refusal, not a jammed lever — no lever. Nothing to capture and no one to petition. The line from Sean's talk abstract puts it as: not *you should*, but *you cannot otherwise*.

The distinction matters because the two conditions have different politics. Ossification can be reformed, captured, sued, or waited out; a jammed lever is still a lever. New Nature is planned around. You do not lobby the tides.

## Three questions that apply it

1. **Does it change on a schedule?** Standards change by version: a fork, a revision, a vote. Those moments are when petition happens. Something that reshapes itself continuously, with no versions, never opens a window in which anyone could weigh in. (Exoskeletal systems change by molting; the molt is the petition window. Continuous remodeling has none.)
2. **How do people explain it?** A changelog names who decided what, and each decision could be reopened. Lore explains how things are. When the people who live with a system stop explaining it by anyone's decision and start explaining it by its nature, the lever is gone — whether or not it was ever formally removed.
3. **Can you insure against it but not sue over it?** A hurricane is insurable and unsuable. A platform is suable. When a market prices a system's failures as *peril* (a risk you cover) rather than *liability* (a party you pursue), the market has recognized nature. This is the strongest of the three because it is a costly signal: insurers are paid to be right about it.

## The existing case

Public-key cryptography passes all three, and it did so without AI. No institution can exempt you from the fact that factoring large numbers is hard. Its origin story is already told as legend rather than as minutes. And when it fails, the failures are priced as weather. It is the proof that the category is real and that the descent can complete.

## How settled this is

The distinction between a jammed lever and no lever is, I think, sound and reusable. The three questions are instruments, and like all instruments can misfire: a system could pass all three and still be petitionable through a channel the questions don't see. The strongest objection is that "no lever" is always a matter of degree — every human artifact has *some* maintainer somewhere. The reply is that the maintainer of a mature protocol cannot grant *you* an exception any more than a physicist can, which is the relevant sense.

## Where it comes from

Working synthesis from Sean Stevenson's preparation for the talk "De-Dramatizing the Digital: How AI Descends Into New Nature" (Inventing Psychohistory session, September 2026). Builds on Sean's essay of the same name (the infrastructure argument), Rao's New Nature slides, and Aneesh Sathe's lever discipline (see Connections) — but the criterion itself is new to these notes and should be weighed as a proposal.
