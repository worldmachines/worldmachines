# The instrument becomes the self

## The idea

David Landes's own observation that a personal timepiece could become a
"psychic luxury" - no longer a tool but part of one's identity -
generalizes past clocks to any sufficiently intimate personal instrument: a
feed, a model, a companion. The claim this row makes is that once an
instrument crosses that threshold, the difficulty migrates from having
tools to having any unautomated interior left at all - a self that retains
the capacity to step outside its own instruments and notice them as
instruments becomes the scarce resource, not the default condition. The
practical acceptance is that some delegation of memory and decision-making
to instruments is inevitable and not itself the problem; what has to be
actively protected is a private interior, maintained on purpose through
periodic "instrument fasts" rather than assumed to persist on its own. The
failure mode inverts the usual worry about tools: the map becomes the
navigator rather than a guide the navigator consults, and a billion
privately-tuned selves, each oriented by its own instrument, cannot share a
world with each other - which loops directly back to the vanishing of a
shared present described elsewhere in this set.

## How settled this is

A working hypothesis; the associated bet is that AI-companion attachment
produces documented harms serious enough to trigger regulation in at least
one major jurisdiction, and that "digital autonomy" or "the right to an
unoptimized self" enters public discourse as a named concept.

## Open

Whether "periodic instrument fasts" are a genuinely sufficient protection
against this drift, or whether the pull toward instrument-as-identity is
strong enough that no merely personal discipline holds against it, is left
as the open practical question.
