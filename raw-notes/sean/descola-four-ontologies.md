# Descola's four ways of sorting the world

## The idea

Philippe Descola is a French anthropologist who spent years with the Achuar in the Amazon and came back with a problem: the people he lived with did not have a category corresponding to "nature." Animals and plants were persons with whom one had social relations. This was not a belief layered on top of a shared reality; it was a different way of sorting what exists. *Beyond Nature and Culture* (2005; English 2013) is his attempt to say how many such ways there are.

His answer is that every collective has to settle two questions about the beings around it. First: do they have an interior life like ours — intentions, a point of view, something it is like to be them? Second: are they made of the same stuff as us — do they share our physical nature? Each question can be answered yes or no, which gives four combinations, and Descola argues that the ethnographic record fills all four and only four.

**Animism** — interiority shared, physicality not. Other beings have minds like ours but different bodies. The jaguar is a person in a jaguar suit; what differs is the body, and with it the perspective. Amazonia, much of Siberia, parts of Southeast Asia.

**Totemism** — both shared, within a group. A clan and its totemic species share both an inner nature and a physical one; the boundaries run between groups, not between humans and non-humans. Aboriginal Australia is the paradigm case.

**Analogism** — neither shared. The world is made of unlike singularities, each different from every other in both mind and body, and the work of the culture is to stitch them together with correspondences: this organ to that planet, this humour to that element, this ruler to that star. Ancient China, Renaissance Europe, the Andes, much of West Africa. The doctrine of signatures is an analogist practice, and so is any system where a chart of resemblances does the work that a theory of shared substance would do elsewhere.

**Naturalism** — physicality shared, interiority not. Everything is made of the same matter and obeys the same laws; only humans have minds worth the name. This is the modern Western default, the ontology science grew up in, and the one in which "nature" and "culture" first appear as separate domains.

The book's central move is that naturalism is one cell in the grid. It is not the neutral standpoint from which the other three are seen as beliefs; it is itself a way of answering the two questions, with its own history and its own blind spots. Descola places these schemas at the boundary between culture and nature — the slowest-moving layer of a collective, so slow that it forgets it is a choice. That is how our way of sorting came to feel like the only one.

## Why it matters here

Two things follow for the World Machines project.

First, any account of how a technology becomes [[new-nature]] is an account of a collective revising its answer to Descola's questions. "Nature" is not a fixed backdrop the technology enters; it is a schema, and the schema can move.

Second, the AI–protocol systems the [[descent-chain]] describes are composite beings: mechanical at the bottom, mind-like at the surface, with no single essence. Naturalism has no good place for such a thing — it must be either a mind or a mechanism. Analogism does: the chimera, a being of mismatched parts held together by correspondence, is analogism's characteristic figure. See [[chimera-of-mismatched-parts]] for that argument.

## How settled this is

Descola's grid is established and widely used in anthropology, though contested — critics ask whether four is really the number, and whether real collectives are as consistent as the grid implies. The application to technology is Sean's, not Descola's.

## Where it comes from

Philippe Descola, *Beyond Nature and Culture* (Gallimard 2005; University of Chicago Press 2013), especially Part III on the four modes of identification. Also useful: Eduardo Viveiros de Castro on Amazonian perspectivism, which is the source of the jaguar example and pushes further toward a "multinaturalist" reading of animism. Summary and application are Sean's; no text from the book is reproduced here.
