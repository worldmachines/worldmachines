# Three occupiable positions, not one Venn diagram

## The idea

The default image for how magic, science, and religion relate is a Venn
diagram — three overlapping circles, with some practices sitting cleanly
inside one and others in the shared regions. That image assumes the three
circles are stable across history, which the anthropological record
doesn't really support. Marcel Mauss's early theory of magic already
argued against treating "magic" as a fixed category at all, calling it
something closer to whatever a given society's dominant knowledge system
excludes and finds alien. Stanley Tambiah's later account divides the
territory differently again — not into three circles but into two realms,
causality and participation, with science claiming causality and religion,
art, and magic sharing participation, and argues for ways the realms need
each other rather than staying cleanly apart.

Put together, this suggests magic, science, and religion are better
understood as positions that get occupied and reassigned across history
than as three fixed content-areas. What's genuinely interesting is
Tambiah's suggestion about when the realms come back together: not
randomly, but specifically where cause and effect are unclear — where a
system produces outcomes without providing a legible mechanism, so
prediction and something more like petition end up living in the same
practice. A system whose outputs are governed by a known but
non-deterministic process fits that description unusually well.

## Grounding

Drawn from Marcel Mauss's *A General Theory of Magic* (the argument against
treating magic as a fixed category) and Stanley Tambiah's *Magic, Science,
Religion, and the Scope of Rationality* (the causality/participation split
and the claim about their reconvergence). The application to systems whose
outputs are non-deterministic but not fully opaque is original to this
note.

## How settled this is

Mauss's and Tambiah's arguments are established anthropological and
historical scholarship, checkable directly against those two books. The
specific extension — that causality and participation reconverge wherever
cause and effect go unclear, and that this describes certain modern
technical systems — is this note's own reading of Tambiah's framework, not
a claim either source makes about technology specifically.

## Open

Is "where cause and effect are unclear" really the condition that reunites
causality and participation, or just the condition under which people
reach for older explanatory vocabulary because a newer one hasn't arrived
yet?
