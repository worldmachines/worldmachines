# Self-realization traded an achievable target for an asymptotic one

## The idea

For Aristotle, a human being's formal cause was the soul and its final
cause was eudaimonia — flourishing, understood as a state a life could
actually reach by living well. The two causes were distinct, and the telos
was, in principle, achievable: a completed life could be judged to have
gotten there or not.

Charles Taylor's account of the Romantic shift to self-realization as the
governing ideal reads as a quiet conflation of those two causes. "Become
fully yourself" folds formal and final cause into a single injunction —
your nature just is your goal — and in doing so, on Taylor's telling,
self-realization stops being straightforwardly achievable the way
eudaimonia was. Full self-realization becomes something closer to an
asymptote: a direction a life can move in without a coherent notion of
arrival, since there's no independent standard (comparable to Aristotle's
account of a flourishing life) against which "fully realized" could be
checked. The teleology got more personal and, in the same move, less
achievable — a strange trade to have made so quietly that it now reads as
simply what selfhood is for.

## Grounding

Drawn from Charles Taylor's *Cosmic Connections: Poetry in the Age of
Disenchantment* on self-realization as the successor to rational teleology,
read against Aristotle's distinction between formal and final cause in the
*Nicomachean Ethics*. The specific framing — that the shift conflates the
two Aristotelian causes and trades achievability for direction — is
original to this note.

## How settled this is

Aristotle's formal/final cause distinction and his account of eudaimonia
as achievable are standard readings of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, checkable
directly against the text. That Taylor's account of Romantic
self-realization amounts to a conflation of those two causes, specifically,
is this note's own reading of *Cosmic Connections* — Taylor documents the
historical shift in detail, but the causal-conflation framing is this
note's synthesis, not a claim quoted from him directly.

## Open

If self-realization really is asymptotic rather than achievable, does that
make it a worse teleology than eudaimonia, or just a different kind — one
suited to a life that expects to keep revising its own standard rather
than meet a fixed one?
