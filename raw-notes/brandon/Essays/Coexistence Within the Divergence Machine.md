# Coexistence Within the Divergence Machine

*Brandon Pink · July 2026. A response to Venkatesh Rao's "The Divergence Machine."*

Earlier this month, Lauren Teixeira published a dispatch from the Hamilton Society, a fast-growing debate club for San Francisco's right-leaning tech intellectuals. The Society hosts parliamentary-style debates on questions such as whether America needs a Caesar. Jackets and ties are mandatory, debaters address one another in the third person, meetings open with the Pledge of Allegiance, and membership is arranged in a hierarchy of titled members and untitled assistants. The founders, observant Catholics who met through Mass, know exactly how all of this looks. Teixeira's read, in "We All Want Daddy to Come Riding In," is that the form is the point: these are young men reaching for a structure and order that the institutions around them no longer provide.

Occurrences like the Hamilton Society, and trends like the tradwife phenomenon, have been happening more and more lately. This seems to me to be further evidence of the decay of the modern world system, its institutions, and its meaning-making logics, which people have lived under for several centuries now. The fact that the Hamilton Society seems goofy is itself evidence that the old world order is dissolving around us. This looks to me like more confirmation of Rao's World Machines thesis, which posits that we are in the early transition between two sets of world-governing forces: the Modernity Machine and the Divergence Machine.

Venkatesh Rao's Contraptions essay, "The Divergence Machine," proposes that we are living through the startup phase of the new machine: a civilizational logic that is replacing the convergent, canonical order that has organized the world since roughly 1600. The Divergence Machine is not an anti-Modernity Machine, and it is not in any systematic relationship to modernity at all. It has its own internal logic, one that assumes the persistence of modernity's historical effects without assuming the perpetuation of the machine itself.

Rao is careful to distinguish the Divergence Machine from three things it is not: late modernity, which persists as an *energetic zombie*, still organizing enormous amounts of the world's energy while animated by no living intelligence; postmodernity, an *intelligent ghost* that organizes very little energy but whose intellectual currents remain strong decades past their peak; and metamodernity, a project of doleful adaptation and sentimental creativity that attempts piecemeal revivals of what modernity flattened. The tradwife phenomenon is metamodern in this sense, and I read the Hamilton Society the same way: a sentimental revival of institutional forms that the modern order built but can no longer maintain, which are simultaneously sincere about their own proceedings, and also aware they are a bit absurd and don't quite fit in modern society.

Rao treats these three as spent perspectives, noise to be filtered out when trying to see the Divergence Machine clearly. For his purposes, that is the right move. But I want to make a claim about what happens to them next, because I do not think they simply evaporate.

## Islands become strands

My thesis is that the islands left behind by the Modernity Machine's decay will become strands of the Divergence Machine. There is room within the Divergence Machine for aspects of modernity, and for the zombie, the ghost, and the metamodern revival projects, in a way that the Modernity Machine never provided for aspects of the world that preceded *it*.

This is a key difference between the two machines. The logics of the Divergence Machine allow for the coexistence of local conditions set by local logics. Whereas, when local conditions existed during the modernity era, it was primarily because the machine had not yet been able to subsume that locality. Locality was not tolerated so much as it was pending. Rao's braid metaphor makes the point: excursions away from the canonical centerline were anomalies, the Modernity Machine's *bugspace*. In the Divergence Machine, that bugspace is the *feature space*.

The elements of Rao's taxonomy-of-things-that-aren't-the-Divergence-Machine will therefore have pockets of space within the Divergence Machine to exist. The zombie, the ghost, and the metamodern revivals can each persist in their own localities, and nothing in the machine's logic requires that any of them be defeated.

## Not the bumper sticker

One of Rao's probes for identifying Divergence-Machine phenomena asks whether it involves people understanding each other less, but getting along better. I see this coexistence as a key emerging feature of the Divergence Machine. But I am using the word a little differently than the connotations that come with the famous bumper sticker.

The Coexist bumper sticker implies an active sort of tolerance, much more closely aligned with ideologies such as DEI and Woke-ism. In Rao's terms, that version of coexistence is itself an attempted universal world-machine logic, one more bid for convergent canonicity. And it is an attempt to *impose* a logic on the world through brute force. Rao's principle applies here: responses to convergent canonicity tend to become part of that convergent canonicity.

The coexisting that will be made possible by the Divergence Machine will be less active. It will be less intentional, in that it will happen regardless of attempts toward active imposition by any ideology. Its logics will be governed by forces largely outside the control of any one ideological group. And it will be much more *weird*, in the sense of Rao's *Permaweird*, than anything the bumper-sticker imagination can picture. The various localities, living through their particular logics, will be far less intelligible to one another than the bumper-sticker-Coexist types envision. They will get along anyway, because the machine's logic does not require mutual legibility as the price of peace.

The Modernity Machine demanded that a locality be understood, mapped and labeled, before it could be accommodated. The Divergence Machine does not. The Hamilton Society will get to keep its jackets and ties, it will go on looking goofy to most of the localities around it, and under the logics of the new machine, that will not matter. What coexistence looks like without mutual intelligibility is, I suspect, one of the questions the next few decades will answer.
