# Loose Threads: What Darwin's Approach May Tell Us About Psychohistory

*Brandon Pink · August 2026. Written after the SIGPSY July meeting on historicism, read against chapter 4 of Dennett's* Darwin's Dangerous Idea.

*Related literature notes: [[Institutional Capacity and Internet Transition]] · [[Networks vs. Hierarchies as a Historical Change Dynamic]]*

## Psychohistory and the Protocol Institute

Since June of this year I have been sitting in on the Psychohistory Special Interest Group (SIGPSY), a working group that grew out of Venkatesh Rao's World Machines project at the Protocol Institute. The name is a joke that is not entirely a joke. Asimov's psychohistory was a mathematics of civilizations so powerful that Hari Seldon could chart the fall of an empire and the length of the dark age that followed it. Nobody in the group believes something like that version of psychohistory is currently available. However, we believe a real-world implementation of psychohistory could be possible. It would look less like Seldon and more like long-range climate forecasting: probabilistic, range-based, less confident the further out you push, and still genuinely useful. A certain kind of pattern tends to produce a certain kind of outcome in a certain kind of context, even if the exact date is impossible to claim.

The current work of the SIGPSY group centers on trying to implement this version of psychohistory. There is a book club that works through sources, and there are side projects that try to turn pieces of the reading into something a model could use. The most recent meeting of the group, held in late July 2026, took up the topic of historicism, the position that history has discoverable regularities that can be studied mathematically. Unfortunately, I was not at the meeting and had to piece together the discussion after the fact.

From what I gathered, the room was more skeptical than what one may expect out of a group with Psychohistory in the name. Here are a few of the objections which were raised. History is not like physics, because the law of gravity keeps changing: humanity reconfigures itself, so any framework is vulnerable to having one of its core assumptions negated underneath it. Mathematical treatment also misses enormous amounts of history that never made it into a dataset, including the ordinary daily life of ordinary people, which was never recorded in a form anybody can code. And predictions about human beings change the behavior of the human beings they are about, so a forecast is partly a cause of its own outcome. The responses in favor of historicism I found most useful narrowed the target rather than abandoning it: look for the places where a long cycle crosses a threshold and produces an event, aim at precise *prophecies* that fail informatively rather than vague forecasts that cannot fail at all, and key them to conditions rather than to dates. And one suggestion I keep returning to, which is where I want to pick the thread up: even if the modeling never becomes predictive, the taxonomy it produces might reveal a topography, and the best use of a large model of history might be an *atlas* we could read to see what terrain the world might hold.

As I reviewed the transcript from this meeting, some vague connections emerged between several threads the SIGPSY group have been tugging on this summer. First is the temporality, or the way historians frame or view the passage of history. Second is historicism:  a field of historical study that attempts to find cycles of history and, in some cases, to find principles that explain those cycles. Third is Darwinism, as presented by Dennett's *Darwin's Dangerous Idea*, which is the book club pick for August; specifically, the way Darwin unlocked an entire field by sidestepping a classification-based orthodoxy and embracing a mechanistic, process-oriented way of viewing species. It seems to me that, when thinking about psychohistory, there may be some lessons to take from Darwin. Darwinism and psychohistory are certainly not analogous problems. However, psychohistory currently seems tantalizing, taboo, and intractable, much like how explaining the origin of species seemed to Darwin. In this essay I want to play with this connection. I want to start by expanding on the threads I listed above to lay out the "field of play" of the SIGPSY discussions this summer. Then I want to lay out Dennett's *Darwin*, up to the fourth chapter, which is where I've read at the point of writing this essay. Then I want to attempt to analogize Darwinism to psychohistory. Finally, I want to close the essay with an embryonic attempt to advance contingency as a mechanism for psychohistory, roughly analogous to the function that natural selection played for Darwinism.

This essay will be long. It will contain some digressions and explanations of ideas like temporality, cycle theory, and Darwinism. I may develop these ideas further in future essays as I clarify my own thinking. With that said, let's attempt to connect some threads.

## Temporality

First I need one piece of vocabulary, because the group has been leaning on it and because the argument I want to make depends on it.

*Temporality* is the historian's framework for how the passage of history works: the shape a scholar assumes time has when they set out to explain why one thing followed another. It is usually implicit, but it is doing a lot of work anyway, because it determines what counts as an explanation in the first place.

William Sewell, in "Three Temporalities: Toward an Eventful Sociology," sorts the usual assumptions into three kinds. **Teleological** temporality treats history as having a direction and a destination, and explains an occurrence by the contribution it makes to that end state. Modernization theory works this way, and so does the Marxist progression toward communism: things happen because history is going somewhere and they are steps along the road. **Experimental** temporality treats history as a supply of cases. It breaks the past into comparable units, holds variables against each other across them, and assumes that the causal machinery stays roughly constant from case to case, which is what makes the comparison legitimate. Comparative historical sociology runs on this. **Eventful** temporality, which is the one Sewell argues for, holds that events transform the structures they occur in. The causal properties of the world are themselves changed by what happens, so the rules operating after an event are not the rules that were operating before it. This is where path dependence lives, and it is Sewell's answer to the experimental assumption that the machinery holds still.

To Sewell's three I would add a fourth that he does not treat as a category: **cyclical** temporality. Here the shape of time is recurrence. History returns to configurations resembling ones it has occupied before, and an explanation consists of locating the present somewhere on a wheel that has turned before and will turn again.

## The cycle theorists

Cyclical temporality is old and it is stubborn. Ibn Khaldun laid out a version of it in the fourteenth century: groups with strong *asabiyyah*, or group solidarity, conquer settled civilizations, grow urban and comfortable, lose the solidarity that got them there, and are conquered in turn by the next group that still has it, on a rhythm of three or four generations. Spengler gave civilizations a life cycle with a season structure and a guaranteed winter. Pitirim Sorokin proposed that cultures oscillate between ideational and sensate supersystems over centuries. William Strauss and Neil Howe compressed the wheel to a human scale with the generational *saeculum*, roughly eighty to ninety years divided into four turnings, a scheme that got a great deal of popular attention and very little academic respect. Carlota Perez, working on technology rather than politics, describes surges of fifty to sixty years in which a techno-economic paradigm gets installed through a financial bubble, crashes, and is then deployed across the real economy.

Peter Turchin is the most recent of these and by a wide margin the most mathematically serious. His *cliodynamics*, developed with Sergey Nefedov in *Secular Cycles* and extended in *Ages of Discord*, inherits Jack Goldstone's demographic-structural theory and formalizes it. The model tracks three interacting components: the general population, the elites, and the state. Population grows while land is still abundant, which eventually makes labor cheap and land expensive and moves wealth upward. Elite numbers then grow faster than the positions available to hold them, which produces the change mechanism Turchin is best known for: *elite overproduction*, where too many aspirants are competing for too few positions of power. Intra-elite competition thus intensifies, the state's fiscal position deteriorates, and the system enters a crisis phase of instability, civil conflict, or state failure. Then population falls, land is abundant again, and the cycle resets. In agrarian societies the full turn takes something like two to three hundred years.

What makes Turchin different from previous cyclical explanations is the apparatus underneath it. Turchin writes the dynamics as equations. He builds a Political Stress Index out of measurable state variables so that the model produces a number rather than a mood. And the Seshat Global History Databank, which he co-founded, codes quantitative variables across hundreds of polities over ten thousand years, so that claims about civilizational patterns can be tested statistically instead of argued anecdotally. The ambition is openly predictive. Turchin wrote in 2010 that the structural indicators pointed toward serious instability in the United States in the 2020s, and he has spent the years since pointing at the scoreboard.

There are, of course, criticisms of Turchin (as well as cyclical theories in general). The dataset underneath any mathematical treatment of history is thinner than the mathematics on top of it suggests. Ben Chugg notes that Turchin's fifty-year cycle in American political violence rests on 1,590 recorded incidents, a corpus worth setting against the 18,830 homicides committed in the United States in 2018 alone. A category that admits fewer cases across the whole span of American history than a single ordinary year of ordinary killing produces is a category doing a great deal of quiet work, and Chugg makes the sharpest version of the point when he observes that Turchin's political violence excludes the American Civil War. What, then, is political violence supposed to mean? The coding elsewhere is subjective in the same way. What counts as a revolution? What counts as a social elite? Chugg also observes that cycles are reliably predictable in nature only where a system is effectively isolated, with few interactions and cooperative initial conditions, and human societies are the opposite of that. Patterns turn up in any dataset that gets analyzed enough times, which is a large part of what produced the replication crisis in the social sciences, and once we have a pattern we are very good at generating a story for why it had to be there. The predictions that follow are then hard to falsify, because the vagueness in the definitions leaves room to relabel a miss as a hit.

Notice what nearly all of that reduces to. The dataset is thin because somebody had to decide what to count. The coding is contested because the categories are contested. The predictions are slippery because the terms are slippery. Underneath the statistical complaints sits one philosophical complaint: *you have not satisfactorily told me what a revolution is, or what an elite is, or what a cycle is, and until you do, none of the machinery you have built on top of those words means anything.* This is the sticking point for most attempts at historicism.

Coincidentally, at the same time the Psychohistory Special Interest Group was wrestling with historical modeling, I was starting to read through the next book pick: *Darwin's Dangerous Idea* by Daniel Dennett. I say coincidentally because a few days after that meeting, in the middle of the fourth chapter, Dennett's discussion of Darwinism provided me with some food for thought regarding historical modeling.

## The Tree of Life

In chapter 4 of *Darwin's Dangerous Idea*, Daniel Dennett spends time walking around the "Tree of Life". The Tree is a dendrogram, one particular way to visually represent a record of evolutionary descent. Every organism that ever lived sits on it, connected to its parents and its offspring, and the whole thing can be drawn as a branching diagram running across deep time. A common next step for folks looking at the Tree of Life diagram is to attempt to find the branches that represent species. This should be easy, right? Just pick one branch and color it in to represent whatever species you want. The process of identifying a species from the Tree of Life diagram, though, is much more complicated than one would think.

Take one of the standard definitions of species, reproductive isolation: two populations are distinct species when they can no longer produce fertile offspring together. That works cleanly when you are holding a gorilla in one hand and a human in the other. Now walk it backwards. But instead of following the dendrogram schematic, imagine you are following a sketch of the evolving organisms themselves, generation by generation, mutation by mutation. Trace both lineages back toward their common ancestor and try to find the generation where the split between the gorilla branch and the homo sapiens branch occurred. There isn't one. The differences between any generation and the next are too small to carry the weight of a category. There is no moment in time where gorillas began, and at no single moment in time is there a clean line in space between the branch heading toward gorillas and the branch heading toward us.

So where do species come from? Dennett's answer is that two things do the work, and neither of them is a principle. The first is extinction. Enormous numbers of intermediate lineages died out, and their dying cut gaps into the Tree. The gaps are what let us treat the surviving clumps as distinct. The second is separation in space. Surviving populations drifted apart geographically, and distance let phenotypes diverge far enough to look obviously different. Species, in other words, is a concept that only resolves at a certain zoom level. At the zoom level of an ordinary person (or a nineteenth century zoologist) comparing two populations, treating "gorilla" and "chimpanzee" as two distinct species is workable and valuable. Zoom out past that level, to the level of all of life evolving over eons, and it dissolves into a continuous smear of parents and children, a continuous lineage of minuscule mutations where the concept of "species" simply doesn't make sense.

## Arrangement and classification

Dennett goes on to explain how Darwin sidestepped this sticking point. He says Darwin gave biology an *arrangement* rather than a *classification*, and that the arrangement was sufficient to get on with the science. That distinction is important and not immediately apparent, so it is worth slowing down on what each of those words means.

A **classification** is a system of kinds. It sorts the world by specifying, in advance, the criteria a thing has to satisfy in order to belong to a category. The kind is prior to its members: there is such a thing as a horse, defined by the characteristics that make something a horse, and individual horses are instances of it. This is what biology inherited from Aristotle and formalized under Linnaeus, and before Darwin it was the whole enterprise. The essences were assumed to be real and fixed, the boundaries between them were assumed to be sharp, and the job of the naturalist was to discover the boundaries God had already drawn. A classification answers the question *what is this thing?* by naming the box it belongs in, and it lives or dies on whether the boxes can be defined without contradiction.

An **arrangement** is a system of relative positions. It does not say what anything is. It says how things stand with respect to one another. The Tree of Life is an arrangement in exactly this sense: every claim it makes is relational, of the form *this organism shares a more recent common ancestor with that one than with this third one*. There is no box anywhere in it. There is only connection, and distance measured along connections. You can build an arrangement without having settled a single definition, because the relation, in this case descent, is observable between individuals and does not require any kinds to exist at all.

Which is why Darwinism was able to walk away from the species problem without losing anything. The explanatory machinery Darwin proposed, descent with modification under natural selection, operates on individual organisms and on populations. It never once refers to a species. Nothing in the mechanism needs the category, so the fuzziness of the category does not propagate into the theory. The vagueness stays where it started, in the labels we apply to regions of the tree for convenience, and it never reaches the physics.

The last thing worth saying about arrangements is what keeps them from being mush. An arrangement is not a license to say whatever you like. It carries real commitments and it can be wrong: you can attach a branch in the wrong place, and modern genetics has spent decades correcting exactly those mistakes. What an arrangement does not carry is *definitional* commitments. It is checkable without being classificatory, and that combination is what let biology proceed in 1859 on a structure whose categories are still unsettled in 2026.

## Historical events and cycles are species-like: arrangements, not classifications

Here is the connection that came to me while I was reading. Just as Darwinism let biology move forward by treating species as an arrangement rather than an airtight classification, psychohistory may be able to proceed without tying itself into knots over what temporality is, what an event is, and whether cycles of history exist and are worth studying.

My current thinking is that these concepts are species-like:  arrangements rather than categories. Defining species is a demand biology never satisfied and, apparently, did not need to. Establishing clean definitions of events and cycles, and then substantiating them, will likely never be satisfied either. Successful psychohistory may not need it.

Critics point at the gooeyness of these terms as though gooeyness were disqualifying. I do not think it is. It is what a concept looks like when it lives at a particular resolution. Ask what a cycle is with enough precision and you will get a smear of causes and conditions with no natural joints in it, in the same way that asking where gorillas begin gets you a smear of parents and children. That does not mean the concept is empty. It means the concept works at the zoom level where it works.

Notice too that the objections about coding and definition are very similar to the objection you would raise against species if you had never heard of Darwin. *You cannot tell me precisely what this category is. Your boundaries are arbitrary. Zoom in and your kinds fall apart.* All true. Biology conceded all of it and lost nothing. Perhaps psychohistory could do something similar.

And arrangements have a practical advantage over classifications, which is that we can actually build them. A classification requires you to settle the definition first and then sort the world. An arrangement only requires you to put things next to each other in a defensible order and see what the ordering reveals. Darwin's arrangement was buildable in 1859. The classification is still not finished.

This is also, I think, why the atlas idea that came out of the recent SIGPSY meeting felt right before I could justify it. An atlas is an arrangement. That is the whole point of the word. It does not tell you what a mountain essentially is. It puts the mountains in the right places relative to each other, which turns out to be enough for nearly everything you would want to do with mountains.

## What cuts the gaps

If viewing concepts like events and cycles as arrangements rather than classifications allows psychohistory to proceed, then I think the next important consideration is gaps: the factors that make an arrangement apparent and workable in the first place, and move us toward mechanistic explanations.

Extinction is what made the Tree of Life legible; it's what shaped it such that humans intuit "species". The intermediate forms died, and their absence is what our categories are drawn around. They created gaps which allow us to see and understand concepts like "species", even if those concepts are not definable.

What is the analogue that creates the gaps that allow us to understand and work with cycles and events? I think the answer is related to another concept the SIGPSY group has been working with: historical contingency. Contingency is the idea that the course that history takes depends on what came before it. World War I is contingent on the assassination of Franz Ferdinand, for instance. Trump winning the 2024 election is contingent on the bullet missing the critical parts of his head by centimeters. What makes an event stand out as an event worth psychohistorical consideration is that it changed the course of history. That is, the alternative historical paths that came after it were foreclosed in an interesting or decisive way: a branch point was genuinely open, one path was taken, the others stopped being available, and enough structure locked in afterward that the taken path became the only one with descendants. The counterfactuals went extinct. The paths taken by history and the paths taken by biological evolution are eerily similar when considered this way.

I find this clarifying for a practical reason. If the gaps come from foreclosure, then a metric of contingency never has to say what an event or cycle essentially is. It only has to describe how much of a gap a given occurrence cut into the space of what came after. That is an arrangement. It sorts occurrences along a dimension without settling the definition of the thing being sorted, and it is checkable, because a claim that one occurrence foreclosed more than another is a claim that can be argued and revised.

Where this all brings me is to mechanistic explanations. Darwinism posits natural selection as the mechanism by which species develop. Natural selection is the process by which some populations survive and pass on their genes while others go extinct. It explains how the "gaps" in the Tree of Life come to be, which is where we get the arrangement of species. Can we identify the components of contingency when looking backward at history? Can we operationalize contingency in a psychohistorical sense, as a prophetic model?

## Cranes, not skyhooks

Chapter 4 of Dennett gave me two further terms that will serve to close this essay: skyhooks and cranes.

As Dennett explains it, a skyhook is an explanation that hangs from nothing, a bit of mind or purpose smuggled in to do lifting that the process below cannot account for. This would be similar to a deus ex machina if the concept were lifted from literature and dropped into philosophy of science. In contrast to skyhooks, Dennett offers what he calls a crane. A crane is a product of earlier ordinary processes that gets used to lift the next thing. An established, scientific explanation that provides the foundation for other, subsequent explanations. His argument across the book is that every apparent skyhook in evolutionary biology has turned out, under study, to be a crane resting on cranes.

I suspect psychohistory is in the position right now of looking at the study of historical modeling and determining what parts of it are skyhooks. We have to use the vocabulary that history developed before anybody tried to model it, and *event* and *cycle* are that vocabulary. Right now those terms are asked to explain a great deal while remaining undefined. The critics are right that this is a bad situation to be permanently in. Where I increasingly part from the critics is on whether or not this precludes psychohistory as an exercise. Critics of historical modeling would have us wait for airtight definitions before proceeding. I'm becoming more convinced in the value of attempting to model history as carefully as we can, with the expectation that terms will get cashed out into mechanism along the way. That is how cranes (in the Dennett sense) get discovered. You find them by building with them.

So here is my thinking, as plainly as I can put it. Modeling history is clearly extremely difficult. Every attempt so far has serious shortcomings and is validly criticized. Making accurate claims about where history goes next looks intractable, and it looks intractable largely because of how hard it is to bring any explanatory framework to something as complex as human history.

But explaining the origin of species looked impossible before Darwin too, and for a comparable reason. Naturalists were trying to account for the diversity of life while holding fixed a set of categories, and a story about those categories, that the phenomenon itself did not respect. What unlocked the problem was not a better definition of species. It was the description of a mechanism, mindless and gradual and operating on individuals, that made the definitional question stop mattering. The intractability was never in the world. It was in the orthodoxy that the answer had to arrive in the form of properly bounded kinds.

Perhaps the key to psychohistory is a similar act of description: finding the mechanisms that actually generate historical change and letting them tell us afterward what our inherited categories were pointing at. Posit the process mechanism, build the arrangement, and work forward from there.
