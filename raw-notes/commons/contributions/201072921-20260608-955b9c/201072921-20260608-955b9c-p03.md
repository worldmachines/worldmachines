# Babel Was a Clue (part 3 of 3)

(I know, I know. Anyone who actually grew up Catholic like me will immediately point out that the Church is not exactly famous for its uncomplicated, joyful view of the body. But institutions contain multitudes, and epochs rewrite priorities.)

I’m not claiming this is the future of the Catholic Church. Things this size start far too small for anyone to claim that. The response to modernity has run for four centuries and is still running. But if something new is beginning inside it, it goes deeper than a new target. The institution is reconstituting itself for the next four-hundred-year era.

There may even be a future hiding in the anti-“Promethean dreams” stance: as the digital world grows more powerful, it may also grow less interesting, and the things we find meaningful drift back toward the physical. Re-centering human life in the given world would be quite the de-dramatizing move. Though there I’m running ahead of the clue.

What Babel/Nehemiah lets us glimpse is a possible direction. A metaphor can take an institution somewhere it never meant to go. Whether this one does, we won’t know for a long time. It is precisely the kind of small, slow clue this book club exists to catch.

In the book club’s shorthand, we call this dynamic the bugspace/feature-space flip: the bugspace of the Modernity Machine becomes the feature space of the Divergence Machine.

I’m taking this characterization of metamodernism from the “Metamodernity” section of Venkatesh Rao’s The Divergence Machine (the 2026 book-club kickoff), where it figures as a late, backward-facing current rather than a forward one — modernity’s afterglow, not its successor. Stating the objection in the framework’s own terms is the point: a strawman metamodernism would be cheap to knock down and prove nothing.

The early criticism has mostly come from the other direction — that the encyclical is too socialist, or reads like a European bureaucrat’s wishlist (procedural safeguards, GDP-beyond metrics, “transparency and accountability” by the paragraph). On our terms that criticism is half right, but mislocates the problem. Those passages are indeed the weakest in the document. They consist of dead, convergence-flavored proceduralism — the machine’s own language borrowed to police the machine. The life in this encyclical isn’t in its policy. It’s in its metaphors. The critics and I are pointing at the same paragraphs and drawing opposite conclusions about why they fall flat.

Worth being precise about what “alive” is doing here. In the framework a live element is generative — it opens pathways, recombines, points past itself — where a dead one merely administers or preserves. By that test most of the document’s prose is administrative and much of its imagery is generative, which is the whole tension this essay is pulling on.

To be clear, I’m decidedly not comparing AI to the Almighty. The point is structural, not theological: defining a thing by what it is not — the via negativa — is a mode of reasoning the Church has run for well over a millennium, back through the apophatic tradition to Pseudo-Dionysius in the fifth or sixth century. Leo is just pointing the old apparatus at a new object.

The pairing is older than it looks. In the Aristotelian-Thomist grammar the Church reasons in, body and image aren’t two attributes bolted onto the human; they are matter and form — the body the materia, the imago Dei something like the forma that makes the matter a person rather than a mechanism. Hylomorphism is the technical name.

Pius XI, Quadragesimo Anno (1931), is where subsidiarity receives its canonical formulation; the deeper root is Leo XIII’s Rerum Novarum (1891), the document this encyclical is consciously written in the lineage of. The dating carries weight in the argument: a roughly hundred-year-old principle surfacing now is a far more legible clue than some timeless Catholic preference for the local, which is what a looser telling would imply.

The encyclical shows you the tempo itself. Its section on slavery calls the Church’s long complicity “a wound in Christian memory” and, “in the name of the Church,” asks pardon (§176), noting that slavery’s “full incompatibility” with the faith took eighteen centuries “to be explicitly recognized.” Same slow-motion pattern: not new doctrine replacing old, but a truth formally held becoming a social reality the institution could finally see. If it can take eighteen centuries to fully see one of its own commitments, a hundred-year-old instinct surfacing in a metaphor is exactly the size and speed of clue to expect.
