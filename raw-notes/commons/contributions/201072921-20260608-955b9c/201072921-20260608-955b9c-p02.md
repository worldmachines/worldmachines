# Babel Was a Clue (part 2 of 3)

Seen this way, divergence runs through the document, not just through one opening contrast. The “rejected stones — the poor, the sick, the migrants” become “the cornerstone” (§16): the places the old machine filed as errors are exactly where Leo builds.1 His verb for what to do with AI is “to disarm,” which he glosses as “restoring it to the plurality of human cultures and ways of life” (§110).

Someone will object before you finish the sentence: this is just metamodernism. In our framework’s telling, metamodernism is modernity that cannot mourn itself — a backward-looking structure of feeling that reaches for re-enchantment and sincerity precisely because the Progress it still wants has stopped arriving.2 On that reading the encyclical looks like a textbook case: re-enchantment, the meaning crisis, the nostalgia that wants what modernity used to deliver. Subsidiarity becomes decentralized modernity, or federalism with incense. This objection frames the encyclical as late-modernity presenting itself as a new era. And much of it is exactly that. But not those metaphors. The metaphors are the part of this document that is alive3 — and they are doing more work than the document quite needs them to.4

Apophatic Machines

When describing AI itself, the encyclical takes an almost apophatic approach. It repeatedly tells us what AI is not. It is not wisdom. It is not judgment. It is not responsibility. It is not relationship. It is not dignity. The document spends surprisingly little time defining artificial intelligence positively and a great deal of time tracing its boundaries. Not this. Not that. Not this either.

The effect is curious. Every limitation assigned to AI throws the human person into sharper relief. Negative theology traditionally approached God through negation, describing what could not be said. Leo uses a similar structure to illuminate humanity. By saying what AI cannot be, he gradually reveals what he believes a person is.5

The encyclical anchors this revelation in two specific claims. First, humanity as embodied incarnation — the “wounded yet beloved flesh” (§231), the body as “a dwelling place of God and a temple of the Holy Spirit” (§239). Second, humanity as being made in the likeness of God — dignity as a gift that “precedes and transcends each person” (§50). You are a body, and you are an image.6 That is what the document is defending when it defends the human. And there’s something quietly strange about needing to say it at all.

It is weird that this seems weird. An institution stands up and says: you are embodied, you are made in the image of God, and that is the ground that no optimizer can touch — and in 2026 this reads as a contrarian, almost eccentric position rather than a banal one. The water we swim in has shifted far enough that you are a sacred body now lands as a provocation. If our threshold for the “weird” continues to shift, that drift is the machine in motion — and we are catching the signal early.

On the Back Foot

Now let’s zoom out. For the entire run of the Modernity Machine, the Church has been on its back foot. It opened with the Counter-Reformation: a convergence project (Trent, the Index, centralization) — the most successful parts of which were aesthetic: Bernini, the Baroque. Beauty as a counter-modern media strategy.

Centuries later, Vatican II tried a different adjustment. It made a structural turn toward divergence — shattering the single language of Latin into a plurality of local vernaculars — but in the process, it largely discarded that Counter-Reformation spirit of beauty. It has been a centuries-long sequence of accommodations, all of them made from inside the pull system. A turning point came when the Church codified a long-held instinct — subsidiarity was named in Leo XIII’s Rerum Novarum (1891) and “systematically formulated” by Pius XI in 1931 (§31) — barely a hundred years ago.7 It is a push-shaped principle.8

One more metaphor to dwell on: “Promethean dreams” (§128). It would be easy to read this as the Church selecting a new enemy. I think that misses how the metaphor is working. Promethean dreams function as a contrast agent, not a target. They make the human person visible. The Promethean dream is the dream of the technocratic paradigm. But the encyclical keeps returning to bodies, relationships, and communities. The point is not merely that technology should be used responsibly. It is that the human being cannot be fully understood in technological terms.

Leo may even have a bit of a blogger’s instinct here. The encyclical arrives wrapped in AI because AI is what everyone wants to discuss. But the deeper subject is Catholic social teaching itself: dignity, subsidiarity, solidarity, embodiment, and the common good. AI supplies the sizzle. The social doctrine is the steak.
