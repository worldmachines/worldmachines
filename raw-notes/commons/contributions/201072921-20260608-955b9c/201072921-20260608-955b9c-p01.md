# Babel Was a Clue (part 1 of 3)

Magnifica Humanitas presents itself plainly enough. On May 25, 2026, the Vatican released Leo XIV’s first encyclical, “on safeguarding the human person in the time of artificial intelligence,” drawing on 135 years of Catholic social teaching from Rerum Novarum forward. Dignity, the common good, subsidiarity, solidarity, social justice. And a clue.

The clue is buried in two biblical metaphors: the Tower of Babel and Nehemiah’s rebuilding of the walls of Jerusalem. From the encyclical:

The story of Babel appears in the Book of Genesis… After settling in a plain in the land of Shinar, the people decided to build a city and a tower “with its top in the heavens” (Gen 11:4). Fearing being scattered across the earth, they sought to guarantee stability and power for themselves, and above all to “make a name” for themselves. It was an impressive feat: a single language, a single technology, a single direction. (§7)

Also from the encyclical:

Nehemiah convened the families, assigned each of them a section of the wall to rebuild, listened to their concerns, coordinated their efforts and addressed any opposition. The narrative shows how the city is reborn, not through the initiative of one man, but through the shared responsibility of all: men, women, priests, artisans, heads of households and young people all play a part. (§8)

If you’re not seeing the clue, that’s because you’re not in the Contraptions book club this year. Our worldmachines.org historiography sets out long eras, called world machines, and we are living through the sunset of one (the Modernity Machine) and the dawn of the next (the Divergence Machine). The Modernity Machine is a pull system dragging everyone toward a single future — rationalization, material abundance, and scientific mastery — and calls the pull Progress. The Divergence Machine is a push system: it produces radically different outcomes depending on local conditions and admits no single destination, no one place we’re all headed. Convergence versus divergence; one future versus many.

Rather than these being a good-versus-evil contrast, the machines offer neutral visions of convergence and divergence. Applying this to the quotes: Babel is the Modernity Machine — one language, one direction, one goal. Nehemiah is the Divergence Machine — many hands, many voices, plurality that coheres without being flattened. The encyclical comes down against convergence, which I did not see coming from the Catholic Church.

So that’s nice and all, but so what? You can read the encyclical anywhere, and a hundred outlets will tell you what it says about AI. But the live question is stranger than that. What if the first important Catholic document of the AI age is not mainly about artificial intelligence, but about the exhaustion of convergence as a way of imagining the human future? If this reading is right, the encyclical is less a warning about technology than a sign that the Church may be repositioning itself after centuries of arguing with modernity on its own terms.

But why would the Pope open an AI encyclical with a distinction right out of our book club? Because…

Metaphors Come First

Metaphors come first, and arguments come second. A writer reaches for an image before the argument is settled, and then, across a long text, the argument shifts to inhabit the space the metaphor opens up. The metaphor carves the riverbed; the prose is the water that rushes in to follow its contours. Metaphors are often where historical reorientations appear first, on the level of intuition. Before institutions change their doctrines, they change their metaphors. And when the metaphor shifts from one tower to many households rebuilding a wall, it is worth asking whether something deeper than an AI policy debate is beginning to move.

We aren’t cataloguing divergence the way birdwatchers tick off species. We’re hunting the small early signals of changes that are big and slow — the kind that take centuries to fully arrive, and that are legible at the start only as a clue. World-machine shifts don’t announce themselves; they show up first as strange little mismatches. Babel and Nehemiah may be one of those mismatches. And I can’t help but notice that these metaphors are doing something real and generative: they do not merely restate the Church’s old suspicion of technocracy, but begin to sketch a more positive picture of plurality as the form of repair.

Describing AI, Leo says these systems are “more ‘cultivated’ than ‘built’” (§98). They are grown within a framework, not fully designed in advance, and for that reason even their makers “do not understand what they produce” (§99). The language here is biological. It presents AI as something that develops inside conditions and remains partly opaque even to the people who create it.
