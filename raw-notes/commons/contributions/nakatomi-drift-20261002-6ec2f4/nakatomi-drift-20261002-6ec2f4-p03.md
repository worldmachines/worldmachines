# Nakatomi Drift (part 3 of 5)

The usual scholar – not you, darling – enters through the lobby and takes the elevator to the designated floor, or is led there by AI. The Nakatomi reader crawls through the ventilation system. An intellectual Nakatomi move – the Nakatomi Drift – occurs when you use an idea through an affordance that was not part of its intended intellectual architecture.

For example:

- treating Tuan not as a geographer describing human experience, but as a theorist of knowledge navigation;

- treating psychogeography not as urban criticism, but as a method for literature exploration;

- treating Feyerabend not merely as a philosopher of science, but as a designer of controlled route-breaking;

- treating an action-film building as a model for the latent topology of a corpus.

This is more specific than interdisciplinarity. Interdisciplinarity often connects the front doors of two buildings. The Nakatomi Drift passes through their service infrastructure.

The Nakatomi Drift separates a thicket from a rubbish heap. The thicket permits almost anything to become a navigational affordance, while retaining later processes of testing, selection, articulation, and cultivation.

The thicket as an anticipatory environment #

There is a danger, though, in treating the Nakatomi Drift as merely a clever way of discovering hidden routes. That would still assume that the building is complete and that every route already exists somewhere inside it, waiting to be found. It would turn the unknown into an unread floor plan.

Tim Ingold gives us a more useful distinction: where intention is predictive, attention is anticipatory. Prediction projects a destination from the present and organizes action around reaching it. Anticipation moves into a world that is still taking form and becomes responsive to what appears there.

Ingold distinguishes not-knowing from ignorance. Ignorance assumes that the answer already exists and that someone simply lacks it. Not-knowing belongs to a world still in formation, where both the path and the thing being sought may change through the act of seeking.

Attention, for Ingold, has two sides: exposure and attunement. Exposure pulls you out of position. It removes the solid ground of understanding and leaves you vulnerable to whatever the world is doing. Attunement is the skill that follows: learning to notice the grain, current, pressure, or affordance well enough to continue moving.

Walking contains both. With every step you fall forward into a brief void and regain your footing when the other foot meets the ground. The next step begins from that newly acquired Place. Anticipation is not knowing where the ground will be while becoming capable of meeting it.

Rebecca Solnit gives the unknown its existential character. Losing something means that the familiar has diminished. Getting lost means that the unfamiliar has appeared and the world has become larger than your knowledge of it. The point is not to erase what you know, but to let it stop governing every possible arrival.

A thicket therefore has two complementary functions. It disorients, loosening the official routes between ideas. And it educates attention, making the thinker more sensitive to affordances that only become perceptible during movement. Without the first function, you remain on predictable paths. Without the second, you merely become lost.

The Portal is not a doorway between two already completed rooms. It is an arrangement of Places that changes what Space can become as you pass through it. A Nakatomi corridor may not simply be hidden in the walls; it may be activated, or even made, through the passage.

Prediction consults the building plan.

Randomness wanders through rooms.

Anticipation feels the wall for hollowness.

How getting lost produces new ideas #

New ideas probably do not arise from Space alone. Completely undifferentiated openness produces noise or paralysis. Nor do they arise from Place alone, because established Places tend to reproduce their own meanings. They arise through a repeated oscillation:

Place → Space → unexpected Place → enlarged Space

Or, with Ingold’s terms folded in:

Place → exposure → anticipatory movement → attunement → provisional Place → replenished Space

You depart from something sufficiently stable to push against. You enter a region where conventional movement is suspended. You encounter or construct a new temporary Place. From there, previously unavailable movement becomes possible.

Thus Thicketing is a combination of partially stabilizing a Thicket with Places, followed by Nakatomi Drifting. The iterations look like ratchets:

- A Place supplies an affordance.

- You misuse or extend that affordance.

- The move exposes an unrecognized relation.

- You pause and give that relation provisional form.

- The new Place changes the topology of the surrounding Space.

- You move again.
