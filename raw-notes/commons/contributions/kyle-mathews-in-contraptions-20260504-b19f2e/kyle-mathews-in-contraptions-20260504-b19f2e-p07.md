# Primitive Accumulation: Field Notes on Pre-Contact World-Machines (part 7 of 14)

"He's trying to steer the machine," I said. "He doesn't have the power to stop it. But he's trying to introduce friction, to create accountability mechanisms, to make it harder for the operators to ignore what they're doing."

"Is it working?"

"Not enough. But it's not nothing." I stopped walking, staring at my reflection in the dark water of the Guadalquivir. "He arrived at the correct conclusion."

"By Culture standards?"[c][d]

"By any reasonable metric. The Indians are fully human. They do possess what we would call rights. Their suffering is morally relevant. His position, stripped of the theological framing, is essentially what any Contact ethicist would affirm."

"And he got there through Catholic theology and Aristotelian philosophy. Frameworks we would consider foundationally mistaken."

"Yes." I started walking again. "That's the puzzle I keep returning to. Wrong premises, correct conclusions. It's happening too often to be coincidence."

"I have a partial explanation," Just Checking said slowly. "Selection bias. We notice the dissidents who converge on positions we recognize as correct. Las Casas, the Venetian reformers, the critics and questioners. But for every Las Casas, there might be ten thinkers arriving at conclusions we'd find abhorrent through equally sophisticated reasoning."

"You think moral convergence is an illusion?"

"I think it might be an artifact of who we pay attention to."

I considered this. "That's a genuinely useful critique. But I don't think it explains everything. The dissidents are too consistent, too widespread, too similar in their conclusions despite their different starting points."

"Then what's your explanation?"

I didn't have one. Not yet. But I was beginning to suspect that the answer had something to do with the machine itself—that certain kinds of coordination problems had limited solution spaces, and sufficiently complex minds kept finding the same answers because there weren't that many answers to find.

[QUERY: GSV EXPERIENCING A SIGNIFICANT GRAVITAS SHORTFALL, 1519 CE]

Sifan: Your analysis emphasizes discontinuity with the 1287 configuration. Clarify: are we observing replacement, collapse, or transformation?

________________

RESPONSE:

Transformation. Violent, uneven, and morally horrifying in ways that test my professional detachment, but transformation nonetheless.

The 1287 system emerged from accumulated local interactions—nobody designed it. The current configuration is being consciously constructed by actors who understand themselves as builders. The Spanish Crown doesn't simply trade with the Americas; it explicitly conceptualizes its activities as creating a new world. The phrase appears in royal charters, administrative documents, theological treatises. They have theories about what they're doing.

Wrong theories, mostly. Distorted by religious assumptions, motivated reasoning, and the economic interests of people who profit from extraction. But theories nonetheless.

What I'm watching is the construction of something new. Call it the Legibility Machine. The 1287 system ran on local knowledge, personal reputation, distributed trust. The new system is designed to make the world readable—enumerable, standardized, interoperable at distances that exceed human relationship.

They're building bureaucracies that can track populations across continents. Legal codes that apply regardless of local custom. Measurement systems that make one place commensurable with another. The machine isn't just connecting existing structures; it's remaking them to be connectable.

This is not an accident. It's a response to the limits of the previous configuration. You cannot extract silver from Peru and deliver it to Madrid using only reputation and relationship. You need paperwork. You need standardization. You need legibility.

The extraction is horrifying. But the infrastructure being built to enable it will outlast the extraction. That infrastructure is what interests me.
