# Primitive Accumulation: Field Notes on Pre-Contact World-Machines (part 9 of 14)

I watched a deputy from Provence make an impassioned argument about the natural equality of citizens. The chamber was electric with possibility. These men—flawed, ambitious, often petty—were nonetheless reaching for something unprecedented.

This was what I had been waiting for across five centuries of observation. Not just the Trade Machine, with its distributed coordination. Not just the Legibility Machine, with its standardization and enumeration. Something else. Something I was beginning to call—in my own notes, not yet in official reports—the Dignity Machine.

The principle that differences between humans did not preclude equal treatment. That rights could be universal precisely because they did not depend on sameness. That a system could integrate differentiated parts without homogenizing them.

"I want to stay," I said. "I want to see what they build."

"Then we stay."

Paris, 1793 CE

Four years later, I returned to a different city.

The men who had written the Declaration were killing each other. The Committee of Public Safety had been in session for hours. I watched from a gallery seat I had no business occupying, using methods I preferred not to think about too carefully. Below me, men who had written documents about the rights of man were debating how many of those men to execute.

Just Checking manifested as a barely perceptible warmth at my shoulder. "You're doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you calculate intervention scenarios. Your biometrics shift. I've learned to recognize it."

I didn't deny it. Below, Robespierre was speaking about virtue and terror as inseparable. Four years ago, I had watched some of these same men draft the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. Now they were eating each other.

"I could—" I started.

"No."

"You don't know what I was going to say."

"You were going to say you could introduce a pathogen that specifically targets Robespierre. Or arrange an accident. Or find a way to strengthen the Girondins before they were purged. You've been running variations for weeks." The drone's voice was flat. "The answer is no. To all of them."

"The Referee decision—"

"The Referee decision is observation only. You know this. I know you know this. What I don't understand is why you keep forgetting."

I was quiet for a long moment. Below, the Committee voted. More names added to tomorrow's list.

"Tell me about the Greater Magellanic Cloud," Just Checking said.

I flinched. It was a small movement, easily controlled, but the drone would have noticed.

"That's not relevant."

"It's entirely relevant. It's the reason you're on this assignment. It's the reason I'm on this assignment." A pause. "It's the reason I was specifically requested by the Fastidious when you came back to Sol."

"You were requested to watch me."

"I was requested to help you. Watching you is how I help." Another pause, longer. "What happened?"

I didn't answer immediately. The Committee session was breaking up below. Men who had condemned their colleagues to death were stretching, joking, discussing dinner plans. The Terror had its own rhythms, its own normalities.

"There was a civilization," I said finally. "In the Greater Magellanic Cloud. Early-stage industrial, approximately equivalent to Earth in the seventeenth century. Very promising. They had developed a philosophical tradition that emphasized—" I stopped. "It doesn't matter what they emphasized. What matters is that they were on the verge of a religious war. Two major factions, incompatible cosmologies, roughly equivalent military capacity. The models predicted eighty to ninety percent casualties over the following century."

"And you intervened."

"I arranged for certain documents to be discovered. Texts that appeared to reconcile the two cosmologies. A synthesis that both factions could accept. It was elegant. It was exactly what they needed. It was—" I stopped again.

"What happened?"

"The synthesis worked. The war didn't happen. Both factions united around the new interpretation." I watched a deputy below gather his papers, chatting with a colleague as if this were any ordinary meeting. "And then they turned outward. The energy that would have gone into killing each other went into expansion instead. They conquered seven neighboring civilizations in forty years. The death toll was higher than the religious war would have been. And the cultures they destroyed—there was a species on one of those worlds that had developed a genuinely novel approach to consciousness. We'd been watching them for three centuries. They were—" My voice caught. "They're gone now. Because I was clever."

Just Checking was quiet.
