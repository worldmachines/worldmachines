# La Selva Oscura (part 2 of 5)

If the cost of refusing the midlife transition were merely private — a few exhausted adults wandering bourgeois suburbs — the stakes might not justify the essay. The midlife crisis can be civilizational as well, and the stakes are nowhere higher than in the Remembrance of Earth’s Past trilogy — the three novels we know in English as The Three-Body Problem, The Dark Forest, and Death’s End. Cixin Liu says it from inside the novel, in the voice of his protagonist Cheng Xin:

I am but an ordinary person. Unfortunately, I have not been able to walk the ordinary person’s path. My path is, in reality, the journey of a civilization.

The trilogy is usually read as a Cold-War parable or a cosmic-scale Hobbesian thought experiment in game theory. We will read it as Dante’s Inferno scaled to a species, and because it occurs in a dark forest, we will interpret it as a midlife crisis for human civilization. Liu is explicit about it. The frame he uses for civilizations is developmental. They have stages, like a life.

Like the first line in the Commedia (la diritta via era smarrita) the straight path for humanity has been lost. First contact with the alien Trisolarans wakes us, and we find ourselves in a cosmos-wide dark forest — a forest of armed hunters in which any civilization that reveals itself is destroyed by the others. The image at the top of this piece — the child opening the door, glancing into the night, slamming it shut — is Liu’s image for humanity’s response. It is the civilizational version of the refusal of the call. Humanity’s outward capacity remains intact — starships are buildable, the technology exists — but the inner confidence to leave collapses.

Leaning on Blumenberg again, this is the clearest recent expression of the absolutism of reality8 — the raw, indifferent totality of a universe that does not explain itself to us. Humanity told itself a story, a myth, of benign ‘first contact’ and ‘cosmic brotherhood.’ But Liu presents a universe that is not designed for us, where human history is cosmically unnoticeable. The moment of horror occurs when our species realizes that our cultural mediations — our myths — were never adequate to the raw, unmediated reality they were meant to tame.

Unable to face up to its predicament, humanity clings to the Earth, clings to the solar system, clings to the comfortable cosmology in which the universe owes us a future. Building enough starships to evacuate even a fraction of the species is technically possible but is considered immoral. “Escapism” is treated as a crime against the social fabric — if not all of us can leave, none of us should.

What humanity refuses, until it is almost too late, is a developmental journey that Liu lays out plainly:

And now we know that this is the journey that must be made by every civilization: awakening inside a cramped cradle, toddling out of it, taking flight, flying faster and farther, and, finally, merging with the fate of the universe as one.9

The cradle, the toddler, the flight — Liu names the developmental stages of a civilization the way we name the developmental stages of a person. It is the same expression Dōgen makes: each stage is its own complete state; firewood does not become ash, and the cradle is not flight. To remain in the cradle past the time of the cradle is to court disaster. The grown-up civilization, like the grown-up person, has to go through its dark adventure, and the boon it brings back is a cosmos-wide consciousness.

A New Thing in the Cosmos — Love

Humanity escapes by the skin of its teeth (I won’t give more spoilers — read the books) through the few ships that decided to leave the solar system decades earlier. They let go of the attachment to the Earth, and wayfound their way to a fruitful future for human civilization — knowing that the wider universe cannot be mapped in the old terms, that they will never return, and that the question now is not whether the map is wrong but how to live without a map at all.

Cheng Xin, Liu’s hero of the final book, has catastrophically failed humanity. Twice she was placed in a position of judgment and twice she chose love over violence. She refused to push the deterrent trigger when the Trisolarans attacked, even though it would have defended Earth. She blocked Wade’s lightspeed-ship program — the only viable escape — because his methods were ruthless. Both times the reflex — mercy, refusal of force, love as default — was catastrophically wrong. (I recognize her reflex. It is also mine.) Liu is unsparing about it:

Twice, she had been placed in a position of authority second only to God, and both times she had pushed the world into the abyss in the name of love.10
