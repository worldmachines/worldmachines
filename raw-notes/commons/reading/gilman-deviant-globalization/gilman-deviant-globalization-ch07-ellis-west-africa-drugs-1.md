# West Africa's Drug Trade: Origins and Regional Pattern

*Chapter by Stephen Ellis (first published in African Affairs 108, April 2009), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).*

Ellis opens by noting that a December 2007 UNODC report drew unprecedented attention to West Africa as an intermediary in the cocaine trade from Latin America to Europe. He reports the UNODC estimate that about a quarter of Europe's annual consumption of 135 to 145 tons of cocaine transits the region. His own argument is that this is not new: the collaboration between Latin American and West African traders goes back more than a decade, and the region's role in drugs goes back over half a century. He then gives a historical account of the origins (Lebanese heroin transit in 1952, Nigerian and Ghanaian marijuana exports from the 1960s, the courier trade in cocaine and heroin from the early 1980s) and a regional survey of how traffickers, politicians, soldiers and outside syndicates exploited the weak states of the region up to the early 2000s.

## Themes

**Longue duree, not sudden shift.** Ellis corrects the news-cycle framing. He traces a continuous trajectory from a 1952 Lebanese syndicate moving heroin from Beirut to New York via Kano and Accra, through 1960s cannabis exports to a British mass market, to a 1980s courier trade and mid-1990s bulk shipments. He floats, as a question rather than a finding, whether a discreet Lebanon to West Africa pipeline has run for fifty years.

**Why West Africa: geography plus political environment.** Ellis argues that the decisive factor is a permissive political and social setting: smuggling is widely tolerated, law enforcement is fitful, politicians are bribed or involved. He cites Bayart's point that smuggling expertise, weak enforcement and official tolerance form a kind of social and political capital that accumulates over time. Latin American traders, he says, are shifting to Europe because of a saturated North American market, Mexican gangs and Caribbean interdiction, and some relocate physically, like a multinational.

**Crisis, structural adjustment and the push into the trade.** Ellis ties the 1980s expansion to economic collapse, IMF and World Bank borrowing, and structural-adjustment cuts to public spending, which he says was undoubtedly an incentive for some. He observes that emigrant traffickers were relatively educated urban people who spoke English and knew state bureaucracies, not the poorest. He cites Nuhu Ribadu dating both 419 fraud and drug problems to the Second Republic. He hedges: Nigeria's marijuana role predated the early-1980s financial crisis.

**State complicity and kleptocracy.** The narrative links the trade to the state throughout: military rulers (Buhari's death penalty decree, then Babangida's reversal), Babangida-era corruption described as a generalized instrument of government, BCCI's Nigerian branches, a former senator convicted in New York, NDLEA heads who allegedly cared more about trafficking than the traffickers, and military officers shielded from search. Elsewhere he reports high-level connections in Sierra Leone, Ghana, Burkina Faso, Liberia and Togo (the latter attributed to police sources). Ellis separates what is documented from what is widely believed, for example the reported link between the murder of editor Dele Giwa and his investigation of elite drug trading.

**Moral economy of the trade.** Ellis reports that many Nigerians saw cocaine and heroin as rich-world luxuries, attached little stigma to couriers, and regarded the trade as legitimate commerce between willing sellers and buyers, with dealers quoted as viewing the black market as the only way to redistribute wealth because mainstream channels are occupied. This is reported attitude, not Ellis's own endorsement.

**Becoming global.** Nigerian traders adapted techniques and routes (body-swallowing of drug packets, switching between Thai, Malaysian, Taiwanese and Hong Kong routes), established bases in South Africa, Cotonou and Abidjan, took over heroin retailing in Moscow, and by the mid-1990s had partners in producing and consuming countries. Colombian cartels sought West African partners from the late 1980s. Ellis reads the arrest statistics cautiously, noting for instance that a US claim of 80 percent of Southeast Asian white heroin matters less because that market was losing ground to Latin American supply.

## Notable passages

**The permissive environment (c1).** Ellis's core explanatory claim: geography matters, but the political and social environment matters most, and he draws on Bayart to frame smuggling expertise and state weakness as accumulating capital. This fits the editors' broader thesis that deviant networks ride on weak or captured states; it also rhymes with the idea in [[illegibility-as-elite-defense]] that illegibility serves those in power, though Ellis is not theorizing legibility.

**Relocation like a multinational (c2).** The strategic shift of Latin American traders to West Africa, framed through market saturation and interdiction, shows regulatory arbitrage at work: the trade responds to enforcement displacement much as legal business does. This is journalistic-analytic description of supply-side adaptation, not a theory of the state.

**The legitimating discourse (c3).** The reported Nigerian view that the trade is a legitimate commerce and a redistribution of wealth from north to south links to the volume's wider claim that deviant globalization is for many in the Global South a route to development. Ellis reports it as attitude and does not adopt it.

**Structural adjustment and the push (c4).** A causal claim linking IMF and World Bank-driven liberalization and public cuts to incentives for illicit income, with Ellis's own hedge that Nigerian marijuana exports predated the crisis. It resonates loosely with the volume's account of the collapse of state-led development, though Ellis does not make that argument in these terms.

**Charles Taylor on the drug trade (c5).** A cellmate's recollection, reported second-hand and decades later, of Taylor arguing that African governments should welcome export of hard drugs and the capital from laundering. It is testimony about a political actor, not Ellis's finding, and should be read with that hedge.

**Fully global by the mid-1990s (c6).** Ellis's summary: Nigerian and Ghanaian traders had the means to invest in bulk shipments and held associates on both ends, while Lebanese, Soviet and South American groups used the region as an entrepot. This sits close to [[farrell-underground-empire]] only in the sense that both read dense networks through chokepoints; Ellis writes as an area historian, not a network theorist.

Overall caution: much of the evidence is official statements, press reports and arrest counts, which Ellis sometimes hedges himself (for example a figure given only as presumably referring to marijuana).
