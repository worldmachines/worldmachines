# Criminal Insurgency: Cartel Phases and Third-Generation Gangs

*Chapter by John P. Sullivan (first published in Small Wars Journal, 31 May 2009), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).*

Sullivan, a policing and intelligence analyst, offers a short framework for understanding how gangs and cartels become war-making actors. He argues that in 'criminal enclaves' or 'lawless zones' where civil governance and social bonds have eroded, some gangs and cartels evolve into 'criminal insurgents' who hollow out the state in order to gain autonomous economic control of territory. He supports this with two typologies, both co-developed with Robert Bunker and published in the late 1990s: three phases of cartel evolution (Medellín, Cali, and a hypothetical 'criminal state successor' modelled on the Ciudad del Este triple-border enclave) and three generations of street gang (turf, market, mercenary/political). He then applies them to Mexico's battles over the 'plazas', and closes with prescriptions: 'analytical red teaming', full-spectrum policing and cross-border co-production of intelligence. The framework is Sullivan's own and earlier work; the chapter is a synthesis, not new fieldwork, and it is largely assertive and predictive rather than empirical.

## Themes

**Criminal insurgency is defined by a narrow political motive.** Sullivan distinguishes criminal insurgents from 'regular' terrorists and insurgents: their 'sole political motive is to gain autonomous economic control over territory', pursued by hollowing out the state and creating enclaves. In his conclusion he adds that gangs and cartels 'seek profit and are not driven by ideology', but that the ungoverned zones they leave behind attract extremists and terrorists. He quotes van Creveld's prediction that future war will be waged by 'terrorists, guerrillas, bandits and robbers', and quotes his own earlier piece with Adam Elkus on criminal insurgency challenging the state's monopoly on force.

**Transnational crime as systemic erosion of the state.** Citing Canadian intelligence analyst Samuel Porteous, Sullivan says transnational crime undermines civil society and sovereignty by normalizing violence and legitimizing corruption, distorting markets and degrading the environment. Groups extend reach by co-opting people through bribery, coercion and intimidation. At neighbourhood level this yields a 'failed community', the 'virtual analog of a failed state'. This sits close to the volume's wider claim that deviant networks are bound up with mainstream globalization, but Sullivan frames the matter as a security threat rather than as a development route.

**Cartel evolution in three phases.** The first phase (Medellín) is an 'aggressive competitor' to the Westphalian state, relying on extreme violence. The second (Cali) is a 'subtle co-opter': distributed, network-like, leaderless, using corruption rather than violence and harder to decapitate; Sullivan says it spread to Mexico via the 'Mexican Federation'. The third, a 'criminal state successor', has 'yet to emerge' but he sees warning signs, notably in Mexico; third-phase cartels would rule enclaves like warlords. Table 16.1 contrasts the three along dimensions such as violence (indiscriminate, symbolic, discriminate), corruption and technology.

**Criminal enclaves.** Ciudad del Este, at the Paraguay-Brazil-Argentina border, is Sullivan's fullest example: a 'free enclave' hosting many national criminal groups, terrorism support, piracy and gunrunning to Brazilian favelas. He names it the 'Ciudad del Este model' of the third phase cartel. His description is sweeping and relies on assertion about terrorist-crime overlap rather than evidence given in the chapter.

**Three generations of gangs.** Turf gangs (first generation) are local and opportunistic; market gangs (second) are drug-centred criminal businesses that sometimes use violence as political interference; third-generation gangs have political and mercenary aims, global reach and infiltration of police. Evolution is driven by politicization, internationalization and sophistication. Sullivan stresses that the overwhelming majority of gangs stay in the first or second generation, and that to date third-generation gangs have been 'primarily mercenary'. Maras (MS-13, M-18, both of Los Angeles origin) are the main transnational example. Citing Bruneau, he lists five ways they challenge states: straining police capacity, undermining legitimacy, acting as alternate governments (taxing in parts of Salvadoran and Guatemalan capitals), dominating informal economies, and infiltrating police and NGOs.

**Mexico as the test case, and the intelligence response.** The plazas (transshipment corridors into the US) are fought over by cartels, state forces and allied gangs, with corrupt officials fuelling violence. Sullivan says the cartels have abandoned subtle co-option for open violence to secure safe havens, and are expanding north, south and to Africa. His remedy is analytical red teaming (scenarios and wargames to anticipate which gangs or cartels will expand where), full-spectrum policing with stability-police units such as Israel's Joint Operations Forces, and integration of intelligence and operations across borders.

## Resonances with World Machines (tentative)

Sullivan's picture of the state as an entity with a 'legitimate monopoly on the use of violence within a specified territory' that is being challenged from below has a loose kinship with the [[modernity-machine]] in decline, and the proliferation of non-state armed actors could be read as the [[divergence-machine]] at work. It also bears on a [[gramsci-gap]]-style interregnum, though Sullivan is a security practitioner, not a historian, and offers no such periodization. The Cali model's stealth and leaderless networks, and the enclaves' reliance on opacity, can be read against [[legibility-cuts-both-ways]], again as a rhyme rather than anything the author claims. Unlike the volume's editors, who see deviant globalization as possibly developmental, Sullivan treats these actors as threats to be countered.

## Notable passages

**The core definition (c1).** Sullivan separates criminal insurgency from terrorism and ordinary insurgency by its single political motive, territorial economic autonomy, achieved by hollowing out the state.

**Third phase cartel (c2).** The 'criminal state successor' is described as the product of unremitting corruption and co-option, ruling enclaves like warlords; Sullivan says Mexico's criminal insurgency could be its genesis.

**The Ciudad del Este model (c3).** Networked enclaves might gain local dominance and 'legitimacy or at least political influence within the network of state actors'; Mexico's plaza battles may be an early form.

**Mara threats to the state (c4).** The Bruneau list, including the claim that maras tax people and businesses where governments have 'all but given up'.

**Mexico's shift from co-option to violence (c5).** Sullivan's conclusion that the cartels now use violence for safe havens, expand globally, and thrive amid governmental crisis.

**Gangs, extremists and the ungoverned zone (c6).** Gangs are partners and foot soldiers of cartels, profit-driven rather than ideological, but their wake of lawless zones may be exploited by extremists.
