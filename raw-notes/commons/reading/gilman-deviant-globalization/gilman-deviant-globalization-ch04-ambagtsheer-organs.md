# The Black Market in Human Organs: Scarcity, Asymmetry and Transplant Tourism

*Chapter by Frederike Ambagtsheer (previously unpublished), in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).*

Ambagtsheer opens by conceding that little is known about the organ trade: few empirical studies exist, and she notes (citing Travaini et al.) that the media, which sometimes reports rumor as fact, is the most consistent source. She therefore builds a synthesis from medical journals, fieldwork by anthropologists and sociologists (who tend to study only victims), and WHO and EU reports. She traces the rise of demand to cyclosporine, which turned transplantation from a risky last resort into a routine procedure, while supply stayed far below need (she cites US waitlists of over 80,000 for kidneys, and European waitlists of 65,000 against about 25,000 transplants a year). She then defines the black market, lists its legal and illicit participants, explains its causes through Nikos Passas's three criminogenic asymmetries, and surveys national cases (China, Iran, India, Israel, European incidents) and transplant-tourism routes.

## Themes

**Scarcity creates the market.** The author's core causal claim is that a global shortage of legal organs, produced by medical success plus finite supply, pushes desperate patients (waits of three to five years, about 35% waitlist mortality in Europe) toward illegal purchase. Black markets, she says, thrive where formal markets "cannot or do not supply the good."

**Moral rather than intrinsic illegality.** The organs are not inherently illegal; the profitable exchange is. She cites the WHO Guiding Principles, which prohibit payment because it exploits the poor and implies that some people are "mere objects." Bans are thus moral and ethical in basis, which fits the volume's moral-arbitrage frame, though Ambagtsheer does not use that term.

**Deviance depends on legitimate infrastructure.** Unlike narcotics, she argues, organ transplantation needs surgeons, nephrologists, sterile facilities, rapid transport, and even ambulance drivers and morticians. Illicit brokers and traffickers are added on top of this legal professional base. She says the degree of organization is poorly known and mostly speculative, so claims of drug-network origins or international crime syndicates should be read with her hedge.

**Passas's criminogenic asymmetries.** Demand asymmetry (governments cannot supply enough organs), motive asymmetry (profit, global income disparities, poor sellers in debt, wealthy buyers who may not care about origin) and enforcement asymmetry (states unable or unwilling to control transnational activity). Her case example is a 2003 syndicate: Brazilian sellers at about $10,000, Israeli buyers paying up to $100,000, all operations in one South African hospital, with fraudulent documents claiming kinship and a up-to-ten-fold markup captured by intermediaries. The ten-fold gap between donor and recipient payment is the textbook shape of arbitrage between price regimes, though she frames it in Passas's terms.

**Enforcement and regime variation.** Whether the trade is open or clandestine depends on social and political structure: open where there are large pools of sellers, lax medical regulation and compromised policing; underground where regulation and enforcement are strong (the US). States respond differently: Iran regulates a legal market through CASKP with a two-contract system; India's 1994 Act has an "affection" loophole and weak enforcement, so the trade went more clandestine; the Philippines also permits regulated trade; Israel let insurers sell 'transplant packages' until a recent law banned them; China's 2007 law is, per critics, vague enough to leave room for harvesting from executed prisoners.

**Direction of flows and victims.** Kidneys flow South to North, East to West, poor to rich, female to male. Ambagtsheer reports that victims are mainly sellers, some coerced or deceived about payment (she quotes Pearson, not her own finding), and that recipients also face risks, though the evidence on transplant-tourism outcomes is mixed and she declines to draw a firm conclusion. Transplant tourism may span three countries (donor origin, recipient origin, surgery site), and demand is not confined to developed countries. The cross-border character is what makes the trade hard to tackle.

**Link to the editors' frame.** The chapter was not written with deviant globalization in mind, but it supplies a vivid case of the dual-use point (same medical infrastructure, same price logic) and of state hollowing through enforcement gaps. It resonates loosely with how [[farrell-underground-empire]] reads infrastructure networks from the state side, though Ambagtsheer's lens is criminological and medical, not about chokepoints.

## Notable passages

**Legal and illegal actors (c1).** Ambagtsheer argues that the organ trade cannot be set up "without the participation of surgeons, nephrologists and other specialized physicians, ambulance drivers, morticians and undertakers." This is the chapter's clearest statement of how the illicit rides on legitimate institutions.

**Open versus underground (c2).** She argues that whether the trade is overt or clandestine depends on state capacity and corruption, and that violence is more likely where the trade is covert and enforcement lax. She notes that most descriptions of its organization are speculative.

**The asymmetries and the 2003 case (c3).** The core analytic passage: Passas's definition, the first two asymmetries, and the Brazil-Israel-South Africa example with its price figures.

**State response (c4).** The third asymmetry, with the Israeli insurer 'transplant packages' ($120,000 to $200,000, covering even bribes to airport and customs officials) as an example of a state tolerating the trade because of low domestic donation rates; the Philippines and Iran prefer regulation to prohibition.

**Transplant tourism flows (c5).** The description of cross-border direction (South to North, poor to rich, female to male) and the three-country structure of routes. Note that the author herself says demand is also found in non-Western countries.

Caveats: the chapter relies on secondary and sometimes media-sourced reports, uses dated figures (waitlists, prices), and several of its claims about China (organs from Falun Gong practitioners) are reported as what 'others report', not as her own finding.
