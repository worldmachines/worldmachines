# Fujian, the Chinese Diaspora and the Mechanics of Snakehead Smuggling

*Chapter by Patrick Radden Keefe (first published in World Policy Journal, 2009), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).*

Keefe opens in Changle, a Fujian boomtown whose prosperity, he reports, owes as much to hundreds of thousands of smuggled emigrants and their remittances as to China's general growth. He frames the case as a possible bellwether: how Chinese laborers respond to the 2008-09 financial crisis may indicate future trends in irregular migration. He then sketches Fujian's long maritime out-migration tradition, the 1950s-60s 'ship jumpers', the emergence of snakeheads in the 1970s, and the surge of Fujianese arrivals in New York from the 1980s. He argues that out-migration is driven by relative deprivation rather than absolute poverty, and closes the unit by correcting two misconceptions: that smuggling is the same as trafficking, and that it is run by centralized mafia-like rackets.

## Themes

**Smuggling as a community-sanctioned service.** Keefe notes that migrants and their communities often see snakeheads not as criminals but as 'resourceful and enterprising tour-guides', and that remittances fund schools, community centers, weddings, businesses and mansions. The black market here is woven into local development; the monument in Changle honors those who left by boat and plane. This fits the volume's broader claim that illicit flows can be a route to development, though Keefe himself does not make that argument in this unit.

**Development produces emigration (relative deprivation).** Keefe's central explanatory claim is a paradox: Fujian's migrants left during double-digit growth and Special Economic Zone reforms. He offers two explanations (dislocation of farmers and fishermen in the new market economy; relative deprivation as neighbors prospered unevenly) and says it is 'seldom the poorest sectors' that produce migrants. The process is self-reinforcing as conspicuous remittance-funded mansions raise the social cost of having no relative abroad. He cites demographers and quotes a Sing Tao Daily journalist's description of a 'frenzy'.

**Illegibility as a research problem.** Keefe stresses that black markets confound journalists and academics because they are illegal, paperless and secretive, and that official estimates are crude extrapolations from apprehensions. Figures are hedged: conservative estimates of tens of thousands a year, a CIA estimate of about 100,000 by the early 1990s, and scholar Peter Kwong's view that 1988-1993 saw the largest illegal Chinese entry in history. This resonates loosely with the WMP theme of illegibility against state legibility ([[Legibility]], [[illegibility-as-elite-defense]]), though here the illegibility is a byproduct of clandestinity affecting the observer, not an elite strategy.

**Smuggling is not trafficking, and not a mafia.** Keefe distinguishes consensual, contract-based smuggling from trafficking (coercion or false pretense), and says smuggling customers ending up in forced sex work is 'exceedingly rare'. He also argues Chinese smuggling is mostly done by diffuse, ad hoc networks of individual entrepreneurs with no central hierarchy, though Triads and Chinatown gangs have occasionally dabbled. The network model (decentralized, atomized, each broker accumulating contacts) is the mechanism he foregrounds.

**Migration as a global-economy bellwether.** He speculates, with hedges, that recession could either deter or sharply increase irregular migration, and that Fujian's manufacturing coast is the place to watch. This is forecasting-by-example by a journalist, dated to early 2009.

## Notable passages

**Changle and remittances (c1).** The diaspora-funded local economy and the sail-and-wings monument, which Keefe's host, the entrepreneur Lin Li, explains honors those who left by boat and plane.

**Snakeheads defined (c2).** Demand for illicit passage creates well-connected brokers; one snakehead at a New York trial gave the 'like a snake' etymology; migrants view the service as a social good.

**Counting the uncountable (c3).** The estimates problem and the 1988-1993 peak.

**The paradox of development (c4).** The self-feeding cycle of remittance, mansions and a 'fever to migrate'.

**Smuggling versus trafficking (c5).** The consensual contract distinction and why commentators conflate the two.

**Atomized networks (c6).** The rejection of the Sicilian mafia model.

For World Machines, the unit shows informal intermediaries riding mainstream economic growth and the border gradient (a loose analogue of the shadow side of global infrastructure), but Keefe is a journalist and offers no machine-level theory, so no further links are made.
