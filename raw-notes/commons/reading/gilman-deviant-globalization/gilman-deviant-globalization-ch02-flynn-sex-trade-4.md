# Moldova's Antitrafficking Unit: Arrests Without Convictions, and the Return of the Trafficked

*Chapter by Sean Flynn (first published in GQ, Aug. & Sept. 2005, as "The Sex Trade", Parts 1–2), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).*

This unit continues Flynn's Moldova reporting. It opens with Bejan, a Ministry of Interior investigator of economic crimes who becomes the head of a small countertrafficking squad, and with the mass post-independence emigration during which, Flynn says, no one noticed trafficked women until they began returning, some in coffins. Flynn then sets the official statistics (839 arrests, 179 convictions since 2002) against the reality of the cases: a 32-year-old recruiter, Lilea Prajac, and a 70-year-old alleged ringleader. He argues that the unit is earnest but ineffectual: understaffed, poorly equipped, blocked by corrupt officials and a corrupt judiciary, so that trafficking charges are routinely reduced to pimping. He then widens to the demographic and economic damage to Moldova, prevention programs run by NGOs, and the eagerness to leave that makes traffickers' promises credible. The last third follows two sisters, Zina and Veronica, through escape, repatriation, retaliation by their traffickers and stigma at home.

## Themes

**The enforcement gap is a state-capacity and corruption problem.** Flynn writes that Bejan's men "can only go after the low-hanging fruit" (the line is a Western trafficking expert's, not Flynn's). The unit has twenty-seven officers against the drug unit's hundred and was equipped with vehicles only after American donations. The expert says that going after the big fruit means "Best case, you're fired. Worst case, you're dead." Flynn cites the US State Department that investigations were limited "due in some instances to pressure from complicit officials", and a diplomat who calls the judiciary "one of the most corrupt entities in the country." Trafficking was not even a crime in Moldova until 2002.

**Official statistics versus actual cases.** Flynn notes that 179 convictions equals exactly the number of "trafficking networks liquidated," and that of 95 convictions in the prior year only 16 were for trafficking adults and 7 for children, with 75 percent for pimping. He questions whether recruiters like Lilea, who previously supported a crippled son by cross-border petty trade, or an elderly woman "recruiting people . . . whose wish was to leave the country", are the "international gangsters" the headlines claim. He concedes that trafficking requires some organized criminal network, but says enforcement reaches only the lackeys. This fits the volume's wider claim that criminality and the yuck factor are imperfect lenses, though Flynn himself condemns the trade throughout and does not argue that it is benign.

**Emigration, collapse and a self-feeding cycle.** Flynn reports an estimated 600,000 to 1 million people leaving since independence, abandoned children (Bejan guesses 25,000; diplomats say over 125,000 orphaned or left behind), unemployment that aid workers put far above official rates, and remittances of almost half a billion dollars a year that are barely invested. He calls it a "disaster feeding upon itself and, in turn, feeding the sex trade", with a quoted estimate that 80 percent of returning trafficked women cannot have children. This is Flynn's reporting with attributed estimates, not firm measurement; he says no one knows how many are trafficked.

**Prevention that traffickers imitate; legibility problems of an international crime.** Hotlines and films run by NGOs and the IOM are funded by charity and foreign aid, and La Strada staff think traffickers have adapted ad language ("Local contracts!"). Flynn argues the trade resists quantification because it is international: laws and mores differ, and slavery "is always masked as either a petty local nuisance or a perfectly legal business." He cites studies showing a correlation between legalization and trafficking (unnamed) and asks how willing and unwilling can be sorted. This rhymes with the WMP interest in [[Legibility]] and illegibility, though Flynn is about victims' and officials' inability to see, not about elite strategies.

**Return is not rescue.** Returned women face stigma, the same villages as their traffickers, retaliation, and the pull to leave again. Social workers say some see leaving as their "last, best hope", and Ana Revenco says some do not know what to do with freedom. The Zina and Veronica story ends with a priest leaving Veronica after she tells him the truth.

## Notable passages

**Statistics versus cases.** The 839 arrests and 179 convictions, with the exact equality to the network count, are Flynn's sharpest critique of official reporting.

**The ineffectual unit.** The paragraphs on twenty-seven officers, State Department language on complicit officials, and the Soviet-era jalopy give the institutional diagnosis.

**Convictions collapse into pimping.** The sentencing figures show how harsh law on paper becomes little time served; Flynn follows this with a victim's line, as she told the judge, that she was made a whore.

**Demographic and economic spiral.** Flynn ties trafficking to unemployment, flight of working-age people and unproductive remittances, a state-failure framing of the kind the volume treats as the backdrop of deviant globalization and a post-Soviet collapse of state-led development.

**Sorting willing from unwilling.** Flynn's passage on international masking and the German World Cup huts is a compact statement of why the phenomenon is hard to measure.

Caveats: this is magazine reportage from 2005; figures are attributed to officials, diplomats and aid workers, and some are explicitly guesses.
