# Niger Delta: A History of Violence and the Oil Combustion Chamber

*Chapter by Sebastian Junger (first published in Vanity Fair, Feb. 2007), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).*

This second half of Junger's reportage on the Niger delta does two things. First it gives a short history of delta violence: the Ogoni campaign and Ken Saro-Wiwa's 1995 hanging, the Ijaw 'Operation Climate Change' of 1998, Asari's 2004 'all-out war', and the shadow economy of oil theft ('bunkering'), smuggled arms and military payoffs that grew up around them. It then describes how that arrangement broke down in January 2006 when MEND arrived, and, under the heading 'Combustion Chamber', explains why a small armed group in a swamp can matter to the world economy, ending with Junger's trip into a MEND camp and a militant leader's threats. Junger is a reporter, so his claims rest on interviews (a bunkerer, an arms expert, a former hostage, a militant commander) and on figures he cites rather than on a systematic theory.

## Themes

**Violence as the state's answer to protest, then protest as armed enterprise.** Junger reports that the Ogoni movement (MOSOP) petitioned Shell and the government, then shut down oil production in Ogoniland with mass protests, and that the military responded with force. He quotes a leaked and disputed military memo urging 'wasting operations' that would make 'constant military presence justifiable'. Later Ijaw groups copied the strategy and an armed militancy developed that the government could not contain. Fighting among factions, many hired by politicians, followed.

**Bunkering as a parallel, functional economy.** Junger describes theft of crude from pipelines as a technically dangerous, widespread business (he cites estimates that about 10 percent of Nigeria's exports, several billion dollars a year, is bunkered). It depends on renting soldiers who earn about $100 a month, on guns bought with oil money, and on arms arriving through informal channels (a contact's account of weapons for bunkered oil, rumors of floating weapons bazaars; these are sources' claims, not verified). A bunkerer tells him he would rather be an oil-company supervisor, and that petroleum engineers without jobs are hired by bunkerers. This is the chapter's clearest picture of a deviant economy running on the same infrastructure, markets and personnel as the legal one.

**A 'brutal functionality'.** Junger argues that the convergence of bunkered oil, smuggled weapons and illegal payoffs 'has worked fairly well' in Nigeria's wider dysfunction: everyone from officials to militants earns more than in a transparent economy, and bunkered oil acts as an extra tax on the companies. Illegality also lets the government dismiss the militants' environmental grievances as criminality, and sloppy bunkering contributes to the pollution villagers then claim for. This resonates with the volume's thesis that criminality is an inadequate lens, though Junger does not use that vocabulary.

**MEND breaks the equilibrium.** Unlike a bunkering cartel, MEND renewed Saro-Wiwa's grievances and disrupted oil flow; its stated aim, per Junger, is control of the delta's oil rather than ransom. A former Shell hostage (an informant, not Junger) says the grievances are legitimate while doubting that kidnappers act for the community, and describes a dark village outside the lit Shell compound.

**Oil's scarcity gives chokepoint leverage.** In 'Combustion Chamber' Junger cites figures: a tank of gasoline equals more than 60,000 man-hours of work, there is virtually no surplus production, the world uses 84 million barrels a day, extraction is getting costlier, and some experts predict depletion by 2040. Because oil sits disproportionately in unstable places, and unstable-country oil is worth less per barrel, MEND's threats (e.g., to hit the Bonny Island LNG facility) are its 'trump card'. Junger sets out the stalemate (MEND may not dare a total shut-in; the military may not dare a sweep) and records disagreement among analysts about how much a shut-in would hurt. A DOE analyst expects short-term effects to fade; the Oil ShockWave panel thought a shut-in combined with another event could trigger a major recession and found no good options.

This fits the WMP interest in the shadow side of global infrastructure and chokepoints (compare [[farrell-underground-empire]] and [[weaponized-interdependence]], which read similar networks from the state's side), though Junger's argument is about physical energy supply, not information or finance networks. The state's hollowed-out reach and dependence on military rent-sharing also echo the weakening-state theme, but the text gives no machine-level theory.

## Notable passages

**How bunkering works (c1).** Junger details the technique and the renting of the Nigerian military, which turns a smuggling operation into a joint venture with the state's own armed forces.

**The functional equilibrium (c2).** The most analytical passage: Junger argues the illegal system works for nearly everyone involved and that it neutralizes the original environmental campaign by recasting it as crime.

**Why oil is valuable and scarce (c3).** The numerical groundwork for the chapter's argument about leverage; the 2040 depletion forecast is attributed to unnamed 'some experts' and carries Junger's hedge.

**MEND's trump card (c4).** The mutual-deterrence logic between MEND and the military, and the risk to the national economy and the companies.

**The fence at the Shell compound (c5).** A former hostage's account of the contrast between a lit compound and a dark village, including an unnamed Shell person's remark that supplying power would make others 'all want that'.

The closing camp scene, with Brutus's statements that 'real violence' is coming, is Junger's report of a militant's boast; he notes that two explosions at Shell and Agip facilities were reported on December 18 and claimed by MEND.
