# Introduction (part 1): deviant globalization and what globalization is

*Introduction written by Nils Gilman, Jesse Goldhammer and Steven Weber for this volume, in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).*

The editors introduce deviant globalization as the cross-border economic networks that produce, move and consume illegal, informal or morally repugnant goods and services, from narcotics and wildlife to dirty money, toxic waste and trafficked people. They set three goals (describe the concept, identify shared structures across disparate activities, interpret what it means for the global political economy) and open with three anecdotes: Albanian sex trafficking after 1990, kidney brokers in tsunami refugee camps, and Taliban revenue diversification. They then lay out their three claims plus a fourth, normative one, preview three dimensions of impact (policy, welfare, morality), and begin a section defining globalization itself, contrasting its enthusiasts and critics, both of whom they say ignore the deviant entrepreneur. The unit ends mid-introduction, before the editors' two conventional and two alternative lenses.

## Themes

**Deviant and mainstream globalization are one system.** The editors' first and core argument is that both are market-driven, run on the same financial, communication and transportation systems, and both break down boundaries in a process of creative destruction. Their phrase is that the global infrastructure is "dual use and value neutral": Craigslist, intermodal shipping and Pakistani cell networks serve licit and illicit users alike, so as the systems become more efficient they grow the formal economy and "its conjoined, deviant twin." This is a claim about the shadow side of shared infrastructure (cf. [[farrell-underground-empire]], which reads the same networks from the state's side, though the editors' interest is markets and development rather than chokepoint power).

**Moral arbitrage: prohibition creates the market.** The editors argue that deviant globalization satisfies demand for what is illegal or unavailable in the licit economy, and that "we" create the opportunity by codifying moral outrage (banning prostitution, methamphetamine, trade in endangered species). It is "the ultimate arbitrage activity, growing at the intersection of ethical difference and regulatory inefficiency," played on a "morally lumpy field" beneath neoliberal rules. They ask readers to suspend moral outrage as a precondition for analysis, since moral repulsion clouds understanding and enables the very profit at the margin.

**Unequal exposure for rich and poor.** For the global rich, deviant globalization meets unfulfilled demand (organs, drugs) and is not a way to get richer; for the global poor it is both an engine of wealth creation ("an individual's fastest ticket out of poverty") and a mark of exclusion, while also funding corrupt officials, warlords and terrorists. Mexico is the illustration: drug money employs a workforce the editors say, citing unnamed estimates, exceeds half a million, larger than the Mexican oil and gas industry, and develops villages, while also financing violence that is crippling the state.

**Not a sideshow; a shift in power since the Cold War.** The second claim is that deviant globalization is no sideshow, and the third and bolder claim, conditional on the first two holding up, is that it is changing the distribution of power in the world economy about as profoundly as any visible trend since 1989, alongside the 2008-09 financial crisis, global economic governance and climate negotiations. The editors hedge it as a claim that must survive scrutiny, and offer no numbers in this unit beyond a UN figure of more than 12 million in sexual servitude or forced labor and the unsourced Mexico estimate.

**Permanent, not a removable cancer.** The central normative claim is that deviant globalization is "a massive, permanent phenomenon" central to the lives of hundreds of millions, which on balance has improved their material lot more than mainstream development and philanthropic projects. The editors insist this need not mean policy despair, and preview three dimensions: governing (formal regulators underestimate deviant entrepreneurs), welfare (it supplies welfare to a large share of humanity) and ethics (inherited judgments reflect winners' power structures).

**What globalization is, and its history.** The editors define globalization as "the cross-border integration of value-added economic activity" through increasing mobility of goods, services, money, people and ideas. They stress it is old (capital flows and migration in the late nineteenth century rivaled or exceeded later levels), and that two accelerations drove the recent phase: the end of the Cold War, with shock-therapy privatization putting state assets into mobile private hands, and information technology (Moore's Law, the collapse of telephone and shipping costs). Politics and institutions move slower than technology, and the earlier globalization ended in a world war and depression, so integration is "anything but inevitable" (resonating with, though not itself a critique of, [[globalisation-as-inevitability-rhetoric]]; compare also [[archaic-globalization-bayly]] on older globalizations).

**Enthusiasts and critics both miss the deviant entrepreneur.** The editors sketch romantic utopian globalization (Friedman's flat world, "The End of History", and similar) and its opponents (Marx, Polanyi, Klein). Both camps agree globalization empowers nonstate actors, mainly corporations, and both overlook a third category, the deviant entrepreneur (they name Gregorio Sauceda Gamboa, Viktor Bout and "Flyman" without explanation here), who acts on the same stage and remains "a mostly invisible part in this ongoing drama."

## Notable passages

**The core claim (c1).** The editors' statement that deviant and mainstream globalization share markets, infrastructure and boundary-breaking, with the dual-use, value-neutral formulation and the Craigslist, Burmese hardwood and Pakistani cell-network examples.

**Moral arbitrage (c2).** The argument that prohibitions generate the market and that deviant globalization is arbitrage on ethical difference and regulatory inefficiency.

**Rich, poor and Mexico (c3).** The asymmetric relationship of rich and poor to the deviant economy, and the double-edged Mexican case in which drug money builds villages and cripples the state.

**Permanence and development (c4).** The central normative claim that deviant globalization cannot be excised and has helped hundreds of millions more than mainstream development projects (the comparison is asserted here, not demonstrated).

**Politics lags technology (c5).** The argument that rapid integration reverses when politics and prejudice intervene, which sets up the editors' later argument about states.

**The third nonstate actor (c6).** The claim that enthusiasts and critics both ignore deviant entrepreneurs, who are bootstrapping to prosperity on the same global market.
