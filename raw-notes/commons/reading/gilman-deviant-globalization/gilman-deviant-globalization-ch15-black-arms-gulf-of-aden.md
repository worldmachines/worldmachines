# Arms trafficking around Somalia and the Gulf of Aden

*Chapter by Andrew Black (first published in Jamestown Terrorism Monitor, 2009), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).*

Black, writing in mid-2009, describes the arms market centred on Somalia as a regional network linking governments and private traders in East Africa and the Arabian Peninsula. He traces its origins to Cold War proxy supply and the arsenals released by the fall of the Dergue and Siad Barre regimes, then to private European dealers in the 1980s and 1990s who traded weapons for access to fishing grounds and toxic-waste dumping. He then maps the contemporary market: Ethiopian and Eritrean support for rival Somali proxies, Yemeni, Saudi and Emirati sources, dhow-borne shipments using ordinary commodity trade routes, domestic hubs in Somaliland and Mogadishu, and leakage from the Transitional Federal Government (TFG), Ethiopian forces and African Union peacekeepers. He judges containment efforts uncoordinated and marginal, and expects the market to survive.

## Themes

**Cold War and state collapse as the origin of the market.** Black argues that the Gulf of Aden arms markets began as a byproduct of great-power proxy competition, and that the end of the Cold War turned official stockpiles into commercial stock. The market is thus a legacy infrastructure, not a novel invention. His claim that this history supplied both material and experienced traders is the chapter's central causal story.

**Private traders and moral/regulatory arbitrage.** European firms are reported (by Black, relying on investigations he cites rather than establishing them himself) to have supplied clansmen and warlords with arms in exchange for permission for illegal fishing or toxic waste disposal, and to have routed arms via third countries to evade U.N. embargoes. Arms here are bundled with other deviant goods and services in a barter logic. The Ilaria Alpi murder is cited as the moment these vendors came to public attention; Black notes the journalists were investigating rumours, and does not resolve who was responsible.

**Governments as both suppliers and opponents.** The market is "sustained and developed primarily by a mix of opportunistic businessmen and foreign governments". Ethiopia and Eritrea back opposing sides; Black repeatedly hedges on attribution (support is "difficult to verify"; the 2006 cargo flights are "widely suspected" to be Eritrean, with a possible Bout link only "rumored"). The line between state and criminal supplier is blurred, and the U.N. is the source of most of his claims.

**Dual-use routes and licit cover.** Arms ride on the same dhows, ports and aid or peacekeeping aircraft as fuel, cement, plastic-ware and food, with traffickers sharing freight costs and avoiding customs duties. Black stresses that routes are fluid and respond rapidly to countermeasures. This fits the volume's thesis that deviant trade rides on mainstream infrastructure, though Black himself does not frame it in those terms.

**Leakage from the counter-insurgent side.** The U.N. estimate Black cites is that up to eighty percent of domestic-market weapons come from the TFG and Ethiopian forces; peacekeepers' translators resell seized caches through middlemen, even to al-Shabaab. A militant spokesman's offer to buy deserters' arms is cited as corroboration. The state apparatus fighting the insurgency is thereby one of its armourers.

**Adaptive, durable markets versus weak containment.** The 1992 embargo, the 2004 Nairobi Protocol and Yemeni coast-guard efforts are judged "uncoordinated, inconsistent, and ineffectual" at stemming flows, with a possible Arms Trade Treaty and anti-piracy naval patrols offered as tentative hopes. His conclusion is that the networks' adaptability and ongoing demand make elimination unlikely.

## Notable passages

**Opening framing (c1).** Black defines the market as a reliable, highly adaptable network linking clans, warlords and militants with governments and traders, from Kalashnikovs to technicals.

**Cold War legacy (c2).** The proxy-state origin and the release of stocks when the Dergue and Barre regimes fell, with a later influx from the Ethiopia-Eritrea war.

**Arms for access (c3).** Italian and other European networks trading weapons for fishing and toxic-waste permissions, with Mafia involvement and alternative routes (Black reports these as publicized cases).

**Routes and dhows (c4).** The mechanics of dhow and aircraft transport, cost-sharing and ports, riding on general trade.

**Official stocks leaking into the market (c5).** The U.N.-sourced eighty percent estimate and the resale chain from government and peacekeeping forces to al-Shabaab.

**Why the market endures (c6).** Black's closing judgement that supply networks and local markets have proven adaptable to counter-proliferation efforts and conflict, so their survival is likely.

## Resonances

The chapter is a compact case of a shadow trade that reuses legitimate trade infrastructure, and its picture of a durable, adaptive market outliving state-led controls loosely rhymes with the proliferation dynamics of the [[divergence-machine]], though Black offers no such theory and is a policy analyst reporting largely on U.N. findings. It also shows state collapse leaving a gap in which private and foreign actors supply force, cf. [[gramsci-gap]] only loosely; Black's concern is Somalia's specific post-1991 situation. Note the reporting is dated to 2009 and concentrates on weapons rather than on piracy, which he mentions only briefly as a possible link.
