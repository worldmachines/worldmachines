# Defining deviant globalization: concept, lenses and plan of the book

*Introduction (second part) by Nils Gilman, Jesse Goldhammer & Steven Weber (the volume's editors), written for this volume, in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).*

The editors define deviant globalization as a distinct category of economic action: value-adding cross-border activity that violates noneconomic, conventional "Western" norms about human rights, violence, health and the sacred. It is neither the same as illegality nor a mere distributional harm of globalization. They argue that it runs on globalization's own infrastructural backbone, is geographically particular, is historically old (piracy, the slave trade, the opium trade) but has accelerated sharply in the past twenty years, and is inseparable from the mainstream economy, which every industry shadows with a "deviant doppelgänger". They then test two conventional lenses, the affective "yuck factor" and the analytic "coercion factor", and find both inadequate, and propose two alternatives: a psychodynamic "return of the repressed" account of demand and a regulatory-economics account of supply, control and arbitrage. The unit closes with a plan of the book: because the phenomenon is hidden, the editors favour an ethnographic over a quantitative approach and present reprinted essays in four sections (human flows, drugs, resource flows, new organizational forms).

## Themes

**Deviance is moral arbitrage, not illegality.** The editors say deviant globalization "cannot, however, be reduced merely to economic activities that violate the law". Some deviant flows are legal (travel to a jurisdiction with a lower age of consent), some illegal acts are not deviant (monopolistic pricing), and some legal harms (offshoring call-center jobs) are only distributional. The test is knowing exploitation of differences in morality between cultures, as in the toxic-waste and sex-tourism examples.

**Same infrastructure, same logic.** "What enables globalization, enables deviant globalization as well." Their four-row table pairs supply-chain technology with counterfeit logistics, the Internet with malware and scams, capital mobility with laundering, and privatization ideology with "everything for sale". Boundaries blur in practice: transplant surgeons, Mafia-run bakeries and listed waste-dumping companies straddle licit and illicit. This is a claim about dual-use infrastructure, close in spirit to what [[farrell-underground-empire]] and [[weaponized-interdependence]] describe from the state's side, though the editors speak of markets and norms rather than chokepoint power.

**Space still matters.** Against theories in which globalization annihilates spatial difference, the editors stress factor endowments (their Romanian hacker example), urban archipelagos from US inner cities to Lagos, and financial nodes like the City of London. This rhymes with critiques of [[aspatial-globalisation]] narratives.

**Four drivers of acceleration.** Technology compressing space and time, the end of the Cold War, ascendant neoliberal ideology, and a fourth modulating factor, "power", meaning the ability to define which things are sanctioned. Whereas globalization reinforces the power structures that enable it, deviant globalization works against them.

**Two inadequate lenses.** The yuck factor is a legitimate starting signal but varies too much across people, cultures and time, and many non-yucky things (the editors cite Chinese coalminers) undermine dignity as much. The coercion factor, with its contract-law "meeting of the minds" logic, is both too broad (buying cocaine is voluntary) and too narrow (licit globalization is not free of coercion). The editors note that read libertarianly, deviant globalization is "the purest expression of free-market capitalism".

**Two alternative lenses.** (1) The return of the repressed: the poor, as innovators, found out which demands the rich repress, and used globalization's rules and infrastructure to supply them, so deviant supply follows deviant demand and "it's not going away". The editors present this as analogy, not clinical theory. (2) Regulatory economics: prohibitions and policing are control systems that shape illicit markets rather than instruments of a winnable war; governments suffer "regulatory capture"; the essence is "regulatory arbitrage" by deviant entrepreneurs. The policy upshot is management of rules, talk of "optimal levels" of trafficking and dumping.

**Benefits as well as bads.** Deviant globalization often offers participants more immediate benefits than Western development models (modernization theory, the Washington Consensus, NGOs). This is the editors' own claim, previewed here and assessed in their conclusion. It bears on the late [[modernity-machine]]'s development project, though the editors do not use that language.

**Method and plan.** Because the activity is hidden by design, formal statistics (World Bank, Transparency International) are insufficient; the editors want ethnographic closeness. The essays, none written with the concept in mind, are by other authors and vary from academic work to investigative journalism. The four sections cover human flows, drugs, resource flows and organizational forms, ending with insurgency and networked guerrillas.

## Notable passages

The opening definition (c1) fixes deviant globalization as a category of economic action defined by norm violation. The doppelgänger passage (c2) states the inseparability thesis. The yuck-factor paragraph (c3) shows the editors treating disgust as a data point rather than a verdict, and the coercion passage (c4) rejects the contractarian reading. The return-of-the-repressed passage (c5) supplies the demand-side psychodynamics, and the regulatory-economics passage (c6) the supply-side and policy frame. Hedges to carry: the editors concede the concept is a lens, not a theory ("A lens is not quite a theory"), and say the book is not a scientific sample. The claim that the poor are the innovators is stated as fact in the text, not demonstrated here.
