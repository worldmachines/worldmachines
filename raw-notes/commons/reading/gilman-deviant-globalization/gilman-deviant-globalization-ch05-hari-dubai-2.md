# Dubai's Dissidents, Servants and Mirages

*Chapter by Johann Hari (first published in The Independent, 7 April 2009), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).*

This is the later part of Hari's first-person reportage on Dubai during the 2009 crash. Hari moves through shopping malls, bars and hotels, letting people speak. He first meets two Emirati dissidents: Mohammed al-Mansoori, a lawyer silenced after criticising the treatment of migrant workers, and Abdulkhaleq Abdullah, who laments a loss of Emirati identity. He then visits an underground gay scene that thrives despite the law, the British expat bars where 'The Lifestyle' rests on cheap servants, and a women's hostel full of escaped maids. After that come the half-empty monuments of the bust (The World islands, Atlantis, the Park Hyatt), a section on Dubai's ecological fragility (water, carbon, sewage in the sea), and a closing scene in a Pizza Hut where Hari generalises Dubai into a compressed global supply chain. Hari is a campaigning journalist and the tone is satirical and polemical. Most claims rest on single named or anonymous informants whom he quotes rather than verifies.

## Themes

**Authoritarian order and silenced speech.** Hari reports (via al-Mansoori) that those who criticise labour conditions are blacklisted, stripped of licence and passport, and kept out of the press, and that companies are mostly government-owned, so human-rights laws threaten state profit. That is al-Mansoori's explanation, offered as his view. Hari adds that new media laws had been drafted to forbid reporting that could 'damage' Dubai or its economy. Expats are described as similarly gagged: an American hotel employee says her phone is bugged and that critics are deported. Hari notes a 1930s episode in which merchants briefly wrested control of state finances from the ruler before being snuffed out with British support. Al-Mansoori warns that suppressed anger could feed Islamism, which is his hypothesis, not a finding.

**Debt, bust and dependence on Abu Dhabi.** Hari calls Dubai 'Creditopolis', says it owes 107 percent of its GDP, and reports that neighbouring Abu Dhabi bailed it out. He attributes to al-Mansoori the claim that Abu Dhabi, more restrictive, will tighten freedom further. The physical evidence is abandoned mega-projects ('Under Construction' now 'Under Collapse') and near-empty luxury hotels.

**A caste system built on cheap servants.** Expats rank nationalities in a hierarchy that one of Hari's interviewees recites with open prejudice. 'The Lifestyle' they praise depends on maids whose passports are held by employers, who control pay, rest and contact. Hari presents this as an 'open secret'. His witnesses include a Filipino woman fielding pleas from other maids, and Mela Matari, an Ethiopian maid in a nearly bankrupt hostel who describes unpaid wages, beatings and a consulate that sent her back to the employer holding her passport. Hari frames this as slavery. The tourists' own cheerful remarks about the 'servant class' are left to condemn themselves. The chapter works through testimony and does not give numbers for how widespread the practice is.

**Selective freedoms.** Dubai's gay scene is illegal (Hari says up to ten years' prison) but tolerated in practice, and draws men from across the region. Hari uses it to show a tolerance that is relative to regional alternatives. Abdullah's complaint (pride on one side, fear of losing the city to expats on the other) shows the Emirati minority also feels dispossessed.

**Ecological overreach.** Hari argues, drawing on Gulf Research Centre environmental director Mohammed Raouf, that Dubai is built where there is almost no usable water, runs on desalination, has a very large per-capita carbon footprint (Hari's figure: more than double an American's), and has almost no water storage. Raouf is quoted warning that falling oil revenue could make supply a catastrophe and that rising seas threaten the artificial islands. Hari then offers the beach-sewage story as a test case for how the state handles environmental problems, summed up by his informant: 'deny it's happening, cover it up'. That prediction is the informant's inference, not Hari's finding.

**Dubai as globalisation distilled.** In the closing section Hari proposes that Dubai disturbed him because it condenses the global supply chain. The semi-enslaved labour that normally lives thousands of miles away is here two miles away, and visible. His label is 'Market Fundamentalist Globalization in One City'. A Filipino Pizza Hut worker closes the chapter by calling everything in Dubai fake: trees, contracts, islands, smiles and water.

## Notable passages

**Mohammed al-Mansoori's fall (c1).** The lawyer who led the Jurists' Association tells how speaking to Human Rights Watch and the BBC led to threats from the secret police and a blacklist for him and his children. His account links labour exploitation to state commercial interests (most companies government-owned).

**Maids and the passport (c2).** The plainest statement of the mechanism: employer control of the passport gives 'absolute power', and the Filipino witness's helplessness shows how the consulates fail the maids. Hari reports as an 'open secret'; he does not cite surveys.

**The expat hierarchy (c3).** Ann Wark and her companions give an unvarnished ranking by nationality. It is evidence from informants about attitudes, not an analysis, and Hari lets the expats condemn themselves.

**Water (c4).** Hari's account of Dubai's dependence on costly, carbon-heavy desalination, with Raouf's warnings about reserves and storage.

**The sewage cover-up (c5).** A concrete case in which a growth-driven city outruns its infrastructure, trucks dump untreated sewage into manholes, and complaints are met with silence and threats of deportation. Single-source and anonymous, so treat as reported testimony.

**Market Fundamentalist Globalization in One City (c6).** Hari's one explicit analytic claim, tying Dubai's labour regime to ordinary global supply chains.

## Connections

Hari's closing claim, that the labour arrangements on view in Dubai are the global supply chain made visible rather than an aberration, sits close to the editors' wider thesis that deviant and mainstream globalization share infrastructure. Hari never uses that frame and writes from outrage, not theory. The narrative also rhymes loosely with [[globalisation-as-inevitability-rhetoric]], since Dubai's boosters present the city as a model for the region, though Hari offers no analysis of that rhetoric as such. The dissidents' blacklisting and the press restrictions are an example of states controlling visibility, which only partly fits [[Legibility]]: the control here concerns what may be said, not what the state can read.
