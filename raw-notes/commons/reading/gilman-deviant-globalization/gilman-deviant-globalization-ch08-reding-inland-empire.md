# Lori Arnold and the Making of the Midwest Meth Economy

*Chapter by Nick Reding (first published in Methland, Bloomsbury, 2009, ch. 3), reprinted in Gilman, Goldhammer & Weber (eds.), Deviant Globalization (2011).*

Reding, reporting from 2005 onward, argues that American meth is a function of farm and food-industry change and of changes in the narcotics and pharmaceuticals industries, and that it is ultimately "a metaphor for the cataclysmic fault lines formed by globalization." He follows the drug to Ottumwa, Iowa, a once-prosperous rail and meatpacking town that, like Oelwein, was hollowed out by the farm crisis, the sale of Hormel to a Cargill subsidiary and a two-thirds fall in wages. The chapter's central figure is Lori Arnold, who by Reding's account (largely her own, corresponded from federal prison, with DEA and agent claims layered in) went from delivering pharmaceutical meth as a teenager to building the Stockdall Organization: runners to Long Beach, a car dealership and houses and racehorses to launder money, and finally a superlab in the wooded hills of her horse farm. In parallel Reding tells of DEA official Gene Haislip's 1985-87 effort to monitor ephedrine imports, which pharmaceutical lobbying rewrote into a law that let ephedrine be imported in pill form unregulated. Arnold was arrested in 1990 and convicted; the chapter ends with her release in 1999 into a meth business that had mutated.

## Themes

**Meth as a symptom of globalization and economic hollowing.** Reding explicitly frames meth as tied to "the emergence of the global economy" and to the divergence of the coast and the middle. Ottumwa's decline (railroad, air base, Hormel sold to a Cargill subsidiary, wages falling from $18 an hour to $5.60 with no benefits, population down 50 percent in twenty-five years) is the gap into which meth "moved." Note that this is his interpretive claim, built on a narrative of one town rather than demonstrated causally.

**Migration routes as trade routes.** In Reding's account the Amezcua brothers (as characterized by DEA) saw that Mexican immigrant workers could move meth through California and the West, and that Midwesterners out-migrating to Southern California created family and social links that "became business connections." Immigration into meat-packing plants and emigration to the coast are the same human tide that carries the product. This echoes the editors' idea that deviant networks ride on the infrastructure of mainstream globalization.

**A vocational drug and a labor-market fit.** Reding describes meth as "vocational, as opposed to recreational": it lets poor and working-class people work harder. As purity rose and price fell just as rural economies collapsed, those who remained felt they needed it most (a view he attributes to Clay Hallberg).

**Deviant entrepreneurship as vertical integration.** Arnold moved from retail to buying direct from the supplier, to owning the car dealership, houses and horse farm that served logistics and laundering, to her own superlab with a borrowed Amezcua chemist, thereby controlling "the entire value chain: Manufacture, distribution, and retail." Reding himself draws the corporate parallel to Cargill and Iowa Beef Packers and to the five Mexican trafficking organizations that, he says, now dominate meth like Cargill dominates food. Arnold's own self-presentation (benevolent employer, Robin Hood, civic duty) is her claim and is flagged by Reding with irony.

**Regulatory arbitrage and capture on the legal side.** Haislip's plan targeted a chokepoint (ephedrine processed in only nine factories worldwide) and modeled on his earlier success against Quaaludes. Reding reports, drawing on a 2004 Oregonian article by Steve Suo, that a Warner-Lambert lobbyist, Allan Rexinger, worked to rewrite the bill and escalated to the White House; the resulting law allowed pill-form ephedrine to be bought in bulk and crushed, becoming "both a mandate and a road map for meth's expansion." The rule written to stop the trade thus protected licit commerce and fed the illicit one. This resonates with Farrell and Newman's chokepoint logic ([[farrell-underground-empire]], [[weaponized-interdependence]]) read from the regulator's side, though Reding offers no such theory, and it also shows an enforcement agency whose attention was elsewhere (DEA's 1987 ambivalence toward meth as a "biker drug" while cocaine cartels held its focus).

## Notable passages

The opening paragraph states Reding's thesis that meth is a metaphor for globalization's fault lines. The Amezcua paragraph gives the most explicit mechanism: legal, unmonitored precursors plus immigrant and out-migrant labor networks. The paragraph on Arnold's superlab and the DEA's ambivalence shows vertical integration and a state looking the wrong way. The ephedrine-bill paragraph is the chapter's account of lobbying altering regulation. The passage on meth as a vocational drug links the economics of work to demand. The paragraph on Arnold's self-image and the Cargill comparison carries Reding's irony about monopoly, deregulation and the next generation of Mexican trafficking organizations.

Caveats: much of the narrative rests on Arnold's own recollection, on DEA sources and agents quoted, and on a single newspaper investigation for the lobbying episode; figures such as street-equivalent weight are Reding's own estimates ("probably more").
