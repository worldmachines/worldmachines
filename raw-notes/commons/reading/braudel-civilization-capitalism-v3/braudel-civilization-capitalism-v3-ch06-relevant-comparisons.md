# Comparisons downstream and upstream: why past 'revolutions' came to nothing before England's took

This unit opens Chapter 6 ('The Industrial Revolution and Growth'). Braudel first relitigates the word 'revolution' itself, insisting the English industrial revolution is simultaneously a sudden eighteenth-century eruption and the visible tip of a process running back centuries. He then runs two comparative tests of that claim: downstream, comparing England's success to the twentieth-century Third World's failed industrializations; and upstream, surveying a series of earlier 'revolutions' — Alexandria, medieval Europe, Renaissance Milan and Germany, and England's own earlier coal-based industrial surge of 1560-1640 — that each had real momentum and each 'came to nothing.'

## Themes

**Revolution as dialectic, not takeoff.** Braudel confronts Rostow's image of an economy 'taking off' at a precisely dated moment (1783-1802) with Kuznets's contrary figures, Phyllis Deane's 'historical continuum,' Landes's 'critical mass,' and Fohlen's emphasis on continuity. His conclusion is methodological and governs everything that follows: no single sector — savings rate, 'attitudes, institutions and techniques' — can be transformed 'at a stroke'; true growth is only ever an 'overall and indivisible process' advancing on several fronts simultaneously, long and short rhythms beating together.

**Downstream: the Third World test, and world-economy position.** Braudel treats the present-day Third World's industrialization failures as a natural experiment that can define, by contrast, the conditions behind Britain's success — Ignacy Sachs's bottleneck-planning method, the recurring 'agricultural barrier,' and the demand for intersectorial harmony and a cohesive national market (France's own slow takeoff, delayed until its railway network was complete, is the internal European parallel). But the deeper argument is structural, not sectoral: England industrialized precisely because she was 'in fact the centre of the world,' while the modern Third World is 'firmly on the periphery' — dependent on licensed technology, borrowed capital, and shipping it does not control. This is Braudel's own core/periphery apparatus (elaborated across Vol. III's account of the world-economy's 'rules') doing explanatory work in miniature: position within the hierarchy of centre and periphery, not the presence or absence of any one input, decides whether growth compounds or dissipates.

**Upstream: a typology of failure.** Braudel then runs the comparison backward through four case studies, each isolating what England alone eventually had. Ptolemaic Alexandria (steam-powered toys, the aeolipile, a real cluster of inventions) never converted its inventiveness into industrial production — blocked, Braudel suggests, by slavery's cheap labour and by Rome's subsequent closing of the Mediterranean world to wider horizons. Medieval Europe's eleventh-to-thirteenth-century mill revolution (fulling-mills, water- and windmills) produced genuine 'sustained growth' — Frederic Lane's and Wilhelm Abel's term — before the demographic and agrarian collapse of 1350-1450 cut it short. Renaissance Germany's mining boom (1470s onward) was undercut when American silver began competing with German ore after 1535; Renaissance Milan's 'revolution in embryo' — a demographic revolution, an early territorial state, capitalist agriculture, and Leonardo-era engineering enthusiasm all at once — crumbled for want of a large national market and, unlike Venice or Genoa, a port giving direct access to international trade. Finally England's own earlier, half-forgotten 'first industrial revolution' of 1560-1640 (John U. Nef's phrase) was built on a forced turn to coal once wood and water power grew scarce and expensive; it stalled, without collapsing, in the general seventeenth-century crisis after 1642.

**Anti-technological-determinism.** The thread tying the four cases together is Braudel's insistence that invention is never sufficient by itself. Milan and Bologna had hydraulic silk-throwing machinery (Carlo Poni) as sophisticated as anything eighteenth-century England would build, and Lynn White had already shown medieval Europe possessed most of the mechanical devices later developed over the next four centuries. Braudel adopts White's aphorism — 'a new device merely opens a door; it does not compel anyone to enter' — as the chapter's real thesis: no industrial revolution 'can be regarded as a strictly economic process,' since the economy 'cannot exist in isolation from the other sectors of human life.'

## Notable passages

The Rostow/takeoff critique (chunk c1) states the unit's governing method before any case study is introduced. The centre/periphery passage (c2) is the payload of the downstream comparison, translating Braudel's world-economy hierarchy into a direct explanation of why the same growth strategy succeeds in one structural position and fails in another. The Alexandria passage (c3) supplies the first, and starkest, 'revolution that came to nothing,' closing on the chapter's organizing question — 'is an industrial revolution possible only at the heart of an open world-economy?' The Carus-Wilson quotation on fulling-mills (c4) is Braudel's strongest positive evidence that a genuine industrial revolution predates England's by half a millennium. The Zangheri/Lynn White passage on Milan (c5) states the unit's central puzzle in its own words and answers it with the 'device merely opens a door' aphorism. The coal passage and 'Newcastle is Peru' couplet (c6) ground the abstract argument in England's own earlier, partial industrial revolution, making the point concrete: coal was adopted from scarcity, not foresight, and even a genuine domestic 'takeoff' can stall for a century before the one that sticks.

**World Machines resonances.** Braudel's argument against Rostow's single dated 'takeoff' — that the English revolution was instead 'a combination of elements, a family complex' assembling silently over centuries before lurching into visibility in the 1760s — rhymes closely with WMP's [[history-machine]] concept (a paradigm that assembles from independent components until it becomes visible, against 'great men' historiography); Braudel is making almost exactly this case against Rostow, Say, and the other 'great men' who missed the machine forming around them. The closing question about an 'open world-economy' as the precondition for a revolution to catch and hold — echoed by Milan's failure for want of a national market and a port — half-resonates with [[world-machines]]'s claim that a machine's Dawn requires the right structural position to lock in and pass into Day; the fit is partial, since Braudel's 'openness' is a trade-network and market-size condition operating on a decades-to-centuries scale, not a civilizational-infrastructure cycle, but both accounts agree that structural position, not invention or intention alone, decides whether momentum converts into an era.
