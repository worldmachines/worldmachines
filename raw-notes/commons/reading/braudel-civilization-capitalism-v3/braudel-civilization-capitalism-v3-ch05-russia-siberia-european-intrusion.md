# Conquering Siberia while being conquered by Europe

Braudel closes his chapter on Russia's place among the world-economies by tracing three linked stories: the improvised, fur-hungry conquest of Siberia that mirrored Europe's colonization of America; the structural weaknesses that left Russia's own frontiers — with China, with the Islamic trading networks, and above all with western Europe — controlled by foreign intermediaries rather than by Russians themselves; and the price Russia paid, after Peter the Great opened St Petersburg as a window on Europe, for becoming a raw-material periphery of the European world-economy even as it built an empire of its own to the east.

## Themes

**Siberia as an improvised, undercapitalized America.** Braudel opens by setting Russia's conquest of Siberia directly against Europe's 'invention' of America: both were tasks too large for their inventors, but Russia lacked Europe's resources, population, and reliable sea routes, so the whole enterprise had to go overland, limes-style, through the Urals rather than by ship. What followed (1583 onward) was not a planned imperial project but a rush of promyslenniki — hunters, trappers, and peasants — moving ahead of, and largely independent of, the state; official administration (the 1637 Siberian prikaz, modeled on Spain's Consejo de Indias and Casa de la Contratación) arrived to tax and monopolize the fur trade only after the fact. Furs ('soft gold') were the entire point: tribute in kind, a state monopoly constantly leaking through smuggling into China, Gdansk, and Venice.

**A periphery inside the periphery.** The Siberian population Russia dominated — Tartars, Buriats, Samoyeds, Tunguses, Yakuts — was itself pushed back into deserts and deep forest and pressed into forced labor, though Braudel is careful to note nothing here matched the scale of plantation slavery in the Americas. The Gmelin quotation on the Tunguses shows the same ethnographic distancing gaze that Europeans elsewhere turned on Russia, turned here, in miniature, by Russians on their own conquered subjects.

**Russia's frontiers were held by everyone but Russia.** In the 'Inferiorities and weaknesses' section, Braudel inventories how thin Russian control actually was at its edges: China's Manchu state forced the terms of Nerchinsk (1689) and Kyakhta (1727), expelling Russian merchants from Peking outright; Islamic-world trade networks (Armenian, Indian, Persian, Ragusan, Greek merchants) simply occupied Russian territory — Astrakhan, Moscow, Archangel — because their networks were older and better established than anything Russian; and on the European side, Hanseatic, Swedish, Polish, Dutch and finally English merchants captured the 'commanding positions,' with the Dutch going bankrupt one after another as the English took over by the end of the century. Braudel's verdict is blunt: the Russian giant was nowhere firmly established.

**The price of the window on Europe.** Peter's founding of St Petersburg cut both ways — it let Russians reach Europe, but it let Europe reach Russia just as easily, and Europe's normal tools (flexible credit, ready cash, the 'haemorrhage of specie') did the rest: the same extraction logic Braudel applies elsewhere to the Levant and the Indies. Russia kept a favorable trade balance in bullion terms, but the real profits were made in the West on resale and redistribution — Russia supplied raw materials (furs, then hemp, flax, tar, grain) and absorbed European luxury manufactures, occasionally exporting grain even during domestic famine. A 1765 memorandum (addressed to Alexander Vorontsov, written — with unmistakable irony — in French) urged Russia to imitate the closed-door trade posture Braudel attributes to India and China: sell raw materials for cash, buy almost nothing back. It went nowhere; the same Russian elite the memorandum criticized had already been thoroughly acculturated to French and English fashion, luxury, and ideas.

**A qualified counter-case.** Braudel does not flatten Russia into simple dependency: unlike Poland, Russia had its own protected domestic manufacturing (state-sponsored mines, foundries, silk and glass works from the seventeenth century on) and, by J. Blum's account, matched or exceeded the rest of Europe's industrial development in the eighteenth century — only falling behind once the real Industrial Revolution arrived in the nineteenth. Russia's position reads as semi-peripheral rather than colonial: extractive vis-à-vis Siberia, structurally dependent vis-à-vis the European core, but never as fully absorbed as Poland.

## Notable passages

The chapter's opening move — that Russia had to 'invent' Siberia much as Europe 'invented' America — states outright that this is a comparative exercise in world-economy structure, not a national epic; everything that follows (the promyslenniki's freelance conquest, the belated prikaz administration, the 'soft gold' fur monopoly) reads as Braudel testing how far the analogy holds and where it breaks (no plantation slavery, no comparable capital, a purely overland frontier).

The Gmelin quotation on the Tunguses is the unit's one passage of direct period voice, and it does double duty: it documents the othering gaze Russia's own colonizers turned on Siberia's peoples, while implicitly rhyming with the gaze Enlightenment Europe turned on Russia itself throughout the unit's third section.

The 'Inferiorities and weaknesses' section culminates in the flat verdict that the Russian giant was not firmly established — the passage naming Peking, Istanbul, Isfahan, Leipzig, Lwow, Lübeck, Amsterdam and London as the 'hidden hands' manipulating Russian foreign trade is Braudel's clearest single sentence on why Russia counts as semi-periphery rather than an independent world-economy of Wallersteinian rank.

The 'haemorrhage of specie' passage is the unit's theoretical core: Braudel explicitly equates the mechanism draining bullion into Russia (and immediately back out again as European resale profit) with what he elsewhere describes for the Levant and the Indies — the same core/periphery extraction logic applied to a new case. This nested hierarchy — Russia's Siberian empire subordinate in miniature to Russia, Russia itself subordinate to the European core — echoes [[world-machines]]'s picture of coexisting orders locked in structural friction, though Braudel's zones are an economic-geographic hierarchy rather than institutional epochs, and the 'Russian giant' here is squeezed between two roles rather than caught between three coexisting infrastructures.

The Siberian prikaz's administrative capture of the fur trade (tribute in kind, forced state monopoly, explicit modeling on Spain's Consejo de Indias) is a small-scale instance of [[legibility-as-machine-core]] — the imposition of measurable, taxable, monopolizable order onto an unruly frontier so it can be extracted from at a distance.

The 1765 memorandum's evocation of an imagined India-and-China model — sell raw materials, refuse to buy back European manufactures — is Braudel's chosen illustration that contemporary Russians diagnosed their own semi-peripheral position with some accuracy. Its closed-door instinct is a faint, mercantilist cousin of [[capture-resistance]]: a recognized reflex against economic absorption, though aimed at protecting a sovereign trade balance rather than at the autonomy and self-organization sense the term carries in WMP — and the memorandum's own composition in French, for an already Europeanized elite, shows how little room the reflex had to act.
