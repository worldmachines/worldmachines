# The World-Economy: An Order Among Other Orders

Braudel opens Volume III's analytical core by refusing his own book's implicit overreach: the world-economy governs a great deal, but it is one "order" among several — political, social, and cultural — that constantly interpenetrate and never permanently subordinate one another. He tests that thesis across six terrains — the international division of labour, the fusion of state and economic power, the survival of world-economies inside and beyond empires, the different faces of war at core and periphery, the synchronic coexistence of labour regimes across zones, and the independent durability of culture — before closing with a considered, partly critical endorsement of Immanuel Wallerstein's world-system model.

## Themes

**No single order rules for good.** Economy, politics, society, and culture are "inextricably mingled," each nibbling at the others' frontiers and creating its own zones of influence (a state's capital/provinces/colonies; a culture's concentric circles of prestige) — "their ranking order, if ranking order there is, is forever changing, slowly it is true, but changing all the same." This resonates with [[world-machines]]'s premise that multiple orders (there, three machines; here, four social spheres) coexist in permanent friction rather than any one absorbing the others for good, though Braudel's orders operate synchronically and don't share WMP's Dawn/Day/Dusk lifecycle. The claim that none of these orders has a single origin or directing will — that they assemble from countless local, uncoordinated encroachments on each other's frontiers — also rhymes with [[Configurancy]]'s non-teleological, relational co-emergence, qualified by the fact that Braudel's frame is a fixed small set of historical "orders," not Rao's open-ended agents-and-worlds ontology.

**Inequality is inherited, not natural.** Braudel rejects Ricardo's comparative-advantage "pseudo-theorem" as ahistorical: the international division of labour was never "a concerted agreement made between equal parties," but "a chain of subordinations, each conditioning the others." He prefers the "deliberately simple-minded tautologies" — "a poor country is poor because it is poor," "growth breeds growth" — because they name path dependency honestly: England's centuries-long shift from raw-wool exporter to industrial power, Portugal's parallel slide, and France's repeated "missed opportunities" (1713, 1722, 1762, and the fourteenth-century loss of the Champagne-fairs advantage) all show that "a loss is never the result of a single throw nor indeed is a triumph."

**State power and economic power fuse at the centre, diverge toward the edges.** At the core (Venice, then Holland, then Britain, now the United States) government is necessarily strong, because "the central position itself" demands effective government. Near the centre but not in it, monarchies govern uneasily with or against their nobilities and turn to mercantilism defensively — "above all a means of self-defence," dropped once a state (Holland, later England) feels secure enough to embrace free trade. At the outer fringes, colonies have no self-government worth the name; in non-colonial peripheries such as partitioned-era Poland or eighteenth-century Italy the state survives as a hollow institution. Losers at the margins — the Barbary corsairs, Sweden under Gustavus Adolphus — have only violence left as a strategy.

**World-economies can survive, and predate, empire.** Braudel grants Wallerstein's claim that world-empires (Mughal, Chinese, Persian, Ottoman, Muscovite) are archaic, politically dominant "command economies" that can stunt a world-economy's development — capitalists under empire could be hanged, executed, or beheaded at a ruler's whim. But he insists economies routinely escape and outlast imperial control: Roman traders in the Indian Ocean, Armenian networks from Julfa, Indian Banyans in Moscow, Chinese merchants across the East Indies. This becomes the pivot for his central disagreement with Wallerstein: rather than dating the European world-economy to the sixteenth century and the Habsburg collapse of 1557, Braudel relocates its birth to thirteenth-century Italy, arguing that Charles V's imperial ambitions never had the power to decide the matter either way. This is a strong instance of [[history-machine]]'s contrast with "great-men" historiography: Braudel explicitly subordinates a sovereign's will and personal project to distributed, already-assembled structural forces (capitalism already resident in Antwerp, Lisbon, Seville, Genoa) that would have proceeded unhurt whether or not Charles V succeeded.

**War wears the face of its zone.** At the core, war is expensive, sophisticated, and — crucially — rule-bound: sieges outnumber battles ("We make War more like Foxes than like Lyons"), prisoners are exchanged, ransoms paid, and the cardinal rule is to fight on the enemy's territory, never one's own, on pain of losing primacy. At the periphery the same historical process takes an unrecognizable shape: Spanish tercios drilled for Flanders are mocked by Arab horsemen outside Oran; the Dutch-Portuguese war in Pernambuco degenerates, once European reinforcement stops, into guerra volante — ambush, dispersal, terrain — a tactic later exported, via Garibaldi, to Sicily.

**Capitalism sits atop, and requires, a hierarchy of coexisting modes.** Wage labour, sharecropping, serfdom, and slavery do not succeed one another in linear time; they coexist synchronically across a world-economy's zones (Venezuela's "feudal" llanos alongside its slave plantations is Braudel's recurring example), and moving between zones means moving between labour regimes, not between historical periods. Endorsing Wallerstein and Rosa Luxemburg here, Braudel argues capitalism cannot exist without the other modes surrounding and subsidizing it, and that it enters peripheries not by conquest but through colluding local "entry points" — a Polish landowner selling to Gdańsk, a Brazilian engenho-owner tied to Lisbon, a Jamaican planter tied to London.

**Culture is the oldest, most durable order, and it does not track the economic map.** Cultural centres and economic centres routinely diverge — Florence, not trade-dominant Venice or Genoa, set the cultural tone of Renaissance Italy; Paris, not London, was eighteenth-century Europe's cultural capital even as Britain led economically. Cultural and religious boundaries could override economic logic outright: the bill of exchange, "the key weapon in the armoury of merchant capitalism," stayed almost entirely within Christendom for centuries, never crossing into Islam, Muscovy, or the Far East, "circulating among friends so to speak." Civilization, in Braudel's image, is "the grandfather, the patriarch of world history" — surviving the fall of Rome and the rise and fall of empires in a way no economic or political order does.

**A qualified yes to Wallerstein.** Braudel calls the world-economy/core-periphery/semi-periphery model "certainly a valid one" and credits it with real explanatory power (why central regions keep surpassing themselves; why "development is the reverse side of underdevelopment"), while pressing his own revisions: an earlier origin date, and a caution against letting the economic frame crowd out the political and cultural orders this whole unit insists are irreducible to it.

## Notable passages

**Society as inextricable orders.** The opening move: economy, politics, culture, and society are "the set of sets," none of them separable in lived reality even though analysis must pull them apart — and none permanently dominant over the others.

**Unequal exchange as inheritance, not nature.** Braudel's clearest statement that global inequality is compounded historical accumulation ("some people have always held better cards than others") rather than a Ricardian natural equilibrium.

**The world-economy outran Charles V's empire.** The passage carrying Braudel's disagreement with Wallerstein's sixteenth-century dating — capitalism's presence in Antwerp, Lisbon, Seville, and Genoa was a structural fact indifferent to whether one emperor's dream of universal monarchy succeeded or failed.

**Sieges at the core, guerrilla at the periphery.** The Dutch-Portuguese war in the Pernambuco sugar country as the concrete case: once European backup stops, the "poor man's war" (guerra volante) supplants the sieges-and-pitched-battles warfare of the core.

**Capitalism installs itself atop a hierarchy of coexisting modes.** Braudel's endorsement of Wallerstein and Luxemburg: capitalism requires the coexistence of other, "lower" modes of production, and reaches into peripheries through colluding local elites rather than replacing what it finds.

**Civilization outlasts the economic map.** The claim that culture is older and more durable than any economic or political order — "the grandfather, the patriarch of world history" — with its own, sometimes uncrossable, boundaries.
