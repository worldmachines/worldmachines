# Colonies Against Europe: Capital, Industry, and the Path to American Rivalry

This unit tracks how the "colonial pact" — the mercantilist arrangement binding American colonies to serve their European mother-countries — broke down under its own contradictions: currency extraction bred smuggling and local capital accumulation across Spanish and Portuguese America; prohibitions on colonial manufacture failed to stop indigenous industries from emerging; and in the English colonies specifically, immigration-driven demographic growth, a hardening North-South social divide, and above all London's monopoly on colonial foreign trade turned economic grievance into the Boston Tea Party and the war for independence. Braudel closes by reading the episode through his own longue-durée lens: contemporaries as early as the 1760s were already predicting that the fledgling American economy would eventually displace England exactly as England had once displaced Holland.

## Themes

**The colonial pact strained by extraction.** Colonies existed, in Jefferson's own bitter phrase, as "a species of property annexed to certain mercantile houses in London"; the mother country enforced a permanent trade surplus that drained currency outward and returned none. Distance, weak customs enforcement, and "ubiquitous and unstoppable" smuggling let colonial administrations quietly accommodate local interests instead of London's instructions, and this laxity is precisely how local merchant capital accumulated — enough that Spanish Creole merchants could quip, by the century's end, that Spain had become "the colony of its colonies." Resentment against gachupín newcomers, and Hidalgo's denunciation of their "sordid avarice," shows the extraction relation curdling into something closer to caste hostility.

**Industry the metropole tried to forbid.** Textile and craft manufacture sprang up across Spanish America — Quito, Cuzco, New Granada, Mendoza — precisely because seventeenth-century crisis in Europe had cut the "American end" loose to find its own salvation. Lord Chatham's reported threat that America would "feel the full weight of British might" for so much as making a stocking captures the metropole's real position: not that colonial industry was economically impossible, but that Europe intended to keep its monopoly on manufacture by force if necessary. Braudel's own gloss — the New World "was quite prepared to manufacture the things it needed" — makes explicit that the constraint was political, not technical.

**Demography and social structure behind the English colonies' revolt.** Waves of Irish, German, and Scots-Irish immigration (indentured servitude Braudel calls "a trade in human beings," explicitly likened to the slave trade in everything but name) fed a frontier-driven population explosion that diluted the colonies' English character and weakened attachment to the mother country. This human tide split structurally along a North-South line — mercantile, freeholding New England against a plantation South of slaveholding grandees — a divide Braudel finds already fully legible at Jefferson's Monticello, and one that "would influence the history of the future United States from the start." He is careful to note the era's blind spot: the same colonists demanding liberty from England did not yet trouble themselves over acquiescing in black slavery.

**Trade rivalry and the passing of commercial dominance.** Braudel credits Adam Smith with isolating the real mechanism of rebellion in one clause: colonial self-government was "complete... in everything except their foreign trade." That exception forced the colonies through London for credit and payments even as New England's shipping, fishing, and triangular trade with the West Indies and Africa built an economy fully capable of independence. The unit's real payload, though, is the succession claim: Accarias de Serionne was already warning in the 1760s that "New England is more to be feared than Old England," and by 1801 a French diplomat could describe England's position vis-à-vis the United States as structurally identical to Holland's position vis-à-vis England a century earlier — a power "worn out with expenses and debts," watching its commercial influence pass to a rival "taking its first steps... in commerce." This is Braudel's succession-of-dominant-centres logic (Venice→Antwerp→Genoa→Amsterdam→London) applied prospectively, with London itself now cast as the aging incumbent — a passage that resonates strongly with [[world-machines]]'s account of eras succeeding one another as the old order exhausts itself before the new one is fully formed, though Braudel's mechanism here is narrowly commercial-financial rather than civilizational.

Two further half-resonances are worth flagging without over-claiming them. The colonies' persistent, illicit workarounds — smuggling, unlicensed manufacture, currency retention — read as a pre-modern analogue to [[capture-resistance]]: local economic actors finding gaps in an extractive regime and refusing to be fully legible to or controlled by it, though the "capture" here is an empire's mercantile monopoly rather than the informational/computational capture the World Machines framework has in mind. And the sheer persistence of colonial merchants in expanding trade and capital despite active suppression — "American" trade being "far too lively and too spontaneous not to seize the freedom it was not granted" — has the flavor of [[Conatus]], a collective economic striving to persist and grow against a regime designed to cap it, though Spinoza's term names an individual metaphysical drive, not an aggregate historical one.

## Notable passages

- The Jefferson quotation on colonial property crystallizes the extraction relation at the heart of the "colonial pact," while the surrounding paragraph shows how distance and smuggling let that relation erode from within.
- The unfinished-industrialization passage ("Having been established... very far from what Europe had in mind") states plainly that colonial manufacture was blocked by policy, not capacity — the seed of later industrial conflict.
- The Boston Tea Party paragraph is the unit's narrative hinge, marking where accumulated grievance becomes open break.
- Adam Smith's "in everything except their foreign trade" is presented as the single sentence that best explains the rebellion's economic cause.
- The passage on freedom-for-whites and acquiescence-in-slavery is Braudel's own aside, flagging a contradiction contemporaries mostly declined to examine.
- The Accarias de Serionne / French-plenipotentiary sequence is the unit's climax: contemporaries reading, in real time, the first signs of a passing of dominance from England to the United States, framed explicitly through the earlier Dutch-to-English succession.
