# The World-Economy and Its Three Rules: Space, the Dominant City, and the Hierarchy of Zones

Opening Volume III, Braudel defines his central spatial category, the 'world-economy' (economie-monde, his rendering of Weltwirtschaft), distinguishes it from the everyday sense of 'the world economy,' and derives from a comparative survey of historical world-economies (Phoenicia, Rome, Islam, Europe, Muscovy, China, India) a set of three durable rules governing how any world-economy organizes itself in space: slow-shifting frontiers, a single dominant city at the centre with cities succeeding one another in turn, and an enduring hierarchy of core, intermediate, and peripheral zones. He closes by asking, via the image of a huge marble block moved by modest levers, how such a materially thin apparatus of credit and price could ever have controlled so vast and inert a mass of ordinary economic life.

## Themes

**A world-economy is a fragment, not the globe.** Braudel opens by separating two expressions that invite confusion: 'the world economy,' Sismondi's 'market of the universe' encompassing all trading humanity, from 'a world-economy,' which 'only concerns a fragment of the world, an economically autonomous section of the planet able to provide for most of its own needs.' He anchors the concept autobiographically in his own prior study of the sixteenth-century Mediterranean as a Welttheater/Weltwirtschaft: a zone with real economic unity imposed from above by Venice and the other Italian cities, that nonetheless cut clean across political, religious and civilizational frontiers (Christian, Muslim, Greek) which everywhere else seemed decisive. The lesson he draws is that a world-economy is 'a sum of individualized areas ... which it brings together,' typically the largest coherent economic zone of its period, and typically larger than the other 'great historical divisions' (state, empire, civilization) that geography also produces.

**Rule One: frontiers change only slowly.** World-economies border each other along zones 'only worth crossing, economically speaking, in exceptional circumstances,' because for ordinary traffic 'the loss in exchange would outweigh the gain.' These frontiers are 'thick shells, hard to penetrate,' usually coinciding with real natural barriers: the Sahara separating Black from White Africa, the Atlantic (an 'empty expanse' compared to the long-trafficked Indian Ocean), the barely-opened Pacific, and above all the sheer difficulty of Muscovy, which several period travellers describe as a roadless, swamp-choked, near-impassable expanse that put it outside the European world-economy's eastern boundary well into the seventeenth century. The point generalizes: a world-economy's edges are the places where a given order's control runs out for reasons of raw distance and difficulty, not by fiat.

**Rule Two: one dominant city — and cities take turns.** Every world-economy has an urban 'logistic heart' where news, capital, credit and instructions converge, ringed by an 'archipelago' of subordinate towns that direct trade toward it and live off its credit. Braudel catalogues the social signature of these 'super-cities' (Venice, Antwerp, Amsterdam, London): cosmopolitan mixing ('a Noah's Ark'), unusual religious and civic toleration (Venice's freedom of conscience, Amsterdam's open house after the Arminian-Gomarist violence, London's proliferation of sects), and a sharpening class structure of patriciate, bourgeoisie and proletariat driven by the city's own high cost of living. Crucially, dominance is never permanent: 'dominant cities did not dominate for ever; they replaced each other,' and each hand-off — Amsterdam over Antwerp, London over Amsterdam, New York over London around 1929 — was 'a massive historical shift of forces, revealing the precariousness of the previous equilibrium.' Braudel treats such shifts as more than economic: China's 1421 move of its capital from Nanking to Peking turned its back on seaborne trade and cost it the race for maritime dominion it had barely realized it was running; Philip II's 1582 retreat from Lisbon to landlocked Madrid, abandoning the best vantage from which to rule a seaborne empire, is called 'a fateful mistake' whose consequences (the Armada's 1588 disaster) contemporaries themselves diagnosed. 'There is only room for one centre at a time in a world-economy'; the eclipse of one nearly always shows up first at the periphery, in the string of colonial losses (Venice's empire, Portugal's Far East, France's Canada, Spain's Latin America) that mark a declining centre's unraveling.

**Not every dominant city carries the same arsenal.** Braudel qualifies the sequence Venice-Antwerp-Genoa-Amsterdam-London: the first three each held only part of the 'complete arsenal of economic domination' (Venice had trade and finance but little industry and political independence via the Terraferma; Antwerp had no shipping and no politics of its own; Genoa staked everything on banking, filling a vacuum as 'no more than an interlude'), whereas Amsterdam and, still more, London commanded shipping, industry, credit and a national territorial base together. The variable is not just economic technique but 'political power or violence' — culminating in London's command of a national market and eventually an empire that dwarfed the city-state model entirely.

**Rule Three: core, intermediate zone, and periphery.** Every world-economy 'polarizes' into zones facing a single centre, 'a narrow core, a fairly developed middle zone and a vast periphery,' an explanation of 'very wide application' that Braudel credits as the basis of Immanuel Wallerstein's *The Modern World-System* — naming Wallerstein as the interlocutor his own model is arguing with and building on. He tests the schema against Johann Heinrich von Thunen's *Der isolierte Staat* (1826), whose isolated city ringed by concentric agricultural zones (market gardens, then cereals and livestock, purely as a function of distance) Braudel treats as a useful abstraction but faults for omitting the one thing that matters: 'the very important concept of inequality' between zones, an inequality he insists has an actual origin and history rather than being a natural given. Identifying the intermediate zone is hard (a resident colony of foreign merchants is his practical marker of a region's subordination — Genoese bankers in Madrid, Dutch merchants in Leipzig, English merchants in Lisbon); identifying the periphery is not: serfdom or slavery, a barely-monetized economy, and startlingly low prices (Hungary, Siberia) mark it unmistakably. He adds that backwardness is not confined to the geographic periphery — 'neutral zones' and 'black holes outside world time' (Breton hinterlands, Alpine valleys, the English Fens before drainage) persist inside the core itself, sometimes defended by violence against modernization. The chapter's closing move (badly garbled in this scan but still legible in outline) extends the same logic one level further in: hierarchy operates *within* the core too, in the price and cost differentials Braudel finds between rival centres like Naples and Palermo in the 'privileged Mezzogiorno of Italy.'

**The envelope and the marble block.** Braudel's closing puzzle is how such 'modest' means — a little ready silver, offered credit, the lure of high prices at the end of a trading chain, 'the force of habit' — could move so much inert economic weight. His own image: a huge quarried marble block, cut and hauled by primitive means (gunpowder, a few levers, ropes, rollers) because the block's own inertness on the ground made it, paradoxically, 'more easily manoeuverable from above.' Elementary economic activity, he suggests, is like the marble: 'trapped, imprisoned, unable to move from the ground,' and therefore governable by a comparatively small set of levers applied at the right points — the same asymmetry that lets a single city organize a whole world-economy from its centre.

## Notable passages

**World-economy vs. world economy** sets the chapter's whole vocabulary: 'the world economy' as Sismondi's universal trading humanity versus 'a world-economy' as one self-sufficient, organically-linked fragment of it — the distinction the rest of Volume III is built on.

**Rule One: frontiers as quiet zones** states the boundary rule directly and then grounds it in real geography (the Sahara, the Atlantic, the barely-crossed Indian Ocean) rather than leaving it abstract.

**Rule Two: cities take turns to lead** gives the succession itself — Antwerp to Amsterdam to London to New York — as 'a massive historical shift of forces,' the mechanism by which one centre's rise is inseparable from another's 'precariousness.' This loosely rhymes with [[world-machines]]'s idea of dominant infrastructures succeeding one another, though the resonance is partial at best: Braudel's centres change hands over decades within one continuous European world-economy, not across the ~1,000-year Dawn/Day/Dusk arc of a whole civilizational infrastructure, and he frames the hand-off as a shift in economic 'equilibrium' rather than the ideological interregnum a [[gramsci-gap]] names — worth flagging as a rhyme in shape (centre succession, real turbulence at the hinge) rather than in mechanism or scale.

**Rule Three: the hierarchy of zones (Wallerstein)** is the chapter's most load-bearing single sentence for this project's purposes: it states the core/middle-zone/periphery structure and explicitly credits Wallerstein's *The Modern World-System* as built on it, marking Braudel's own position relative to world-systems theory.

**The marble block: envelope and infrastructure** is Braudel's own explanatory image for the whole apparatus — inert mass moved by minimal, well-placed leverage — and is worth holding onto as the chapter's answer to its own question of how domination from a centre is possible at all.
