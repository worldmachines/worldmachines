# Was the Dutch Republic a State? Oligarchy, Regressive Taxation, and Commerce as Raison d'État

This unit takes up the long-standing historiographical claim — voiced by P. W. Klein, Pierre Jeannin, a hostile Portuguese envoy, and Turgot — that the United Provinces could scarcely be called a state at all, and traces the formal weakness of its central institutions (the Council of State, the States-General, the perpetual friction between Holland and the princes of Orange) alongside the informal, remarkably stable regent oligarchy that actually ran the country underneath that surface churn. It then offers two proofs of underlying state capacity that a purely constitutional reading misses: a fiscal system that spared capital and taxed the poor's necessities into the ground, and a foreign policy effective enough that Johan de Witt could negotiate with Louis XIV's ambassador as an equal. It closes by showing commerce operating as the Republic's actual raison d'état — merchants arming and trading with the country's enemies, selling colonial conquests as private property, provoking foreign observers' moral outrage — while tracing how the sober, self-effacing Golden Age elite gradually curdled into a rentier class withdrawn from trade and cut off from the rest of society.

## Themes

**A weak constitution is not the same as a weak state.** Braudel opens by staging the consensus view — Klein's 'anything resembling a state', Jeannin's 'state with little capacity for intervention', Sir William Temple's joke that the United Provinces should be called the 'Disunited Provinces' — only to spend the rest of the unit undermining it. His method is to separate constitutional form (seven sovereign provinces, unanimous consent required for every decision, no central institution with real power in theory) from actual capacity (a debt-financing system that never failed to pay interest, a customs and excise apparatus that reached every foodstuff, a foreign policy that held its own against the Sun King). The closing question of the tax section — 'if the needs of the state were met at the expense of the community, is that really the sign of a 'weak state'?' — is the unit's real thesis stated as a rhetorical question.

**A closed regent patriciate persists beneath the alternation of power.** The surface political history is one of violent swings — Oldenbarnevelt's execution in 1619, William II's failed 1650 coup, the stadtholderless period, the 1672 massacre of the de Witt brothers, the 1788 Orangist restoration — driven by the standing rivalry between the province of Holland (commerce, peace, the fleet) and the Stadtholders (army, war, personal power on the model of a monarch). But Braudel is explicit that 'the ruling class as a whole remained in position, however, whether Holland or the princes of Orange had the upper hand': a patriciate of roughly 2,000 regents, self-recruiting, controlling every town, province, the States-General, the Council of State, and the VOC, absorbed newly wealthy merchant families by marriage or crisis but otherwise closed ranks. Change of government was 'musical chairs' among the same class, not a change of who actually held power — the state's real machinery is the class structure, not the officeholders who cycle through it.

**Golden Age discretion gives way to rentier withdrawal.** The unit's clearest decline narrative sits inside 'Internal structures': during the seventeenth-century Golden Age the regents cultivated deliberate plainness — Sir William Temple could not tell Johan de Witt or the admiral Michiel de Ruyter apart from ordinary burghers, and the Herengracht houses had no magnificent facades. After the 'republicans' took power in 1650, the oligarchy bureaucratized, wealth accumulated, and, per Isaac de Pinto, brokers built 'fairy-tale palaces' their predecessors would never have owned; by the eighteenth century trade had become secondary, surplus capital moved into government stocks and credit, and 'this society of rentiers with too much money gradually closed its ranks, becoming more and more cut off from the rest of society.' The same period saw the elite abandon its own cultural tradition for French fashion and language — a *dusk* internal to Dutch dominance, distinct from and preceding its external eclipse by England and France after the 1680s. This compressed arc (a generation or two, driven by class formation and capital's flight from production into rent, rather than institutional legitimacy collapse over centuries) only loosely echoes [[world-machines]]'s Dawn-Day-Dusk lifecycle — worth naming as a rhyme, not a fit, since Braudel's timescale and mechanism are both much smaller and more sociological than the Modernity Machine's centuries-long infrastructural cycle.

**Fiscal injustice was engineered, not incidental.** The Heere Geld servant tax was 'curiously regressive' by design (barely more per servant as the count rose), income under 300 florins was untaxed while a 1% levy hit the middle, and no death duties fell on direct inheritance — capital was spared at every point. The real weight fell on excise duties covering almost every article of consumption ('there is no kind of foodstuff which does not pay the excise'), and merchants could self-declare customs values with no check, so 'the rich evaded them or could stand the burden better.' Braudel's own aside — 'Can a more systematically unjust society or state possibly be imagined?' — is uncharacteristically blunt, and pointedly followed by the observation that even a 50,000-strong state customs service changed nothing structural.

**Commerce as raison d'état.** The unit's final movement catalogues cases foreign observers treated as scandalous betrayals — Dutch capital backing rival East India companies in England, Denmark, Sweden, France, and Ostend; investment in French privateering against Dutch ships; grain shipped to a starving, warring France in 1693–94 under neutral or even Dutch flags despite official prohibitions — and reframes them as consistent with a single logic: 'for the Dutch, commerce was king, and in Holland commercial interests effectively replaced raison d'état.' Pieter de la Court's 'Commerce desires to be free' and La Thuillerie's 'Gain is the sole and unique compass by which these people are guided' are offered as native confirmations of what French critics denounced as national faithlessness.

## Notable passages

The opening historiographical framing (Klein, Jeannin, the Portuguese envoy, Turgot) sets up the 'weak state' consensus that the rest of the unit works to complicate, ending on the pointed question of whether the same judgment even holds for Holland as it might for eighteenth-century Venice.

The regents passage quantifies the oligarchy Braudel treats as the real seat of power — 'perhaps 2000 regents' controlling every lever of the state and the VOC alike — against B. M. Vlekke's higher estimate of 10,000, which Braudel accepts only if it counts entire families rather than officeholders.

The hinge passage on the 1650 'republicans' documents the pivot from Golden Age austerity to rentier luxury in the regents' own economic behavior, ending on the image of a class 'cut off from the rest of society' — the clearest statement of internal social costs to Dutch dominance.

The indirect-tax passage carries Braudel's sharpest editorializing — merchants' privileged self-declaration at customs, a state customs service of 50,000 that fixed nothing — and the rhetorical question that gives the tax section its edge.

The de Witt–Pomponne passage is where Braudel speaks in his own voice ('I do not have the impression, from a careful study of their relations, that the Dutchman felt the slightest inferiority complex') to argue that Dutch statecraft matched Louis XIV's France on equal footing — his positive proof of state capacity, set against the 'weak state' consensus opened at the top of the unit.

The closing passage on commerce as raison d'état assembles the unit's most concrete evidence — de la Court, La Thuillerie, and the VOC directors' 1644 claim that conquered strongholds were the 'property of private merchants' rather than national conquests — for the thesis that in Holland, commercial interest itself performed the function a sovereign state's reason of state would elsewhere perform.
