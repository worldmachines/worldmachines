# Spain, Portugal, and the American Periphery: Marginalization, Smuggling, and a Society Neither Feudal Nor Capitalist

This unit closes Braudel's treatment of the Americas by arguing that Latin America's colonial condition is best described not as 'exploitation' but as structural marginalization within the European world-economy: Spain proved unable to exploit its own American empire unaided, so the rest of Europe seized the benefit through massive, semi-institutionalized smuggling; the Bourbon monarchy tried, late and only partially successfully, to reclaim control; Braudel then attempts a rough quantitative estimate of the 'treasure' actually extracted; and the section ends with independence delivering Latin America not to freedom but to a new dependency on the City of London, before Braudel takes up the long historiographical argument that neither 'feudalism' nor 'capitalism' adequately describes American colonial society.

## Themes

**Marginalization, not exploitation.** Braudel opens by rejecting the textbook binary of 'settler colonies' versus 'exploited colonies' as unable to capture reality (every settler colony also exploited, every exploited colony also had settlers). His preferred term is marginalization: the condition, within a world-economy, of a zone being told what to do by an international division of labour it does not control. This is Braudel's periphery concept doing real analytical work — Latin America occupies the same structural position after independence as before it, since one master (Iberia) is simply replaced by another (England).

**Spain as intermediary, not exploiter.** Because Spain's own industry declined from the late sixteenth century, she could not supply the 'colossal' American market alone and had to let the rest of Europe in — contemporaries called Spain 'hardly more than an entrepot for foreigners.' The real transfer of American wealth ran through massive, durable smuggling networks: Dutch traders 'at pikestaff's length' from Curaçao, English from Jamaica, French from Saint-Domingue, Scots at Darien, even North American whalers off Peru, plus a huge contraband river of Potosí silver diverted into Portuguese Brazil. Braudel treats this smuggling as an entire parallel economy, at times exceeding official trade in volume for over a century. This dynamic reads as a legibility project undone by its own subjects: Spain's licensed ports, convoy fleets, and laws against 'transporting' silver were an attempt to render the Atlantic economy legible and controllable from Madrid — see [[legibility-as-machine-core]] — but the actual traffic dissolved into a diffuse, adaptive counter-system that the Crown could never fully see, let alone govern. The resonance is partial: this is a mercantile-era control apparatus, not the mature bureaucratic Modernity Machine, and the 'counter-system' here is profit-seeking evasion rather than anything like liveness or autonomy-seeking self-organization.

**The Bourbon reconquista of empire.** From 1713 on, Madrid moved to take the empire back in hand: a Ministry of the Navy and the Indies, chartered companies, the transfer of the monopoly from Seville to Cadiz, the 1778 decree of free trade among Spanish ports, and the 1776 viceroyalty of Buenos Aires (which choked Rio de la Plata smuggling). The intendant system and the 1767 suppression of the Jesuits are read by Braudel as deliberate moves to break Creole aristocratic power, installing a harder, more 'military' colonial order — one that outlasted Spanish rule and was inherited by the newly independent states. The reforms even survived two major rebellions, Tupac Amaru (Peru, 1780) and the Comunidades (Venezuela, 1781).

**The treasure of treasures, quantified.** Braudel attempts real numbers: using Jose Maria Quiros's 1810 figures for New Spain and Coatsworth's estimates for Brazil, he arrives at a rough hemispheric GNP near 1,000 million pesos, with per capita income (50 pesos for Spanish America, versus roughly 151 for the United States) far below the northern colonies. Net treasure flowing to Europe he puts at roughly £6 million a year — several times what all of Europe drew from India in the same period — while stressing that the official figures, which exclude contraband, are a floor, not a ceiling. This is Braudel's own quantitative historiography in miniature: fragile, admittedly hypothetical numbers used anyway, because a bounded wrong estimate is more useful than none.

**From Spanish to English dependency.** Independence (1810s–1820s) did not free Latin America; it transferred it from Iberian political rule to English financial rule, as British capital rushed into the ex-Spanish and ex-Portuguese states from 1825 and Latin American fortunes came to be quoted on the London Stock Exchange. Braudel treats this as confirmation that the region's peripheral position was structural, not merely colonial-administrative: even the United States, he notes, struggled for decades to extricate itself from a similar financial subordination to London.

**Neither feudalism nor capitalism.** The closing section is explicitly methodological. Braudel surveys the long argument over whether Spanish/Portuguese America was 'feudal,' 'capitalist,' transitional, or (per B. H. Slicher van Bath) neither, and sides with the last position while pushing further: American society was a mosaic of coexisting, non-reducible forms — semi-closed subsistence economies, partially-open ones, and, at the top, mines, plantations, and wholesale trade, where capitalism existed only as one commercial echelon (the aviadores of mining towns, consulado merchants) tightly bound into the European world-economy rather than an autonomous national capitalism. Encomiendas gesture toward feudalism in theory but functioned as 'livings' captured by local power in practice; haciendas mixed subsistence and market accounting; plantations were capitalist only in the person of the planter and his financiers. This refusal to force a single European teleology onto a genuinely mixed, co-existing set of social forms rhymes with [[Configurancy]]'s picture of worlds and forms co-emerging relationally rather than converging on one predetermined model — though Braudel's mosaic is a static hierarchy of coexisting types rather than an ongoing generative process, and the rhyme should not be pushed past that.

## Notable passages

The opening redefinition of the colonial relation as 'marginalization... within a world-economy' rather than simple exploitation sets the terms for everything that follows. The 'entrepot for foreigners' passage captures the paradox that Spain legally owned the silver but structurally could not keep its benefit. The Corregidors-and-Excisemen smuggling anecdote (Lima to Panama, 1692) shows corruption as a routine, load-bearing feature of the system rather than an aberration. The treasure comparison — roughly £6 million a year from America versus £1.3 million from all of India — is Braudel's clearest attempt to put a number on the 'treasure of treasures.' And the closing 'neither feudalism nor capitalism' passage is the section's thesis statement: American society as a hierarchy of incommensurable forms, with capitalism confined to its uppermost commercial layer.
