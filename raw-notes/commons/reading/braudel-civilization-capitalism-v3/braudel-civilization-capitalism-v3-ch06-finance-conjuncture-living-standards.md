# Finance, the Conjuncture, and the Price of Progress

This unit closes out Braudel's chapter on the British industrial revolution in three movements. First, a re-periodization of finance capitalism that refuses to let early-twentieth-century corporate finance monopolize the term, tracing England's own financial revolution — Bank of England, country banks, joint-stock banks, the Clearing House — running alongside, not after, industrialization. Second, a methodological digression on how British and French historiography read the short-term "conjuncture" differently (national causation versus a world-economy-wide exogenous force), tested against Kondratieff-cycle price data for Britain and France. Third, an argument, built on the Phelps Brown–Hopkins wage series and contemporary eyewitnesses, that Britain's material progress in 1760–1815 was purchased through a real decline in workers' living standards — closing with Braudel stepping out of historical narration to wonder, from inside the 1972–4 crisis, whether the world was seeing an ordinary Kondratieff downswing or a reversal of the secular trend itself.

## Themes

**Finance capitalism is a recurring late stage of capital accumulation, not modern capitalism's final form.** Braudel takes up Hilferding's "unholy trinity" — industrial capitalism as God the Father, commercial capitalism the Son, finance capitalism the Holy Ghost penetrating everything else — but rejects treating finance capitalism as a unique invention of the 1900s corporate economy. Finance capital "did not wait for the triumph of the City of London to come into being": in Genoa and in Amsterdam, once commercial capitalism generated capital beyond the normal channels for investment, finance capitalism already took over "all the activities of the business world," for a while. This is the same logic behind Braudel's succession of dominant centres across the volume (Venice, Antwerp, Genoa, Amsterdam, London): each dominant city-economy cycles from a commercial phase into a financial one, a pattern England repeats on a national scale.

**England's banking system grew from local, improvised credit relationships into a national structure anchored by the Bank of England (founded 1694).** Country banks were often just "an extra counter" opened inside an established milling, ironmongering, or wool-merchant business to meet local credit needs; their numbers swelled and periodically collapsed (the South Sea Bubble, 1793, 1816) because the Bank of England functioned, unofficially, as "lender of last resort." Joint-stock banks after 1826 and admission to the London Clearing House in 1854 completed the system. Braudel stresses continuity over novelty: Rubichon's 1811 description of the Clearing House — forty London cashiers netting the kingdom's payments each evening — restates, at daily rhythm, exactly what the old fairs of Lyon or Besançon-Piacenza did four times a year. The infrastructure intensifies in tempo without changing in kind.

**British and French historians read the short-term "conjuncture" differently, and Braudel wants both readings held together.** French historians (Labrousse, Villar, Baehrel, Meuvret) treat the conjuncture as an exogenous force that "determines the processes which accompany it"; British historians (Ashton and others) explain the same swings by national events — 1778–91 price stagnation as the American war's doing, not an "international intercycle." Braudel grants Ashton's list of genuine short-term drivers (war, harvests, the trade cycle, the money supply, the London Stock Exchange as a self-triggering "seismograph") but insists the price graphs of Britain, Germany, the US and France "dip together" in a way no purely national account explains.

**Britain functioned as the "sounding-board" and core of a world-economy whose climate it only partly authored.** The climate that raised or lowered English prices was governed by "world time," not a time-scale peculiar to England — London may have been the epicentre, even in part dictating that time, but "the rest of the world helped to shape and determine" the movement. Shared climate is not shared experience, though: a conjunctural crisis "does not and cannot have the same impact on the strong and the weak," typically "making the strong stronger and the weak weaker" — Braudel's core/periphery logic of the world-economy operating even within one shared cyclical rhythm.

**Material "progress" in the first industrial revolution was bought directly against workers' real wages and living standards.** Using the Phelps Brown–Hopkins stonemasons' wage series, Braudel argues that rising prices between 1760 and 1810–20 tracked a fall in real wages: "under the conditions of the ancien regime, progress was always at the expense of the living standards of the workers." He backs the statistic with eyewitness testimony — the French prisoner-of-war Commander Pillet's account of English factories "without work" and workers paid in unsold goods sold at "two-thirds of their real value"; Louis Simond's observation that Glasgow cotton workers' wages had fallen to "a quarter what they were nineteen years ago" while prices doubled. Braudel's verdict, "two generations were sacrificed to the creation of an industrial base," extends past labor into military recruitment, where common soldiers and sailors are described as "the scum of the earth" — England's true sub-proletariat.

**Long secular cycles of growth and well-being trade off against each other across centuries, and Braudel treats this as a genuine, if unexplained, historical rhythm.** Following Wilhelm Abel, he traces alternations back to the twelfth century: population and output rising while well-being falls (the sixteenth century, the eighteenth century), against population and output falling while survivors' well-being improves (1350–1450, the post-1650 "seventeenth-century crisis"). Only after 1850 do population, prices, GNP, and wages seem to rise together in a "secular trend" of sustained growth — until Braudel, writing from inside the 1972–4 crisis, wonders whether that trend itself might be reversing. This is Braudel functioning almost as his own oracular forecaster, reading a live crisis through deep, only partly-explained historical rhythms — a stance that half-resonates with [[world-machines]]'s ambition to read a present transition against the longue durée, though the resemblance only goes so far: Braudel is explicit that the mechanism behind these cycles is a mystery he can only gesture at, where WMP aims at a more mechanistic, falsifiable Spine-and-Gate account rather than a hunch. The banking system's growth (local, distributed, improvised country banks cohering only gradually into a national structure anchored by the Bank of England) also loosely echoes a [[history-machine]]-style assembly — a paradigm accreting from independent components until it becomes visible as a system — though here the "lurch into visibility" is institutional and financial rather than a shift in historiographic paradigm.

## Notable passages

The Hilferding passage (c1) is the theoretical spine of the finance section: Braudel accepts the "unholy trinity" image only to historicize it, insisting the financial phase is a recurring feature of capital accumulation wherever it outgrows its investment channels, not a twentieth-century novelty.

The Clearing House passage (c2) is Braudel's clearest demonstration-by-analogy: an admiring contemporary's description of London's daily netting mechanism is, structurally, the same mechanism as the old provincial fairs, just run at a faster clock speed — a small case study in how financial infrastructure evolves by intensifying rather than replacing itself.

The conjuncture passages (c3, c4) carry the chapter's central historiographic argument. c3 states the French/British fork directly, with a concrete dueling example (1778–91 price stagnation read as intercycle versus as war-effect). c4 gives Braudel's own resolution: Britain's price climate is "world time," with London as an epicentre embedded in, not separate from, a wider world-economy.

The Pillet/Simond passage (c5) is where the chapter's statistical claim about falling real wages gets its human weight — contemporary testimony of unsellable goods, underpaid cotton workers, and Braudel's own summary verdict that "two generations were sacrificed."

The closing passage (c6) is Braudel breaking frame to speak in his own voice about a crisis he is living through (1972–4), asking whether it is an ordinary Kondratieff downswing or a reversal of the secular trend — the passage that most directly anticipates the kind of long-cycle, admittedly uncertain forecasting WMP itself attempts.
