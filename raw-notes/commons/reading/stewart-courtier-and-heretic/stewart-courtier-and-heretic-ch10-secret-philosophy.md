# God, or Nature: Spinoza's Secret Philosophy of the Whole of Things

This chapter is Stewart's expository set-piece on the *Ethics*. It opens from Leibniz's one-sheet notes on his talks with Tschirnhaus, which already name the signature doctrines ("God alone is substance," "mind is the very idea of the body"). It then rebuilds Spinoza's system under the three headings the book keeps reusing to set its two philosophers side by side: God, Mind, Salvation. Stewart lets Spinoza speak in his own formulas at the decisive points. There are the Short Treatise's "Man is a part of Nature and must follow its laws, and this alone is true worship", the identity of "Thinking substance and extended substance", the claim that "Nobody as yet has learned from experience what the body can and cannot do", and the two propositions that a loving God is not to be expected. The chapter closes by crowning Spinoza the inaugurator of an "active form of modernity". It then turns to Leibniz, who faces the same questions with "a mind with tastes and proclivities very much its own."

## Themes

**God is Nature, and Nature is fully intelligible.** Stewart frames the seventeenth-century problem as God's *function*. If mechanical science can explain everything, the providential, miracle-working God "would be out of a job." Spinoza's answer is to deny the conflict: "God, or Nature." God "is the immanent cause of things, and not the transitive cause". God is inside the world the way a circle's nature is inside its roundness, not outside it like a watchmaker. One Substance with infinite attributes exists, everything else is a mode of it, and everything follows necessarily. Stewart qualifies the determinism: from any human vantage "everything must always seem contingent." Underneath all of it is a declaration of total intelligibility: "there is nothing that cannot be known—even if we do not necessarily know everything." This is the book's purest statement of the confidence that [[legibility-as-machine-core]] names. Spinoza states it as metaphysics and a form of worship, though, not as an instrument of administration.

**The mind-body problem is dissolved by denying man's special kingdom.** In two block quotations, the Cartesian premise and Spinoza's rejection of it appear in Spinoza's own words. His opponents "appear to go so far as to conceive of man in Nature as a kingdom within a kingdom". Against them stands the Short Treatise's creed that man "is a part of Nature and must follow its laws." Stewart says that with this proposition Spinoza "drives a stake through the heart of two millennia of religion and philosophy." Having removed the premise, Spinoza must explain how one substance shows itself two ways. He answers with the attributes of Thought and Extension. "Mental decision on the one hand, and the appetite and physical state of the body on the other hand, are simultaneous in nature", one thing named differently under each attribute. Ethics II, Prop. 7 turns this into parallelism. Stewart notes the doctrine's positive weakness candidly: it may be "kicking the mind-body problem upstairs, from humankind to God."

**The body is asked to do everything.** If decisions are just the body's appetites, the body must be able to embody any thought. Spinoza meets the obvious objection head-on: "Nobody as yet has learned from experience what the body can and cannot do," and the human body "surpasses in ingenuity all the constructions of human skill." Stewart reads this as a prophecy of neuroscience three centuries early. It also gives Spinoza a continuum of minds (animals, babies, sleepers, the brain-damaged) where Cartesians faced dilemmas. The costs follow quickly: no free will (the stone that thinks it flies freely), no faculty of will, no self-transparent mind, and no personal immortality.

**Salvation from the conatus, disciplined by reason.** All emotions reduce to pleasure, pain, and the conatus, "the desire to persist in one’s own being". Stewart takes the drive seriously as the engine of [[Conatus]]: "desire is the essence of man," and "Nothing but grim and gloomy superstition forbids enjoyment." Reason orders the passions into virtue and teaches acceptance of necessity, which is closer to amor fati than to resignation. It ends in "the intellectual love of God," an impersonal, timeless union rather than a rewarded afterlife. Stewart sets out the transvaluation plainly: religion trades happiness for virtue, Spinoza says happiness is virtue.

**A God that will not love you back.** The chapter's emotional hinge is a pair of quoted propositions from the Ethics: "It cannot be said that God loves mankind…" and "He who loves God cannot endeavor that God should love him in return." Stewart draws the human cost from them. This God works no miracles for you and shows no concern, so it is fair to ask whether loving it is reasonable, and the road is "difficult and rare."

**Politics before metaphysics; Spinoza as the first modern.** Twice Stewart notes that Spinoza's God and mind are clearer in the negative: no judging God, no immortal soul to be threatened with hell. He infers that the "political commitments would seem to be prior to his philosophy", with the overthrow of theocracy as the project. The closing section uses Nietzsche (philosophy as "a kind of involuntary and unperceived memoir") and Hegel (philosophy as "its own time apprehended in thought", the owl of Minerva rising "at dusk"). Spinoza stood in "a world in flux between the medieval and the modern." That rhymes with a [[gramsci-gap]], but Stewart's Spinoza does not suffer the interregnum. He is the one figure who already embraces the new order, "a secular, liberal, democratic order a full century" early.

## Notable passages

The immanent/transitive definition (c2) is the technical core that turns "God, or Nature" from a slogan into a claim. The "kingdom within a kingdom" passage (c7) is the chapter's decisive move: the Cartesian premise and its rejection, both in Spinoza's words, and Stewart's "stake through the heart" verdict. The Thought/Extension passage (c8) supplies the mechanism, and the Prop. 7 parallelism line (c3) gives its canonical formula. "Nobody as yet has learned from experience what the body can and cannot do" (c9) is the most forward-looking sentence in the chapter and the textual basis for Stewart's neuroscience claim. The stone-and-free-will passage (c4) pairs Spinoza's determinism with Leibniz's own gloss. The conatus definition (c5) grounds the ethics. The passage on a God that will not love you back (c6) includes the two propositions it rests on, and distils the system into its human stakes: awe without comfort, intelligibility without providence. The closing questions matter for the book's larger argument: "Does Spinoza’s God really deserve the name of God?" These are the questions Leibniz carried to The Hague.
