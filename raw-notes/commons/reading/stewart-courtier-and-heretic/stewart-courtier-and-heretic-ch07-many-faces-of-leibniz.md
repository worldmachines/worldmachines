# The Many Faces of Leibniz

This chapter reconstructs Leibniz's first contact with Spinoza in 1671–72: a period in which Leibniz denounced the anonymous Tractatus Theologico-Politicus in outraged letters to Thomasius, Graevius, Arnauld, and Spitzel, while simultaneously opening a clandestine, cordial correspondence with its author under the pretext of discussing optics. Stewart uses this documented two-facedness to argue that Leibniz was not simply a liar with a hidden Spinozist agenda, but something stranger: a thinker so genuinely multiple that no single "true" motive or position can be extracted from behind his performances. The chapter closes by showing Leibniz, freshly bereaved and trailed by rumors of treachery, departing incognito for Paris to pursue the secret "Egypt Plan."

## Themes

**Public denunciation, private courtship.** Leibniz's correspondence forms a deliberate hall of mirrors. He calls the Tractatus "intolerably impudent" to Thomasius, "the terrifying work on the liberty of philosophizing" to Arnauld, and urges Spitzel to "savage" it — all while writing directly to "Mr. Spinoza, celebrated doctor and profound philosopher" under an optics pretext, receiving a reply in which Spinoza clearly recognizes the ruse and offers to correspond "in a clandestine way." Each addressee gets a different, incompatible account of how well Leibniz knows the "Jew Spinoza" and what he thinks of his book. Stewart reads this not as ordinary hypocrisy but as symptomatic of something structural in Leibniz's character.

**Ambition and genuine sympathy intertwined.** Stewart resists reducing the courtship to careerism alone (Leibniz as "grand conciliator" of the republic of letters who could not ignore its newest "supernova") or to secret doctrinal affinity alone. Instead he traces real convergences: Leibniz's disdain for scriptural argument in favor of pure reason, his self-description as a "priest of nature," and a proposed "popular religion" of justice and charity in the Tractatus that eerily anticipates the exoteric doctrines Leibniz himself markets as socially "useful." The chapter suggests Leibniz and Spinoza are "two very different faces of the same philosophical coin."

**The Wedderkopf letter and the shared root in reason.** The chapter's philosophical center is Leibniz's May 1671 letter to Wedderkopf, written the same month he read the Tractatus, in which he follows the logic of divine omniscience and omnipotence to the "hard conclusion" that God is "the absolute author of all," including the sins of a Pontius Pilate. This is essentially Spinoza's own doctrine that "nothing happens in Nature that does not follow from her laws," arrived at independently through Leibniz's own commitment to the principle of sufficient reason — the demand that everything, without exception, have a reason, on pain of undermining the very proof of God's existence. This drive to render the whole of existence fully accountable to reason, with no residue of brute unexplained fact, is a philosophical ancestor of the totalizing, order-seeking impulse the World Machines framework calls [[legibility-as-machine-core]]: Leibniz's God, like a modern administrative apparatus, is not permitted to leave anything outside the chain of explanation. Leibniz later scrawled "I later corrected this" in the letter's margin, but Stewart treats the marginal note as evidence of anxiety, not resolution: correcting the *appearance* of Spinozism would occupy the rest of his career.

**Multiplicity, not duplicity.** Stewart's key interpretive move is to reject both the prosecutorial reading (Leibniz the "self-serving careerist," per Bertrand Russell) and the apologetic reading (Leibniz the misunderstood public-spirited visionary, per Rescher and Mercer). Motives cannot be sorted into a stable hierarchy of "real" versus "cover" because, Stewart argues, no such hierarchy exists: ambition, truth-seeking, public spirit, and vanity are simultaneously present and mutually implicated, with each layer of unmasking revealing another rather than a stable core. He borrows Lewis White Beck's line that Leibniz was "all things to all men" and therefore "no one thing to everybody."

**The monad's irony.** The chapter ends the biographical material with a pointed philosophical joke: Leibniz would go on to build a metaphysics whose fundamental unit, the monad, is defined by unity, permanence, and total immunity to outside influence — an explicit rejection of Spinozist substance-monism. Yet the man who founded philosophy on indivisible unity was himself, biographically, "incomparably fragmented, multiplicitous, exposed to the influence of others, and impossible to pin down." The irony frames Leibniz's entire life's project as an attempt to secure, at the level of metaphysics, a wholeness he could not locate in himself.

## Notable passages

The optics pretext that opens the correspondence — "Illustrious and most honored Sir... your remarkable skill in optics" — shows Leibniz's diplomatic tradecraft in miniature: an ostensibly technical letter engineered to open a channel he could not admit to wanting, routed through an intermediary "Mr. Diemerbroek, lawyer" so no direct trail existed.

The Wedderkopf letter passage on sufficient reason ("it is necessary to refer everything to some reason, and we cannot stop until we have arrived at a first cause") is the chapter's philosophical hinge: it demonstrates, in Leibniz's own words and independent of any acknowledged debt to Spinoza, that his mature rationalism was always one inference away from the very necessitarianism he spent his career disavowing.

Stewart's methodological reflection on motive-hunting ("Guessing motives... is not just risky... one will find not a 'mean' spirit, but no spirit at all") and his formal definition of Leibniz's "multiplicity" ("not that he did not always tell the truth, but that he was... constitutionally... incapable of telling the truth") together constitute the chapter's thesis statement, reframing the entire biographical puzzle as a problem of metaphysics rather than ethics.

Finally, the monad passage ("How could a monad be so multifarious, not to say nefarious?") crystallizes the chapter's central irony in a single rhetorical question, linking the biographical portrait directly forward to the technical philosophy Leibniz would spend the rest of the book developing.
