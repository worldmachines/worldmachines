# Surviving Spinoza

Leibniz reaches provincial Hanover in December 1676 and at once does two things. He runs a courtier's campaign for salary, title and schemes, and he privately reverses his verdict on the man he has just visited. The chapter braids these two threads. It also reconstructs Spinoza's death and the clandestine printing of his works. Unlike most of the book, it relies heavily on long primary-source quotations: Schuller's letters offering and then withdrawing the *Ethics* manuscript, Leibniz's breezy letter to Gallois, the "precipice" confession in *On Freedom*, the dialogue of Theophile and Polidore, the 1680 identification of Spinoza's doctrine as the "proton pseudos", and the 1683 letter to Count Ernst von Hessen-Rheinfels. Stewart places them so that Leibniz's public voice and his private one can be heard against each other.

## Themes

**The courtier's hustle as counterpoint.** Leibniz haggles his salary up to 500 and then 600 thalers. He writes memoranda proposing a demographic survey, a bureau d'addresse, department stores, widows' insurance and an Academy of the Sciences, which makes him "the first apostle of the welfare state". Above all he sells the Harz windmill scheme. By 1683 it is "two years behind schedule and 800 percent over budget", and the miners call him "a dangerous man and evil to deal with." This is the worldly counterweight to the metaphysical drama.

**An almost instantaneous reversal.** A note dated December 12, 1676 was written either on arrival or in the carriage from Holland. It already concludes that "a God of the kind in which the pious believe would not be possible" if all possibles exist. Marginalia on Spinoza's letters go further: "Therefore moral philosophy is destroyed." Stewart's thesis rests on this chronology. Everything later works out a judgment formed at the meeting itself.

**Spinoza's death, the manuscript market, and the fear of exposure.** Colerus refutes the rumours of recantation and poison. Stewart then identifies "L.M.", the doctor who attended Spinoza and hurried off to Amsterdam as the ducatoon and silver knife went missing, as Georg Hermann Schuller, "Leibniz's bungling, two-timing, uncredentialed, alchemical friend" and "the only suspect in the theft". Schuller's letters show what came next. Five days after the death Schuller offers "The manuscript of the Ethics, in the author’s hand—the same one you saw at his house", "for sale, provided the price (150 guilders, I believe) corresponds with the dignity of the object." Leibniz plans to pay out of the ducal library budget without telling the Duke. Four weeks later Schuller reverses himself ("I have changed my mind entirely") and claims credit for a consensus to publish. Stewart doubts the claim: between the two letters Rieuwertsz had already received Spinoza's manuscripts in Amsterdam. Leibniz's alarm then turns to his own letters among Spinoza's papers.

**Two voices.** In public Leibniz is detached. To Gallois he writes: "Spinoza died this winter. I saw him when passing through Holland, and I spoke with him many times and at great length. He has a strange metaphysics, full of paradoxes…." In private he plots a secret trip to Amsterdam, sends "panicky letters" to Schuller, and could have stopped the publication with "A word to Steno" but stays silent. When the *Opera Posthuma* arrives and prints his 1671 letter, he is "beside himself". His fifteen pages of notes on the *Ethics* are contemptuous ("It seems that our author’s mind was most tortuous"). Stewart reads them as sincere precisely because they were never meant to be read.

**Confession and recovery.** *On Freedom* (1678/79) admits the lapse outright. Leibniz writes: "I found myself very close to the opinions of those who hold everything to be absolutely necessary…. But I was pulled back from this precipice by considering those possible things which neither are nor will be nor have been." Stewart dwells on the metaphor: a precipice, a fall, a pulling back. He links it to the *New Essays*' "leaned to the side of the Spinozists" and the pseudonym Theophile, and treats the whole as the language of recovery ("Spinozists Anonymous"). In the 1678 dialogue, Polidore voices a Spinozism stripped of enchantment: God "cares not at all for what we call justice and that he takes pleasure in destruction…. Individuals must give way; there is room only for the species." Yet Polidore, not Theophile, speaks the closing creed ("I see that virtue and honor are not chimeras…let us love this God who so loved us"). Stewart takes this as the sign that the doubting self is the real Leibniz.

**Descartes as proxy, Spinoza as atheism's source.** The sudden 1677 attack on Descartes's "dangerous idea, that matter assumes all forms of which it is capable successively" becomes clear by 1680. That doctrine means a universe where nothing is "so absurd nor so bizarre nor contrary to that which we call justice" that it will not happen. "Spinoza explained more clearly" this thought, which Leibniz calls "the proton pseudos [first lie] and foundation of the atheistic philosophy." Stewart's gloss: "The problem with Descartes, in a word, is Spinoza." He adds that Leibniz is perhaps the first to treat atheism as a philosophical condition of modernity rather than a charge of debauchery.

**Ambivalence hardening into denial.** The 1683 letter to Ernst swings three ways. Leibniz opposes "fire and iron", then hesitates over "Vanini and Spinosa", then writes "I do not yet dare to decide if one has the right to sentence them to the ultimate punishment." Finally he steels himself and belittles Spinoza. He "was truly Atheist", "The God he puts on parade is not like ours", "what he knew best was to make lenses for microscopes", and Leibniz "conversed with him for a few hours while passing through The Hague." Stewart lines these words up against the Gallois letter ("many times and at great length") and against the documented purpose of the trip. He adds Leibniz's forty-year silence about Schuller's role at the deathbed.

## Notable passages

The December 12 note (c1) is the chronological linchpin. The death scene (c2) builds a quiet mystery out of chicken broth, church services and missing valuables. Schuller's offer and retraction (c7) shows Leibniz ready to buy a dead heretic's manuscript on his employer's account. The Amsterdam publishing effort (c3) was a distributed, undercredited assembly of a body of thought that "careened forth" against a coordinated Vatican dragnet. It fits the [[history-machine]] pattern, though here the assembly was deliberate and conspiratorial rather than silent. It also resembles a [[The Thicket]]-like defence of a way of thinking against capture ([[capture-resistance]]). The marginalia (c4) and the "precipice" confession (c8) are the private record of attraction and recoil. The Polidore creed (c9) and Stewart's reading of it (c5) give the divided self. The 1680 "proton pseudos" passage (c10) is where Leibniz names Spinoza, not Descartes, as the problem. The 1683 letter (c6, c11) shows him first unable to condemn Spinoza to death and then writing him down to "a few hours" and lens-grinding. He kept up that minimization for the rest of his life.
