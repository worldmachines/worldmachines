# Bento

Chapter 2 is Stewart's biographical portrait of Spinoza's first twenty-four years: born into a Sephardic merchant family that had fled the Portuguese Inquisition for the tolerant Dutch Republic, young Bento passed through an intensive Talmudic education, absorbed Descartes and the Latin classics under the disreputable freethinker Frans van den Enden, quietly formed heretical convictions about a corporeal God and a mortal soul, and was, in 1656, excommunicated from Amsterdam's Portuguese-Jewish community in one of the harshest cherem ever issued. The chapter argues that this expulsion — not any single doctrine — is the hinge of Spinoza's life and thought: it cast him into a "double exile," heretic to the Jews and Jew to the Christians, and it was from that marginal vantage that his mature philosophy of a stripped-down, undespotic God and a secular tolerant state took shape.

## Themes

**Refuge and commercial tolerance as the precondition for heresy.** Stewart spends the chapter's opening third narrating the Spinoza family's flight from Ferdinand and Isabella's 1492 expulsion edict, the Portuguese Inquisition's pursuit of "conversos," and their eventual settlement in Amsterdam. He is emphatic that Dutch toleration of Jews was not chiefly idealistic: "Behind Dutch tolerance of their new neighbors was something perhaps more durable than the love of liberty, namely, an enlightened understanding of commercial self-interest." This is the material soil from which Spinoza's heresy becomes possible — a mercantile, religiously plural [[divergence-machine]]-like Amsterdam of "freedom of Speculation and Enquiry" in which a Jewish merchant's son can encounter Descartes, bookshops, and an ex-Jesuit tutor without being burned for it, even as that same freedom has firm limits once he crosses them.

**The making of a heretic: education, van den Enden, and Descartes.** Bento's rigorous Hebrew schooling under Rabbi Morteira gives him the tools (and, Stewart notes with irony, the biblical familiarity) he later turns against orthodoxy. The real rupture comes through Frans van den Enden — "Bookseller, Latinist, medical doctor, amateur thespian, champion of radical democracy... accused of 'sowing the seeds of atheism'" — whose household and Latin school open Bento to Descartes and the wider republic of letters. Descartes's own maxim, "that nothing ought to be admitted as True, but that which has been proved by good and solid reasons," becomes the acid that dissolves Bento's inherited Bible and eventually Descartes's own philosophy too.

**Double exile as philosophical vantage point.** The chapter's real argument is that excommunication did not just alter Spinoza's biography but generated his metaphysics and politics. Expelled first by the Jews and then permanently marked as a Jew among Christians, Spinoza occupied a position of radical marginality that Stewart treats as epistemically productive: standing at "the extreme margins of society," he could see "that the old God was dying and that his theocratic rule on earth was crumbling." This is squarely a [[gramsci-gap]] observation — the old theocratic legitimacy expiring while a new, secular-tolerant order (Spinoza's own [[modernity-machine]]-adjacent vision of a state answerable only to reason) has not yet been born, and Spinoza's philosophy is offered as the interregnum's proposed remedy.

**Character as a bundle of paradoxes rooted in self-sufficient striving.** Stewart's closing pages read Spinoza's personality — his icy sarcasm toward Morteira, his refusal of a thousand-guilder bribe to recant, his serene indifference to the verdict — as continuous with the metaphysics he would later write. The passage on his "innate sense of superiority" and "clinical level of self-sufficiency," and the "fierce rejection of any authority that did not emanate entirely from within," previews (without yet naming) the doctrine of [[Conatus]]: a self grounded and asserting itself from its own nature rather than from external or received authority, refusing "submission to an external power" as a matter of temperament well before it becomes a matter of doctrine.

**Freak or world-historical figure — a historiographical tension.** The chapter opens by framing Spinoza as caught between two readings: "the kind of monster our dear Holland produces" to medieval-minded contemporaries, or "an extraordinary individual—the kind of person who can change history" to modern observers, and insists Leibniz held both views simultaneously. This ambivalence about whether Spinoza is explained by impersonal historical conditions (Iberian persecution, Dutch commerce, the Amsterdam book trade) or by singular individual genius echoes the [[history-machine]] question of paradigms that assemble silently from distributed conditions versus "great man" narratives that credit a single lurching personality — Stewart's biography self-consciously refuses to resolve the tension.

## Notable passages

The chapter's dramatic center is the reconstructed interrogation scene in which two supposed friends press Bento to reveal his real views, and he answers with what Stewart calls uncharacteristic guilelessness: he confesses that Scripture nowhere rules out a corporeal God, that spirits are "mere phantoms," and that the soul's immortality has no scriptural support. This is the confession that triggers the rumor campaign, the assassination attempt, and ultimately the synagogue tribunal — the philosophical content of the chapter is delivered almost entirely through this one piece of reported dialogue rather than through exposition.

Stewart frames the 1656 cherem itself as the chapter's structural hinge, moving from "secondhand accounting" to "a piece of solid fact" once the actual excommunication document enters the narrative, and he uses the comparatively mild fate of Spinoza's associate Juan de Prado (who recanted) as a foil that measures just how uncompromising Spinoza's own refusal was — refusing a bribe of a thousand guilders because accepting it "would make him a hypocrite." The chapter closes on Spinoza's reported serenity at the verdict, comparing his expulsion to the Exodus, and on Stewart's own summary judgment that "he rejected the orthodoxy of his day not because he believed less, but because he believed more" — the line that sets up the double-exile thesis carried into the rest of the book.
