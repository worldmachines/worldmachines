# The Hero of the People

Stewart opens with the legend of Masaniello, the Neapolitan fisherman who led a ten-day popular uprising against Spanish rule in 1647 before being murdered and decapitated by his own former followers, then reveals that Spinoza secretly sketched himself in Masaniello's iconic costume — net, fisherman's shirt, smoldering eyes — evidence of a self-image the chapter uses to frame Spinoza's transformation, via the 1670 publication of the *Tractatus Theologico-Politicus* (TTP), from a private metaphysician into a self-appointed leader of a "global revolution" against theocratic power, a role that provoked a continent-wide firestorm of condemnation and exposed a deep tension in his own philosophy between self-interest and self-sacrifice.

## Themes

**The Masaniello self-portrait as a hinge in Spinoza's self-conception.** The chapter's central image — Colerus's report that Spinoza's charcoal sketch of the rebel fisherman "bore a striking resemblance" to Spinoza himself, and van der Spyck's insistence that the resemblance was deliberate — marks what Stewart calls "a subtle yet decisive transformation in the self-image as well as the public image" of a man whose earlier life had been organized around solitary, cloistered meditation. The passion for freedom and longing for glory, present all along but sublimated, becomes overt once Spinoza takes up the pen (and brush) as a revolutionary rather than only a metaphysician.

**From private heresy to public political manifesto.** Stewart traces the genesis of the TTP through the 1665 Voorburg pastor controversy (where Spinoza is denounced by name as an atheist "pernicious element") and the 1668–69 martyrdom of Adriaen and Johannes Koerbagh, whose Spinoza-influenced tracts against clerical deception earned Adriaen a death sentence by imprisonment. Spinoza's stated motives for finally publishing — countering theological prejudice, defending his own reputation against the atheism charge, and vindicating the freedom to philosophize — read at first as defensive, but Stewart argues the underlying agenda was always more radical: not merely protecting philosophy from politics, but making philosophy interfere in politics.

**The TTP's argument: religion as an instrument of despotism.** Spinoza's biblical criticism (multiple human authors, no chosen people, no real miracles, prophets as gifted rhetoricians rather than seers) is in service of a political point: that "the supreme mystery of despotism...is to keep men in a state of deception," using religion's "specious title" to make people "fight for their servitude as if for salvation." Organized religion, in Spinoza's diagnosis, is "the relics of man's ancient bondage," an organized fraud sustaining clerical and monarchical power over a frightened, ignorant populace.

**A secular theory of the state grounded in self-interest — [[Conatus]].** Spinoza inverts the theocratic order (state serves God, clergy interpret God's will) into a modern one: the state serves humankind, and the people direct the state. This rests explicitly on what the *Ethics* names the drive to self-preservation — "no virtue can be conceived prior to this one, namely, the drive to preserve oneself" — the conatus that grounds all legitimate political authority in self-interest rather than divine sanction. From this Spinoza builds a renewable social contract (unlike Hobbes's one-off surrender), an inalienable freedom of conscience, and a preference for democracy as the regime best expressing collective will. Stewart credits this combination as making Spinoza "the first truly modern political philosopher," anticipating the American and French revolutionary traditions. The old theocratic legitimacy was already hollowing out by his time — Stewart notes the "problem of authority" had become "the subject of intense concern" among Hobbes and Machiavelli well before Spinoza wrote — and the TTP names and completes a transition already underway, an interregnum of dying and not-yet-born legitimacy in the spirit of the [[gramsci-gap]].

**The exoteric/esoteric split and state-controlled religion.** Despite grounding legitimacy in individual self-interest, Spinoza does not extend full epistemic freedom to the masses: a "good" popular religion, administered by civil rather than ecclesiastical authority, is "very salutary," even though its doctrines will always be, in the eyes of philosophers, at best half-truths. Spinoza distinguishes an exoteric philosophy (adapted for popular consumption, aimed at desirable political effects) from an esoteric one (reserved for reason's "exclusive fellowship," revealing truth) — a state-administered, doctrinally managed public faith that anticipates a Modernity Machine-style project of rendering belief legible and governable rather than leaving it to unregulated clerical authority.

**Reception: a Europe-wide conflagration.** The TTP's publication under anonymity and a false imprint (Hamburg) did not protect Spinoza once his authorship became an open secret. Dutch synods called it "the most vile and sacrilegious book the world has ever seen"; European critics competed in invective (Huet wished him "covered with chains and whipped with a rod"; Henry More called him "the most impudent of mortals"). Yet the book also circulated widely underground, Bayle noting sarcastically that "all the strong spirits flocked to him," evidence of the divide between public denunciation and private fascination that Spinoza's ideas provoked.

**Caution as prescription, not description — and the paradox of the liberal hero.** Spinoza's signet ring, engraved with a thorny rose and the motto *Caute* ("Caution"), and his own withdrawal of Dutch translations and the *Ethics* from publication, suggest a man aware of danger — yet Stewart insists this caution was aspirational rather than actual: "it never occurred to him to get off the slopes." The chapter closes on the paradox Stewart has been building toward: a philosophy that makes self-preservation the root of all virtue nonetheless produced a man who "brazenly crossed the line that divides self-interest from the common good," risking his life, like Masaniello, for a cause beyond himself. This sets up the question that will matter for Leibniz, who claims a monopoly on political charity: "Can a liberal be a hero?"

## Notable passages

The Masaniello self-portrait passage is the chapter's organizing image — Stewart uses the reported resemblance between Spinoza's face and the sketched rebel's to license the entire chapter's argument that Spinoza had begun to see himself as playing an analogous role: philosopher as martyr-revolutionary for a "civilization-wide struggle for freedom."

The despotism quotation from the TTP's preface is the chapter's clearest statement of Spinoza's diagnosis of religion's political function, and functions as the pivot from Stewart's biographical narrative (Voorburg, the Koerbaghs) into the doctrinal content of the TTP itself.

The "purpose of the state is freedom" paragraph lays out Spinoza's constructive political theory in condensed form — the renewable social contract, the state as guarantor of security enabling free cooperation, and the "lapidary formula" that both echoes and diverges from Hobbes.

The conatus/paradox passage in the chapter's close is the most directly load-bearing for Stewart's argument and for [[Conatus]] as a recognized WMP concept: it states the drive-to-self-preservation doctrine in Spinoza's own words and then shows Spinoza's practice exceeding his own theory, risking death for a cause larger than his individual persistence.

The closing rhetorical question — "Can a liberal be a hero?" — is the chapter's thesis stated as an open problem, explicitly framed by Stewart as a challenge that will recur when the narrative turns to Leibniz's competing claim to political charity.
