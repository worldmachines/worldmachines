# The Return of the Repressed

Stewart closes his comparison of the two philosophers by running Leibniz's system against Spinoza's on the three great questions it structured earlier chapters around — God, Mind, Salvation — and showing that on each one, Leibniz's attempt to preserve a transcendent, choosing, personal God dissolves under its own logical pressure into something Leibniz himself would call Spinozism, or worse, atheism. The chapter's controlling image is Freudian in its title and structural in its argument: what Leibniz represses in his official doctrine keeps resurfacing in his own words, his own notebooks, his own logical commitments, until the distinction between the two philosophies becomes a difference of "meaning or value" rather than of observable fact.

## Themes

**Spinoza pre-empted the Theodicy before it was written.** Stewart opens with a 1674 letter — two years before the two men met — in which Spinoza already dismisses as "quite absurd" the idea that God could have "refrained from creating the world," on the grounds that a God who must have reasons for acting cannot also possess the option not to act. This lets Stewart show that Leibniz's later distinction between "moral necessity" (God chooses the best world because it is good) and "metaphysical necessity" (God could not have done otherwise) is not a private eccentricity but a move Spinoza had already anticipated and rejected as incoherent — a critique later echoed nearly verbatim by Samuel Clarke and Arthur Lovejoy.

**The "higher world" dilemma has only two exits, and both are fatal to transcendence.** Leibniz needs God's choice among possible worlds to occur somewhere — his answer is the "pyramid of all possible worlds" in the Theodicy. But Stewart presses the obvious question: who built the pyramid, the game-table, the constraints by which possibilities are ranked? If God made that higher structure too, there is really only one possible world and "Spinozism" follows; if the higher structure simply exists independently of God, then God is subject to a prior nature and is "no longer God, but just a logical operator" — atheism, or "a form of Spinozism without Spinoza's belief in the divinity of nature." Leibniz is caught regardless of which door he takes.

**Monadology, pressed hard, produces the same dilemma at the level of mind.** The parallel-but-non-interacting harmony of mind and body via pre-established harmony is empirically indistinguishable from Spinoza's parallelism of mind and body as two attributes of one substance — a charge Leibniz's own contemporaries made as early as 1712, calling pre-established harmony a plagiarism of Spinoza. Stewart's sharpest formal move is the "Is God a monad?" dilemma: if God is not a monad, monads are mere dependent modes of the one substance that is God — straight Spinozism; if God is a monad, he cannot interact with or create other monads and everything he does follows with logical necessity from his own concept — meaning "God" is just another determined thing among things, i.e., atheism ("if God is a monad, Leibniz is an atheist"). Personal immortality fares no better: a mind-monad that survives only as a "seed" or a "cinder" bound to some parallel body-monad is a doctrine Leibniz's critics found hollow, and it undercuts the very individuality he was trying to protect from dissolving into Spinoza's undifferentiated substance.

**In ethics, Leibniz reproduces Spinoza's vocabulary almost word for word.** Because each monad "has a conatus of sorts" and wants to "become what it is," Leibniz defines pleasure as "the feeling of an increase in perfection" and freedom as clear rational comprehension unclouded by passions imposed by outside bodies — formulations Bertrand Russell says could be "cribbed from the Ethics." The chapter treats this as the strongest evidence yet that the two systems, whatever their metaphysical packaging, cash out in the same practical and ethical commitments: a determinism that makes prayer pointless, sin a matter of finite nature rather than free choice, and happiness identical with self-realizing rational activity — Spinoza's program under a different name. **Leibniz's philosophy is fundamentally reactive — defined by what it negates, not what it affirms.** Stewart's synthesis is that Leibniz's positive doctrines (immateriality of mind, pre-established harmony, a designed world, a transcendent God) each reduce to an argument that Spinoza has not definitively *disproved* the traditional alternative, never to an independent positive proof. "Nature is not God" is the one negation the whole system exists to protect, "even as the rest of Leibniz's philosophy collapses into something observationally indistinguishable from Spinozism." The chapter's closing metaphor — Spinoza opening a door onto a lawful, all-determining, scientifically legible universe that Leibniz cannot bear to accept as divine, and spending a lifetime trying to shut a door he cannot actually close because he is "already standing on the other side" — frames the 1676 meeting as the moment a new, disenchanted world-picture became visible to a mind that would spend the rest of its career resisting the very thing it had already, in substance, adopted. This is close to a private philosophical instance of the transition WMP calls a [[gramsci-gap]]: the old transcendent, providential order is dead in substance for Leibniz well before he can say so, and his entire mature system is an attempt to hold the door on a legitimacy that has already collapsed. The "law-abiding, all-determining cosmos that is the proper subject of scientific investigation" that both philosophers end up describing is also an early articulation of the world later organized by [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and the [[modernity-machine]] — a universe rendered orderly, ruleful, and knowable, whichever metaphysics is draped over it.

## Notable passages

The chapter opens with an extended analogy — two competitive friends describing the same unnamed city in incompatible terms — that frames the entire argument as a question about whether Leibniz and Spinoza are describing different universes or merely narrating the same one differently: "is philosophy about what you see, or the way you see it?" This governs the chapter's method: rather than adjudicate metaphysical vocabulary, Stewart tests for observable difference and finds none.

The 1674 letter passage does real argumentative work by establishing chronological priority — Spinoza's critique of a "fixed target" God predates his acquaintance with Leibniz's actual views, which strengthens Stewart's claim that the convergence is structural, not personal borrowing in either direction.

The most technically dense passage is the "Is God a monad?" dilemma, which Stewart lays out with a rigor closer to logical proof than to biography — walking through both horns (God as non-monad, God as monad) and showing each terminates in a heresy Leibniz explicitly wanted to avoid, quoting Hegel's summary of the contradiction and Russell's blunt gloss that if God is a monad, Leibniz is an atheist.

The salvation-ethics passage juxtaposing Leibniz's account of pleasure as "an increase in perfection" against Spinoza's Ethics is doing comparative philology as much as philosophy — Stewart wants the reader to notice that the resemblance is not thematic but nearly lexical.

The closing passage reframes the entire book's central 1676 meeting in light of everything just demonstrated: Spinoza "opened a door" onto a reality Leibniz recognized as his own eventual world, but where Spinoza saw a new divinity, Leibniz "saw instead the death of God," and his life's philosophical work became an attempt to shut a door he could never really close because he was, by his own logic, "already standing on the other side."
