# Gottfried: The Making of a Courtier-Philosopher

This chapter traces Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz from his 1646 birth in war-ravaged Leipzig through his childhood prodigy years, his rejected doctoral application, and his meteoric rise as secretary, diplomat, and "God's attorney" in the courts of Mainz — building, in deliberate counterpoint to the previous chapter's portrait of Spinoza, a psychological account of a man whose restlessness, need for patronage, and hunger for total solutions would shape both his statecraft and his metaphysics before he ever met Spinoza in 1676.

## Themes

**The Thirty Years War as the negative space of Leibniz's world.** Stewart opens not with Leibniz but with the catastrophe that preceded him: a German population cut nearly by half, the Holy Roman Empire's authority reduced to "historical fiction," sovereignty splintered among hundreds of petty princes and bishops, and a war-weary continent settling for the "Peace of Exhaustion." This is a world machine in visible collapse — imperial and religious order dissolved into fragments, its old legitimacy dead and nothing coherent yet built to replace it, the very condition a [[gramsci-gap]] names. Leibniz is explicitly framed as a child of this interregnum, born craving the security and unified order that had vanished just before his birth, and his entire career reads as an attempt to rebuild that "glittering empire" from the wreckage.

**Parallel prodigy, opposite temperament.** Stewart repeatedly cross-references Spinoza ("Bento") to sharpen Leibniz's character by contrast: both were schoolboy prodigies who outpaced their teachers, but where Spinoza was reserved and concealed his thoughts, Leibniz broadcast his brilliance without restraint ("I invariably took the first rank in all discussions"). Both suffered a formative rupture in young adulthood — Spinoza's excommunication, Leibniz's rejected doctoral application at Leipzig — but their responses diverge completely: Spinoza withdraws into self-sufficient solitude, while Leibniz "threw himself back at the mercy of society with an even greater desperation to succeed." Stewart treats this divergence as the psychological seed of their later philosophical systems.

**Politics and metaphysics as a single deductive craft.** Leibniz's rise under Baron von Boineburg and the Elector of Mainz shows philosophy and statecraft fused: he defends a candidate for the Polish throne through chains of quasi-geometrical propositions borrowed from Plato and Epicurus, and later drafts "Catholic Demonstrations" defending transubstantiation and the trinity on behalf of a patron who needs the arguments more than he holds the convictions. His juridical training becomes a totalizing method — he becomes, in Stewart's phrase, "God's attorney," litigating metaphysical questions with the same tools he'd use for a Polish succession dispute. His youthful tract on the "universal characteristic" — a symbolic logic meant to reduce all philosophical disputes to mechanical calculation — is the purest expression of this impulse: a dream of total legibility for thought itself, prefiguring the [[legibility-as-machine-core]] ambition that Stewart will later associate with the broader modern project.

**The Egypt Plan and the psychology of the single master-stroke.** Leibniz's vision on the Rhine — diverting Louis XIV's aggression into a crusade against Egypt, thereby securing Germany, uniting Europe, and reviving medieval Christendom all at once — crystallizes a recurring pattern Stewart identifies: Leibniz as "one of history's greatest multitaskers, a master of solving any number of problems at a single stroke." The chapter explicitly ties this compulsion for total, unifying solutions back to the trauma of the Leipzig rejection and, more deeply, to the question "How can life be so unfair?" — which Stewart argues becomes, in Leibniz's mature philosophy, the problem of theodicy: "Why is there evil?"

**Restlessness elevated into metaphysical principle.** Leibniz's inability to stay still — moving from patron to patron, city to city — is not mere temperament in Stewart's account but the germ of a philosophical doctrine: "The human mind cannot rest... To be motionless... is to torment the mind." The chapter notes that Leibniz would later "explicitly identify the principle of activity in all things with the soul itself," tracing a direct line from the young courtier's compulsive itinerancy to the mature theory of monads as centers of ceaseless perceptual activity.

## Notable passages

The chapter's opening paragraphs on the Thirty Years War establish the devastation and institutional collapse — 21 million Germans reduced to 13 million, mercenary armies spreading plague and famine — that forms the backdrop against which Leibniz's lifelong hunger for order must be read.

The account of the Leipzig doctoral rejection ("His application was refused") is staged as the chapter's psychological hinge, deliberately mirrored against Spinoza's excommunication from the previous chapter: both men suffer an early, formative expulsion, but Leibniz's response — packing his bags the next morning and setting off to "ransack the world for friends and influence" — inaugurates the pattern of dependent, peripatetic ambition that defines the rest of his life.

The Egypt Plan vision on the Rhine is the chapter's most vivid set piece, rendered with almost mock-epic imagery ("the very hills skipped like lambs for delight") to underline both Leibniz's genuine idealism and Stewart's gentle irony about the grandiosity of his schemes.

The closing meditation on Leibniz's "insatiable... neediness" for affirmation directly sets up the book's central encounter, explicitly linking his psychological hunger to "the nature of the reception he received from and his subsequent reaction to the man he met in The Hague in November 1676" — the meeting with Spinoza around which the entire book is organized.
