# The Hague Years: Mob Violence, Betrayal, and the Road to Leibniz's Letter

The chapter narrates Spinoza's final years in The Hague as a period of mounting danger and social attrition: the 1672 French invasion and the lynching of the de Witt brothers nearly cost him his life for a defiant Latin placard; a trusted mentor, Frans van den Enden, is executed in Paris for conspiracy; a string of new acquaintances—Graevius, Stouppe, Limborch—prove to be false or hostile friends even as they socialize warmly with him; and it is precisely through this unreliable new circle, via the disreputable Georg Hermann Schuller and the brilliant but indiscreet Walther von Tschirnhaus, that Spinoza's unpublished writings and a letter of introduction finally travel to Leibniz in Paris, setting up the 1676 meeting the book is built around.

## Themes

**Political violence as a constant backdrop to philosophical work.** Stewart frames Spinoza's last years not as quiet withdrawal but as continuous exposure to mob violence and state terror: the graphic lynching of the de Witt brothers, Spinoza's own near-fatal impulse to publicly protest it, and later the judicial execution of van den Enden. The chapter insists that Spinoza's radical philosophy was produced under the direct shadow of a state and populace capable of literally tearing dissidents apart, which recontextualizes the caution and encryption of his later correspondence as pragmatic self-preservation rather than mere temperament.

**The unreliability of Spinoza's social world.** A structuring irony runs through the chapter: nearly everyone who draws close to Spinoza in this period—Graevius, Stouppe, Limborch, Oldenburg, Schuller, even eventually Tschirnhaus—either betrays him, misreads him, fears him, or proves untrustworthy in some way, and yet it is exactly this compromised network of "friends of friends" that transmits his ideas onward. Stewart quotes Lucas's judgment that "most of these friendships were feigned," casting Spinoza as strategically naive or simply undiscriminating about the company he kept, even as that same naivete is what makes the Leibniz meeting possible at all.

**Van den Enden's fate as a cautionary parallel.** The account of van den Enden's descent from schoolmaster and salon host to convicted conspirator, tried personally by Louis XIV and hanged (denied even the nobleman's ax) as a foreigner and commoner, functions as a dark mirror for Spinoza's own position: a former teacher whose radical political theory Spinoza absorbed and who tried, fatally, to enact that theory directly in the world. Stewart notes this must have "reinforced the message" that Spinoza should stay clear of French entanglements, linking the mentor's execution directly to the caution shaping Spinoza's later reception of Leibniz.

**The unraveling of the Oldenburg friendship as proof of Spinoza's isolation.** The chapter closes the long Spinoza–Oldenburg correspondence by showing it curdle from mutual encouragement into fear and doctrinal horror: Oldenburg goes from urging Spinoza to publish to begging him not to. In the middle of the exchange Spinoza's own letter records the aborted 1675 publication of the *Ethics*: once "a rumor became widespread that a certain book of mine about God was in the press, and in it I endeavor to show that there is no God," he "decided to postpone the publication until I should see how matters would turn out." Their final exchange over the allegorical status of the Resurrection makes explicit that the two men were never united by a shared philosophy of reason, only by a personal bond built on mutual misunderstanding. Stewart reads this as an "inversion of Spinoza's ideals concerning friendship among men of reason"—the theorist of rational friendship whose actual friendships are almost uniformly built on illusion.

**Tschirnhaus as the fragile hinge of transmission.** The chapter's real plot function is to establish how Spinoza's still-unpublished Ethics and his personal introduction reach Leibniz: through the disreputable alchemist-schemer Schuller, who nonetheless introduces the earnest young mathematician Tschirnhaus, who wins Spinoza's confidential trust, promptly breaks his promise of secrecy (with Oldenburg, at least, "happy results"), and ultimately carries the manuscripts and a letter of introduction across the Channel to Paris—setting up the encounter that is the book's organizing event.

## Notable passages

The de Witt lynching and Spinoza's placard episode dramatize how close mob violence came to swallowing Spinoza directly; Leibniz's own secondhand account—"he was moved to go out in the night and put up a paper somewhere near the site of the murders saying: ultimi barbarorum"—is one of the few surviving personal anecdotes Leibniz ever recorded about their meeting, and Stewart treats it as evidence that Spinoza believed his interventions could have real political weight.

Van den Enden's execution is rendered with flat, ceremonial precision—"At four o'clock in the afternoon of a November day in 1674...a gang of noble men and women were beheaded, one after the other... As a foreigner and a commoner, he was deemed unfit for the ax. So he was hanged"—a passage whose understatement underlines the brutal class logic of the punishment and the real stakes of the radical political theorizing Spinoza's circle engaged in.

The introduction of Tschirnhaus and the moment Spinoza entrusts him with manuscript extracts of the Ethics is the chapter's structural hinge: it is the first concrete step in the transmission chain that will eventually place Spinoza's ideas, and an introduction to the man himself, in Leibniz's hands.

The Resurrection exchange that ends the Oldenburg correspondence—Spinoza's flat claim that "the death and burial of Christ I accept literally, but his resurrection I understand in an allegorical sense," met by Oldenburg's horrified rejoinder that this is "the same as if one were to set about destroying the entire truth of Gospel history"—crystallizes the doctrinal gulf that the personal friendship had managed to paper over for over a decade, closing their correspondence on a note of mutual, belated recognition.

The Limborch dinner-party anecdote, in which Spinoza is accused of mocking a table of praying guests through unspecified "gestures," shows how thoroughly Spinoza's mere presence had become a screen onto which pious observers projected demonic irreligion—Stewart's skeptical aside about "an absentminded yawn or naturally drooping eyelids" underscores how much of Spinoza's fearsome reputation was constructed by others rather than demonstrated by him.
