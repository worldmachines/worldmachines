# Leibniz in Love: The Paris Years (1672–1676)

Leibniz's four years in Paris (1672–1676) are the hinge of his intellectual life: a young courtier who arrives as a diplomatic cover for a quixotic scheme to redirect Louis XIV's ambitions toward Egypt, stays on for love of the city itself, cultivates "excellent people" while running chronically short of money, and — almost incidentally, amid the striving — invents the calculus and makes his first indirect approach to Spinoza through his new friend Tschirnhaus. The chapter closes on the eve of that contact bearing fruit, with Leibniz recording the first note that will absorb the rest of his intellectual life.

## Themes

**Paris as the site of a civilizational hinge.** Stewart frames 1670s Paris — tripling in area, its mud and stench still medieval even as Colbert's administrators lay pavement, install street lamps, and take "a comprehensive approach to the matters of sanitation, water supply, transportation, security, health, education, and aesthetics" — as the exact seam between two worlds. The narrator even steps back to note that "an omniscient historical observer might well have detected the silent forces at work that were setting the stage for a tremendous clash between the medieval world and the modern world." This is Colbert's administrative apparatus turning a city into a legible, governable object — a small, concrete instance of what the [[modernity-machine]] does at civilizational scale, rendering circulation, sanitation, and security countable and controllable. Leibniz, arriving just as this machinery switches on, is himself a creature of the coming order: a diplomat-philosopher who treats status, patronage, and institutional standing as the substance of a well-lived life.

**Leibniz's "life of the mind" is a life of spectacle.** Stewart's central move is the explicit contrast with Spinoza (echoing the book's structural device of paired subsections across chapters): both men are said to lead a "life of the mind," but where Spinoza's is defined against the pursuit of riches and fame, Leibniz's is constituted by exactly that pursuit — "a respectable subspecies of the pursuit of money and fame." Leibniz dresses ostentatiously to hide his physical shortcomings, cultivates dukes and cardinals for their power rather than their reason, and openly justifies public deception as benevolent management: "It is necessary to snare the world in the trap, to take advantage of its weakness, and to deceive it in order to heal it." The chapter reads this maxim as diagnostic of the whole man — dissembling not as vice but as method, inseparable from his sociability and his ambition.

**Chronic financial anxiety as a structuring force.** Much of the chapter tracks Leibniz's serial failures to secure durable income — the collapsed Egypt plan, the fired tutorship, the half-paid legal fee from the Duke of Mecklenburg-Schwerin, the calculating machine Colbert declines to buy, the rejected Academy candidacy, a far-fetched tax-office investment scheme. Stewart crystallizes the point with a comic unit of measure: "1 Leibniz Unit = 11 Spinoza Units," dramatizing not just a gap in income but a gap in what each philosopher took a flourishing life to require. Leibniz's ceaseless "running after people" for money and title sits in pointed, ironic tension with the providentialist metaphysics he would later publish, in which all is for the best and the soul is immune to external buffeting.

**Independent, near-simultaneous intellectual triumph.** Beneath the courtly striving, Leibniz pursues mathematics with unusual focus, driven by a metaphysical intuition he calls "the labyrinth of the continuum" — the problem of how indivisible points compose a continuous line, which he takes to mirror the problem of how pointlike, immortal souls relate to the material world. Working these threads with his new friend Tschirnhaus in the Hôtel des Romains, he arrives at calculus notation (the integral sign, then dx) in the autumn of 1675, unaware that Newton had reached an equivalent result a decade earlier. The chapter treats this as the fruit of the very same restless, status-hungry, omnivorous energy that drives his social climbing — mathematics and courtiership as two faces of one temperament.

**The first, rebuffed approach to Spinoza.** Tschirnhaus's arrival brings Spinoza back into Leibniz's orbit: he re-reads the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus intensively, and through the intermediary Schuller sends word that he is "ready to receive" Spinoza's unpublished masterwork. Spinoza, made wary by van den Enden's execution and mob threats against him in The Hague, refuses the same day: "I think it imprudent to entrust my writings to him so hastily. I should first like to know what he is doing in France." Stewart's gloss is that he was, in effect, asking whether Leibniz might be a spy. The rebuff does not stop Leibniz; weeks later Tschirnhaus shares what he knows regardless, and Leibniz sets down the sentence that inaugurates "the story that came to dominate the rest of his life."

## Notable passages

The Paris urban-improvement passage is the chapter's clearest resonance with legibility as an administrative project — Colbert's deputies engineering circulation, sanitation, and lighting into a formerly chaotic, "muddy" city, with the historian's judgment that "the start of the urban sciences" dates from exactly this moment and place.

The "snare the world in the trap" maxim, and the paragraph built around it, are the chapter's psychological key: Stewart uses Leibniz's own words to justify a museum-and-spectacle scheme as evidence of a man for whom benevolent deception and self-promotion are not in tension.

The paired-portrait paragraph contrasting Spinoza's and Leibniz's "life of the mind" is the chapter's thesis statement, restating in explicit terms the structural comparison that runs through the whole book: one philosopher's inwardness defined against society, the other's constituted by it.

The "Leibniz Unit / Spinoza Unit" arithmetic is the chapter's most vivid image for that same contrast, converting a psychological and philosophical difference into a comic, memorable ratio of guilders.

Spinoza's wary, same-day refusal to let Tschirnhaus share his writings ("Still, I think it imprudent to entrust my writings to him so hastily") — reduced by Stewart to the blunt question "Is Leibniz a spy?" — marks the real stakes of the correspondence: this is not academic caution but a man freshly reminded, by an execution and a mob, of what open philosophy can cost.

The closing note — "Tschirnhaus has told me many things about the book of M. de Spinosa" — is deliberately anticlimactic in scale and enormous in consequence, the private jotting that Stewart identifies as the true beginning of the book's central drama, arriving in the same months as the calculus itself.
