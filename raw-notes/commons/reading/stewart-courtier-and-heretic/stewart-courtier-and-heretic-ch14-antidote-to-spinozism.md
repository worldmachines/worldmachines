# Building the Antidote: Leibniz's Monadology Against Spinoza's God

This unit narrates the decade (1676-1686) in which Leibniz, in between quixotic engineering ventures in the Harz Mountains, worked out the mature metaphysics of the Discourse on Metaphysics and the later Theodicy — and argues that nearly every distinctive feature of that system (a transcendent, choosing God; a plurality of indestructible mind-substances called monads; the doctrine of pre-established harmony) was engineered as a direct, point-by-point rebuttal to Spinoza, whose name Leibniz privately wrote into an early draft of the Discourse before political caution changed it to "the latest innovators."

## Themes

**Philosophy progressing underground.** Stewart frames Leibniz's fruitless windmill-building in the Harz Mountains as a structural metaphor for the invisible gestation of his metaphysics: "philosophy progresses underground... its advance may depend on the slow clearing of inundated passageways, one by one, in a seemingly random and invisible way, until at last all the chambers connect and the enterprise breathes with life." The years of visible failure in the mines were, per the chapter, exactly the years the Discourse and its "secret philosophy of the whole of things" assembled themselves out of scattered notes and reading — a paradigm coalescing silently before lurching into visible form, in the manner of a [[history-machine]].

**God: a chooser versus a necessity.** The chapter's core theological dispute is whether God has a will. Leibniz insists any God worthy of the name must choose among possible worlds and be praiseworthy for the choice; Spinoza's God/Nature simply is what it is, with no more capacity for choice than a coffee bean has to be black rather than pink. Leibniz calls this a categorical failure to earn the name "God" at all — "truly Atheist," in his verdict on Spinoza. Stewart also flags the deeper paradox: if the totality of things is beyond judgment, Spinoza cannot coherently call the world "perfect" either. Leibniz's answer — God chooses among possible worlds rather than possible things, redeeming the principle of sufficient reason while still permitting genuine choice — culminates in the Theodicy's baroque vision of Theodorus touring God's palace of possible worlds, arriving at "the best of all possible worlds," the target Voltaire would later skewer as Dr. Pangloss's creed.

**Mind: monads as an insurance policy against Spinozist dissolution.** Where "Modernity reduces God's creation to a silent, colorless, odorless world of weights and measures—a pointless machine" and "dethrones humankind" by making it an object of mere natural science — a description that resonates with the [[modernity-machine]]'s drive toward [[legibility-as-machine-core]] — Spinoza embraces the collapse of mind into matter, while Leibniz builds an entire ontology to resist it. By replacing Spinoza's single Substance with an infinity of self-contained, "windowless" monads, Leibniz manufactures indestructibility, freedom, and metaphysical dignity for the individual soul that Spinoza reserves for God alone. The chapter traces this to a precise textual maneuver: Leibniz's marginal rejection of one clause in Spinoza's definition of substance, which — if it holds — breaks the Ethics' Proposition 5 proof that only one substance can exist.

**Pre-established harmony as a bloodless miracle.** Because monads cannot interact (they are windowless), Leibniz needs to explain why mind and body appear coordinated anyway. His answer, pre-established harmony, has God synchronize all monads once, at creation, like two perfect watches that "tell the same time throughout eternity... because each on its own progresses through the same series of seconds on its own devices." Stewart positions this as a refinement of Malebranche's occasionalism (continuous divine intervention) and as the deliberate replacement for Spinoza's parallelism (mind and body as the same thing under two attributes) — and links it to the ancient argument from design, whose logical weakness (a probability, not a proof) Leibniz well understood but found rhetorically undiminished.

**Salvation: being loved versus loving.** The two philosophers again invert each other on happiness. Spinoza holds that virtue is its own reward and the intellectual love of God cannot be reciprocated; personal immortality is therefore irrelevant to the wise man's salvation. Leibniz needs immortality and a God who loves back, because without a cosmic ledger of rewards and punishments he thinks virtue goes systematically unrewarded — "Spinoza finds happiness in loving God; Leibniz finds it in God loving us back."

**Political theology and the City of God.** Leibniz explicitly ties his metaphysics to a political program: a "City of God" uniting Christendom, protecting against the "out-and-out anarchy" he associates with Spinoza's naturalism, and grounding an egalitarian cosmopolitanism ("Justice is that which is useful to the community... not of a particular nation, but of all those who are part of the City of God"). Stewart notes the irony that the modern secular state resembles Spinoza's free republic in its institutions, even as its individual-level values (the sanctity of the person, the ideal of charity) descend from Leibniz's explicitly antimodern theocratic vision.

**Metaphysics as autobiography.** The chapter closes by reading the entire monadology psychologically: an "ontological hologram" of its maker, a "metaphysics of reassurance" answering the same childhood craving for paternal security that drove Leibniz's earlier writing. Stewart suggests the ten-year delay in consolidating the system reflects an interior rehearsal — Leibniz replaying and re-editing his 1676 meeting with Spinoza until, in his own mind, he wins the argument he could not settle in the room.

## Notable passages

The chapter opens its "God" section by stating outright that rejecting Spinoza's deity "represents the first principle of Leibniz's mature philosophy," and the excised word "Spinozists" from the Discourse's early draft is offered as material proof that the whole treatise was conceived as a counter-text. The immanent/transcendent contrast — Spinoza's God as the "here and now," Leibniz's as the "before and beyond" — is the chapter's cleanest statement of the metaphysical fork between the two systems, cast in the watchmaker-versus-essence imagery Stewart returns to throughout.

On mind, the "kingdom within a kingdom" exchange is presented as Leibniz's own summary of the stakes: Spinoza denies any such kingdom exists apart from Nature's single Substance, and Leibniz answers by making every substance one. The "windowless" monad is the chapter's most vivid image for this — a unit of reality that cannot causally touch anything else, exactly the corollary needed to keep any given mind from being dissolved into, or contaminated by, its surroundings the way Spinoza's modes are contaminated by Substance.

The pre-established harmony passage shows the mechanism that lets monads seem to interact without ever doing so — the passage doubles as Stewart's clearest example of Leibniz replacing a rival's real-time miracle (Malebranche's occasionalism) with a single miracle front-loaded at creation. The Theodicy passage on Spinozistic "brutish necessity" versus Leibniz's God who "does everything with our good in mind" crystallizes the salvation dispute into the chapter's most quotable formula. Finally, the closing paragraph on Leibniz's "neediness" is the chapter's own explicit thesis statement about how to read everything that came before it — not as disinterested philosophy but as a permanent, unresolved rematch with the man from The Hague.
