# Of Experience, opening movement: diversity, law and the turn to oneself

This first fifth of the final essay of the *Essays* begins by calling the desire for knowledge the most natural of all, and experience a weaker, cheaper substitute for reason that is still worth using. Its first discovery is that experience is no more certain than reason, because no two events, faces or opinions are alike. From that Montaigne argues, at length and mostly in his own voice, against the multiplication of laws and the proliferation of commentary: both try to fix the infinite variety of human action in fixed words, and both only breed more doubt. He gives two cases from his own time of justice done by rote, then reports ancient opinions that justice is relative or expedient, and says plainly that laws are obeyed because they are laws, not because they are just. The slice ends by pivoting to the claim that he studies himself more than any other subject, that self-knowledge is a better teacher than Cicero or Aristotle, and that his judgment keeps a 'magisterial seat' apart from his appetites. The slice stops mid-essay; it does not contain the later material on the body, illness or diet.

## Themes

**Experience is as unreliable as reason.** Montaigne says experience has 'no fewer' forms than reason, and the inference from comparing events is unsure 'by reason they are always unlike'. Similitude is the limit case: eggs, the polished backs of cards, faces. Yet he balances this: things neither entirely resemble nor entirely differ, 'an ingenious mixture of nature', and without likeness we could not tell man from beast. Every example 'halts'. This is a sceptical point (see [[socratic-thinking]] only loosely; Montaigne is less interested in refutation than in the texture of difference).

**More laws, more license.** Montaigne rejects the view (held by an unnamed predecessor he reports) that a multitude of laws can curb judges. Interpretation is as free as invention; France has the most laws and the fullest judicial license. Fixed laws cannot match actions 'in perpetual mutation'; he would prefer few, simple, general laws, or none. There is a faint resonance with the project's [[Legibility]] theme (codification as an attempt to make a complex social world readable), though Montaigne's point is that such codification fails by its own measure and he offers no programme, only a preference for judgment on the spot ('For every foot its own shoe').

**Commentary swallows the text.** Subdividing questions, like children chasing quicksilver, multiplies doubts; glosses never close a book; 'There is more ado to interpret interpretations than to interpret things'. The mind's 'natural disease' is inquisitive restlessness, which Montaigne also praises: a generous mind that rests satisfied is 'half alive', and La Boetie's image of waves chasing waves (quoted in Cotton's translation) fits an inquiry without aim or end. He thus holds both a critique of endless glossing and a defence of unending inquiry; these are not reconciled in this slice. He also concedes that his own book talks about itself, and offers the excuse that his subject is himself.

**Justice as institution.** He reports two failures of legal form: peasants who abandon a dying man for fear of being implicated, and men hanged for a murder after the real culprits had confessed, because reversing a sentence was judged a worse precedent. He sets against these an exemplum about Philip (possibly another, Montaigne says) who compensated a wrongly fined man from his own purse. The ancient opinions he lists (justice follows utility, custom makes justice, the Stoic view that nature acts unjustly) are reported rather than endorsed, but sit beside his own conclusion that laws 'keep up their credit, not for being just, but because they are laws; ‘tis the mystic foundation of their authority'. A short aside on China is a reported example of the world being larger than ancients or moderns have known.

**Liberty and the civil wars.** Montaigne has never been before a judge or in a prison; he loves liberty so much that he would move to another jurisdiction at the first threat from his own laws, and his 'little prudence' in the civil wars is devoted to keeping his freedom of movement. See [[michel-de-montaigne]].

**The self as nearest experience.** If experience from foreign examples does little good when we profit so little from our own, then 'I study myself more than any other subject; ‘tis my metaphysic, my physic'. He is content to be led by the general law of the world and sceptical of philosophers' painted pictures of nature; ignorance is a 'soft, easy, and wholesome pillow'. His self-knowledge is concrete: memory of his anger, his mistakes, the unreliability of his memory; from each particular error he learns to distrust his whole understanding. Judgment holds a 'magisterial seat' but plays its game apart from his appetites. Compare [[self-construction-through-writing]] for the essay as a vehicle of this self-study.

## Notable passages

**Diversity as the one universal.** The opening argues that comparison of events yields uncertain conclusions since 'There is no quality so universal in this image of things as diversity and variety.' It is the premise for everything that follows (c1).

**Law cannot keep up with action.** The argument that a hundred thousand laws will never match the infinite diversity of cases, and that fewer would be better, is the most direct statement of his legal scepticism (c2).

**Interpreting interpretations.** The famous complaint that we 'do nothing but comment upon one another', with authors scarce and commentaries everywhere, is Montaigne's critique of learned culture, and implicitly of his own book (c3).

**Hanged by the forms.** The account of men executed after the real murderers confessed shows justice losing to its own procedures: 'How many condemnations have I seen more criminal than the crimes themselves?' (c4).

**Laws obeyed because they are laws.** The 'mystic foundation' passage, with the pivot to self-study, ties legal scepticism to the claim that one's own experience is the proper school (c5).

**Learning from one's own errors.** The passage on suspecting his own understanding as a whole, not just the single mistake, is his clearest account of self-knowledge as practical wisdom (c6).
