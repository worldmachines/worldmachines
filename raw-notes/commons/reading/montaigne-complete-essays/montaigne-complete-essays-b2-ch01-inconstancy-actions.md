# Of the Inconstancy of Our Actions (Book 2, ch. 1)

A whole short essay. Montaigne opens with the observation that human actions contradict one another so sharply that they seem to come from different persons, and that writers who try to give a man a single "constant and solid contexture" must either bend his deeds to fit or write off the awkward ones as dissimulation. He then argues that irresolution is the commonest vice of our nature; that we are driven by occasion rather than steered by any settled design; that apparent virtues such as valour are often accidents of anger, necessity, company or circumstance; and that his own self-observation finds every contrary quality in him. He concludes that to judge a man one must follow his trace over a long time and look past outward acts to the springs that move them, a task he calls high and hazardous and wishes fewer would attempt.

## Themes

**Biography and the demand for coherence.** Montaigne criticises "even the best authors" for choosing a "general air of a man" and interpreting everything by it. Augustus is the exception who "slipped away" from such critics because of his obvious variety. His own recommendation is to judge "in detail and distinctly, bit by bit", which he says would more often yield truth. This is Montaigne's own judgment, offered with the hedge that he "can more hardly believe a man's constancy than any other virtue."

**Constancy as the ideal, drift as the fact.** He reports, without dissent, ancient views that wisdom is to will one thing always (with the proviso, he reports, that the will be just) and that deliberation begins virtue while constancy completes it (attributed to Demosthenes). Against that standard he sets what we actually do: follow appetite "as we are wafted by the breath of occasion", "We do not go, we are driven." The Latin verse he quotes (untranslated in this text) supports the picture of puppet-like movement; I do not quote or translate it here beyond that general sense.

**Virtue is not a single act.** Valour in particular must be a habit that holds in every setting, "the same alone as in company", in bed or on the field; otherwise "the action is commendable, not the man." The exempla (Antigonus's cured soldier, Lucullus's plundered soldier, Chasan the Janissary commander, Cicero's Greeks and Cimbrians, Alexander's flaws) are borrowed from classical and other authors and serve as illustrations of valour that depends on circumstances. The maid thrown from a window is a contemporary anecdote from the civil disorders, told to show that one dramatic act should not conclude a character, and that later information reversed the first impression.

**Design for a life.** Chance rules us because we have not designed our lives toward an end; without the whole form in view we deliberate only "by pieces." The archer and port images make the point: "No wind serves him who addresses his voyage to no certain, port." He also rejects inferring wide competence from one sample (Sophocles's tragedy, the Parians choosing well-run farmers as magistrates).

**Self-report and the divided self.** Montaigne notes others' theory of two souls, then gives his own account: he is moved by every accident and also unsettled by his own posture; "If I speak variously of myself, it is because I consider myself variously"; he lists contrary traits he finds in himself and says "'Distinguo' is the most universal member of my logic." This is the essay's most distinctive move and links it to the project of the *Essays* as self-study ([[self-construction-through-writing]], [[michel-de-montaigne]]). It sits in tension with his praise of constancy: he does not claim to have it.

**Motives beneath actions.** Since ambition, avarice and even love can produce courage, temperance or boldness, outward conduct cannot show virtue; the understanding "must penetrate the very soul". Montaigne hedges that this is risky work.

## Notable passages

The opening lays out the problem of contradictory actions and names irresolution as the commonest vice (c1). The plain statement of method follows: distrust constancy, judge bit by bit (c2). The images of drift and of being driven by occasion (c3) lead to the self-portrait and the "Distinguo" passage (c4). The argument that one brave act or one setting does not make a valiant man (c5), the argument that a life needs an end to aim at (c6), and the "lumps" passage (c7) complete the case. A resonance with [[habit-and-identity]] is partial: Montaigne here stresses the absence of settled habit, rather than habit's grip.
