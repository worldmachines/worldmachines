# Of Prayers; Of Age

Two brief chapters. In "Of Prayers" (ch. 56) Montaigne opens by submitting his "formless and undetermined fancies" to Church censure, then argues that prayer is only worth anything when the soul praying is clean: people pray out of custom, bargain with God over their vices, and compartmentalise devotion from conduct. A long digression defends restricting Scripture and theology to qualified persons and doubts vernacular translation, and he closes on Plato and Horace: the gods do not accept the gift of a wicked man. In "Of Age" (ch. 57) he reverses the usual picture of a long "natural" life: dying of old age is rare, so anyone past forty has already beaten the odds; laws and customs that delay adulthood and public service are mistaken; and by his own report his understanding and constitution have declined since thirty.

## Themes

**Prayer as custom versus conscience.** Montaigne's complaint is not against prayer but against prayer detached from amendment: "We pray only by custom and for fashion’s sake". He mocks the person who crosses himself at grace and gives the rest of the day to malice, "One hour to God, the rest to the devil, as if by composition and compensation", and wonders at a conscience that can host "both the crime and the judge". The Lord's Prayer, he says, is the one he himself uses everywhere, repeating rather than varying it. The point about ritual running on autopilot and shaping a person's apparent character without touching it has a loose kinship with [[habit-and-identity]], though here the diagnosis is moral and theological, not a theory of habit.

**Praying for one's own vices.** Citing Plato, Xenophon and Latin verse (the Latin is untranslated in this text), he lists the covetous, the ambitious and the thief who all invoke God, and compares a man who calls on God while in vice to "a cut-purse" calling a magistrate. Prayers are treated as a "jargon" or incantation whose efficacy is imagined to lie in sound and solemn countenance. The Pythagorean remedy he reports is that prayers be said publicly, so no one dares ask for something indecent; he also reports Oedipus, whose wicked prayer was granted as punishment. Montaigne's own rule: pray not that things go as we wish but as is most consistent with prudence.

**Sacred matter kept to the qualified.** Montaigne (not merely reporting) says the Church is right to restrict casual use of the Psalms and the Bible, that Scripture is "not a story to tell, but a history to revere", and that translation into the vulgar tongue breeds "temerity and presumption"; he backs this with exempla (a Greek historian on factional theological fury, advice to Theodosius, Andronicus threatening disputants with the river, Plato on the old and the young, the island of nominal Christians who understand nothing of their religion). This is a conservative, anti-vernacular, anti-disputation position set in the Wars of Religion; he even allows that an edict silencing laymen such as himself on religion would be reasonable. He also recounts being told that people assume talented Catholics secretly hold the reformed view, and calls that a disease of being "riveted to his own belief".

**Self-positioning of the essays.** At the outset and again mid-chapter he frames his writing as "matter of opinion, not matter of faith", done "after a laical, not clerical, and yet always after a very religious manner, as children prepare their exercises, not to instruct but to be instructed". This is a compact statement of the essay as exploratory, self-submitting form (see [[personal-essay-as-form]]); note the submission is explicit and formal ("into which I was born and in which I will die"), and the reader should carry his hedge rather than read it as irony or as sincere fideism alone.

**Old age is not the natural measure of life.** In ch. 57 he argues that we mislabel as "natural" only the rare death from decay: "we ought rather, peradventure, to call that natural which is general, common, and universal." From this he infers that past forty one has already outlived the ordinary term and should expect little more. Cato's remark at forty-eight is the reported exemplum he leans on.

**Against late adulthood and early retirement.** He faults the law for letting men manage an estate only at twenty-five, and Roman custom for retiring soldiers early or judges being thirty; his own view is that employment should be extended "for the public good" and begun sooner. He holds that "our souls are adult at twenty", that more great deeds are done before thirty than after (Hannibal and Scipio cited), and, speaking for himself, that his "understanding and my constitution have rather decayed than improved". He allows experience may grow with years for some, but vivacity and steadiness fail, and mental decay is dangerous because painless and obscure. He ends by complaining that too much of life is given to "childhood, idleness, and apprenticeship". Hedge: he states this as his opinion and personal experience, not as a general law.

## Notable passages

The opening submission to Church censure (c1) sets the register for both chapters: tentative, self-effacing, and formally obedient. The central argument on prayer (c2, c3) ties the efficacy of prayer to the state of the soul and denounces compartmentalised piety. The Scripture passage (c4) is the clearest statement of his restrictive stance on lay reading. The framing of his essays as human fancies (c5) is the key self-description in the prayers chapter. The two passages from "Of Age" (c6, c7) give first the inversion of what counts as natural death and second his self-report on youth and decline.

Two caveats for downstream use: much of ch. 56's support is Latin verse that is untranslated in this text, and many of the long digressions are exempla borrowed from other writers rather than Montaigne's own findings.
