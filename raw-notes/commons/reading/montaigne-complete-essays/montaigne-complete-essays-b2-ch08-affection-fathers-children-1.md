# Of the Affection of Fathers to Their Children (first part): self-portrait, reasoned love, and the old father's duty to yield

The slice opens with a dedicatory letter to Madame d'Estissac, in which Montaigne describes his book as a strange, "fantastic" product of melancholy solitude, and praises her maternal devotion as a feature of the portrait he is drawing of himself. The essay proper then asks what "natural" affection of parents for children really is. Montaigne grants it a place near the instinct of self-preservation, explains with Aristotle why givers love more than receivers, and then insists that instinct must be subordinated to reason and judgment. From there he turns to practice, all in his own first-person voice with illustrative cases: fathers who withhold money from grown children, the harm of harshness and whipping, the proper age to marry, and the obligation of an ageing father to cede goods and authority to his heirs while retaining a power to revoke. The slice ends mid-argument with a portrait of a tyrannical, suspicious old householder who is robbed and mocked by his own household. It is the first part of the essay only.

## Themes

**The book as self-portrait, born of melancholy.** In the dedication Montaigne says that a melancholic humour, bred by solitude, put "this idle fancy of writing" in his head, and that, finding himself "empty of other matter", he "presented myself to myself for argument and subject." He claims the book is the only one of its kind and that its only merit is its extravagance. This is a hedged, self-deprecating statement of the essay as an instrument of self-study; compare [[michel-de-montaigne]] and [[self-construction-through-writing]], and [[personal-essay-as-form]]. The praise of Madame d'Estissac is framed as a feature he would have omitted from his own picture had he left it out.

**Nature as a machine, with parental love only half-trustworthy.** Montaigne is tentative: "if there be any law truly natural" (he adds that this "is not without controversy"), then parental affection ranks second after self-preservation. He speaks of nature's "successive pieces of this machine of hers", so that love flows downward to the next generation more than upward. The Aristotelian considerations (reported, not his own invention) explain this: the benefactor loves more than the beneficiary, the creditor more than the debtor, and the artificer his work, because "every one has in some sort a being in his work."

**Reason must govern inclination.** God gave us reason so that we should not be "servilely subject" to the laws we share with brutes; we may yield to nature but not be "tyrannically hurried away" by her. Montaigne reports his own disgust at the doting on newborn infants and says he has not willingly had them nursed near him; real affection should grow with the knowledge children give of themselves, and should judge them even if they turn out worthless. He observes that parents who delight in children's games, "like monkeys", are often stingy once the children are grown.

**Hoarding fathers wrong their children.** He thinks it "cruelty and injustice" not to share goods and household knowledge with capable children. Avarice drives young men to theft; he gives anecdotes of gentlemen made thieves by a miserly father and then unable to stop, which resonate with the theme of custom hardening into compulsion (compare [[habit-and-identity]], though here habit is a moral fact about thieves, not a general theory). He adds that he hates theft more by disposition than by reason, and is careful to note it is a vice of other provinces too. He also reports, without endorsing, a lord's defence that hoarding keeps an old man respected, and answers that a father who holds his children only by their need is "very miserable" and must be loved for virtue and kindness.

**Against violence in education.** He condemns "all violence in the education of a tender soul designed for honour and liberty", reports that he himself was almost never beaten and raised his daughter Leonora, the only one of his children to survive infancy, with gentle words alone, and says whipping only renders boys "more cowardly, or more wilfully obstinate." He hedges that if his method fails there are other causes to blame.

**When to marry, and when to step aside.** He married at thirty-three and weighs Aristotle, Plato and Thales on the right age, and gives learned exempla (the Gauls, Muley Hassam, Greek athletes, a custom in the Spanish Indies) as reported lore rather than as his arguments. A man of thirty should not yet cede to a twenty-year-old son; but a father worn by age wrongs himself and his family by amassing treasure, and should, like Charles V, strip himself in time. Montaigne's own advice to an old gentleman to give his house to his son worked out well. He would give his heirs the use of his goods but keep a power of revocation and live nearby; he contrasts this with a dean who shut himself in his chamber for twenty-two years.

**Authority through affection.** He objects to the custom of forbidding children to call their father "father", and to stern countenances that make old fathers "ridiculous to their own children": "Though I could make myself feared, I had yet much rather make myself beloved." The slice closes with a negative example, an imperious old householder whose family robs and mocks him while he boasts of his command, a man who "is fallen from it like a child."

## Notable passages

**Self as subject (c1).** The dedication's account of melancholy, solitude, and "presented myself to myself for argument and subject" is a compact origin story for the whole project and a rare moment of Montaigne explaining why he writes.

**Nature, benefit and work (c2).** The Aristotelian argument for why parents love children more than children love parents, with the "machine" of nature, and an unusual claim that to be "consists in movement and action", so the maker is invested in what he makes. The "machine" here is a metaphor for generational continuity, not a WMP-style world machine, though it echoes the idea of infrastructure that spans generations ([[world-machines]]).

**Reason over instinct (c3).** The key methodological passage of the essay: affection should follow judgment, and the natural impulse should "walk hand in hand with reason". It is a good example of Montaigne cutting against sentiment while recognizing nature's authority.

**Hoarding and theft (c4).** Money withheld by fathers produces thieves, with anecdotes; Montaigne's claim is a causal one about habit and upbringing, buttressed by his own disposition as a Gascon.

**No violence in education (c5).** A short, firm statement of his pedagogy of liberty, with a report of his own upbringing.

**Called "father" (c6).** The passage on fatherly dignity and the king of the household who commands but is loved by no one.
