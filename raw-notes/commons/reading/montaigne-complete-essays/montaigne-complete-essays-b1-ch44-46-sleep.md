# Sleep, Dreux, and Names: three short essays on composure, command and the vanity of a label

The three chapters are unrelated in subject. "Of Sleep" is a string of anecdotes (Alexander, Otho, Cato, Augustus, young Marius) about leaders who slept through the crisis, and Montaigne reads this as evidence of a soul above events, while leaving the physiology of sleep to the physicians. "Of the Battle of Dreux" is a brief defence of M. de Guise's decision to hold back, argued by analogy with Philopoemen and Agesilaus. "Of Names" openly announces itself as a "hodge-podge": it runs from fashions in baptismal names and the convenience of a pronounceable name, through the corruption of French family names and arms, to a sceptical question about what "glory" can possibly attach to a name that is only a sound or a few strokes of a pen.

## Essay 1: Of Sleep

**Reason's pace.** Montaigne opens with a principle: reason fixes the direction but not the speed. A wise man may let the passions quicken or slow him, and should not be "a motionless and insensible Colossus"; virtue given flesh would have a faster pulse going to an assault than going to dinner. This is his own judgment, and it sets up the sleepers as something "extraordinary" rather than as a model of insensibility.

**Sleep as a sign of composure (exempla, not self-report).** The stories (Alexander and Darius, Otho and Cato before suicide, Cato before the Metellus confrontation, Augustus in the Sicilian naval fight) are borrowed ancient anecdotes. Montaigne draws one inference explicitly for Cato: his indifference came "from a soul so much elevated above such accidents" that it gave them no more hold on his fancy than an ordinary incident. He frames this as something the rest of Cato's life "may warrant us" to judge, a hedged inference, not a rule.

**Counter-case and open question.** Augustus's sleep is reported alongside Mark Antony's reproach that he lacked the courage to look at his own fleet, so the same behaviour can be read as cowardice. Young Marius slept through his own rout, but Montaigne offers exhaustion as the explanation ("nature could hold out no longer"). He closes by leaving the physiological question to "the physicians": Perseus was reportedly killed by being kept from sleep, Pliny cites those who lived long without it, Herodotus reports nations who sleep in half-years, and Epimenides is said to have slept fifty-seven years. He does not resolve these reports.

## Essay 2: Of the Battle of Dreux

**Guise's halt, defended by analogy.** Montaigne reports that Guise's critics blame him for delaying while the Constable's battalion was shattered and the Constable captured. He sides, with a hedge ("he who will consider it without passion or prejudice"), with the view that a captain's, even a private soldier's, aim must be the victory as a whole, not a particular loss however near to his interest. The supporting case is Philopoemen, who let his own skirmishers be cut down before his eyes, then struck the disordered pursuers. The Agesilaus anecdote (from Xenophon) is the mirror image: he refused the safe rear attack for the show of valour, was beaten and wounded, and had to do what he had first neglected. The essay is short and stays within military judgment; it takes no position beyond Guise's conduct.

## Essay 3: Of Names

**A deliberate hodge-podge.** The sallet image announces a series of "Item" paragraphs. Some names are unlucky or fatal to dynasties; a feast sorted by name produced a hundred and ten Williams; a well-sounding name is a practical advantage because kings and masters call and remember those whose names come easily to the tongue (Henry II failing to recall a Gascon gentleman's name is Montaigne's own witness). Two edifying anecdotes (the Poitiers youth struck by the name Mary; Pythagoras calming drunken youths with spondaic music) show sound acting on conscience through ear and sense.

**Reformation and antiquity mocked lightly.** Montaigne ironically asks whether posterity will praise the Reformation for replacing Charles, Louis and Francis with Methuselahs and Ezekiels, and reports a neighbour who found Don Grumedan and Quedregan nobler than Pierre, Guillot and Michel. Both are small sketches of taste and prejudice about sound; Montaigne's stance is amused, not argued.

**Custom and legibility of lineage.** On Amyot he prefers keeping Latin names unaltered, noting that custom, "by the authority of his Plutarch", has already overcome the initial harshness (a small instance of [[habit-and-identity]] style familiarisation, though Montaigne frames it only as a matter of reading). He objects that Latinising names (Vaudemont into Vallemontanus) means "we know not where we are". His sharpest complaint is the French habit of naming people after their manor, which confounds families and descents: names and arms change hands by inheritance, partition and purchase, and the newly risen acquire invented genealogies. Here a name is a poor record of lineage, a kind of unstable label, which loosely rhymes with the project's interest in legibility, though Montaigne's concern is family honour and not administration.

**The friend's farce.** A gentleman friend, hearing a room of squires each claim royal kin, mock-deferentially declines to sit among "so many princes" and ends by urging them to be content with "what we are". This is reported as an anecdote Montaigne enjoyed, and the speech carries his sympathy.

**Glory and the name.** Moving from arms to fame, Montaigne asks what glory is attached to: "Peter or William" is only a sound or "three or four dashes with a pen", easily varied (Guesquin, Glesquin, Gueaquin), shared by many people (three Socrateses, five Platos), and available to his groom who might call himself Pompey. He questions what Epaminondas or Africanus could feel of the verses praising them, concluding that survivors project their own feelings of jealousy and desire onto the dead. The essay ends on a Latin verse (untranslated in this text) about the thirst for fame exceeding that for virtue; the unit stops there. This is a sceptical deflation of fame, in Montaigne's own voice, but note he does not say striving for it is wrong: he calls hope a "valiant faculty" and fame a "pretty toy" Nature gave us to play with.

## Notable passages

- The opening principle that reason fixes direction, not pace, and the Alexander, Otho and Cato sleep anecdotes (c1).
- Montaigne's own reading of Cato's sleep as a mark of a soul beyond accident (c2).
- The case for Guise's delay at Dreux, argued from Philopoemen (c3).
- Amyot and the preservation of names as given (c4).
- The friend's mock-deference to the self-made princes (c5).
- The deflation of fame: what is "Peter or William" but a sound? (c6).
