# Honour, rank and dress: three essays on appearances

Three short essays on one theme: the gap between what a person or office is and the outward show that gets credited to it. Ch. 41 ("Not to Communicate a Man's Honour") calls the pursuit of glory the most universal folly, one even philosophers cannot shake, and then collects exempla of men who deliberately handed their credit to someone else. Ch. 42 ("Of the Inequality Amongst Us") argues that the real differences between people lie in the soul and body, not in titles, retinues or revenue. It turns into a long, mostly Xenophon-derived anatomy of why kings are not happier than anyone else. Ch. 43 ("Of Sumptuary Laws") argues that forbidding luxuries to the common people only raises their prestige. Fashion could be steered by the court's own example, and Montaigne ends by fearing change in manners as a sign of a cracking edifice.

## Themes

**Glory as an almost ineradicable folly (ch. 41).** Montaigne's own judgment is that the "solicitude of reputation and glory" is the most universally received of follies, and that reason "clearly accuses the vanity" of it without dislodging it. He professes not to know whether anyone has ever fully freed himself, and reports (via Cicero) that even writers who attack glory want their names on the books. The conceit of the title is that honour, unlike goods and lives, is nearly never lent. The exempla (Catulus Luctatius, Antonio de Leva, Archileonida, King Edward at Crecy, Laelius, Theopompus, the Bishop of Beauvais) are borrowed from other writers and are all presented as the rare exceptions. The Bishop of Beauvais closes the essay on a mildly ironic note, a "subtlety of conscience" that kills with a mace but will not wound.

**Judge the man, not the wrapping (ch. 42).** Beginning from Plutarch's remark that men differ more than beasts do, Montaigne goes further: the gap between some men is greater than between a man and a beast. His central argument is that we praise a horse, hound or hawk for its own qualities, never its caparison, collar or bells, yet appraise a man by what is "about him, but not in him": train, palace, credit, income. The remedy he proposes is a thought experiment of stripping ("let him present himself in his shirt") and asking about body and soul. The sage of his Horatian verses, self-sufficient and master of fortune, is "an absolute monarch in and to himself." Montaigne's own judgment of the common run of men is harsh ("stupid and mean-spirited, servile, instable"), and he says custom's blindness hides how great the distance is, while the visible distinctions of rank differ "no more ... than in their breeches."

**The unhappiness and unfreedom of kings.** Most of the essay is an argument, backed by actors-in-costume imagery, Alexander's blood and Antigonus's quip about the close-stool, that royalty adds nothing to health, sleep or appetite. Satiety dulls the great man's pleasures, and he is too visible for any vice to pass as ordinary. Montaigne leans heavily on Hiero in Xenophon, and on Cyrus and Seleucus, but speaks in his own voice too: "I have often been moved rather to pity than to envy" kings, and he thinks it "far more easy and pleasant to follow than to lead." The sharpest point is the loss of friendship: where service is compelled, "The honour we receive from those that fear us is not honour." The ceremony paid to the wicked king and to the beloved one is identical, so the prince can read nothing from it. This inverts the glory-seeking of ch. 41 from the other side. The tale of Julian and the courtiers who would not dare condemn him makes the same point. Diocletian's melons and the Pyrrhus-Cyneas dialogue (conquer the world, then rest, which you could do now) close it as exempla about enough, with Montaigne finishing on a verse, "Mores cuique sui fingunt fortunam." Montaigne also shrinks sovereignty by scale: a provincial lord, or a gentleman who "loves his own fireside," is almost as free as a Duke of Venice.

**Custom steers fashion more than law does (ch. 43).** The argument is that sumptuary edicts reserving turbot, velvet or gold lace for princes make those things more desirable. Montaigne observes how "suddenly and with how much ease custom in these indifferent things establishes itself and becomes authority": after court mourning for Henry II, silks became contemptible to everyone within a year and fell to physicians and surgeons, and chamois and linen doublets became the fashion in armies. His proposal is that kings simply stop wearing the finery (or that scarlet and goldsmiths' work be proclaimed fit only for courtesans and tumblers), and he approves Zeleucus's version of the same inversion. He accepts that outward distinction of rank is "very requisite in a state." A cluster of grievances against court fashions (breeches, doublets, long hair, kissing of hands, bareheadedness) shows his conservatism, and he reads these as "superficial errors" that are still omens of a "crazy and tottering" fabric. The essay ends with Plato's Laws against youthful innovation, and a firm claim that only laws of such long standing that nobody knows their beginning are "in their true credit." Note that this last is Montaigne's endorsement of the antiquity of custom, offered with an attribution to Plato for the first part.

## Resonances

The stripping thought experiment and the passage on borrowed greatness belong with Montaigne's wider concern for self-knowledge and judgment of what is properly one's own ([[michel-de-montaigne]], [[personal-essay-as-form]]). The ch. 43 account of how fashion and distinction pass downward from court example, and how custom turns arbitrary things into authority, rhymes with [[habit-and-identity]] and, loosely, with WMP's interest in how legitimacy is infrastructure rather than command. It is secular and anecdotal where WMP is structural, and Montaigne does not theorise it, only observes it. The reading of rank as costume, and of royal ceremony as hollow, is also an early version of a legibility/performance critique of the ornament of power ([[Legibility]]), though only suggestively, since Montaigne is not talking about institutions as machines.

## Notable passages

**The glory folly and the rare exceptions.** The opening paragraph of ch. 41 states the folly, notes that philosophers are not free of it, and sets up the single exception the essay explores: honour is the one good that is hardly ever shared.

**Edward at Crecy.** The king declines to rescue his son because it would take the honour of the victory from him, and he accepts the hazard. It is the most developed exemplum in ch. 41.

**Strip him to his shirt.** The most quotable passage in ch. 42: the pedestal is no part of the statue, and the check on a man is body and soul. It is the essay's methodological core and shows Montaigne's habit of arguing from the market for horses.

**Hiero on friendship.** The ruler cannot know whether he is loved, since the respect shown is owed to royalty rather than to him: a clear example of the system outweighing the person.

**Fashion and sumptuary law.** The ch. 43 opening, and its observation on how quickly custom sets and reverses, is the best-evidenced claim in the essay (the Henry II mourning example is Montaigne's own report).

**Against change.** The closing Platonic and Montaignian defence of old, unknown-origin custom, with its hedge "saving only in those that are evil."
