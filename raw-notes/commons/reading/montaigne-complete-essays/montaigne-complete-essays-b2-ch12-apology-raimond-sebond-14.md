# Law, Interpretation and the Limits of the Senses

This is the fourteenth slice of the Apology for Raimond Sebond; it opens mid-argument and ends mid-example, on the deceptions of the senses. It runs in four movements. First, Montaigne reports a judge who annotated a hard case "a question for a friend" and notes that the multiplicity of judgments makes legal certainty impossible; he then glances at the licence of philosophers on sex and shame (Arcesilaus, Epicurus, Chrysippus, the Cynics, with the Stoic and Peripatetic views on modesty). Second, he says that laws draw their authority from custom and that tracing them to their source finds "but a little spring". Third, from Heraclitus, Protagoras and Democritus drawing opposite conclusions from the same experience of bitter and sweet wine, he turns to the way any text can be made to say anything (the alchemist-divine with his Bible passages, the Sibyls, Homer, Plato). Fourth, he turns to the senses as "the greatest foundation and proof of our ignorance": all knowledge comes through them, no sense can check another, we may lack senses we cannot know we lack (the blind gentleman who hunts and talks of sight), and the Epicurean defence of the senses and the Stoic attack on them cancel into "no science at all".

## Themes

**Law and custom as accumulated momentum.** Montaigne's own claim, stated flatly: "Laws derive their authority from possession and custom." They are like rivers that grow by running on, and examining their sources reveals "light and weak" considerations. This is why philosophers who "weigh and reduce every thing to reason" differ from the public. He does not endorse the philosophers' conclusions (he notes some opinions "are better concealed than published to weak minds"), so the passage is a diagnosis of why custom feels solid rather than a call to abandon it. It resonates with [[habit-and-identity]] and the invisible-machinery idea in the project's frame, though Montaigne here is describing legitimacy by prescription, not a lifecycle of institutions.

**Contradiction in institutions.** The judge's marginal note and the practice of running "from judge to judge, and court to court" show that in so "infinite a science" judgments conflict, and that judges find bias to suit themselves. This is Montaigne's report and judgment, with the dry remark that the judge's only fault was not writing the note throughout.

**Reported opinions about modesty (attribution).** The sexual-licence material is a catalogue of what Epicurus, Arcesilaus, Chrysippus, Diogenes, Crates and Hipparchia are said to hold or do, set against a "great and religious author" who thinks the Cynics only mimed. Montaigne's own sympathies are mixed: he writes that "Modesty is a thing of weight; secrecy, reservation, and circumspection, are parts of esteem", while presenting the Stoic view that concealment is "foppery" as the other side. The Latin verses are untranslated in the text and are not relied on here.

**Texts can be made to say anything.** Wine bitter to the sick and sweet to the well led Heraclitus and Protagoras to say everything is in everything, and Democritus to say objects have nothing in them of what we perceive; the Pyrrhonians suspend judgment. Montaigne's own observation follows: no sense or aspect of anything exists that "the human mind does not find out in the writings it undertakes to tumble over". His examples (the divine who finds alchemy in the Bible, Homer quoted by every profession, Plato bent to every new opinion) show interpretation as a creative force driven by the interpreter's wit, and ambiguous authors profiting from "a number of wits, shaking and sifting him".

**The senses as unverifiable gateway.** Montaigne's own turn: all knowledge arrives by the senses, "They are our masters", and science "begins by them, and is resolved into them", yet there is nothing beyond them to audit them, "not so much as one sense in the discovery of another". He doubts that we have all the senses there are: animals live full lives without sight or hearing, so we may be missing one, two or many. The blind man who uses sight-words without knowing sight is his analogy for a whole species missing a sense and content with its lot. The animal examples (cocks, chickens, ants, stags) are offered as hints of faculties we lack, hedged with "perhaps" and "who knows".

**Dogmatic sects refuted by each other.** He reports the Epicurean position that senses never deceive (the sun is as big as it seems; Timagoras and the doubled candle) and gives the Latin-and-English argument that denying the senses undermines life itself. His own verdict on that advice is sharp: human knowledge supports itself "by reason unreasonable, foolish, and mad", out of unwillingness to confess "necessary ignorance". Setting Epicureans (no knowledge if appearances are false) against Stoics (appearances are so false they give no knowledge), he concludes that "there is no science at all". The slice ends on the examples of echoes, islands and moving ships that illustrate sensory error.

## Notable passages

The passage on the origin of laws is the clearest statement here of custom as authority, and should be read alongside [[habit-and-identity]]. The reflection on how texts yield any meaning ties to the Apology's scepticism toward learned authority and to commentary culture. The blind gentleman and the "eight or ten" senses are Montaigne's most original epistemic move in the slice: our truth is a product of the equipment we happen to have. The closing verdict on Epicureans and Stoics shows the typical Pyrrhonian technique of letting two dogmatisms cancel; note that this is Montaigne reporting and weighing, not asserting a positive doctrine.
