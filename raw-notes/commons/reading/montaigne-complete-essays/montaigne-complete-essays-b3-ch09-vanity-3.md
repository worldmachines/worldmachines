# Freedom from Obligation, Civil War, and the Case for Travel and Absence

This slice of Of Vanity (the third of five) begins mid-argument. Montaigne first declares himself as free and as little indebted as any man alive, and explains his horror of owing favours: giving is authoritative, accepting is submission, and an obligation is never truly dissolved. He then turns to the cost of the one great debt he cannot avoid, his protection in the civil wars, and describes sleeping in fear in his own house, though he cushions this with indifference toward danger and a preference for open enemies over those "in a gown". From there he explains his travelling humour: love of Paris, a self-declared cosmopolitanism (with reservations about Socrates), travel as a school of human variety, his physical stamina and habits on the road, and replies to friends who object that he is married and old. The slice closes with arguments that absence strengthens marriage and friendship, that age licenses pleasure, that he walks "for the walk's sake", and that he wants a solitary, unceremonious death.

## Themes

**Independence as the aim of the self.** Montaigne reports of himself, not of any school, that he owes only "foreign obligations" and prays never to owe "essential thanks to any one". "I have nothing mine but myself, and yet the possession is, in part, defective and borrowed." He cites Hippias of Elis, who learned to make his own shoes and clothes, as an exemplum of self-provision, and Aristotle (as reported by him) on flattery; but the temperament he gives is his own and half-confessional: "a little natural pride, an impatience at being refused", idleness and freedom. He admits he avoids receiving more than he seeks to give, and that he would, if great, rather please than profit. This resonates with the self-as-subject thread ([[self-construction-through-writing]]), though here the self is a fortress against dependence, with the hedge that even self-possession is partly "defective and borrowed".

**Civil war as domestic insecurity.** He says plainly that he has gone to bed fearing betrayal and murder, and that civil war makes "us stand sentinels in our own houses". Custom, he says, is "a great bounty of nature, which benumbs our senses to the sufferance of many evils" (the text reads "out senses"), a small instance of habit as the machinery that makes intolerable conditions bearable (compare [[habit-and-identity]]). He consoles himself with indifference and a fantasy of sudden death, preferring open enemies to the treacherous, and "an enemy in arms, than an enemy in a gown": legal cover makes cruelty worse. The Latin verse here is untranslated in the unit and carries the lament about a country never at peace; the gloss on its content is Montaigne's own prose.

**Cosmopolitanism, with limits.** He is a Frenchman "only through" Paris, loved "even to her warts and blemishes", and fears for her only from her own divisions. Yet he "look[s] upon all men as my compatriots", insists that chosen friendships beat those of climate or blood, and compares attachment to country to the Persian kings who drank only from one river. He explicitly holds back from Socrates' more extreme examples (preferring banishment-as-worse-than-death, refusing escape in order to obey corrupt laws): some surpass "the force of my action" and "my judgment". This is Montaigne weighing a quoted model, not adopting it.

**Travel and bodily self-report.** Travel is "a better school wherein to model life" than any other, by exposing the soul to "the diversity of so many other lives, fancies, and usances"; he gives concrete details (riding eight or ten hours despite the stone, travelling by night in heat, the Spanish fashion of one long stage, watering horses). He travels without destination: "I only walk for the walk's sake", with the aim only "to keep myself in motion". His chief regret is that he must always plan to return to "the common humour". The disposition is anti-teleological and fits the essay's drift as a form (see [[personal-essay-as-form]]).

**Absence, marriage and friendship.** He argues, against friends' criticism, that a house is best left once it can run without him; he demands "economical virtue" of wives and says he leaves his own to govern his affairs. Absence refreshes conjugal affection, and fruition "principally appertain[s] to the imagination". The sorites about near and far (a woman telling her husband what step ends the near) turns into a small sceptical point: nature gave us "no cognition of boundaries" (the Latin tag, untranslated). The remarks on wives reflect his own social assumptions and are self-report, not argument from his sources. Of true friendship he says he gives himself more than he draws a friend to him, and that with his absent friend they "better filled and further extended the possession of life in being parted".

**Age and a private death.** Youth should submit to common opinion; the old have "too much ado to please ourselves alone". He would sooner die "on horseback than in bed", far from the crowd of mourners that "chokes" the dying, wants a wise friend or a stranger who will "scratch him just in the place where he itches", and does not mean to put on a show of constancy, since reputation will then be gone. He sets this against Roman custom as he reports it. Dying is "the act of one single person". This is hedged: he is "of a lower form" than the Stoic ideal of disdainful vigour and hides from death "not by fear, but by art".

## Notable passages

**Obligation never dissolves.** The passage beginning "I have nothing mine but myself" moves from self-sufficiency through the exempla of Bajazet, the Emperor of Calicut and Hippias to the claim that favours are "sometimes, peradventure, fully paid, but ... never dissolved". It is the core of his economy of gifts and debts: giving is ambitious and authoritative, accepting a quality of submission.

**Sentinels in our own houses.** His account of nights spent expecting murder, and of custom numbing misery, is a first-person record of what the Wars of Religion did to domestic safety.

**All men as compatriots.** Montaigne's rejection of narrow national ties, with the Persian-river image of self-imposed limits ("Nature has placed us in the world free and unbound; we imprison ourselves in certain straits"), set against his tenderness for Paris.

**Walking for the walk's sake.** The image of those who "run at base" rather than after a hare gives a purposeless, divisible design for life and journey, "every day concludes my expectation".

**Absence enriches friendship.** The argument that presence "relaxes your attention" and that distance makes the conjunction of wills "more rich", with the Stoic image of sages feeling one another's finger-lifts as a quoted foil he does not endorse literally.

**A solitary death.** The close of the slice: no show of constancy for an audience, a death "involved within itself, quiet, solitary, and all my own".
