# Of ambassadors, fear, and the judging of a life by its end

Three brief essays (Book 1, chapters 16-18) in Cotton's translation. Chapter 16 opens with Montaigne's habit of steering each companion or author toward the subject he knows best, then uses a passage in Langey's history, where French ambassadors withheld Charles V's insults from their king, to weigh a servant's duty to report everything against the discretion an envoy needs. Chapter 17 is a catalogue of what fear does to men, ending with Montaigne's admission that he fears fear itself. Chapter 18 retells the Croesus and Solon story and a run of falls of the great, then offers Montaigne's own reading: death is the one scene where the mask comes off, so the dying is the assay of a whole life.

## Themes

**Learn from the expert in his own province (ch. 16).** Montaigne reports it as his own practice to "put my company upon those subjects they are the best able to speak of". Against it he sets the common vanity of preferring another man's province, with Caesar the engineer and the elder Dionysius the poet as exempla borrowed from the ancients. The same rule governs his reading of histories: weigh each author by profession (physicians on air and health, lawyers on laws, ambassadors on negotiations). This is source criticism by expertise.

**Obedience versus discretion (ch. 16).** Montaigne argues both sides. First he holds that an ambassador should have faithfully reported everything so that "the liberty of selecting, disposing, judging, and concluding" stayed with the king, and that obedience matters more to a superior than the cleverness of his agents; the Crassus whipping of the Greek engineer is the exemplum. He then qualifies it: strict implicit obedience is due only to "positive and limited commands", ambassadors "form and model their master's pleasure", and the Persian kings' tight rein is criticized as harmful. The essay ends on a question, not a verdict. It touches a legibility problem, in which a principal must see everything to command, though the text never frames it so (see [[Legibility]]).

**Fear as the breaker of judgment (ch. 17).** Montaigne says he is no naturalist and cannot say how fear works, but reports the physicians' view that no passion sooner dethrones judgment. He supports it with soldiers' panics (ensigns running at the enemy, a man dead without a wound, armies running opposite ways), fear that paralyzes, fear that produces a "valiant despair", and fear that suspends even grief. His own view: "The thing in the world I am most afraid of is fear". He also says anticipated loss is worse than actual poverty or exile, and that fear is "more importunate and insupportable than death itself". The Greek "panic terrors" at Carthage is reported as a kind of fear sent from heaven.

**Judge a life by its last act (ch. 18).** Croesus, Agesilaus on Priam, and the fates of Pompey, the Macedonian kings and Ludovico Sforza illustrate the mutability of fortune, and Montaigne even entertains envious powers above. Then he gives his own, deeper reading of Solon: a philosopher cares little for fortune, so Solon meant that true felicity, a tranquil and well-ordered soul, can be shown only in the last scene, where "there is no more counterfeiting". Death is the "assay of the fruit of all my studies". He hedges: he has seen three execrable men die composed, and God orders things as He pleases. His personal conclusion is that his chief concern is to "die well--that is, patiently and tranquilly". The pull toward self-testing resonates with [[self-construction-through-writing]], though here the test is death and not the page.

## Notable passages

**Speak of your own trade (c1).** The opening statement of method, with Caesar and Dionysius as negative examples.

**The ambassadors who concealed (c2).** The Langey passage and Montaigne's judgment that the servant should report everything and leave the judging to the master; he adds that he himself would not wish to be so served.

**The counter-case (c3).** Positive limited commands versus the wide discretion of ambassadors; shows Montaigne reversing himself within the chapter.

**Afraid of fear (c4).** Pompey's friends fleeing before grieving is his example of fear overriding even the most just affliction.

**Solon's further aim (c6).** The pivot of ch. 18, from fortune's reversals to the soul's self-disclosure at death.

**The master-day (c7).** Death as the judge of all prior years, with Epaminondas's reply, and Montaigne's wish to die well.
