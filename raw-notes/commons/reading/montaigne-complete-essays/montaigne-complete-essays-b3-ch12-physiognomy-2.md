# Dying Well Without Rehearsal: Civil War, Plague, and Socrates

This slice of Of Physiognomy (the middle of three parts) opens on Montaigne's own situation: a France in dissolution for thirty years, which he claims to watch as an instructive "spectacle of our public death", and his own modest sufferings in it, culminating in a plague that ruins his household and his lands. From the plague he draws the central observation: his rustic neighbours met death with a plain steadiness that learned consolation cannot match. That becomes an attack on the philosophical "preparation for death" (premeditatio mortis), which he judges to torment more than death does, and a case that nature teaches dying when the time comes. The slice closes with Socrates' speech to his judges, quoted at length and praised as the pattern of an unartificial, truthful defence. The slice begins and ends mid-essay; it does not contain the essay's opening on physiognomy.

## Themes

**Civil war as spectacle and as self-report.** Montaigne says every Frenchman is hourly on the brink of ruin, and that he takes a curious, half-theatrical interest in watching his country's decline, comparing it to the pleasure of tragedy. He is candid about his own cheapness of patience for public evils that do not hit him privately, and about the hedge that his affection, being dispersed, is weaker. He also qualifies the loss: the "health" fallen from was already corrupt, and he finds corruption in office worse than brigandage in a wood. The pose is not heroic; he says the convulsion "animated" rather than pressed him, helped by a good conscience and good health, and he disclaims any wish to provoke Fortune.

**Plague and the fear that outruns the disease.** The plague is an aggravation "in the tail of the rest": his house is frightful to him, his family a distracted caravan, and he serves six months as its guide. He observes that imagination and quarantine rules turn fear of disease into disease, and that he carries his own antidotes, "resolution and patience". He rates death by plague as not the worst kind: short, without ceremony, consoled by the public condition.

**The peasants' death and custom.** The villagers renounce care of life, leave the grapes on the vines, and await death with composure; Montaigne notes that their resolution is fragile and depends on company and on the hours' difference, and that the only worry he saw was burial. Customs of other peoples (the Neorites feeding their dead to beasts, men digging their own graves, Roman soldiers after Cannae burying their heads) show how varied and how powerful common usage is: a whole province reached, by usage, a courage equal to "the most studied and premeditated resolution". This is custom as invisible machinery, though here it works to make death bearable rather than to deform judgment.

**Nature versus the artifice of learning.** Science, he says, has abandoned Nature and now borrows patterns of constancy from the unlearned and from beasts; men have "sophisticated" nature as perfumers do oils, so that she is "variable and particular to each". Even beasts swerve, but only at the length of the halter. This is a recognisable nature-versus-artifice argument, in which reason's endless novelty erases the universal face of nature. Note it is paired with qualifications: he later asks whether the peasants' patience is merely stolidity, and says that if so, we should "teach nothing but ignorance", leaving the dilemma partly open.

**Against premeditating death.** He quotes Latin maxims and a severe-sect master (reported, not Montaigne's own) that anticipating evils costs the present for fear of the future; he adds that preparation torments more than the thing, that nature will teach us to die, and that "we prepare ourselves against the preparations of death". Philosophy, like physicians who throw us into diseases to have work for their drugs, creates the anxiety it then treats. The Stoic and Ciceronian line that the whole life of philosophers is a rehearsal for death is stated (in Latin) and countered: death is the end of life, not its aim; life's true study is to "order, govern, and suffer itself", with dying a light article of knowing how to live. Caesar's preference for the least premeditated death is reported in support.

**Socrates as the model of unadorned truth.** Montaigne gives Socrates' speech (Montaigne's rendering of the trial, as he introduces it "as I remember") in which Socrates professes ignorance of death, weighs it as either removal to better company or a sound dreamless sleep, avoids known evils, will not supplicate, and entrusts himself to the gods. Montaigne calls it "an innocent child's pleading of an unimaginable loftiness", preferable to Lysias's polished defence, and notes the Athenians' later revulsion toward his accusers, who hanged themselves. The same Socrates invoked as simple teacher here is the figure who elsewhere models self-knowledge; compare [[socratic-thinking]], though here the emphasis is on Socratic plainness before death rather than on method.

## Notable passages

The opening paragraphs (c1) show Montaigne's mix of candour and aesthetic distance toward public catastrophe, and the line that historians skip calm narration for sedition and war. The plague and peasants passage (c2) is the empirical core: what he saw, with its detail of the untouched grapes and the labourer pulling earth over himself. The nature-versus-science passage (c3) gives the project-relevant claim that learning has dissolved nature's constant face. The "we trouble life by the care of death" passage (c4) is the essay's most quoted formulation on the aim of life. The peasant-versus-Aristotle passage (c5) gives his equivocal inference to "stolidity" and ignorance. Socrates' speech (c6) is the exemplum he holds up, long enough to be cited whole.
