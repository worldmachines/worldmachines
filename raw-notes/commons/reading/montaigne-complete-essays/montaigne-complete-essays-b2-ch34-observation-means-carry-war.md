# Caesar as pattern of the military art

Montaigne opens by noting that great captains have favoured particular books (Alexander with Homer, Scipio with Xenophon, Charles V with Comines, and, he says, Machiavelli in his own day), and endorses Marshal Strozzi's choice of Caesar, whose writings he judges unmatched "as to that business". He then announces that he will set down "some rare and particular passages" of Caesar's wars "that remain in my memory" -- so the chapter is a loose, memory-driven string of exempla rather than an argued treatise. Most of the content is report of Caesar's conduct, supplemented by Montaigne's own comparisons (Caesar against Alexander, Caesar's later caution) and two digressions on the siege of Alexia.

## Themes

**Managing the army's mind.** Caesar inflated the enemy's strength to his frightened troops rather than lowering it, following Cyrus in Xenophon; he kept his designs secret until the point of execution and changed orders if the men guessed them; he stalled the Swiss envoys to gain time to assemble. Command here is information control over one's own side as well as the enemy.

**Speed and personal exposure.** Montaigne dwells on Caesar's promptitude (the catalogue of campaigns from Gaul to Spain, Egypt, Pontus, Africa), his reconnoitring in person, swimming rivers, and crossing seas alone. He says that in many exploits he reads "a determinate resolution to throw himself away to avoid the shame of being overcome" -- this is Montaigne's own interpretation, offered hesitantly ("methinks"), not something Caesar says.

**Caesar versus Alexander; the ageing of ambition.** Montaigne finds Caesar "a little more temperate and considerate" than the impetuous, wine-inflamed young Alexander, and reports (via Oppius) that Caesar grew slower with age, unwilling to risk accumulated glory on one blow of fortune. He glosses the Italian jibe "Bisognosi d'onore" and argues there may be "some moderation, some satiety" in the appetite for glory. Yet he also records Caesar's saying that men "must embark, and not deliberate, upon high enterprises" and that he preferred victory by counsel to victory by force: the portrait holds boldness and calculation together without resolving them.

**Discipline, indulgence and affection.** Caesar demanded only valour and punished chiefly mutiny, let troops run licence after victories, dressed them in gilded armour so they would defend it, called them fellow-soldiers (which Augustus abolished as beneath imperial dignity), yet cashiered the ninth legion. The soldiers' devotion (self-financed levies, a cohort holding four legions, Scaeva, Granius Petronius's suicide, the women of Salona) is offered as evidence that "passion has a more absolute command over us than reason".

**Limits on means.** Caesar was free of the old Roman scruple of winning only by pure valour, yet "more conscientious than we should be in these days": he refused to exploit a skirmish during a parley with Ariovistus lest he be charged with treachery. Montaigne sets this beside his own age's lower standards, though he elsewhere notes Caesar was not scrupulous about exploiting treaties.

**Montaigne's own tactical judgments.** On Alexia he observes the Gauls' decision to dismiss part of their host and concludes that armies should be of moderate size, citing Cyrus, Bajazet, and Scanderbeg; and he argues that Vercingetorix, as overall commander, erred by shutting himself in a single city instead of staying free to govern the whole. These are Montaigne's reasoning from examples, stated with "it stands to reason".

**Attribution.** The facts come from Caesar's own Commentaries, Suetonius, Oppius, and other ancient sources, remembered rather than checked; Montaigne flags this ("if we may believe Suetonius", "I do not at present remember"). The remark on Machiavelli is hearsay ("'tis said"), not an assessment. For the author see [[michel-de-montaigne]]; for the mention of Machiavelli see [[niccolo-machiavelli]].

## Notable passages

The opening anecdote of Caesar exaggerating Juba's forces shows deception of one's own troops, with the reasoning that a pleasant surprise (finding an enemy weaker) matters less than an unpleasant one. The secrecy-and-surprise paragraph extends this to routine command. The block on Caesar's daring contains Montaigne's most personal reading of him and the maxim about embarking rather than deliberating. The Alexia digression holds his own doctrine on army size, and the later passage on Caesar's slowing with age pairs it with a reflection on moderation in the thirst for glory. The Ariovistus scruple is the chapter's one moral qualification on Caesar's methods. The chapter ends abruptly on the Salona sortie, which Montaigne calls unique in his memory as a besieged garrison's total victory.
