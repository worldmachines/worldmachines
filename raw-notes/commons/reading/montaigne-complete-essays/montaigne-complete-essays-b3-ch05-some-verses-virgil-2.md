# Marriage, Desire and the Double Standard

This slice (the second of five of 'Upon Some Verses of Virgil') opens mid-argument on marriage and runs through a sustained, often ironic defence of women against the sexual rules men write for them. Montaigne first confesses that he took a wife through custom and 'extrinsic occasions' rather than choice, yet holds that once bound a man owes the duty. He then reads Virgil's picture of a marriage with accord but little loyalty, separates love (pleasure, sting, difficulty) from marriage (utility, justice, honour, constancy), and turns to the contradiction in male demands on women: men believe them more ardent than themselves, make them hot and well fed, and still enjoin chastity under extreme penalties. A long stretch of learned exempla (philosophers' erotic treatises, phallic rites, the codpiece, the gelding of statues) argues that sexuality is universal and that concealment breeds worse fancies. The slice ends with praise of the difficulty of female chastity, advice on courtship and reputation, and contempt for men who boast of favours.

## Themes

**Marriage by custom, not choice.** Montaigne reports of himself that he hates 'all sorts of obligation and restraint' and would not have married Wisdom herself, yet 'the most of my actions are guided by example, not by choice'. He says he has kept the laws of marriage more strictly than he promised or expected. His rule is a hedged middle: a man should manage his liberty prudently beforehand, but once he has submitted he must keep within common duty, and at least love and acknowledge it ('treachery to marry without espousing'). He rejects the women's maxim of serving a husband while guarding against him, and notes that hating superstition does not oblige one to run to irreligion. This is a close-up of custom as the machinery that carries a self into an institution (see [[habit-and-identity]]), reported with his usual irony about his own reputation as a libertine.

**Love and marriage as different ends.** Reading Virgil's couple, he argues the two designs 'have several paths leading to them', that few who make a wife of a mistress do not repent it, and that love lives on pleasure 'inflamed by difficulty' whereas marriage has 'utility, justice, honour, and constancy'. The Socratic remark about Athens (everyone likes to visit, no one to inhabit) frames the point. He adds a note against husbands who hate wives merely because they themselves wrong them.

**The double standard exposed.** Montaigne observes that women do not make the rules ('the men make them without their help'), that men credit them with greater erotic capacity (via the Tiresias priest, the Roman Emperor and Empress, the queen of Arragon's ruling of six times a day, Solon's three a month), and yet demand continence as if it were worse than parricide. He also says they are trained from infancy for love and that governesses imprint the very idea they forbid, as shown by his daughter and the word 'fouteau'. He takes the rhetoric of male honour apart: men fear the shame of a wife's vice more than their own and 'weigh vices, not according to nature, but according to our interest'. Attribution care: the opinions on female appetite are largely reported from authors and 'the doctors', which Montaigne uses against male hypocrisy; he is not simply asserting them in his own voice (and he says he believes 'no miracles out of religion' about the Cataluna wife).

**Nature, concealment and artifice.** The long catalogue of philosophers' books on love, nations that deified or ritually offered the sexual member, the codpiece, and the censor who gelded statues builds a case that sexuality is general and sacred to many cultures, so that prudish concealment is arbitrary. He argues that 'it were a chaster and more fruitful usage to let them know the fact as it is betimes', and that fancy substitutes parts 'three times more extravagant'. Plato, Livia, and Lacedaemonian practice appear as exempla of familiarity cooling desire, but he also reports Augustine's worry about nudity at the resurrection. This is a case of customary relativism ([[personal-essay-as-form]] in tone, the cultural examples in the spirit of [[noble-savage]] discourse, though Montaigne here is mostly drawing on antiquity and reported peoples), and the position wavers: he allows that an 'entire appetite is more sharp than one already half-glutted by the eyes'.

**Chastity as the hardest virtue; gallantry and reputation.** He says he does not know whether 'the exploits of Alexander and Caesar' surpass a young woman's resolution and that carrying armour for life is easier than keeping a maidenhead. He tells women to wear the difficulty as glory, advises men that a refusal of chastity should not end pursuit, and says 'there lies on the frontier some space free, indifferent, and neuter'. Rarity, not quantity, gives a favour its value, and reputation is eventually restored by constancy (Plato's 'Let them talk'). He closes by condemning the new fashion of boasting about favours. The advice to women is partly tongue-in-cheek and sits uneasily with his critique of the double standard; the slice does not resolve that tension.

## Notable passages

The first chunk (c1) is Montaigne's self-report on marrying by custom and keeping its duty. c2 states the contradiction in male demands of female continence. c3 uses his daughter and the 'fouteau' anecdote to argue prohibition teaches what it forbids. c4 is the clearest statement of the interest-based weighing of vices and the shame men feel more over wives' faults than their own. c5 reverses the usual ranking of virtues, treating chastity as the hardest labour. c6 sets reputation, constancy and the decline of discretion in speech about women, with Plato's retort as exemplum. Note that the philosophers' book list, the Priapic rites and the codpiece digression are mostly exempla and anecdote from ancient authors, and the Latin verse quotations are untranslated in this text. The broader frame of [[michel-de-montaigne]] as a writer who treats his own habits as evidence applies here, since marriage is discussed through personal report rather than doctrine.
