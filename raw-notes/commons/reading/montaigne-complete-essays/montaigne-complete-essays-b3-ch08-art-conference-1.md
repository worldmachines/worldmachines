# Montaigne on Conference: Disputation, Impatience and the Uses of Learning (first half)

The essay opens by presenting Montaigne's own faults as a public example, as a hanged man is a warning to others, and then makes conversation the "most fruitful and natural exercise of the mind". Most of this slice (the first of two) is a study of how disputes go well or badly. Montaigne welcomes contradiction from strong minds, hates disorderly and passionate argument, turns on the scholastic logicians and the book-bound learned, and then accuses his own impatience as a vice. He ends by moving to a different register: the way gravity, robes and office lend false authority to idle words, and the way "experience" is reckoned rather than digested. The slice ends mid-argument, on the burden that rulers owe to those they govern.

## Themes

**Self-accusation as public service.** Montaigne models his self-portrait on judicial example: "we do not correct the man we hang; we correct others by him". He may do good "in making my manners avoided". He also notes that accusations of oneself are believed and praises are not. Learning by contrariety (the bad harpist's neighbour, the awkward rider) is a method he claims for himself: "The time is now proper for us to reform backward". This is the essay as a technology of self-knowledge, with the hedge that he proposes to himself "impracticable measures". It resonates with [[self-construction-through-writing]] and [[personal-essay-as-form]], though here the self is displayed as a negative exemplar rather than a model.

**Conversation over books.** Books are "a languishing and feeble motion that heats not"; conversation "teaches and exercises at once". A rough disputant stimulates him "to something above myself". The corollary is that commerce with weak spirits degrades the mind like a contagion. He disclaims competitive parade as unbecoming. The Italians, Athenians and Romans are cited as keeping the practice up.

**Openness to opinion, and its basis.** Montaigne says his mind is "a soil very unfit for penetration", so no belief offends him. This follows from having deprived judgment "of the right of determining" (a sceptical posture), and he even lets an old wife's dreams tip an empty scale. The openness has a hedge: the vulgar opinion imports "inanity, but they import that", and refusing a hearing risks "the vice of obstinacy, to avoid that of superstition".

**The ethics of being corrected.** He loves frank, "quarrelsome" friendship, gladly takes correction in conference rather than authority, and prefers being known and judged. He is prouder of beating himself in argument than of beating an opponent. He prefers company that ruffles him to company that fears or flatters him. The Socrates exemplum is turned: Socrates' smile at contradiction might merely reflect his certain advantage, whereas it is the weaker who ought to welcome correction. Montaigne reports this as his own judgment and as one he knows his contemporaries do not share.

**Order over force; the pathology of disputes.** What he asks of a dispute is order and understanding, as in shepherds' and shop-boys' wranglings, not subtlety. He catalogues the failure modes (one flies east, another west; catchers at words, railers, preface-mongers, circumvallation by dialectic formulas). He admits that in irregular disputes he himself falls into "wilful, malicious, and imperious" argument and is ashamed. He suggests disputes should be "interdicted and punished" and that the fruit of disputation is "to lose and annihilate truth". Plato (as reported) bars fools from the exercise.

**Distrust of scholastic learning.** The disorder of disputation leads him to doubt whether the sciences yield "any solid fruit for the service of life". Logic has not produced understanding; masters of arts are none the better, "being the more learned, they are none the less fools". He loves knowledge in its "true use", but hates those who "appeal from their understanding to their memory" and "can do nothing but by book". Learning improves fortunes, not minds, and is "in the hand of some 'tis a sceptre, in that of others a fool's bauble". This links to the WMP theme of judgment against stored learning, and to [[socratic-thinking]].

**Inquiry as the human business.** Socrates, in Plato and Xenophon, disputes "more in favour of the disputants than in favour of the dispute", aiming to clear understanding. Montaigne's claim: "To hunt after truth is properly our business"; possession "belongs to a greater power". He reads authors for manner not subject and seeks out wits "that I may know him". This is a fallibilist posture, truth located "in the divine knowledge", and is not a full scepticism.

**Turning the accusation inward.** Impatience at the world's folly is itself "equally vicious" in the right and in the wrong, since "we wound ourselves with our own weapons". Examples: Miso laughing alone, the man mocking another's genealogy while extolling his wife's pedigree, Plato's saying and Socrates' precept that one should present oneself first to justice. He distinguishes: one need not be clean to reprove, but judgment should not spare oneself. A reproof is true even when its source is guilty.

**Appearances, authority and experience.** Man is "wonderfully corporal", so politics consists largely in ceremony and superficial appearance. Montaigne remarks that those who wanted "a contemplative and immaterial" religion should not wonder if it survived chiefly as "a mark, title, and instrument of division and faction" (his report on the religious wars, kept at the level of observation). Robes, offices and fortune lend reputation to vain words; those who crush others with "experience" must "weigh, sort and distil" it as a surgeon must. He hates "all sorts of tyranny, both in word and deed", and observes that the learned are often fools because knowledge is a heavy burden for weak natures. The people who govern must be "infinitely above us" if they promise more. This partly rhymes with [[Legibility]]-style concerns about the performance of authority, though Montaigne's frame is personal and moral, not systemic.

## Notable passages

**Conversation as the best exercise of the mind (c1).** The programmatic statement: he would sooner lose sight than hearing and speech. It sets the essay's standards: strong disputants, few of them, in private and not as spectacle.

**Contradiction rouses rather than offends (c2).** The ideal of frank friendship, "like love in biting and scratching". The passage is both a self-portrait and a norm (and, per the next paragraph, one his contemporaries do not meet).

**Against the schools (c3).** The attack on logic and the master of arts, with the juggler comparison: dexterity imposes on the senses but does not work on belief. The passage uses Latin tags (untranslated in the text), and its point is plain from the prose.

**Learning, the sceptre and the bauble (c4).** The distinction between true use of knowledge and bookish dependence, ending in the sceptre/bauble image.

**Truth as hunt, not possession (c5).** The central claim about inquiry: "we are born to inquire after truth".

**Plato's saying (c6).** The turn inward: unsoundness perceived in others reflects on oneself. Montaigne calls it "a wise and divine saying".

**Digesting experience (c7).** Experience must be sorted and distilled; this anticipates the essay "Of Experience" only in theme, and this slice does not say more.
