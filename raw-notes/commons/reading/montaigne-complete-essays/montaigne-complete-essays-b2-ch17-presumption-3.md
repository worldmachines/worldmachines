# Montaigne's confession of ignorance, irresolution and self-esteem (Of Presumption, closing section)

This is the final slice of the essay on presumption, picking up mid-confession. Montaigne lists his own deficiencies (slow wit, poor at games, ignorant of farming and household things), accepts that his project is trivial so long as it is true to what he is, and then turns his irresolution into a general remark on how reasons cut both ways. That leads to a conservative argument about political change, then to a reflection on why everyone thinks well of their own judgment, his own inward-turned habit, and the second half of presumption (undervaluing others). The slice closes with an attack on bookish education, a catalogue of admirable contemporaries, a tribute to Marie de Gournay, and a one-line verdict that only valour is plentiful in the civil wars.

## Themes

**Self-portrait through deficiency.** Montaigne gives his lacks as matter for the book: he cannot "cast accounts", cannot tell cabbage from lettuce, and was lately caught out over leaven and the vat, though he is lord of an estate. He does not apologise for the triviality of the subject; the standard he claims is fidelity, "whatever I deliver myself to be, provided it be such as I really am, I have my end". The essays are the exercise of a judgment that "does not contradict itself". He also justifies self-portraiture by analogy with the king who drew his own portrait in crayon. Compare [[self-construction-through-writing]] and [[personal-essay-as-form]], though Montaigne presents it as a modest, even foolish project rather than a method.

**Irresolution and the two-edged reason.** He can "maintain an opinion" but "cannot choose one": for whichever sect he leans toward, he finds enough likelihood to fix him there. He delays, then trusts to chance (the die, the casting of lots in scripture), and prefers to follow someone more confident than himself. Reason is "a two-edged and dangerous sword", even in Socrates' hands. Yet he does not change easily, since he sees the same weakness in the contrary opinion. This is his own report of his temperament, hedged by his admission that it is a defect for public business.

**Conservatism about government.** Political debates, he says, are endless because arguments rest only on experience and the variety of events; Machiavelli is cited as an example of a writer easy to contradict (cf. [[niccolo-machiavelli]]). His view, stated as "in my conceit", is that any government that is ancient and constant is better than change; the worst thing is instability. He would, if he could, "put something under to stop the wheel". Reformers find it easy to destroy and hard to build better. The reasoning is explicitly pragmatic and the surrounding sentences admit that manners and laws are corrupt and in places monstrous. It also touches how custom stabilises order (cf. [[habit-and-identity]]).

**Everyone thinks well of their own judgment.** Sense is the one faculty nobody admits lacking, so confessing a defect of it is self-cancelling. We concede others' advantage in courage or knowledge but not in judgment. He sketches his readers as the learned (who value only erudition), the vulgar, and a third rare sort not worth courting. His own opinions he takes to be sound partly because he esteems himself little. He turns his sight inward while others look outward, "I circulate in myself", and claims that his strongest notions were "born with me" and later fortified by ancient authority. He seeks reputation in "order, correspondence, and tranquillity of opinions and manners", not in brilliance.

**The second half of presumption: esteem for others.** He admits he finds little worthy of admiration, perhaps from long company with the ancients, perhaps because the age is poor. He praises friends and enemies truthfully (the Persian custom), "my affection alters, my judgment does not". He singles out Etienne de la Boetie as the greatest natural soul he knew. The people he lists as notable (Guise, Strozzi, L'Hospital, Turnebus, Ronsard, Montmorency, La Noue) are his own report and judgment of his time. Gournay is described as an adopted daughter and as promising capable of a sacred friendship; this is his own estimate of her.

**Against learned education.** The learned are as vain and weak in judgment as anyone, honouring "Cicero, Galen, Ulpian, and St. Jerome alike". Education aims to make us learned, not good: we "know how to decline Virtue" but not to love it, and are taught its genealogy without friendship with it. He prefers the rude plainness of country people, and the example of Polemon reformed by one lecture of Xenocrates is offered as what education should do. Compare the standard Montaigne theme of judgment over stored learning.

## Notable passages

The passage on ignorance of husbandry (c1) is the clearest case of self-exposure turned into method. The irresolution passage (c2) is his own report, with its comparison to casting lots. The political passage (c3) is the most quotable statement of his preference for stability, with the sentence "Obedience is never pure nor calm in him who reasons and disputes"; note that it sits beside admissions that existing laws can be barbarous. The "nobody thinks himself lacking sense" passage (c4) is an argument about the structural blind spot of self-judgment and about audiences for books. The inward-turn passage (c5) is a direct statement of the self as sole business. The education passage (c6) is the unit's sharpest critique of pedantry.
