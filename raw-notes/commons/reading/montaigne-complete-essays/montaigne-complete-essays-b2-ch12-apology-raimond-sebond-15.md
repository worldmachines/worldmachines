# The senses, the regress of judgment, and the flux of being (Apology for Raimond Sebond, closing section)

This is the final part of the long Apology. It opens by showing the senses are "masters of our reason" (the musket-ball illusion, the philosopher in the cage, music, dress and desire, passion distorting sight), then turns to the diversity of perception across animals, ages, health and disease, and drops the claim that we can know things as they are rather than as they appear. It next argues that no judge can be found who is not a party to the dispute, and that any instrument or reason needing verification falls into infinite regress. It ends with a long passage on universal flux (Plato, Heraclitus, Epicharmus, the Stoics), attributed in its closing quoted stretch to a pagan whose "religious conclusion" is that God alone truly is, and Montaigne's own final verdict: man cannot lift himself above humanity; only God's "extraordinary hand" can, and that belongs to Christian faith, not Stoic virtue. [[michel-de-montaigne]]

## Themes

**The senses overrule reason.** Montaigne (in his own voice, with personal reports: his dizziness on mountain precipices, his susceptibility to sung verse) holds that knowing a thing to be false does not stop the senses compelling belief. The feeling of pain "subverts and overthrows all those fine Stoical resolutions", so Stoic indifference to colic is the target. Exempla borrowed from the poets (Narcissus, Pygmalion), from Theophrastus, and from the story of Gracchus's piping prompter support the point. He argues the philosopher who blinded himself should by the same rule have stopped his ears and ultimately all his senses, "that is to say, of his life and being".

**Senses and soul deceive one another; waking is a kind of dreaming.** Passion falsifies what we see; the mind's preoccupation blinds us to open things. Montaigne speculates ("perhaps") that those who compared life to a dream were more right than they knew, and asks why we should not doubt whether waking is "a certain kind of sleep". This is his reasoning, hedged and not asserted outright.

**No privileged human standpoint.** Beasts have keener senses (Democritus reported); what poisons one creature feeds another (spittle and the serpent, Pliny's sea-hares); jaundice, hyposphagma, pressed eyes and coloured theatre curtains alter appearances. Montaigne asks who could judge between beasts and men, or between healthy and sick: "Which shall we believe, the fish of the man, or the man of the fish?" Health is just another condition that stamps things with its own character. Snow may appear white to us, but asserting it is so "in its own essence" is more than we can justify, and "all the knowledge in the world must of necessity fall to ruin" (a conditional drawn from a Lucretian crooked-rule image).

**Regress and the judge that never was.** A fit judge would need to be exempt from age, youth, health, sickness, waking and sleep, so "a judge that never was". Instruments need demonstration, demonstration needs instruments, "and here we are round again upon the wheel"; reason rests only on other reason "to all infinity". Appearances belong to the passion of the sense, not to the subject, and the soul cannot verify resemblance, as one who never knew Socrates cannot say his portrait is like him. This is the classical Pyrrhonist trope of the criterion, here given in Montaigne's plain-spoken Cotton English. It resonates faintly with WMP's concern with legibility (instruments that make the world readable) though Montaigne's point is that every measure is itself uncertain, not a political critique.

**Flux and the vanishing present.** Both judge and judged are in continual motion. The passage on being "always in the middle, betwixt being born and dying", grasping water, then a catalogue of flux doctrines (Plato's "bodies had never any existence, but only birth", Pythagoras, the Stoics' no-present-time, Heraclitus's river, Epicharmus's debtor). A quoted speaker (the long section in quotation marks, framed afterwards by Montaigne as the "religious conclusion of a pagan") concludes that we should not fear one death when we have passed through many, that time's "now" splits into future and past, and that only God "with one sole now fills the for ever". Attribution matters: the doctrine of God's sole being is voiced by the quoted pagan, while Montaigne endorses it as a conclusion while limiting its use.

**Fideist close.** Montaigne cites another pagan saying man is vile "if he do not raise himself above humanity", calls it "a good word and a profitable desire, but withal absurd", comparing it to making the handle bigger than the hand. Man can be exalted only if "God will lend him an extraordinary hand", by renouncing his own means. "It belongs to our Christian faith, and not to the stoical virtue", to pretend to that metamorphosis. This is the fideist hinge of the Apology: reason's failure is the occasion for grace, not a licence for despair.

## Notable passages

The opening musket-ball example and Stoic pain (c1) fix the claim that sense rules reason, and pit Montaigne against Stoic self-sufficiency. The cage on Notre Dame (c2) is his best embodied argument, with first-person testimony. The dream passage (c3) is a hedged sceptical thought-experiment. The spittle and sea-hare run (c4) displays perspectival relativism across species. The regress passage (c5) is the epistemological core. The water, being and Heraclitus section (c6) states the flux thesis, and the closing fideist turn (c7) fixes how Montaigne handles scepticism without dissolving into it.

For WMP, the flux argument rhymes with the decay-and-change vision of institutions as processes rather than things, though here it is a metaphysical claim about being, and Montaigne uses it to point to divine eternity rather than to historical cycles.
