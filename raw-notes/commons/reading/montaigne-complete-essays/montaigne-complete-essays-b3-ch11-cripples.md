# Of Cripples: reason that explains what never happened

The essay opens with the ten days struck from the French calendar: Montaigne's neighbours still sow and reap on the old schedule, and the reform shows how uncertain even our measure of time is. From this he turns to the "free and roving" nature of reason, which rushes to explain how a thing happens before asking whether it happens. He describes how rumours and miracles grow as they pass from hand to hand, admits that he himself exaggerates when heated, and gives local cases (a gouty prince cured by imagination, a village ghost hoax run by three young people). He then argues that the abuses of the world come from fear of professing ignorance, recommends the tentative idiom of "it seems to me" and "what does this mean?", and applies this to witch trials, recounting his own examination of accused witches and concluding they needed hellebore, not hemlock. After a disclaimer that his talk is discourse, not advice, a seemingly digressive passage on the proverb of the lame mistress (the title's "cripples") serves as his demonstration of how reason supplies explanations for a fiction, and the essay ends with Carneades, Aesop and the claim that sceptic and dogmatist are symmetrical excesses.

## Themes

**Causes before facts.** Montaigne's own judgment: men "more willingly study to find out reasons than to ascertain truth", asking "How is such a thing done?" rather than "Is such a thing done?". Reason "builds as well in the air as on the earth". He sets this against the knowledge of causes as belonging to those who command, not to those who merely undergo and use things. He admits he would often like to answer "there is no such thing" but dares not, since this passes for ignorance or rudeness, so he "juggles for company".

**How falsehood accumulates.** Rumour is a social process: the first teller caulks the weak point of his tale with a false piece, each believer thinks persuading others a work of charity and adds his own invention, so that "the particular error first makes the public error" and the public error the particular one. Montaigne includes himself (he swells his narration when opposed, but surrenders to anyone who asks for the bare truth) and rejects number and antiquity as tests of truth: what he would not believe from one he would not believe from a hundred and one. The frivolous true causes of famous wonders escape notice because we look for weighty ones. Compare the way [[history-machine]] describes paradigms assembling silently and distributedly, though Montaigne's focus is credulity, not infrastructure.

**Ignorance as a form of honesty and a civic remedy.** Montaigne claims the world's abuses stem from being taught to fear confessing ignorance. He favours modest phrases borrowed from Roman testimony, would train children to inquire rather than resolve, and wants courts able to declare "The court understands nothing of the matter". He distinguishes a "strong and generous" ignorance from the ordinary kind. This is close in spirit to [[socratic-thinking]], though Montaigne frames it through judges, witnesses and the Areopagites, not dialogue.

**Witch trials and the moral weight of doubt.** The most pointed political claim: conjecture is priced too high when it burns a person. Montaigne reports his own examination of prisoners before a sovereign prince (he saw confessions and an "insensible mark") and still judged them mad, not criminal. He argues that human testimony suffices for human things, but supernatural claims need supernatural warrant; it is more likely that two men lie than that one flies from east to west. He hedges: he does not untie arguments from experience and fact, he "cuts" them, and he cites Augustine on leaning toward doubt. He also cautions that his own talk is "nothing by way of advice", only a tumultuous and wavering thought, meant to clear judgment, not to compel it.

**The self as the chief marvel.** Amid the talk of miracles he says he has "never seen greater monster or miracle in the world than myself", and that the better he knows himself the more his own deformity astonishes him. Self-study here is an extension of sceptical astonishment, not a source of certainty; see [[michel-de-montaigne]] and [[self-construction-through-writing]].

**Lameness and the all-purpose reason.** The proverb that Venus is only known with a lame mistress, the Amazon queen's "Lame men perform best", and the supposed learned physiological explanations are Montaigne's test case: reasons are forged for a mere saying, and his imagination itself made him believe he had more pleasure with a woman "by reason she was not straight". Tasso and Suetonius draw opposite conclusions from the same horse-riding, and the understanding is "the shoe of Theramenes, fit for all feet". This is Montaigne's own admission against himself, delivered half-jokingly ("Whether it be to the purpose or not").

**Symmetry of dogmatism and doubt.** Carneades, who "eradicated consent from men", arose out of the self-conceit of the professors of knowledge; the Aesop anecdote makes the point that one extreme provoked the other, and "man is immoderate throughout". Montaigne reports this as his opinion about the origin of scepticism, not as an endorsement of it as final.

## Notable passages

The passage on reason's free roving states the essay's thesis (Is it done? before How is it done?). The account of rumour in transit is a compact theory of social epistemics, with Montaigne implicating his own heated speech. The attack on fear of ignorance gives the positive programme: moderating idiom, inquiry for children, and a judicial declaration of non-understanding. The "monster or miracle" sentence ties scepticism to self-knowledge. The witch-examination passage is the practical consequence, with the line about roasting a man alive on mere conjecture. The Aesop anecdote closes the essay by explaining dogmatist and sceptic as mirror-image excesses of pride.
