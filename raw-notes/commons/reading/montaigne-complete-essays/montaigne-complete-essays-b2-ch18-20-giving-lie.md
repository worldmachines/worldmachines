# Of Giving the Lie; Of Liberty of Conscience; That We Taste Nothing Pure

Three consecutive short essays. In "Of Giving the Lie" Montaigne answers the objection that only famous men may write about themselves: his book is a private portrait for friends and kin, and the act of painting himself has shaped him in turn. That leads him to the corruption of truthfulness in his age, why "the lie" is the supreme insult, and why the word is the one bond of society. "Of Liberty of Conscience" opens with the French civil war, warns that good intentions without moderation produce vicious effects, and spends most of its length on a long, fairly balanced portrait of Julian the Apostate, ending on the ambiguity of his policy of toleration. "That We Taste Nothing Pure" argues that pleasure and pain, virtue and vice, justice and injustice, are inseparably mixed, and then turns to the claim that overly subtle minds are poor at managing affairs.

## Themes

**The book as self-making, not monument.** Montaigne disclaims the public statue: the book is for "some corner of a library, or to entertain a neighbour, a kinsman, a friend". He is bold to speak of himself precisely because the subject is poor, so he cannot be suspected of ostentation. He then makes the central claim that the writing is reflexive: composing the portrait made him "temper and compose" himself, so the copy "has in some sort formed itself", and "I have no more made my book than my book has made me". This is a self-report of the essay as a technology of self-knowledge ([[self-construction-through-writing]], [[personal-essay-as-form]]). He hedges: painting himself for others, he admits he shows a better colouring than his natural complexion. He also describes his reading as purloining from authors "to assist, second, and fortify" opinions already held, not to form new ones, and says he has studied "because I had made it" rather than made the book from study.

**Lying as the corruption of manners and the betrayal of the word.** Montaigne (his own judgment) asks whom to believe in a self-report "in so corrupt an age". Truth now is "what every man persuades another man to believe", like counterfeit coin that circulates if it will pass. He cites Salvianus on the French, for whom lying is "a way of speaking". He offers two explanations of why being called a liar is the supreme insult: we most defend the faults we are most tainted with, and the charge implies cowardice. He then gives a social argument: speech is the only channel by which minds communicate, so falsifying it "breaks all our correspondence, and dissolves all the ties of government". He reports (as exemplum, not endorsement of the theology) the Indies peoples who offered blood from tongue and ears to expiate lies, and notes the conquest destroyed even their names. He closes by contrasting modern honour codes with the Greeks and Romans, who could trade insults without quarrel; he defers the rest to another time.

**Zeal, moderation and the double edge of toleration.** Montaigne says the cause of the old religion is the better one, yet even good men of that party are carried by passion into counsels that are "unjust and violent", and he separates them from the opportunists. He uses the early Christians' destruction of pagan books (the case of Tacitus) as a parallel. The Julian portrait is long and mostly admiring: justice, chastity, sobriety, learning, courage, a Socratic-looking death. Montaigne weighs the testimony of two eyewitness historians (Marcellinus and Eutropius, as he reports them) and concludes Julian was sharp but not cruel. Julian is also faulted as superstitious and besotted with divination, and Montaigne judges it more probable that he never truly embraced Christianity. The pivot is Julian's call for every man to follow his own religion, in hope of deepening division. Montaigne then states both sides without choosing between them: freedom of opinion can sow division or mollify it, and the same device is used by Julian to inflame and by the French kings to extinguish. He ends with a hedged judgment about the kings' show of willingness.

**Nothing is pure.** Gold must be alloyed to be useful, and so must virtue and pleasure. Extreme pleasure has groaning in it; joy is more severe than gay; the gods "sell us all the goods they give us". Montaigne (self-report) thinks there is design and complacency in melancholy, and cites painters on the weeping/laughing face. His self-examination finds his best virtue tinged with vice, and he suspects Plato would hear "some jarring note of human mixture" in his own purest virtue: "Man is wholly and throughout but patch and motley." Law cannot be cleared of injustice (Plato's hydra; Tacitus quoted in Latin). The final turn is practical: a certain bluntness of mind suits business, "common and less speculative souls" manage affairs better, and over-subtle inquiry (Simonides and Hiero) paralyses choice. He offers acquaintances who talk well but manage badly as examples. This is Montaigne's own position, against the philosophers who set up unmixed ends (Aristo, Pyrrho, the Stoics, the Cyrenaics, as he names them).

## Notable passages

The self-forming passage in ch. 18 (c1) is the clearest statement in this unit of the book as a self-technology, and it is explicitly consubstantial with its author rather than addressed to an audience. The passage on the truth of the age and the word (c2, c3) ties individual honesty to the cohesion of government, an early version of a legibility-through-language worry, though Montaigne frames it morally and politically, not as infrastructure. The Julian conclusion in ch. 19 (c4) is a rare moment where Montaigne states both sides of a Wars-of-Religion policy question and withholds a verdict, with relevance to [[history-machine]]-style thinking only loosely, since his point is about tactics of toleration rather than institutional drift. The pair c5 and c6 in ch. 20 give his anti-purist anthropology and the argument that blunted perception fits the "dark and earthly life", a half-rhyme with [[socratic-thinking]] in its distrust of unmixed ideals, although Montaigne's concern here is practical competence, not elenchus.
