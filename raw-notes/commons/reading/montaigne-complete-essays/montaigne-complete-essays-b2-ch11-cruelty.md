# Of Cruelty: Effortful Virtue, Innocent Nature, and Compassion for the Suffering

Montaigne opens by claiming that virtue is something nobler than natural good temper, since virtue "presupposes difficulty and contention". He supports this with Stoic and Epicurean reports and with exempla (Epaminondas, Socrates' scolding wife, Metellus, Cato of Utica), then turns on his own argument. Socrates and Cato seem to have had no inner struggle, so he reconciles the two by proposing a higher stage in which virtue has become a settled "complexion" through long practice. He then judges himself. His own goodness is by his account "casual and accidental", an innocence owed to fortune, birth, father and nurse rather than to reason. From there the essay moves to cruelty, which he hates "as the very extreme of all vices": he is moved by a chicken or a hare, disapproves of any punishment beyond simple death, recoils from the cruelty of the civil wars, and extends a duty of "humanity" to beasts, and even to trees and plants. The slice ends on a run of ancient examples of honour paid to animals, closing with Plutarch's report of Xantippus and the ox. The essay argues from more than one side and carries some hedges of its own ("I know not how", "methinks").

## Themes

**Virtue requires an opponent, then is complicated by it.** The opening thesis is that goodness and virtue differ: the first is gentle disposition, the second needs a struggle with passion, and so "cannot be exercised without an opponent". Montaigne reports this view from the philosophers ("not only Stoics, but Epicureans") and supports it with the exempla of Epaminondas refusing riches and Metellus facing execution. He then tests it against his own sense of Socrates and Cato, and the argument turns: if virtue needs vice to contend with, Socrates would be of little worth. The problem is resolved by calling the highest virtue a habit so complete that the vicious passions find "no entrance", and by treating the struggling virtue as a middle rank. It is a worked example of Montaigne arguing against his own opening line. It rhymes with [[habit-and-identity]], though here habit is the product of philosophical training on a fine nature, not the invisible machinery of custom.

**Self-report as evidence.** Having ranked three kinds of virtue, he places himself below even the second: "My virtue is a virtue, or rather an innocence, casual and accidental." He disclaims credit, notes that friends mislabel his fortune as prudence, and offers possible causes (father, upbringing, birth, or the stars quoted in verse) without choosing. This is the self-study that [[self-construction-through-writing]] and [[michel-de-montaigne]] concern: character is described, hedged and not flattered. He also notes that his manners are better than his opinions, as with Aristippus and Epicurus in the anecdotes he cites.

**Opinions reported and doubted.** Montaigne repudiates the Stoic claim that the wise man acts by all the virtues together and, by symmetry, the wicked by all the vices ("sharp, unsubstantial subtleties"). He sets beside it the Peripatetic denial of the connection, and the reports that Socrates and Stilpo corrected natural inclinations by discipline. Other exempla in the unit (Arcesilaus's quip, the Italian lord's remark on national temperaments, Caesar's clemency, the Persian and Egyptian substitutions) come from other voices and are used to test or illustrate, not asserted as his own.

**Cruelty as the extreme vice.** His own stated position is that he hates cruelty "by nature and judgment", but he also holds that it should be judged by its end: in justice, "all that exceeds a simple death appears to me pure cruelty". The soldier who attempts suicide for fear of torture, and the crowd at Rome that cries out at the quartering of Catena, are his illustrations. He suggests that exemplary severity be exercised on the dead rather than the living. Cruelty in its purest form, he says, is killing "without hatred, without profit" for the spectacle alone, which he claims to have seen in the civil wars.

**Animals and the limits of human sovereignty.** Montaigne extends compassion to beasts, adding that those "sanguinary towards beasts discover a natural proneness to cruelty" and giving the Roman progression from animal spectacle to gladiators. He hedges: he does not much admit kinship with beasts, and he reports the Druidic metempsychosis and Egyptian animal worship (with Plutarch's gloss) as others' beliefs. Yet arguments for resemblance "abate a great deal of our presumption". The ground of the duty is humanity, with justice owed to men and "graciousness and benignity" to other creatures. He admits to playing with his dog. [[noble-savage]]-style cultural contrast appears only in passing, in the aside that the savages offend him less in eating the dead than others do in tormenting the living.

## Notable passages

**The thesis on virtue (c1).** The opening distinction between the one who "did well" by temperament and the one who does "virtuously" after conflict. God is called good but not virtuous since His acts are without endeavour.

**Virtue become habit (c3).** After the Socrates and Cato digression, the resolution: the great souls have a "habitude to virtue" so perfect "that it was turned to a complexion", natural and ordinary.

**The confession (c4).** The self-assessment as casual and accidental innocence, with the speculation about its causes.

**Hatred of cruelty (c5).** The statement that he mortally hates cruelty, with the chicken's neck and the hare's cry as proofs, and the aside on the chase.

**The civil wars (c6).** The sharpest contemporary passage in the unit: cruelty practised only to enjoy "the pleasant spectacle" of a dying man, which he says he could scarcely have believed before seeing it.

**Duty beyond humans (c7).** The claim that a "general duty of humanity" extends to beasts, trees and plants, with justice owed to men and benignity to other creatures.
