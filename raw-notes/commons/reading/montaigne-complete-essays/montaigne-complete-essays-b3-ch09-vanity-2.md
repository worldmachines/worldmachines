# Against Innovation, and the Debts of a Self: Of Vanity (middle section)

This slice of 'Of Vanity' (Book 3, ch. 9) begins mid-essay with a run of political reflections on the civil troubles of France and ends mid-argument on Montaigne's personal scruples about obligation. It has three movements. First, he dismisses speculation about ideal constitutions and argues that a nation's best government is the one it already has, that innovation cures by killing, and that tottering states (Rome above all) endure far longer than expected, so that he does not despair of France. Second, he turns abruptly to his own habits as a writer: a failing memory that makes prepared speeches impossible, a refusal to revise published essays (he only adds), and a suspicion that his own judgment does not improve with age. Third, he reports his precarious position in a war-torn country and a general dislike of living by favour rather than by right, which spills into a stance on promises, gratitude and the rationing of his own affection. Montaigne's voice is the first person throughout, with exempla (Solon, Varro, Pacuvius, Lyncestes, Curio, Antiochus) serving as evidence; the Latin verse quotations are left untranslated in the source and are not leaned on here.

## Themes

**Custom as the real constitution.** Montaigne holds that discussion of the 'best form of society' is an exercise of wit with 'no life but' in agitation and controversy; we take 'a world already made, and formed to certain customs; we do not beget it, as Pyrrha or Cadmus did.' Solon and Varro are cited as precedents for legislating or writing 'according to use than nature'. His own claim: the best government for a nation is the one under which it is maintained, because its form 'depend[s] upon custom'. This is a strong statement of the [[habit-and-identity]] theme at the scale of the polity, though it is an argument from the inertia and fragility of existing order, not from its justice.

**Reform as surgery that can kill.** Innovation 'gives form to injustice and tyranny'. A reformer who only removes what offends him falls short, since 'good does not necessarily succeed evil'; the surgeon must also fill the wound with better flesh. Caesar's murderers and the French of his own day are his proofs, and Pacuvius Calavius's staged election of replacement senators in Capua dramatises the point: consent in removing the old was easy, agreement on the new impossible, and the crowd concluded that 'the oldest and best known evil' was the more supportable. Note he allows that a loosened piece may be propped up; what he rejects is refounding 'the foundations of so vast a building'.

**Resilience of old, sick states, and a hedged hope.** Montaigne consoles himself by comparison with those worse off (Solon's heap of ills), and with Rome, which was soundest 'when it was most sick'. 'Everything that totters does not fall': a great body holds 'by more nails than one', even by its antiquity, and 'universal sickness is particular health'. He then hedges: perhaps God will bring recovery as a body purges itself through long illness, but he also sees natural, heaven-sent symptoms alongside human imprudence, notes the stars suggest the state has lasted beyond the ordinary term, and fears most 'dissipation and divulsion' rather than change to the whole mass. The note is mixed, neither prophecy nor despair. It rhymes loosely with long-lived institutional machinery that persists past its foundations (compare [[history-machine]]), though Montaigne reasons from Roman example and astrology, not from any cyclical theory.

**The writer's confession: memory, preparation, revision.** He fears repeating himself, hates to review what he has written, and says his memory is failing; being tied to a prepared speech 'looses' him from it. The story of Lyncestes, who stammered a memorised defence and was killed by soldiers who read his confusion as guilt, makes the point. He prefers unpremeditated ease to visible preparation, since preparation 'begets a great deal more expectation than it will satisfy'. On the book he says: 'I add, but I correct not.' Reasons given: a published work belongs to the public, and he doubts that later judgment is better ('I now, and I anon, are two several persons'), citing Antiochus. This is a key passage for [[self-construction-through-writing]] and for the essay as accumulative, non-final form; he also disclaims printers' errors that 'wrest me to their conception'.

**Liberty, obligation and a self held to its word.** Living in the centre of the new religion's turmoil, he claims to pay dearly for defending the laws while his house has stayed 'a virgin from blood and plunder'. But he is troubled that his safety comes by chance and the favour of others rather than by law: he wants to live 'by right and by authority, and not either by recompense or favour'. Gratitude binds him more than a scrivener's bond, and he treats his own word as nearly sacred, hence makes promises uncertain and conditional. He values acts done freely ('If the action has not some splendour of liberty, it has neither grace nor honour') and even welcomes others' ingratitude as a discharge of debt, while hedging that he does not go as far as those who injure benefactors. He is notably candid about the limits of this temperament.

## Notable passages

The custom-as-constitution paragraph (c1) pairs Montaigne's claim that desiring another government than the one established is 'both vice and folly' with a quatrain of Pibrac, left untranslated here. The innovation and surgeon passages (c2, c3) are the core of his political conservatism and should be read as an argument about the cost of change under civil war, which he lived through. The Pacuvius story (c4) is exemplum, not Montaigne's own experience. The totters-does-not-fall passage (c5) is his most distinctive political reasoning and carries his hedges ('peradventure', 'who knows'). The 'add, but correct not' passage (c6) is the clearest account in this slice of how he understands his own book. The final obligation passage (c7) is self-report, and shows how his private ethic of freedom reappears in his account of public safety. See also [[michel-de-montaigne]].
