# Of Diversion: why we manage grief, love, anger and death by turning the mind aside

Montaigne opens with a self-report: asked to console a truly afflicted lady, he did not argue her out of grief but gently digressed until she was calm, and admits that this was only palliation, since he "had not gone to the root." From there the essay generalises. He gives exempla of diversion (the Liege negotiator, Atalanta's apples, physicians shifting a catarrh), then turns to death: most people facing it, from scaffold penitents to men fighting in battle, are looking elsewhere, and only Socrates meets it head on. He reports his own uses of diversion against displeasure, love and violent fancies, and his observation, drawn partly from kidney-stone fits, that our grief and our clinging to life rest on petty particulars and mere words. The last third dwells on how imagination, rhetoric and ceremony manufacture real emotion out of nothing, and ends on the body-versus-soul complaint of a Latin couplet addressed to Prometheus (untranslated in this text).

## Themes

**Consolation by indirection (Montaigne's own practice).** He reports that opposing a grieving person only hardens them, and that a physician-like gentleness, first favouring the grief, then sliding to other topics, works better. He sets this against the philosophical consolations he declines to use (the positions he attributes to Cleanthes, the Peripatetics, Chrysippus, Epicurus and Cicero). He is candid that he has an "unlucky hand in persuading" and that diversion treats the symptom, not the cause.

**Diversion as a general mechanism (exempla and observation).** The Liege story, Pericles's military diversions, and Hippomenes with the golden apples are borrowed or reported cases of a force being turned aside rather than met. The medical analogy (diverting a catarrh to a less dangerous part) is applied to diseases of the mind, supported by a Latin quotation advising change of pursuits and place.

**Death is faced sideways.** Montaigne distinguishes the rare few who consider death itself (Socrates) from nearly everyone else, who fix on a new being, devotion, vengeance, glory, children, or just the heat of a fight. He credits the scaffold penitents' devotion but not strictly their constancy. Subrius Flavius, mocking the ill-dug grave, is the one he says kept his thoughts "rightly fixed upon the subject." This is his reading; the anecdotes (Xenophon, Epaminondas, Silanus, Hegesias's disciples) are from classical sources.

**Philosophy itself only glances at its target.** Zeno's syllogisms against death and drunkenness are judged to miss the mark, and Montaigne takes comfort that great souls "cannot rid themselves of our company." This is a recurring Montaignean deflation of stored learning against lived human weakness, though here it is gentle rather than scornful.

**Self-management by variation.** For revenge, love, and violent imagination he recommends dividing, weakening or substituting the passion, and reports using it on himself (taking up a love to escape a wound dealt by friendship). He frames this as imitating nature, which uses inconstancy and time to loosen first apprehensions. He also reports Epicurus's view (as he gives it) that time does not alleviate grief by foresight or antiquity but by the traffic of other thoughts.

**We are moved by rinds, words and tones.** A little thing diverts us because a little thing holds us: a farewell, a robe, a name. Examining his own grief he finds "a grammatical and word complaint." His kidney-stone crises showed him the atoms (a dog, a horse, a book, a glass) that make up the regret of life. He insists he is moved in detail though not in gross, and treats total insensibility (Polemon) as a symptom of a hard nature, not wisdom.

**Emotion is manufactured by performance and imagination.** Orators, hired mourners, comedians, and his own funeral convoy for Monsieur de Grammont show that acted feeling becomes real feeling. The soldier with no stake in a siege becomes furious at the sight of steel and noise. Dreams and omens (Cambyses, Aristodemus, Midas) kill. The conclusion is that mind has no object but itself to sustain these passions, a view that sits with Montaigne's scepticism about reason's mastery over the body, and the close of the essay ironises the soul's boast of triumph over the body.

## Resonances

The essay is a clear instance of the self used as the specimen: nearly every claim is tested against what Montaigne has observed in himself, which fits [[self-construction-through-writing]] and [[personal-essay-as-form]], though here the self-study is turned to a practical, almost therapeutic question. His claim that mourning rests on repeated words and ceremonies, and that acted feelings become real through habituation, rhymes with [[habit-and-identity]], although Montaigne is describing local emotional habituation and not custom at the scale of whole societies.

## Notable passages

**The failed consolation (c1).** Montaigne's account of consoling the lady sets the essay's method: not refuting grief but gradually changing the subject, with an honest verdict that it did not cure her.

**Physicians and the diseases of the mind (c2).** The catarrh analogy and the Latin warning that the mind must sometimes be drawn to other pursuits and places state the thesis; the line that jostling infirmities directly only makes a man "decline and evade" the attack gives his admission that diversion evades rather than defeats.

**Zeno and the human company of great souls (c3).** Montaigne mocks the philosophical syllogism against death as not hitting the white, and finds relief in the humanity of the great.

**Violent displeasure and love (c4).** His first-person account of substituting a love for a wound, with the principle that variation relieves, dissolves and dissipates.

**Rinds and words (c5).** The theory that we are held by superficial circumstances, with the Plutarch, Caesar's robe, and word-complaint examples as support for it.

**Death in detail (c6).** From his stone: death looked on universally is easy to scorn, but the footman's tears and a friendly touch soften him.
