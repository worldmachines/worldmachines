# The Unstable Judge: Reason Swayed by Body, Passion and Fashion

This slice sits in the middle of the Apology for Raimond Sebond (the twelfth of fifteen slices); it starts mid-argument and ends mid-argument. It begins by pressing the sceptical point that if man cannot know himself he cannot know anything, and by weighing the Academics' 'probable' judgment against the bolder Pyrrhonians. Montaigne then argues from disagreement, from his own changeable opinions, and from the effect of illness, weather, mood and passion on the judging mind. He turns to himself as a specimen, describes his own volubility, and says it has made him conservative: he keeps to the ancient faith and distrusts new doctrines. The slice closes on Copernicus, Paracelsus, Pyrrho and the discovery of a new half of the world, which suggest that the present consensus may be as provisional as Ptolemy's.

## Themes

**The self-knowledge argument against reason.** If the soul knew anything it would know itself first, and the (medical) gods still dispute our anatomy. Montaigne says we may have some real knowledge, but 'by chance', since errors enter the soul by the same channel as truths and the soul cannot tell them apart. He presses the Academics: inclining toward the more likely already presupposes some grasp of the true, so either we can judge absolutely or we cannot. The Pyrrhonian position is called 'more bold, and also somewhat more likely'. These are positions he weighs and tests; the Pyrrhonian suspension is not simply declared his own.

**Disagreement and the instability of the judge.** No proposition is not debated, so our natural judgment 'does not very clearly discern what it embraces'. Montaigne reports that he has embraced with full conviction what he later judged false 'a hundred, a thousand times', yet the present opinion always feels certain. Everything received comes from a 'mortal hand' to a mortal hand; only what comes from heaven carries the sole mark of truth, and even that needs God's grace to be received. This is the fideist hinge: human frailty ought to make us moderate about innovation.

**Body, mood and passion as hidden machinery of judgment.** Reason is 'an instrument of lead and of wax', pliable to any bias. Sickness, weather, a corn on the toe, a hungry stomach, a judge's friendship or anger all shift verdicts (the Areopagites judging by night, Cleomenes' remark about being another man when sick). Yet Montaigne also reports the Peripatetic view that valour, virtue and eloquence need passion, and then mocks philosophy for implying that fury and sleep are our best access to the divine and that 'the worst place we can take is in ourselves'. This is quoted opinion pushed to an absurd conclusion, not a doctrine he adopts.

**Self-portrait as evidence.** The most distinctive part is first-person: his moods, his own books that he cannot find the first sense of, his judgment that 'floats and roams', how arguing the contrary opinion carries him away 'by my own weight', and an episode of youthful desire in which the same things looked utterly different before and after ("which of the two most truly? Pyrrho knows nothing about it"). His self-study is the empirical ground of his scepticism. See [[michel-de-montaigne]] and [[self-construction-through-writing]], though here self-observation is used to dismantle confidence rather than to build a stable self.

**Conservatism from scepticism.** Knowing his own volubility, he keeps the opinions that were 'first and natural', takes 'other men's choice', and stays in the ancient faith. He treats sceptical doubt as a reason not to trust the new, since every new doctrine displaced an old one that was equally well received and may be displaced in turn. The caution is explicitly personal ('simplicity such as mine'), and he adds that he would rather follow effects than reasoning, as in his exchange with the man who demonstrated that ancient sailors were wrong about the winds.

**The overturned world picture.** Cosmography, Copernicus against the old heavens, Paracelsus against physic, a geometrical demonstration that contradicts experience, and the discovery of an 'infinite extent of terra firma', 'nearly equal in greatness to that we knew before', all serve as exempla that paradigms lapse. This loosely rhymes with the World Machines idea that the infrastructure of an era's knowledge feels permanent until it is replaced, though Montaigne's point is epistemic humility, not a cyclical or structural theory of eras. He ends with the thought that the world may be 'quite another thing than what we imagine'.

## Notable passages

**Errors and truths share a channel (c1).** The argument that we may hold truth only 'by chance', because the soul has no means of distinguishing it from error, is the compact form of the sceptical claim in this slice.

**Variously judging, always certain (c2).** Montaigne's report that the present opinion always feels infallible, however often the last was overthrown, is a first-person phenomenology of conviction and ends on the Lucretian lines about the newest discovery seeming best.

**I, that watch myself (c3).** The long self-description of mood, reading and his own revisions of his writing, ending with the image of judgment floating and roaming like a small boat in a storm.

**Fury, sleep and the place in ourselves (c4).** The satirical turn on philosophy's praise of passion, madness and prophecy, ending with the point that a voice from a 'dark, terrestrial, and ignorant' man is not to be trusted.

**Constancy from volubility (c5).** The personal turn to conservatism, grace and the ancient faith, and his remark that each good ancient author seems right in turn though they contradict one another.

**Distrusting novelty (c6).** The general rule that a new doctrine should be doubted because a third may come that damns the second, with the Aristotle point that his principles are no more exempt from being thrust out than their predecessors.

**Ptolemy and the new world (c7).** The closing exemplum, in which the geographers' claim that 'all is found' echoes Ptolemy's earlier certainty, now overturned by the discovery of a new half of the world.
