# Opinion, Death and Pain: Testing the Claim That Evil Is Judgment

This is the first part of a long chapter (it breaks off mid-argument, with a Cicero citation on custom and nature). Montaigne opens by reporting an "ancient Greek sentence" that men are tormented by opinions of things, not by things themselves, and sets out to examine "how that can be maintained" rather than asserting it. He argues from the diversity of human judgments (death is dreadful to some, a harbour to others), then piles up exempla: condemned men joking on the scaffold, suttee-like burials in Narsingah, mass suicides at Milan and Xanthus, martyrs and the expelled Portuguese Jews who died for an opinion. He then turns to the harder objection, pain, where he partly concedes the opponents' point, then recovers a qualified version: pain is the worst incident of our being, yet the soul can lessen it, and custom (Lacedaemonian boys, Swiss camp-women) shows how much room we allow it.

## Themes

**Opinion as the medium through which evil enters.** The argument is conditional and Montaigne frames it as a hope ("It were a great victory ... could this proposition be established for certain and true throughout"). His evidence is variation: if evils lodged in us by their own authority they would lodge alike in all; because judgments differ, things "only enter us by composition". Attribution: the opening sentence is a reported Greek saying, not Montaigne's own assertion; he adopts it as a hypothesis to test.

**Death met with indifference, jest or zeal.** Most of the first half is exempla, mainly borrowed or heard: the gallows jokes (Picard, Dane, Toulouse servant, buffoons), Theodorus and Lysimachus, the Xanthians, the Greek oath against Persian laws, Arras, Albigeois at Castelnaudari. Montaigne offers them as evidence that "Every opinion is of force enough to cause itself to be espoused at the expense of life", and he notes the point holds for every religion. Personal testimony enters sparingly: his father's report of the Milan suicides, and an intimate friend who ran at death "with a real affection". The long Portuguese narrative is told on the authority of Bishop Osorius and ends with Montaigne's own aside that "custom and length of time are much more powerful counsellors in such changes than all other constraints whatever". He does not moralise on the cruelty; the case serves the argument about conviction.

**Reason as torment, or as instrument.** The Pyrrho story (the tempest and the hog) is used to ask whether reason was "given us for a torment". Montaigne's own answer is that reason ought to serve our advantage in line with nature's design. This is a rhetorical question, not a settled doctrine, and it sits uneasily with the claim he makes later that the soul can remake our experience.

**Pain: the limit of the thesis, then a qualified recovery.** Montaigne raises the objection himself (Aristippus, Hieronymus, "most of the sages") and dismantles the Posidonius anecdote: if the pain did not move him, why interrupt the discourse? Pain is certain, "our senses themselves are judges", and the beating of Pyrrho's hog would make it cry out. He argues that death is felt only by reason, that much of what we call fear of death is imagination, and that pain's sting is doubled because it threatens death. Speaking for himself he grants pain is "the worst incident of our being" and that he hates it most, having had "so little traffic with it". Still, it is in us "to lessen it by patience". Virtue needs pain to play its part, and violent pain is brief ("Si gravis, brevis"), a Cicero consolation he quotes.

**The soul's jurisdiction and custom.** The body has one bent; the soul is "variable into all sorts of forms". Montaigne departs from Plato (who fears soul and body too tightly knit): he fears they are too separated. Pain is like a stone's lustre, it varies with its setting. The examples (childbearing, Lacedaemonian boys, the fox and the burning coal) turn on cultural training. This runs close to the chapter's custom-theme, with Cicero's line closing the slice that custom cannot conquer nature.

## Notable passages

**The thesis stated as a hope.** The opening frames the "ancient Greek sentence" as something it would be a victory to establish, and spells out the consequence: if fancy gives evil its quality, we are "very strange fools" to side with what offends us.

**Opinions worth dying for.** "Every opinion is of force enough to cause itself to be espoused at the expense of life" is the hinge between the death exempla and the religious cases.

**Gallows humour.** The cluster of condemned men's jokes is the comic centre of the death evidence, ending with the man who would "carry my recommendations myself".

**Pyrrho's hog.** Montaigne asks whether reason, our claimed mastery, was given us for a torment, and invokes nature's design for use of our faculties.

**Posidonius and the limits of the thesis.** Montaigne's pivot: pain is not simply opinion, because the senses judge it. He lets the sceptic's analogy (skin, tongue, hog) cut against the Stoic story.

**The soul's empire.** The chapter's constructive claim: we should study the soul and incline her biases toward repose; pain "surrenders upon much better terms to them who make head against her".
