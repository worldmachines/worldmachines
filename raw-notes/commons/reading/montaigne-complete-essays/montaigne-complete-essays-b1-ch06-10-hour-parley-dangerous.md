# Early essays on parley, intention, idleness, lying and speech (Book 1, chs. 6-10)

Five short essays, each moving from a general question to a stack of anecdotes and then, in the last three, to Montaigne himself. Ch. 6 collects sieges in which a garrison was seized while its deputies were parleying, and Montaigne sides against the licence of war. Ch. 7 argues that the will is the only thing we truly command and so the only thing we can be bound by. Ch. 8 is the earliest report of the origin of the book: a retired mind left idle breeds monsters, and he begins to write them down. Ch. 9 turns a confession of a defective memory into an attack on liars. Ch. 10 sorts speakers into quick and slow and ends with an unflattering self-portrait of his own unprepared wit. Montaigne is the voice of judgment throughout; the surrounding exempla are borrowed and are tested, not endorsed.

## Themes

**Good faith in war (ch. 6).** Montaigne begins from recent French religious-war experience at Mussidan, where men were cut down while their deputies were treating, and concedes that the practice of arms now leaves "no confidence in an enemy excusable till the treaty is finally sealed". The reported opinions are mixed: Cleomenes says whatever harm one does an enemy is "above justice", and Xenophon is cited as an authority who allows great licence, which Montaigne says he cannot accept. Against them he sets Chrysippus (the foot race: run fast, but do not trip your rival), with whom he says he concurs, and Alexander's refusal to "steal a victory". Note the hedge: Montaigne grants that war "has naturally many privileges that appear reasonable even to the prejudice of reason" and is not a simple pacifist; the line he draws is at exploiting an opponent's lack of understanding during a truce. The exempla (Phocaea, Argos, Casilinum, Capua, Genoa, Ligny) are historical anecdotes, not his own judgment.

**The will as the only thing we own (ch. 7).** The famous saying that death discharges all obligations is tested against Henry VII's deathbed order to kill a prisoner delivered on a promise of safety, and Egmont's wish to mount the scaffold first. Montaigne's own principle: "we are masters of nothing but the will, in which, by necessity, all the rules and whole duty of mankind are founded and established". Hence intention, not outcome, decides: Egmont would have been absolved even had he outlived Horn, while the king, who wilfully planned a breach, is not excused by postponing it until after his death. He extends this to deathbed restitution and posthumous grudges (penitence requires penalty) and ends with a self-commitment: his death shall "discover nothing that my life has not first and openly declared". The title is Montaigne's, the Egmont reading is his own interpretation ("methinks").

**The idle mind and the origin of writing (ch. 8).** Fallow land and unfertilised women bearing formless lumps are the analogies for a mind not fixed on a study. Montaigne reports that on retiring from affairs he expected leisure to settle his mind and found the opposite: it ran like a horse that has broken from its rider, producing chimaeras. His response is the first account of the essay as method: he writes them down "hoping in time to make it ashamed of itself". The Latin tags (one amounting to "he who lives everywhere lives nowhere") are quotations he offers in support, not translated here. This passage is the clearest seed of [[personal-essay-as-form]] and of [[self-construction-through-writing]], though the motive stated here is corrective and half-comic, not yet a programme of self-portraiture.

**Memory, judgment and the vice of lying (ch. 9).** Montaigne opens with his own near-total lack of memory, rejects the inference that this shows folly or ingratitude, and claims that a strong memory is commonly coupled with infirm judgment. He lists consolations: it cured ambition, it kept him from relying on others' reports without exercising his own judgment, it keeps him from rambling, it softens his memory of injuries (against Darius's page reminding him of the Athenians), and it lets re-read books smile with fresh novelty. He then turns to deliberate liars (distinguishing an untruth from a lie by the grammarians' definition) and argues the memory trap: a fabricated story has no hold and the truth keeps pushing back. His moral claim is strong and his own: lying is "an accursed vice", "We are not men, nor have other tie upon one another, but by our word". Truth has one face and falsehood "a hundred thousand forms", which he links to the Pythagorean pairing of good as finite and evil as infinite. The long anecdotes about Francis I and the ambassadors Taverna and the papal envoy are reported exempla of liars undone by their own blunders.

**Quick and slow speech, and the self as an unreliable instrument (ch. 10).** Montaigne assigns slow speakers to the pulpit and quick ones to the bar, then complicates his own rule with Poyet, whose prepared harangue was useless when the Pope changed the topic. Wit is prompt and judgment is slow, he suggests, and either extreme without the other leaves a speaker unhappy. He then describes his own nature: left to itself it "flags and languishes"; "agitation only gives it grace and vigour". Accident, company and even the pitch of his voice draw more from him than solitary effort, so that what he says is better than what he writes, "if either were to be preferred, where neither is worth anything". He adds that he loses his thread mid-sentence and that a stranger often finds it before he does. A self-observation made with irony, not a general theory of rhetoric.

## Notable passages

**Against stealing victory (ch. 6).** Montaigne's admission that war has privileges against reason, followed by his refusal of Xenophon's licence, sets the limit of fair dealing in conflict; his agreement with Chrysippus and Alexander makes the limit positive (fair play as honour in victory). This is Montaigne weighing opposed authorities, not adopting one.

**The will is all we master (ch. 7).** The Egmont and Henry VII contrast is the argument in miniature: obligation lives in intent, so neither a death nor impossibility of performance dissolves bad faith or excuses good.

**The horse that has broken from its rider (ch. 8).** Montaigne's report of retirement, the idle mind and the decision to write it down.

**Memory as defect and mercy (ch. 9).** A self-defence that becomes an epistemology: without a stocked memory he is forced to use his own judgment. Compare with the later essays on pedantry; the tie is only thematic, since nothing in this unit mentions them.

**Lying as the bond of society (ch. 9).** "We are not men, nor have other tie upon one another, but by our word" ties the opening of the book on parley to its theme: faith kept or broken in public life and speech. This chapter has some bearing on [[michel-de-montaigne]]'s ethic of candour, even as he offers himself as a poor witness to his own past.

**Occasion over premeditation (ch. 10).** His own testimony that he works best when stirred by accident and worst when "wholly at my own disposition".
