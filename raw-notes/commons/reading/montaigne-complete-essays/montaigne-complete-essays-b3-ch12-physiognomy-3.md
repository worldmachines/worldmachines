# Montaigne on borrowed learning, faces, and being spared by his countenance

This is the last slice of 'Of Physiognomy' (Book 3, ch. 12). It opens mid-argument, still on Socrates, whose discourse on death Montaigne calls the 'pure and first impression and ignorance of nature'. He then turns on himself: his book is a nosegay of foreign flowers, and he distinguishes sharply between those who merely cite commonplaces and himself, who tries to steal and alter what he borrows. He explains why he writes in old age rather than waiting for 'maturity', then returns to Socrates' ugly face, to beauty, and to whether faces can be read for character. He closes with two personal anecdotes of ambush in the civil-war countryside, in which his open face and frankness saved him, and a coda on his reluctance to punish.

## Themes

**Nature before art, and Socrates' simplicity.** Montaigne says death is not naturally feared, only pain, and that death is useful to nature in 'the succession and vicissitude of her works'. Beasts do not imagine death. He praises Socrates' manner as beyond art: it is easier 'to speak like Aristotle and to live like Caesar than to speak and live as Socrates did'. Yet he distances himself: unlike Socrates he has not 'corrected my natural composition by the force of reason' but 'let myself go as I came', following the ancient rule that we cannot fail in following Nature. He attacks a 'scholastic probity' that is a 'slave to precepts' and 'fettered with hope and fear', preferring a virtue rooted in universal reason that laws and religion perfect rather than create. (This is Montaigne's own judgment; the Socratic reason that made Socrates 'courageous in death, not because his soul is immortal, but because he is mortal' is his reading of Socrates.)

**Borrowing, authorship, and the self as the real subject.** Montaigne concedes he is a compiler (the nosegay image) but insists the borrowed ornaments 'shall not cover me and hide me'; he wants to show only 'what is my own by nature'. He mocks commonplace-gatherers, ghost-compiled books, and a president who boasted of two hundred commonplaces in a judgment. His own practice is to steal and 'disguise and alter' a quotation 'for some new service' and give it 'a particular touch of my own hand', preferring 'invention over allegation'. This is a pointed self-description of the essay as a technology of self-knowledge ([[personal-essay-as-form]], [[self-construction-through-writing]]), hedged by his admission that he loads himself with quotations 'upon the account of idleness and the humour of the age' and that this may 'misbecome' him. He also defends writing in his prime, mocks old authors' 'dotage', and says he writes of 'nothing but nothing', 'that inscience', with his life 'wholly before me'.

**Beauty and the limits of physiognomy.** Montaigne repeatedly affirms the power of beauty ('a short tyranny', per La Boetie; 'the privilege of nature', per Plato) and gathers exempla (Phryne, Cyrus, Alexander, Caesar, Aristotle's remark). He grieves that Socrates was ugly, since body and soul probably correspond, then qualifies: superficial ugliness is 'of least prejudice to the state of the mind', as in La Boetie, while substantial deformity 'strikes deeper'. Faces are 'a feeble warranty; and yet it is something considerable too'. He believes there is 'some art' in reading faces but will 'leave undecided' whether future events can be foretold from them. Much of the reasoning is hedged and Montaigne explicitly does not commit.

**Fortune over prudence.** Of his own prudent actions he says a third at most was his, 'the other two-thirds were absolutely hers' (Fortune's). Heaven 'is jealous of the extent that we attribute to the right of human prudence'. The rescue stories are told with real uncertainty: he says of the second, 'I do not yet rightly understand' the cause, and offers 'it is possible' that Divine goodness used his 'vain instrument'. The resonance with a [[history-machine]]-style view of silent, distributed causation is faint at best; this is personal providence and luck, not structural history.

**Trust, suspicion, and mercy.** Montaigne is by temperament 'very little inclined to suspicion and distrust' and takes men 'according to the common order'. His openness, he claims, disarms would-be betrayers. He says he is slow to offend and as a magistrate 'rather chosen to fail in point of justice'; the horror of a first cruelty makes him 'abhor all imitation of it'. He quotes Aristotle ('merciful to the man, but not to his wickedness') and Plutarch's two contradictory readings of Charillus, which he lets stand.

## Notable passages

**The nosegay and the claim to what is 'my own'.** Montaigne's most direct statement of how he sees his own use of quotation: the thread is his, the flowers are foreign, but he does not want the ornaments to hide him (c1, c2). He sets his practice of altering stolen goods against the display of commonplaces.

**Writing now rather than later.** In c3 he declines to wait for old age to publish, calling the aged mind 'costive and thick', and describes his book as ignorance delivered 'in pomp and state'; he anticipates giving an account of his death 'should I find it a prating death'. The tone mixes self-mockery and real programme.

**On beauty.** c4 collects Montaigne's esteem for beauty, borrowing from La Boetie, Plato and Aristotle, and his admission that he values it even in servants and beasts. It is followed (outside the chunk) by the qualification that faces do not simply reduce to beauty.

**The two ambushes.** c5 and c6 are the narrative centre. In the first, a neighbour feigns flight to get into Montaigne's house with twenty-five or thirty armed men; Montaigne suspects, yet decides to go through with the courtesy; the leader later says that Montaigne's 'countenance and frankness had snatched the treachery out of his hands'. In the second, relying on a truce, he is captured, robbed and nearly killed, then abruptly freed, the captors' chief insisting that he owed his deliverance to his countenance and the 'liberty and boldness of my speech'. In both Montaigne refuses to rationalise fully, crediting Fortune and possibly divine use of a 'vain instrument'. These anecdotes are valuable as first-hand evidence of civil-war insecurity (the armed neighbour, the truce that does not hold) and of Montaigne's self-portrait as a man who survives by being legible: his face is transparent, and that transparency is trusted ([[michel-de-montaigne]]). The word is mine; Montaigne himself speaks of countenance, frankness and innocence of intention.
