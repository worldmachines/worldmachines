# Of Anger: passion as a weapon that holds its wielder

The essay opens with Plutarch and Aristotle, via Montaigne, lamenting that most states leave children to the whim of parents, and describes the beatings he has seen in the street. From there it argues that anger is the passion that most removes men from right judgment, so punishment (of children, servants, anyone) belongs to calm. A long digression asks whether a preacher's or philosopher's life bears on the truth of what he says, and uses Plutarch's cool handling of his own slave's whipping as the model of anger mastered. A run of anecdotes (Archytas, Plato, Piso, Celius, Phocion) shows anger deferring, feeding on itself and being starved by silence. The last third turns to Montaigne's own practice: how he judges the "most choleric man in France", why he prefers venting anger to hiding it, the household rules he lays down, and a frank self-portrait of his sharp but short temper. It closes with a reported quip against Aristotle that anger is a weapon that moves its user.

## Themes

**Anger voids judgment, so punishment must wait.** Montaigne's own claim: no passion so transports men from right judgment, and correction done in anger is "revenge". He compares anger-clouded perception to seeing bodies through a mist, and says the punished will, rightly, discount the verdict of a man visibly in a rage. He frames the failure of the household as a public one: children are members of the commonwealth, yet "our justice takes no cognisance". Aristotle and Plutarch are cited here for the point about education left to parents; the indignation and the street scenes are Montaigne's.

**Words and the man who speaks them.** In a digression Montaigne separates sermon from preacher (a church's truth is not refuted by its ministers' vices; a bad man can preach truth), yet insists that sincerity carries force: the ancients who speak what they think "strike much more home". He contrasts Brutus and Seneca with Cicero by felt conviction, and says he always asks what kind of man an author was. This is his judgment, hedged by the first half of the argument; the Eudamidas, Cleomenes and Spartan Ephori anecdotes are reported exempla. The link to anger is Plutarch's slave, who tries to convict his master out of his own written teaching.

**Anger mastered versus anger suppressed.** The exempla (Plutarch, Archytas, Plato, Carillus) show masters who defer punishment because they know they are angry. Montaigne's own turn is that he values not how a man behaves but "how much it costs him to do no worse", and he suspects the outwardly serene man of grating himself within. Suppression, in his view, incorporates the anger (the Diogenes remark is reported, the application is his). He would rather vent it, preferably at the right target and sparingly, since constant scolding "runs into custom" and loses effect.

**Anger feeds on itself and on opposition.** The Piso story shows fury doubling when its premise is refuted; Celius's "deny me something, that we may be two" and Phocion's silence show anger needing an interlocutor, and contempt wounding it most.

**Self-report, with hedges.** Montaigne describes his own anger as sharp, short and private, aimed with the tongue and without malicious aim; small provocations take him by surprise where great ones, being expected, do not; once seized he is carried away. He proposes a mutual treaty ("when you see me moved first, let me alone"), admits it is "hard to execute", admits that he sometimes feigns anger to govern his house, and says he resists the sharpening of his humours with age. This is the essay as self-study, a record of the self observed, not a prescription. It resonates loosely with [[self-construction-through-writing]], though here the self is examined in passing rather than as the declared topic.

**The weapon that wields the hand.** The closing quip, attributed to opponents of Aristotle, inverts the claim that anger arms virtue: "this moves us; our hand guides it not". Montaigne grants Aristotle is "probable" and then lets the retort stand last, so he does not flatly reject it.

## Notable passages

**Anger and the judge** (c1). The central argument, with the physician analogy: correction is a medicine, and an enraged doctor is not tolerable.

**Sermon and preacher** (c2). The most digressive stretch, Montaigne's insistence on knowing the man behind the book and his comparison of Cicero's and Seneca's felt conviction. He admits he has wandered from his subject.

**Plutarch and the slave** (c3). The set-piece: Plutarch lists the physical signs of anger and shows he has none. Montaigne reports it from Aulus Gellius, and the story answers the question of whether a philosopher's practice matches his writing.

**Piso's three criminals** (c4). An antique exemplum of anger angry at truth and innocence itself, with fury redoubling through shame.

**The cost of not doing worse** (c5). Montaigne's own measure of virtue: the effort, not the outward result.

**Against the mask** (c6). The anti-Stoic preference for visible anger over hidden, with the Diogenes and Demosthenes saying.

**His own temper** (c7). The self-portrait, including the contrast between great and small provocations and the pact with quarrellers.

**Anger as a weapon** (c8). The closing exchange on Aristotle.
