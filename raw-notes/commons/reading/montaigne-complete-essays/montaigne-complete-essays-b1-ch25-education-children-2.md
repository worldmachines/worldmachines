# Education of a gentleman's son: judgment, the world as book, and a gentle philosophy

This is the middle slice of the essay on the education of children (it opens mid-argument on the tutor's duties and breaks off mid-argument on Plato and the gaiety of youth). Montaigne, speaking in his own voice about an imagined pupil and "my governor" (tutor), sketches a programme in which the pupil learns by conversing with every kind of person, by reading history for the judgment of men rather than for dates, and by contemplating the vast variety of the world, which relativises his own fortunes and his country's troubles. He then turns on school philosophy: it should teach how to live, early and cheerfully, before logic or astronomy; virtue is pleasant, not a rugged peak; and body and mind are trained together, without rods or the pedants' fury.

## Themes

**Judgment and sincerity over obstinacy.** The slice opens with the pupil being taught that to admit an error "found out by himself" is "an effect of judgment and sincerity", while obstinacy and contention belong to "mean souls". Attentiveness is part of this: he should keep his eye and ear "in every corner", since the best remarks are often made at the lower end of the table, and every man, even a peasant or bricklayer, has something to teach. Montaigne adds from his own experience that honours seldom go with the ablest parts.

**History read as judgment, not memory.** The tutor must not "imprint" the date of Carthage's ruin but the manners of Hannibal and Scipio, and teach the pupil to judge history, not recite it. Montaigne is reflexive about reading (his own Livy differs from another's, and Plutarch's from his) and praises Plutarch for pointing "with his finger" and leaving us to "grope out the rest". He offers the Asian "No" remark as a possible seed of La Boetie's "Voluntary Servitude", reported as his guess ("perhaps"). This is an exemplum-and-authority passage: Plutarch, Plato's report on the Lacedaemonians, and Alexandridas supply the material, while the preference for implication over stuffing is Montaigne's own.

**The world as mirror; scale and relativism.** Conversation widens us beyond the "length of our own noses". Socrates answered that he was of the world, not Athens (reported anecdote). Montaigne contrasts the parish priest who reads a frosted vineyard as God's wrath on mankind, and those who see civil-war havoc and cry that "the machine of the world is near dissolution", with the wider view that worse has been seen and others are merry elsewhere. He recommends "this great world" as "the mirror wherein we are to behold ourselves"; "so many humours, so many sects... laws, and customs" teach us to judge our own and to see our understanding's "imperfection and natural infirmity". This is custom-relativism as a pedagogy of humility, and the vanity of fame (conquests "drowned and swallowed in oblivion") reinforces it. Pythagoras' Olympic-games image (athletes, merchants, spectators) casts the spectator who watches "how and why everything is done" as a model. The "machine of the world" phrase is only an idiom for cosmic collapse, not a framework, but the passage rhymes with the habit of reading a local crisis as a civilisational ending, and so with the WMP's care about what is a [[world-machines]] transition and what is merely a hailstorm on one's own head.

**Useful knowledge first; philosophy as joy.** Of the sciences, begin "with that which makes us free", the doctrine that regulates manners and teaches self-knowledge; astronomy, logic and rhetoric come after, once judgment is formed. Anaximenes' reply (reported) about the stars while death or slavery loom is generalised into an inner list of enemies: ambition, avarice, temerity, superstition. Montaigne blames "ergotisms and petty sophistries" for making philosophy look grim; its true mark is "continual cheerfulness", and virtue sits in a "fair, fruitful, and flourishing plain", not on a precipice. Virtue is learned "by order, and not by force", and Socrates is its model (see [[socratic-thinking]]); a pupil who prefers morris-dancing to battle should be apprenticed to a trade, per Plato's rule, according to the faculties of his own soul.

**Start early, spend little time.** Philosophy should reach children, since "They begin to teach us to live when we have almost done living." Fifteen or sixteen years are owed to education, the rest to action. Aristotle's real gift to Alexander (via Plutarch) was precepts on valour and temperance, not syllogisms; Epicurus says the young should not refuse philosophy nor the old tire of it.

**Body and mind together; against the college.** Montaigne does not want a bookworm (Carneades neglecting his nails) or a pedant's slave. Lessons occur everywhere, as steps in a gallery tire less than a formal journey. "'Tis not a soul, 'tis not a body that we are training up, but a man." He hardens the pupil to heat, cold and danger rather than to punishment, and condemns the colleges as a "house of correction" whose "furious countenance, and a rod in hand" breed vice. He claims to have held this view constantly from childhood. Plato's concern for games and music in the Laws is the closing support.

## Notable passages

The passage beginning "Human understanding is marvellously enlightened by daily conversation" (c1) is the centre of the slice's relativism: perspective from scale, with the parish priest and the Savoyard as comic cases of provincial vision, and Montaigne's own dry remark that he wonders civil-war disorders are "so moderate". The following paragraph (c2) turns the world into a book and the self-knowledge motive explicit.

The history-reading passage (c3) is the most explicit statement of judgment over stored learning, plus a self-aware note on idiosyncratic reading and Plutarch's allusive style. It is also, in miniature, an account of the essay form itself, which hints and leaves the reader to finish.

The philosophy-as-cheerfulness passage (c4) reverses the schoolman's image of virtue on a rock; the "They begin to teach us to live" paragraph (c5) gives the argument from time. The body-and-mind passage (c6) and the attack on colleges (c7) give the practical and affective core. See also [[michel-de-montaigne]].
