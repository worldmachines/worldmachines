# Judgment, Words, Desire and the Middle Sort: Essays L-LV

This unit gathers six short chapters. "Of Democritus and Heraclitus" opens with Montaigne's account of his own method (judgment sounding subjects "at a distance", with ignorance as his "governing method"), argues that the soul gives every thing its colour so that even chess or table-talk reveals a man, and then sides with laughing Democritus over weeping Heraclitus. "Of the Vanity of Words" attacks rhetoric and grand terminology. "Of the Parsimony of the Ancients" is a bare string of exempla of Roman frugality. "Of a Saying of Caesar" is a brief diagnosis of insatiable desire. "Of Vain Subtleties" moves from pointless feats to a list of things that meet at their extremes, to the claim that ignorance comes before and after learning, and ends by placing his own book in the despised middle. "Of Smells" is a self-report on his nose, perfume and plague.

## Themes

**The essay as unsystematic judgment.** Montaigne says he takes subjects as fortune presents them, never sees "all of anything", and gives "a stab, not so wide but as deep as I can". He treats his unfinished, scattered handling as a liberty (he is "not responsible" for keeping to his subject) and names ignorance, doubt and uncertainty as his method. This is a statement of the form itself, in the spirit of [[personal-essay-as-form]] and [[michel-de-montaigne]]'s self-presentation; note that he frames it as modesty about his own inability, not as a programme for others.

**The soul colours what it receives; everything reveals the self.** "All motion discovers us": Caesar in battle and in love, Alexander at table or at chess. Things have their own weights perhaps, but once taken in, "the soul forms them as she pleases"; death is terrible to Cicero, coveted by Cato, indifferent to Socrates. He concludes that we should stop excusing ourselves by external qualities. He uses his own distaste for chess as a self-test (the game stirs anger, spite, ambition). This is self-study as method, close to [[self-construction-through-writing]], though here the point is that trivial moments are the best mirror, not that writing builds a self.

**Laughter over tears (Montaigne's own verdict, with reported Stoic-ish company).** He is "clearly for the first humour" (Democritus) because laughter expresses more contempt, and compassion implies esteem. We are not so unhappy as vain, not so malicious as foolish. Diogenes is a juster judge than the man-hater Timon, since "what a man hates he lays to heart". The closing anecdotes (Statilius, Hegesias, Theodorus) are reported opinions that a wise man should not hazard himself for fools, offered as being "of the same strain", not necessarily as his own policy; he ends "Our condition is as ridiculous as risible."

**Rhetoric as a poison and a political symptom.** Opening with the rhetorician who makes little things great (a shoemaker making a great shoe for a little foot), he cites Sparta, Crete, Aristo, Socrates and Plato (reported views) to argue that eloquence flourishes where the people are disorderly, as weeds in untilled soil, and matters less under monarchy. This is a reported-and-endorsed argument built from anecdote rather than a systematic claim. The cardinal's kitchen clerk lecturing on salads in the language of empire, architects' jargon that is only his kitchen door, grammar words that are his chambermaid's babble, Roman titles for French offices, and Aretin called "divine" all show inflation of vocabulary beyond things. He explicitly says he speaks "of words only", not effects. There is a faint rhyme with [[Legibility]] (names and orders imposed on things that do not match them), but Montaigne's concern is vanity and deception, not administration.

**Ancient frugality (exempla only).** Regulus, Cato, Scipio Aemilianus and Gracchus are presented with almost no comment; the chapter is evidence for a contrast with Montaigne's own age that the text does not itself spell out.

**Desire never rests.** Chapter LIII argues that philosophers have never agreed on the sovereign good and that present goods fail to satisfy not for lack of worth but because "we seize them with an unruly and immoderate haste". We feed on the idea of unknown things and fear the hidden more, as in the quoted saying of Caesar (Latin, untranslated in this text).

**Extremes meet; ignorance before and after knowledge.** In "Of Vain Subtleties", after condemning admiration for the merely rare or difficult (Alexander rewarding the needle-thrower with bushels of grain), Montaigne plays a family game of finding things that hold by both extremities: fear and courage both loosen the belly, stupidity and wisdom meet in suffering, infancy and decrepitude, avarice and prodigality. He then states that an "Abecedarian ignorance" precedes knowledge and a "doctoral ignorance" follows it. Simple peasants and true philosophers are both good; the half-educated "mongrels" (among whom he places himself) "trouble the world". He retreats toward the "first and natural station", though by his own admission "so vainly" attempted to leave it. The faith passage (simple believers, middling doubters, higher illuminated believers) is his report of three conditions; he carefully excludes overzealous partisans who blemish the cause with violence. He praises artless popular poetry over middle-sort poesy, and expects his own Essays to please neither common nor excellent readers but "hover in the middle region". A modern editorial note on the term "poesie populaire" is embedded in the text.

**The body and the nose.** In "Of Smells" he holds that the best bodily excellence is to smell of nothing (Plautus, in untranslated Latin), that heavy perfume suggests concealed defect, yet he himself loves good smells and abhors bad ones, scents them at a distance, and finds odours cling to his skin and moustache. He reports that scents alter his spirits, wonders why physicians do not use odours more, interprets church incense as meant to cheer and fit us for contemplation (a report: "which makes me approve of what is said"), notes his own lack of epidemic illness, and says Venice and Paris lose his affection by their marsh stench and dirt. The Socrates-and-plague line is an anecdote he repeats, not a claim of his own.

## Notable passages

**Method of the Essays (c1).** The opening paragraph is Montaigne's most compact statement of how he writes: judgment is an oar in everything, he stays on the shore when a subject is too deep, chooses arguments by fortune, and takes a single face of a hundred.

**The soul gives things their colour (c2).** The passage on death, health, riches and the rest "stripping" and receiving "a new robe" from the soul is the strongest statement of inward responsibility in the unit.

**Democritus preferred (c3).** His reasons for laughing over weeping, with Diogenes against Timon, set the tone of contemptuous detachment, qualified by the fact that he is arguing a preference, not proving a truth.

**Grand words for kitchens (c4).** His own imagination being possessed by the "palace of Apollidon" when architects speak, only to find "the paltry pieces of my own kitchen door", gives a vivid example of language inflating things.

**The two ignorances (c5).** The central argument of "Of Vain Subtleties", with the self-placement among the mongrels.

**Hovering in the middle region (c6).** The closing forecast about the reception of his own book completes the middle-and-extremes thread and ties it to his authorship.
