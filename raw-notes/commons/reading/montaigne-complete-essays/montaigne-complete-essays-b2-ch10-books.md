# Of Books: Montaigne on how and what he reads

Montaigne opens by disclaiming mastery: the book is an essay of his natural parts, not acquired ones, and he claims to lay open himself rather than to discover things. He then reviews his reading by taste and purpose. For amusement he names a few moderns and a short list of Latin poets, with Virgil's Georgics at the top and Lucretius, Catullus, Horace and Terence close behind. For profit, in order to marshal his own opinions, he reads Plutarch and Seneca in loose pieces, and he criticizes Cicero's preambles and Plato's dialogues. The long last half concerns historians, whom he values as the best view of man in general, and ends with three marginal verdicts he wrote in his own copies (Guicciardini, Commines, du Bellay). The essay is a self-report of taste, hedged repeatedly as the measure of his own sight and not of the books.

## Themes

**Method over matter; borrowing as weighing.** Montaigne asks readers to attend to his method, to whether he has chosen well what he borrows, since the invention is always his own. He says he weighs his borrowings and does not count them. He admits that he sometimes conceals the author of a transplanted argument, so that critics will rail at Seneca or Plutarch when they think they are railing at him. This is his own account of his practice, and a self-protective one. Compare [[personal-essay-as-form]] and [[self-construction-through-writing]], though here self-display is explicitly paired with self-doubt about retention.

**Judgment and its confessed limits.** He says that he has an impatient understanding, gives up on obscure passages, and does nothing without gaiety. When he finds himself disgusted with Plato's Axiochus, he says his judgment does not believe itself and condemns itself rather than the author. The verdicts he gives are meant to show the measure of his own sight. He names the confession of ignorance one of the surest testimonies of judgment. Knowledge and truth may exist without judgment and judgment without them.

**Reading for self-knowledge and living well.** The stated purpose of study is the knowledge of himself and how to die and live well. He wants Plutarch and Seneca for this, and he wants arguments that go straight to the point. Cicero's divisions and etymologies smother the marrow in a long preparation. Plato's preliminary interlocutions are, he says, lost time. This is a claim about his own appetite, offered with an apology for his ignorance of Greek, not a general rule.

**The man behind the book.** He prizes the Epistles to Atticus and the lives in Plutarch because they show private humours. One may judge an author's parts by the writings but not his manners or himself. He would rather know what Brutus said in his tent the night before a battle than the harangue next day.

**What a historian owes the reader.** He ranks historians in three kinds. The simple ones, like Froissart, record everything and leave judgment to the reader. The excellent ones have judgment. The middle sort chew the meat for us and bend the narrative to their bias. He argues that the best histories are written by participants, and he adds an anecdote of Asinius Pollio correcting Caesar to show how delicate the inquiry after truth is. He asks historians to leave the reader something to judge.

**Annotating books against forgetting.** To compensate for his weak memory he notes at the end of each book the date he finished it and his judgment. The three transcribed notes are exemplary: Guicciardini is faulted for attributing every motive to vice (Montaigne suspects the historian's own taste was vicious), Commines is praised for plain good faith, and du Bellay's Memoirs are called an apology more than history.

## Notable passages

The opening method statement (c1) is the essay's thesis on his own book: attend to method and to how he chooses what to borrow. The reading-for-living passage (c2) gives his stated purpose in study. The Cicero complaint (c3) shows his taste for plain argument, confessed as bold and partial. The passage on judging an author by private conduct (c4) links reading to curiosity about souls. The historians' taxonomy (c5) is his best-developed argument, about the transparency of sources and the reader's right to judge. The annotation custom (c6) introduces the three verdicts and ties memory to the writing process.

The essay is not systematic by its own admission: he says he heaps things up as they come to mind with fortune as his only officer. Nothing here is about the Wars of Religion or custom; the faint resonance with [[michel-de-montaigne]]'s other self-portraits is the self as the subject of the book.
