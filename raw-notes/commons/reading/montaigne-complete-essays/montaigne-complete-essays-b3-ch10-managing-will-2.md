# Avoiding Quarrels at the Source: Montaigne on Beginnings, Honour and a Quiet Mayoralty

This is the second half of "Of Managing the Will" (the slice opens mid-argument, on Caesar and Pompey's restrained enmity). Montaigne's practical rule is to stop passions, quarrels and entanglements at their first stirring, because once engaged a man is governed by the affair rather than governing it. He confesses that he evades passions more easily than he moderates them, and he prefers flight to the Stoic wrestling of Cato. He applies the rule to law-suits, gaming, property and friendship, and to the shameful patched-up quarrels of his day. He then turns to his own conduct as a municipal officer, answering critics who call him languid and ineffectual, and attacks ambition, ostentation and the hunger for fame for petty deeds. The slice breaks off with his claim to have left no hatred or regret behind him.

## Themes

**Check the beginning, because afterward the affair drives you.** The core claim is stated in the image of the reed and in the line "We guide affairs in their beginnings, and have them in our own power; but afterwards, when they are once at work, ‘tis they that guide and govern us, and we are to follow them." He also says: "He who stops not the start will never be able to stop the course." Trifling causes (a cartload of sheepskins, the graving of a seal, an apple) set great commotions going. Montaigne reports this as his own experience and rule of thumb, and he hedges that it has not spared him hard struggles: passions "are not always to be governed according to the measure of occasions."

**Flight over resistance, and a deliberately modest ethics.** He acknowledges that "some wise men" (Stoic exemplars such as Cato) have chosen to meet misfortune head-on, but says "Let us not attempt these examples; we shall never come up to them." The authorities he cites for flight are reported exempla: Zeno rising from his seat, Socrates' advice to "Fly it", Cyrus entrusting Panthea to another, and the Lord's Prayer (not to be tried by temptation). The Diogenes snow anecdote is told by a mocker, and is used to argue that to measure constancy one must know the suffering. He would "provide for sentiment, and not for patience" and rely on a "popular stolidity" of temperament in place of Stoic virtue. This is self-report offered as suited to "commoner souls", not a universal prescription, and he concedes that the sage and the peasant both find tranquillity while the middle region "harbours storms and tempests".

**Quarrel, honour and sincerity.** Montaigne denounces the "shameful and false" accommodations of quarrels, which save appearances and make men disown their real meaning. His rule is to maintain your "own true and sincere interpretation" whatever it costs, and he judges unsaying under compulsion worse than obstinacy. He also admits a self-diagnosis: the same difficulty that keeps him out of a quarrel would, once engaged, make him obstinate, and he calls this "the tyranny of custom" (compare [[habit-and-identity]]). Bias's "Undertake coolly" "but pursue with ardour" is cited approvingly.

**A self-portrait in office.** He offers an account of "this civic employment of mine" (he speaks of a "corporation" and "this city") that is half defence, half confession: critics say he was supine and his administration left no trace. He answers that want of care and want of sense are different things, that he is suited to short, direct and hazardous work rather than "long, subtle, laborious, artificial and intricate" work, and that he omitted nothing his duty really required, "to my knowledge". He is frank about his unfitness and that he does not much mind it. This is the essay as self-study ([[michel-de-montaigne]], [[self-construction-through-writing]]), with his hedges intact.

**Against ambition and noisy virtue.** He says the public wants noise: "if they hear no noise, they think men sleep." He argues that a good deed done for the noise is half spoiled, that the Stoics do not grant mere esteem to every virtuous act, and that his own virtue is that of preservation: "I had but to conserve and to continue, which are silent and insensible effects." He grants that ambition may be excusable in great souls such as Alexander and Alcibiades (exempla as he reports them), but not in "wretched and dwarfish little souls". There is a loose rhyme with the WMP concern with legibility (the visible, measurable act versus unseen maintenance of order, see [[Legibility]]), though Montaigne's point is moral, about reputation and the cost of display, not about institutions or infrastructure.

## Notable passages

**Sheepskins and the seal (c1).** The slice opens on Caesar and Pompey's moderated hatred and Montaigne's habit of opposing his will where it runs too warm; the Diogenes anecdote there is told by a mocker, not endorsed outright.

**Stopping the first sally (c3).** The most compact statement of his technique: "With very little ado I stop the first sally of my emotions." Passions admit no mid-course control.

**The reed and the drive of affairs (c4).** The reed image (long straight shoot first, then knots) turns the principle into advice about spending effort: begin gently and save vigour for the height of the business.

**Patched-up quarrels (c5).** The longest moral argument on honour and sincerity: "We give ourselves the lie, to excuse the lie we have given to another."

**The mayor's defence (c6).** His self-presentation to critics, including the line that he does not condemn "a magistrate who sleeps, provided the people under his charge sleep as well as he: the laws in that case sleep too."

**Conserve and continue (c7).** A defence of unglamorous maintenance over innovation, in a time when "we are pressed upon and have nothing to defend ourselves from but novelties", and of the dignity of inaction: "To forbear doing is often as generous as to do".
