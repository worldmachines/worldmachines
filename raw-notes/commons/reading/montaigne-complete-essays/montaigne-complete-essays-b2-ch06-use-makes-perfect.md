# Use Makes Perfect: Rehearsing Death, Painting the Self

The chapter opens on a general claim: argument and instruction will not carry us into action unless the soul is also exercised and formed by experience, which is why some philosophers went out to meet hardship rather than wait for it. Death is the exception, since we can experience it only once. Montaigne nonetheless holds that we can approach it and learn its avenues, through sleep, through swoons, and through the way imagination magnifies what we dread. He then tells at length how a fall from his horse in the civil wars left him unconscious and apparently dying, and what that felt like from inside. The second half turns the anecdote into a justification of his project: the accident taught him something for his own use, and that leads to a defence of speaking of oneself, of describing his thoughts and essence rather than his deeds, and of honest self-assessment.

## Themes

**Experience over doctrine.** The opening premise is that instruction alone leaves the soul "at a loss when it comes to the pinch of the business". The philosophers he cites (those who embraced poverty, austerity, even self-mutilation) are reported as examples of this principle, not endorsed in their extremes; Montaigne simply notes their motive. This is a first appearance of the idea that habit and trial make a person, close to [[habit-and-identity]], though here habit is a deliberate training regime and not an unnoticed cultural force.

**Death cannot be practised, but its approaches can.** Montaigne says outright that "we are all apprentices when we come to it". The Roman Julius Canus, who at his execution intended to watch the soul's departure so he could report back, is an exemplum he admires ("philosophises not unto death only, but in death itself"), but it is borrowed material and he follows it with "And yet I fancy" a more modest path: sleep as a likeness of death, swoons as near views of its face, and the argument that the moment of passage must be painless because pain needs time. The approaches, not the passage, are what we should fear, and those fall within experience. He hedges throughout ("methinks", "peradventure").

**Imagination inflates.** From his own health and illness he reports that imagination "enhances near one-half of the essence and reality of the thing", and he hopes death will prove likewise less than the preparation spent on it, while allowing that "we cannot give ourselves too much advantage".

**The accident as first-person evidence.** He narrates being run down by a follower on a stronger horse, lying senseless, being carried home, vomiting clotted blood, and returning to awareness slowly. The felt state was sweet, languid and painless. His body and senses acted (he tore at his doublet, ordered a horse for his wife) without his soul taking part; he reasons that passions which touch only the outward bark are not ours without the concurrence of the whole man. He uses this to confirm an old belief, contrary to La Boetie and many others, that the apparently agonised dying are insensible and not much to be pitied. He still names the one state he finds dreadful: a vivid, afflicted soul unable to declare itself. Recovery then brought real pain, and he notes that others invented stories to spare the man who caused the fall.

**Self-study as his own undertaking.** "This is not my doctrine, 'tis my study": he claims no authority beyond himself, notes that almost no one has followed this path, and calls it a rugged, new undertaking of probing the soul's "intricate internal windings". This is the self as subject, the essay as a technology of self-knowledge (compare [[self-construction-through-writing]] and [[personal-essay-as-form]]). He admits he is "perpetually tricking myself out", so self-description is also self-presentation.

**Against the custom that forbids self-talk.** He reports the prevailing custom as condemning all speech about oneself as boasting, and answers that this is like condemning wine because some get drunk. Socrates, saints and philosophers speak of themselves; "My trade and art is to live"; he paints thoughts, not acts, since acts speak more of fortune than of him. Honest report is the rule: understating oneself is folly, overstating is vice, and pride "dwells in the thought", so forbidding speech does nothing for it. The remedy for self-love is to look at oneself fully, against past ages and the nothingness of the human condition. He closes with Socrates, who alone "digested to purpose the precept of his god", and who may therefore speak boldly. Note the hedge: Montaigne says he is "not very well pleased with my own progress".

## Notable passages

The passage on death as the one thing without apprenticeship (c1) frames the problem. The sleep and swoon argument (c2) gives his constructive answer, followed by the imagination passage (c3) on dread exceeding reality. The account of the swoon and the claim about the dying (c4) is the empirical core, written in the first person with careful limits (he does not claim to speak for all dying). The pivot into self-study (c5) states why he told such a slight story. The defence of describing himself (c6) is among the clearest early statements of the essay's own method: "I do not write my own acts, but myself and my essence."

Note that Latin verse quotations in the unit are untranslated in this text, so they are not summarised here beyond their evident role as illustration of dying and swooning.
