# Self-publication, a private decline, and travel as unrest (Of Vanity, middle slice)

This slice is the fourth of five parts of "Of Vanity" and begins and ends mid-argument. It opens on the manners of illness (do not fish for pity), moves to what publishing his own character does for Montaigne, then to how he wants to age and die (privately, unburdensomely, at ease), then to why he travels and how he regards foreign customs. It closes with a long imagined rebuke ("you will everywhere follow yourself") and Montaigne's answer that the advice to be content is just "be wise" restated, and that all wisdom is vanity. The slice ends on the question of what use elevated philosophy is. Latin verse quotations here appear untranslated and are not relied on.

## Themes

**Illness without theatre.** Montaigne reports that he is weaning himself from the "childish and inhuman humour" of making his sufferings move friends to mourning. He gives the paradox that the man who always complains gets pitied by no one, and describes the sick who resent being told they look well. His own practice is to describe his infirmities "such as they really are" and to "study sickness whilst I am well". This is self-report, not a general doctrine; he calls the wise sick man one who still enjoys health in others.

**Publication as rule and as defence.** The book serves him "in some sort for a rule": the public image obliges him "to keep my way" and not to "give the lie" to it. He pre-empts slander by confessing his faults so fully that an attacker is left "fighting with the wind", and cites Bion's shameless account of his slave parentage as the model (an exemplum Montaigne approves). The book also works as a shortcut to friendship: a sympathetic reader learns in three days what close acquaintance would take years to reveal. This is the self-as-subject theme in its most practical form; see [[self-construction-through-writing]] and [[michel-de-montaigne]]. It half-fits the modern idea of self-curation, though Montaigne's stress is on being held to his own truthfulness rather than on projecting an image.

**Not a burden on others.** Montaigne argues that dying privately and far from home is no great harm. Prolonged decline teaches "your best friends to be cruel perforce", and one may lean on others but not "lay our whole weight upon others". He reports (without endorsing) that some Indian provinces knocked incapacitated men on the head and others abandoned them. He is sociable "even to excess" yet judges that decrepitude "is a solitary quality" and he should draw into his shell "like a tortoise". He keeps his religious and testamentary affairs settled while well.

**Language is unstable, so the book is for few.** He writes "for few men and for few years" because the French language has altered by half in his lifetime and each age calls its own speech perfect. This lets him include private allusions meant for those who knew him, and he wants no posthumous mythmaking about what he "would have said". He wants to leave "nothing to be desired or to be guessed at concerning me". The point about language change is an observation about his own era and not a general theory.

**Choosing a form of death.** He wants his death to "share in the ease and conveniences of my life", prefers weakness and stupor among natural deaths, and notes that imagination makes distinctions between modes of dying that the effect does not warrant. He cites Petronius and Tigellinus (as exempla, from his source) who rocked death asleep amid pleasures, setting aside the exemplary efforts of philosophy and religion. He finds it convenient that his death will neither help nor harm those concerned in him.

**Travel and custom.** He claims a body and palate as indifferent as any man's, welcomes the diversity of nations as "the pleasure of variety" and asserts "every usage has its reason". He mocks countrymen who cling to compatriots abroad and condemn foreign manners as barbarous merely because they are "not French", and young courtiers who are lost outside court intrigue. He seeks Greeks and Persians rather than Gascons, and hedges that he has "not travelled very far". Companionship is the thing he lacks on the road: no thought pleases him unshared, and he agrees with Archytas (as reported) that even heaven would be dull alone, though better alone than with fools. This is a plain statement of custom-relativism as lived habit; see [[habit-and-identity]] for the larger theme in other essays, though here it is a personal attitude rather than an argument.

**"Be wise" is not advice.** A voice (the imagined rebuke, built on Latin maxims) tells him he carries his trouble with himself and should reform. Montaigne replies that this is like a physician telling a patient to "be cheerful" rather than "be well": the resolution to be content is wisdom's "precise work and product", not a means to it. He admits that variety and "uneasiness and irresolution" govern him, that he loves private life because he chose it freely, hates any single dependence ("one cord will never hold me fast enough"), and concedes the charge of vanity: "all wisdom is vanity". He chooses to serve life "according to itself" over "elevated points of philosophy" that exceed our force. This is a sceptical and anti-heroic turn, but it is Montaigne's own report and hedged as a common man's view.

## Notable passages

**Publication as rule (c1).** The "unexpected advantage" of printing his manners, with the confession-as-defence logic ending in "fighting with the wind".

**Bion (c2).** The anecdote of the philosopher who preempts Antigonus's reproach with his own account of his origins, then Montaigne's gloss that a free confession "enervates reproach and disarms slander".

**Few men, few years (c3).** The statement about language change, private allusions and the wish to leave nothing to be guessed, with the vow to return from the dead to give the lie to anyone who misreports him.

**Death at his ease (c4).** His wish for a death that shares in the ease of his life and his remarks on how fear fixates on means rather than effect.

**Travel and foreign custom (c5).** Indifference of palate, ridicule of compatriots abroad, and the confession that he has not travelled far.

**Be wise (c6).** The physician analogy, the concession of irresolution, and the turn to vanity as a verdict on all wisdom.
