# Of the Force of Imagination; That the Profit of One Man Is the Damage of Another

Chapter XX opens from the schoolmen's tag that a strong imagination generates the event, and Montaigne starts from himself: he is among those "most sensible of the power of imagination," catching coughs and pains from the sight of others. A long chain of exempla follows (the man who dies of his own pardon, Cippus's horns, Mary turned Germain, the ecstatics in Celsus and Augustine), then a sustained, first-person treatment of imagination-induced impotence, including a story in which he rigs a ridiculous charm for a bridegroom. This leads to a mock defence of the male member as one of many body parts, and of the will itself, that disobey command. He turns to the placebo-like power of physicians' credulity-building, to imagination working across bodies (contagion, the evil eye, maternal impressions, animals), and ends by stating how he handles borrowed tales and why he declines to write contemporary history. Chapter XXI is a short piece: Demades condemned a funeral-supplier for profiting by death, but Montaigne says all gain is at another's expense, and nature herself operates by one thing's dissolution being another's birth.

## Themes

**Imagination as a bodily force, felt first in himself.** Montaigne's authority is partly self-report: he "usurp[s] the sensations of another person" and avoids what he lacks "force to resist." The doctor Simon Thomas's advice that the old patient gaze at Montaigne's youth, forgetting that this might harm Montaigne, shows the traffic between bodies. Most of the vivid cases (Cippus, the sex-change stories, Gallus Vibius) are exempla borrowed from ancient and contemporary report; the Vitry le Francois Germain and the friend's impotence are given as things he saw or knows by experience. He counts these as reported, not proved, and reasons that credit for visions and enchantments "derive[s] ... principally from the power of imagination" working on "vulgar and more easy souls." That is his own judgment, hedged with "very probable."

**Imagination can cure what imagination causes.** The friend's impotence comes from hearing another's story and is relieved "in another fancy" by confessing it beforehand. Montaigne's own gold-plate charm for the Count works because "their very inanity gives them weight and reverence"; he then disowns the trick ("I am an enemy to all subtle and counterfeit actions"). The clyster merchant, the woman with the pin, and the baked-cat feast carry the same point: belief substitutes for or produces the physical event. He reports physicians deliberately feeding patients' credulity so that imagination may "supply the imposture of their decoctions," which is a suspicion about his own era's medicine and fits his wider scepticism about learned authority.

**The disobedient body and the disobedient will.** The comic advocacy for the member extends to a serious claim: hair, skin, voice, bowels and heart all act without our consent, so the member is only one rebel among many. The sharper turn is against the accusers: the will itself often wills what reason forbids. The self, here, is not a sovereign commanding a body but a federation of partly autonomous motions, and the will is not exempt. The ending gestures at the member as author of "the sole immortal work of mortals" and cites Socrates, a mock-heroic register Montaigne uses while arguing something real.

**Imagination across bodies.** Beyond one's own flesh, he lists contagion, the evil eye, maternal impressions, and animal cases such as the cat and the bird. He is explicit that "Magicians are no very good authority with me" while still reporting what "we experimentally see." Attribution matters: the Scythian women, Pliny's sex-change and the falconer are borrowed; the stance toward them is open-minded but hedged.

**How he uses examples, and why not history.** Montaigne says the tales are charged to their sources while "the discourses are my own"; true or fabulous, instances serve if "possible," since his subject is human capacity ("what may happen") rather than what did. He forbids himself to alter even a circumstance, yet doubts that divines or philosophers, careful of conscience, are fit to write history, and refuses the invitation to write his own time's affairs, citing his short breath, poor command of words and dislike of continuous narrative. This is a rare explicit methodological statement of the essay as a form of testing judgment on exempla, and it resonates with [[personal-essay-as-form]] and [[self-construction-through-writing]], though Montaigne frames it as limitation, not programme. He cites Plutarch as an example of the divided responsibility: facts belong to others, the use for virtue to Plutarch.

**Profit and loss (ch. XXI).** Demades's judgment against the funeral-seller is called "ill grounded": merchant, farmer, architect, lawyer, even divines profit by others' vice, ruin or death, and "let every one but dive into his own bosom" and find private hopes growing at another's expense. He then reaches for nature's own economy, citing physicians, that the birth of everything is the dissolution of another. This is a zero-sum observation delivered as moral levelling, and the unit holds only this opening; later parts of the essay are not here. ## Notable passages

The opening self-report fixes the essay's evidence: his own susceptibility. The "ape's tricks" passage is the key analytic sentence about why inane rituals work. The catalogue of involuntary organs recasts the shameful failing of one member as the general condition of a body that does not obey. The physicians' credulity passage is a pointed medical-sceptical remark. The statement on borrowed tales is Montaigne's method in miniature. The Demades opening is the whole of chapter XXI here.
