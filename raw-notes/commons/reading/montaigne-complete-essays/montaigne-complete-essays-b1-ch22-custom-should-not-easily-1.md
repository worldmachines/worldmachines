# Custom as violent schoolmistress: how habit makes the strange natural

This is the first half of the essay (the slice ends at the claim that whatever is off the hinges of custom is thought off the hinges of reason; the essay's argument about not changing received laws is not in this slice). Montaigne opens with the story of the woman who carried a calf until it was an ox, and uses it to personify custom as a treacherous teacher whose authority arrives unnoticed and then becomes tyrannical. He moves from diet and the dulling of the senses (bells, noise, perfume), through the formation of moral character in infancy and his own self-report on honest play, to the plasticity of the body (the armless man of Nantes), and then to the mind: custom plants beliefs and even laws of conscience. A very long catalogue of foreign customs, followed by anecdotes about filial beating, serves to show that reason can be recruited to back any practice.

## Themes

**Custom as slow, unperceived power.** The opening image is of authority entering "by little and little, slily and unperceived" and only later unmasking itself. Montaigne frames custom as a teacher whose lessons we can no longer examine, because it has become the condition of looking at all. This is closely related to [[habit-and-identity]], though Montaigne treats habit as a force over whole peoples as well as individuals.

**Habituation dulls perception.** The senses are his first evidence: smiths cannot hear their own trade, his perfumed doublet stops pleasing him after three days, and the great bell in his tower, once insupportable, is now barely heard. The music of the spheres is reported as a philosophers' belief (not his own assertion) that we are deafened to it by constant exposure; he uses it as an illustration of the point.

**Moral character is formed in infancy.** Citing Plato's retort ("Custom is no little thing"), Montaigne argues that cruelty, tyranny and treason have their roots in what parents applaud in children. He rejects the excuse of triviality (crowns versus pins) and says children should be taught to abhor vices "for their own contexture". He then offers himself as an instance: his upbringing left him with a native aversion to cheating even at cards for farthings. This is a self-report, hedged as what he knows "for what concerns myself".

**The body adapts too.** The armless man who uses his feet as hands, and the boy who handled a halberd with his neck and shoulders, are anecdotes from Montaigne's own observation, offered as proof that custom can rewrite even bodily function.

**Custom governs belief; reason follows.** The central move: no opinion is so extravagant that custom has not established it as law somewhere, and "our reason does not ground and back up" any absurd fancy that meets with public practice. He sets aside religions from this claim, saying that errors there are more excusable since they lie beyond human reason and depend on divine illumination; that is a stated carve-out and should not be read as a sceptical attack on religion. The French gentleman who blows his nose with his fingers shows how an argument against our own habit can seem reasonable, and how Montaigne himself grew accustomed to it. From this he draws the relativist point that "Barbarians are no more a wonder to us, than we are to them", and that human reason is a tincture infused equally into all manners. This anticipates the cultural relativism of other essays and resonates with [[noble-savage]]-style debates only loosely; here the point is symmetry of strangeness, not the superiority of the foreign.

**The catalogue as method.** The long "where ... where ..." list of customs (marriage, funerary, religious, political, sexual, hygienic) is reported, not endorsed, with no sources named in this slice. Its force is cumulative: every norm seems to have a counter-example, so no single custom can claim to be nature's.

**Laws of conscience come from custom.** Montaigne asserts that "The laws of conscience, which we pretend to be derived from nature, proceed from custom". We suck custom in with milk, so what is off the hinges of custom is taken as off the hinges of reason, "how unreasonably for the most part, God knows". He is cautious in tone about how general this is ("for the most part").

## Notable passages

The calf-and-ox opening gives the essay its image of custom as a tyrant that enters gently. The ordinary-experience passage on bells and noise grounds the abstract claim in Montaigne's own tower. The passage on Plato and the boy is the moral heart of the slice: habit shapes character from the nursery. The transition into custom's power over judgement, with the nose-blowing story, shows the relativist turn and the symmetry between ourselves and foreigners. The closing paragraphs with Pindar's "ruler of the world", the family where sons beat fathers by hereditary custom, and the assertion that the laws of conscience come from custom, give the thesis in its strongest form.
