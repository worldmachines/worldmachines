# Learned ignorance and the Pyrrhonian suspension of judgment

This is the sixth slice of the long "Apology for Raimond Sebond" and begins mid-argument. It opens with madmen who were happier in their delusion, then with the philosophical tradition of leaving life when reason fails. From there Montaigne argues that simplicity is better than knowledge: the unlettered New World peoples, Lycurgus's Sparta, and St. Paul are his witnesses. He also argues that learning breeds malice and pride, that Socrates's wisdom was knowing his ignorance, and that human attributes cannot be applied to God. He then takes "man in his highest ground", the best philosophers, and says their long inquiry has taught them only their own weakness. He sorts philosophy into dogmatists, Academics and Pyrrhonians, and gives a long and sympathetic account of Pyrrhonism: how it argues, why it is livable, and why it leaves the sceptic docile and open to faith. The slice ends before the essay moves on, and it does not say what comes next.

## Themes

**Ignorance as a condition of happiness and goodness.** The slice opens with a man who believed he was always at the theatre and a man who believed all the ships in the harbour were his. Both resented the cure that gave them back their reason. Montaigne takes this to the Greek verse that "there is a great deal of convenience in not being over-wise", and to Ecclesiastes. The same strain runs through the reported philosophical cases of suicide (Democritus, Sextius, the Stoic "drink or go"). He reads these as "a confession of his impotency" and a retreat to "stupidity, insensibility, and nonentity". He is reporting what these authorities say and does not endorse the suicides.

**Learning versus innocence, and the New World.** Montaigne sets Lycurgus's letterless Sparta against the learned emperors who condemned letters. He sets the "new world" peoples who live "without magistrate and without law" against Europe, where lawyers outnumber other trades. A Roman senator is cited for the contrast between garlic breath with a clean conscience and sweet odour with inner vice. Montaigne's own gloss is that "curiosity, subtlety, knowledge, bring malice in their train". The New World reports are second-hand ("such as return ... testify"), and he uses them as a counter-example to legalism. He does not describe those societies for their own sake here.

**Pride, curiosity and the unknowability of God (a Christian frame).** Curiosity was the first ruin of man, and pride sends him into novelty and error. The Socrates anecdote is the model: he concluded that his only wisdom was not thinking himself wise. Montaigne argues that human predicates (anger, prudence, justice, temperance) cannot properly be said of God, and that faith is a gift and not an achievement. He backs this with Augustine, Tacitus, Cicero and Paul. This is theological fideism, and what he calls the incredible is for Christians an occasion to believe.

**The inquiry has only taught weakness.** Taking man "in his highest ground", Montaigne says the philosophers' long search has confirmed natural ignorance. He uses the image of ears of corn that droop when full, and the testimony of Cotta, Cicero, Pherecydes and Socrates. The claim is that even what we think we know is "a piece, and a very little one, of our ignorance".

**The three kinds of philosophy and the defence of Pyrrhonism.** Those who think they have found the truth are the Peripatetics, Epicureans and Stoics. Those who conclude it cannot be found are the Academics. Those who are still seeking are the Pyrrhonians. Montaigne reports the Pyrrhonian criticism that the Academics' denial claims too much, and he agrees that "the ignorance that knows itself ... is not an absolute ignorance". He renders the Pyrrhonian practice at length: argue both sides, assert nothing, and keep consent "ambiguous". This frees the sceptic from the tempest-driven attachment that fastens most people to whatever school they happened on, and it leads to ataraxy. The attraction is stated in terms of freedom and social peace, because dogmatism breeds "wrangling and seditious divisions".

**The sceptic lives by custom.** Montaigne rejects the caricature of Pyrrho as an immovable, unsociable figure. In the actions of life the Pyrrhonians follow natural inclinations, "the constitution of laws and customs" and the tradition of arts. The sage sails when the vessel is tight and the pilot is experienced, and follows "appearances". This makes custom the working substitute for knowledge, which resonates with [[habit-and-identity]], though here custom is defended as the sceptic's practical guide and not unmasked as an invisible machinery.

**The sceptic as a blank page for faith.** The slice ends by presenting the emptied sceptic as the best material for religion: "a blank paper prepared to receive such forms from the finger of God". This is a fideist argument. Doubt clears the ground for faith and does not replace it. How far Montaigne himself holds this position is a matter of reading, since he is here describing what the sceptical posture offers.

## Notable passages

The **New World and lawyers** passage uses reported evidence from returning travellers and a lawyers' verse to argue that learning and legalism do not make people better. It sits beside [[noble-savage]] themes, though Montaigne's point is about law and officials and not about a primitive ideal. The **Socrates** passage fixes the Apology's central move, that wisdom is the recognition of ignorance, and ties it to the scriptural "Dust and ashes". See [[socratic-thinking]]. The **Pherecydes** letter is a vivid anecdote: a sage tells Thales that his writings "contain no certainty with which I myself am satisfied" and that he "open[s] rather than discover[s] things". The **three kinds of philosophy** passage is the Apology's clearest map of the dogmatic, Academic and Pyrrhonian options, and it argues that Pyrrhonism is the more consistent. The **Pyrrhonian method** passage describes the both-sides argument. The **life by custom** passage corrects the legend of Pyrrho and states the working rule of appearances, laws and customs. The closing **blank paper** passage joins scepticism to faith. For the broader Montaigne see [[michel-de-montaigne]].
