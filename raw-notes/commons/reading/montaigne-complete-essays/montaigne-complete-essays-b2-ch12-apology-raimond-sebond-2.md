# Man Without Privilege: Animals, Stars and the Levelling of Human Reason

This is a middle slice of the *Apology for Raimond Sebond*; it opens by asking us to consider man "alone, without foreign assistance", stripped of divine grace, and to say on what grounds he thinks the world made for him. Montaigne then runs a sustained campaign against human presumption: first through the heavens (man's claim to understand bodies whose power over him his own reason concedes), then through animals, where he argues that beasts speak, reason, build, and provide for themselves at least as well as we do. He rebuts the common complaint that nature treated man worse than other creatures, argues that custom and luxury, not nature, make our needs, and ends on servitude: human beings are enslaved to one another more abjectly than animals are to us. The slice breaks off mid-argument on predation among animals.

## Themes

**Presumption as the original disease.** The slice's engine is the claim that "Presumption is our natural and original disease." Man, lodged in "the lowest story of the house", imagines himself above the moon. Montaigne mocks the idea that the cosmos exists for human service and demands to see the "commission" or "patent" that licenses it. Notably, he quotes a (Stoic-sounding) argument that the world was made for those with reason, "gods and men", and calls its conjunction impudent. This is a reported opinion he rejects, not his own.

**The astral argument turned on itself.** He grants, as "our reason teaches us", the premise that the stars govern even our thoughts and wills, then draws the consequence: if our reason is a gift of heaven, it cannot make us equal to or masters of what produced it. The quoted verse (including one about fate prompting the speaker's own discourse on fate) frames this as a self-undermining move: the theory of astral influence is itself an effect of astral influence. He treats this as a reductio of confidence, not as an assertion that astrology is true.

**Animals as rivals in reason and speech.** The cat passage ("who knows whether I do not make her more sport than she makes me?") reverses the observer's position; failure of communication may lie on our side, "we do not understand a Basque or a Troglodyte." Gesture is offered as proof that a full, public, undisciplined language exists apart from words. Bees, swallows and spiders are cited as displaying deliberation and geometry. Montaigne argues from "like effects" to "like faculties". Much of the evidence is exempla from ancient authors (Plato, Aristotle, Pliny, Lactantius, the Thracian fox story), reported rather than tested.

**Nature has not been a stepmother.** He answers the complaint that man alone is born naked and helpless: our skins serve, nations go unclothed, swaddling is unnecessary, eating is natural, newly discovered peoples live without tillage, and our arms are as natural as the ichneumon's mud cuirass. The balance is hedged ("If some beasts surpass us in this advantage, we surpass many others"). He even conjectures, flagging that the experiment is hard to make, that an isolated child would have some speech. This sits close to the nature-versus-artifice theme and shades toward [[noble-savage]] primitivism, though Montaigne's claim here is levelling, not praise of the savage, and the notion of provision "without cookery" is brief.

**Imagination as the dearly bought privilege.** If man alone has "liberty of imagination and irregularity of thoughts", it is "an advantage dearly bought": the source of "sin, sickness, irresolution, affliction, despair." He adds that being guided by nature is more honourable and safer than trusting our own fortuitous liberty, and that virtues naturally one's own are worth more than acquired ones. This is Montaigne's own judgment, tentatively put ("in my opinion").

**Servitude and the master-beast reversal.** The closing movement says our mastery over animals is only the advantage we have over one another. Examples of human self-surrender (the gladiator oath, Thracian wives, Scythian funerary killings, Diogenes: "'tis he that keeps and nourishes me that in reality serves me") make the point that men serve for less than animals are served. It anticipates a critique of power as mutual dependence, and the claim that humans yield to authority in ways beasts do not (a lion never submits to another lion) is Montaigne's flourish, not a settled doctrine.

## Notable passages

The opening (c1) sets the experiment of "a man alone" and the demand for his patent. The diagnosis of presumption and the cat (c2) are the best-known and most characteristic lines, a case of [[michel-de-montaigne]] turning his own pastime into an epistemological reversal. The swallow and spider passage (c3) is the central pro-animal argument from craft. The paragraph beginning "All this I have said to prove the resemblance" (c4) states the slice's thesis in his own voice: "We are neither above nor below the rest." The fox on the ice (c5) is the most compact exemplum of animal inference, rendered as an explicit syllogism. The slavery passage (c6) turns the argument to human power. As elsewhere in the Apology, this levelling is in service of scepticism about reason; in this slice the fideist remedy (divine grace) is only named at the start, not developed.
