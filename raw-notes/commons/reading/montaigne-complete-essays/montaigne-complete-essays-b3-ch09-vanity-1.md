# Vanity, scribbling and the burden of a household (Of Vanity, first part)

This is the opening of Book 3, chapter 9. Montaigne begins by conceding that writing about vanity is itself the most manifest vanity, and that his book, having no actions to report, must be made of his "fancies". He then blames the glut of writing on a disordered age, counts himself among the weak who contribute "folly, vanity, and idleness" to the common corruption, and turns to a run of self-portraits: how he behaves in bad and good fortune, why he loves foreign things and travel, and why governing his house (inherited late, and in his father's pattern) is a steady tax of petty vexations. The slice closes on a different register: a reflection on the civil wars showing that human society coheres, and even forms laws, out of necessity and vice, which makes designed polities look ridiculous. The essay is mid-course; this slice does not reach its later discussion of travel in full.

## Themes

**Self-writing as acknowledged vanity.** Montaigne does not defend his project but lays the charge against himself first: he can only give account of his life "by my fancies", and offers his pages as "the excrements of an old mind", set against the gentleman who displayed his basins. See [[michel-de-montaigne]] and [[self-construction-through-writing]]; the hedge is that the self-mockery is a stance, not a retraction, since he keeps writing and says he will "proceed so long as there shall be ink and paper".

**Scribbling as a symptom of a sick age.** He proposes, and says he is not joking, a legal restraint on "foolish and impertinent scribblers", and argues that people write most in troubled times ("since our troubles"; the Romans "upon the point of ruin"). The corruption of the age is the sum of each person's contribution; he places himself among the weak, who give vanity and idleness. The Philotimus anecdote and the report of a man issuing petty reforms of cloths and cookery are exempla he borrows to say that trivia are no remedy for grave disorder.

**Temperament and fortune.** He reports his own habits as self-observation, not as precepts: he lets things go to ruin by halves ("I scorn to mend myself by halves"), is more devout and modest in good than in bad fortune, and is stiffened by threats and bent by favours.

**Household government as petty vexation.** The central self-report: he came late to managing a house, does it by inattention rather than philosophic detachment, cannot bring himself to read his deeds, and keeps a deliberately "perplexed, uncertain knowledge of my money". Small ills prick deeper than great ones ("as little letters most tire the eyes, so do little affairs most disturb us"). He enjoys another man's house and wine more than his own, is calm abroad and irritable at home. He also honors his father's building and domestic rules out of respect, not satisfaction, and admits he is angry at himself for lacking the taste for husbandry; he denies this is disdain, calling it folly. He says the most honourable calling is public service, but disclaims it for conscience and "cowardice", citing Plato's abstention.

**Living by show.** He criticizes the habit of living by reference to others: we "care not so much what our being is, as to us and in reality, as what it is to the public observation". His own spending is half implicated (he lets money run if it shines). Keeping and spending are themselves indifferent, taking colour from the will.

**Society cohering without design.** Prompted by the civil wars, he says societies stick together "at what price soever", like shuffled bodies that settle of themselves "often better than they could have been disposed by art"; he cites King Philip's city of rascals and says even savage laws have sustained bodies as healthy as any devised by Plato or Aristotle. He reports with horror and half-admiration the vigour in signal villainies. This resonates with the [[history-machine]] idea of order assembling distributedly, though Montaigne's claim is a moral-observational one about necessity and is not a theory of eras, and he admits his own revulsion.

## Notable passages

The opening (c1) states the paradox of the project. The passage on scribbling and the age (c2) links writing to disorder and implicates himself. The passage on bad and good fortune (c3) is self-report on temper. The passage on appearances (c4) carries the critique of living for the public eye. The domestic vexation passage (c5) is the clearest case of his claim that small ills outweigh large. The closing passage (c6) is the strongest statement that order emerges from necessity, with designed commonwealths judged ridiculous.
