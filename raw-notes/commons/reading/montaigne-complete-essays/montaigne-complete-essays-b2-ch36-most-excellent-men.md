# Three Most Excellent Men: Homer, Alexander, Epaminondas

A short essay of personal ranking. Asked to choose among all men known to him, Montaigne names three: Homer (the poet), Alexander the Great (the conqueror) and Epaminondas (the Theban general), saving the highest place for the last. Each is judged by a different yardstick: Homer by the scale and permanence of his creation, Alexander by the extent of his achievement weighed against his faults and against Caesar, Epaminondas by conscience and manners, which Montaigne says outweigh everything else. The essay is mostly built from borrowed anecdotes (Alexander's cabinet for his Homer, Alcibiades and the book, Mohammed II's letter to Pius II), but the weighing and the final preference are explicitly his own, with hedges ("in my opinion", "methinks").

## Themes

**Homer as a cultural machine.** Montaigne admires Homer as the source from which later poets, legislators, soldiers and philosophers drew, a poet who defies the ordinary rule that things are born imperfect and grow. He also concedes that Virgil may equal him and that Virgil derives from him, deferring on the comparison to those who know both. Notably he remarks that Troy, Helen and the war are universally known "when perhaps there was never any such thing": a text creates the reality in which families, nations and even a Turkish emperor claim descent. Montaigne's wonder is at the afterlife of a fiction, presented as a stage on which kings and empires play parts. This sits loosely beside WMP interest in shared founding narratives as infrastructure, though Montaigne offers no theory, only admiration. See [[michel-de-montaigne]].

**Greatness weighed against its crimes.** On Alexander, Montaigne lists massacres and ruined cities as faults not to be excused, then argues that such men cannot work within the strict rules of justice and must be judged "in gross by the main end of their actions." He repeats an ancient saying that Alexander had his virtues from Nature and his vices from Fortune, and treats his vanities as youth's allowances. This is a consequentialist-leaning, tolerant verdict that Montaigne himself qualifies: the preference over Caesar rests on Caesar's aim being the ruin of his own country, and on Alexander's exploits owing more to fortune, Caesar's more to himself.

**Constant virtue over fortune-dependent virtue.** The third man wins because his innocence is "peculiar, sovereign, constant, uniform, incorruptible", whereas Alexander's virtue is subject to something else, variable and fortuitous. Montaigne makes manners and conscience, the thing that "alone truly denotes us for what we are", the decisive criterion, rating Epaminondas level with Socrates. Glory is explicitly demoted to "but a part of the substance of the thing." Montaigne's own frame (conscience over reputation) is consistent with the self-scrutiny theme of [[self-construction-through-writing]], though here he judges others, not himself.

**A hedge on the ideal.** Even of his hero Montaigne admits a reservation: Epaminondas's obstinate poverty is a little too scrupulous for him to wish to imitate. He then adds that the richest life he knows, for an ordinary gentleman of moderate ambition and no saint, is that of Alcibiades: a deliberate counterweight to the heroic ranking.

## Notable passages

**Homer's maturity at birth.** Montaigne calls it contrary to nature that the first poet is also the most perfect, hence "the first and the last of the poets." (c1)

**Homer's fame and the noble farce.** The anecdotes of Xenophanes and Hiero, the descent claimed by Mohammed II, and the image of the universe as a theatre for the Homeric farce. (c2)

**Alexander's faults.** The argument that great deeds exceed ordinary justice, closing on virtues from Nature and vices from Fortune. (c3)

**Alexander against Caesar.** Two fires or torrents overrunning the world, with the decision tipped by Caesar's ruinous object. (c4)

**Epaminondas.** Valour planted by wisdom and reason rather than ambition, and innocence compared with Alexander's. (c5)

**Alcibiades and the ordinary gentleman.** The turn to a different standard of the richly desirable life. (c6)

**The poverty reservation.** The one point at which Montaigne declines to imitate his hero. (c7)
