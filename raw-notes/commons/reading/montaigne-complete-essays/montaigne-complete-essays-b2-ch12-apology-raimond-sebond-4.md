# Beasts, Bees and Beauty: Human Pre-eminence Deflated

This is a middle slice (4/15) of the "Apology for Raimond Sebond", and it begins and ends mid-argument. It opens with the bees' wars (a quoted Latin poem) and uses them to expose the vanity of human warfare: armies are "an ant-hill disturbed and provoked", and the great causes of princes are as petty as ours. It then pivots into a long catalogue of animal virtues (fidelity, gratitude, mutual aid, magnanimity, repentance, clemency, mathematical and architectural skill) drawn heavily on Plutarch, Apion and hearsay. It turns to the imaginative faculty of dreaming beasts, and closes on human beauty, which varies by custom and in which man is not privileged over animals; the slice ends on nakedness and shame.

## Themes

**War as a spectacle of vanity.** Montaigne reads the quoted poem's bees as a picture of "human folly and vanity". The thunderous embattling of thousands is "pleasant to consider" given the idle occasions that start such wars and the light ones that end them. He cites Paris, and "the most victorious emperor that ever was" jesting over Fulvia in a quoted epigram. The mighty body is "yet man--feeble, calamitous, and miserable" and a sunbeam, a little dust, or a flight of ravens can undo it. His conclusion is levelling: "The souls of emperors and cobblers are cast in the same mould", and kings are moved by the same springs as ordinary people, only with more power to do harm. This is Montaigne's own judgment, backed by exempla (Portuguese besieging Tamly, routed by bees).

**Animals outdo us in the very virtues we claim.** The claim is made through reported anecdotes rather than first-hand observation: Pyrrhus's dog and Hesiod's dog (convicting murderers), the temple dog, Androdus and the lion (told on Apion's authority as an eye-witness), Æthon the grieving horse, the scarus and mullet freeing their fellows, the whale and its guide-fish, the wren and crocodile, the naker and shrimp, tunnies swimming in cubes, Alexander's dog, a penitent elephant, a clement tiger. Montaigne opens with a provocative line: "there is no animal in the world so treacherous as man". The method is the Apology's: each human virtue (fidelity, gratitude, society, magnanimity, repentance) is given its animal counterpart. Most of the examples are borrowed and credulous by modern standards; Montaigne signals sources (Plutarch, Apion, "'tis said") rather than vouching for them himself. Plutarch is explicitly noted as delivering the dog story "for a certain truth".

**Wonder before what we cannot imitate.** The halcyon's nest, whose construction not even "the most inquisitive into the secrets of nature" can fathom, is a case where an animal's art exceeds our understanding. Montaigne adds that even Plutarch's description does not give "sufficient light into the difficulty of this architecture", and asks what vanity lets us "despise and look down upon, and disdainfully to interpret, effects that we can neither imitate nor comprehend". This is the sceptical move: ignorance of the mechanism undermines contempt. (The halcyon is also framed providentially: God stills the ocean for her brood.)

**Beasts also imagine.** Against the soul's supposed privilege of conceiving things stripped of bodily qualities (Paris imagined "without greatness and without place"), he argues that dreaming horses and hounds do the same: the dog's quarry is "a hare without fur and without bones", and the watch-dog's intruder is a "man spiritual and imperceptible". He claims this is "almost certain" for the horse, hedging.

**Beauty is custom, not nature.** Montaigne doubts we know what beauty is, since there is no natural rule "as the heat of the fire" and each people "fancy the forms according to our own appetite and liking". A catalogue of Indian, Peruvian, Mexican and European ideals follows, and he sets Plato's spherical ideal beside the Epicureans' pyramid. Cultural relativism is applied to bodies. He then turns on human claims: many animals surpass us in beauty; our erect stature is merely "poetical" (camels and ostriches carry their heads higher); the beasts most like us are the ugliest (the ape, the hog). Man is the only animal whose nakedness offends his own kind, with more reason than any other to cover up.

## Notable passages

The levelling of emperors and cobblers is the unit's clearest statement of its anti-great-man psychology; it makes causes of history trivial, and rhymes with [[history-machine]] only loosely, since Montaigne is concerned with the pettiness of motives, not with distributed infrastructure. The ant-hill image and the bees show how fragile organized force is. The halcyon passage models Montaigne's sceptical argument from incomprehension. The dreaming-animals passage attacks a specific claim of reason's privilege. The beauty passage is a good early example of the relativism that links to [[habit-and-identity]]-type concerns about how custom makes things feel natural, though here the target is taste rather than law.
