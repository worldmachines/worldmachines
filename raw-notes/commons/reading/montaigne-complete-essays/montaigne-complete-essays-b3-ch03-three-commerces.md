# Of Three Commerces: Pliancy, Company, Women and the Tower Library

Montaigne opens by praising versatility (the elder Cato as exemplar) and then immediately confesses that he himself lacks it: his soul cannot rest on light things, throws its whole force at whatever is offered, and its chief study is itself. This unsuited temperament makes him choosy in company, so he sketches the three "commerces" that have occupied him: the conversation of a few sincere and able men, the company of beautiful women, and books. The first two are fortuitous and depend on others; the third is more certain and "much more our own". The essay closes with a first-person tour of his round study in the tower and a defence of reading for pleasure, not profit.

## Themes

**Pliancy as ideal, fixity as confessed fault.** Montaigne states the norm: "those are the bravest souls that have in them the most variety and pliancy", and a man tied to one course is a slave led "by the nose by one's self". But he reports this as something he fails at, not something he has mastered: he cannot easily disengage from "the importunity of my soul". He then concedes that a person whose ends are the ordinary conveniences of life ought to fly such delicacy "as much as the plague", and commends a "soul of several stages" that can chat with a carpenter or gardener. The essay holds the ideal and his own unsuitability to it side by side without resolving them.

**Self-study and meditation over furnishing.** The soul's "chiefest and hardest study is to study itself"; books are "a sort of employment that debauch it from its study"; and "I had rather fashion my soul than furnish it". Reading serves to rouse reason and judgment, "not my memory". This is a clear statement of the essay-writing self as its own subject (compare [[self-construction-through-writing]] and [[personal-essay-as-form]]), though here it is framed as a temperament he has, not a method he promotes.

**Against pedantry and borrowed lustre.** In the passage on learned men and ladies, Montaigne attacks those who "quote Plato and Aquinas in things the first man they meet could determine as well" and who shine "by a borrowed lustre". The remedy is judgment, natural gifts and a learning that is "suffragan and docile" in conversation. His advice to women on what learning they may take (poetry, history, the moral part of philosophy) is of its period and he offers it as his own opinion; it is not an exemplum from a source.

**Conversation as an exercise of souls.** The first commerce is with "sincere and able men", whose end is "simply privacy, frequentation and conference, the exercise of souls, without other fruit". Subjects do not matter; judgment, freedom and friendship do. He distinguishes this rare friendship from "numerous and imperfect friendships" and glances at the one perfect friendship of his youth, and at the danger of speaking of the world in his own age "either with danger or falsehood".

**Love as engagement, not performance.** On women he speaks from experience ("I there scalded myself in my youth") and rejects both extremes: furious fixation, and playing a part "like comedians" without love, which he calls a cowardly safety yielding no fruit to a noble soul. He argues Venus and Cupid cannot be separated and draws on animal behaviour for the point. Some of his remarks on beauty and on men governing the world are claims of his age that he states flatly; the Brahmin and Grand Signior details are reported anecdote.

**Solitude as an inward act.** His solitude is "to withdraw my thoughts and affections into myself", not to flee people; the crowd "thrusts me upon myself" at court. He denounces the "crowd of business" more than the crowd of men, and finds ambition a servitude (the public statue, a Latin tag untranslated in the text). He would rather be always alone than never so, which he applies to monastic communities.

**Books as the third, reliable commerce.** Books are constant and always at hand, a "viaticum" for the human journey; he enjoys them "as misers do their money", in the right to read them. He admits the costs: the body grows heavy and sombre. He studied first for show, then to be wiser, and now "for my diversion, but never for any profit".

## Notable passages

**The soul's own matter (c1).** The mind needs no foreign matter to exercise itself; its hardest study is itself, and nature has given it subjects "where it may either invent or judge". The compact statement of his self-regarding temperament.

**Against learned display (c2).** The learned strew their books everywhere and the learning "that cannot penetrate their souls hangs still upon the tongue"; a sharp version of his recurring contrast between judgment and stored learning.

**Solitude within company (c3).** Montaigne says he is "all without and in sight, born for society" and that solitude is turning one's thoughts and cares inward; at home he leaves his guests free while he sits "mute, meditating and shut up in my closet". It qualifies any reading of him as a hermit.

**Love without play-acting (c4).** The shortest route to safety in love is cowardice, he says; one must desire in earnest what one expects to enjoy.

**Books as the third commerce (c5).** The turn from the two fortuitous engagements to the book as the one that is "much more our own", with the miser comparison and the "viaticum" image.

**The tower study (c6).** A physical description of the round library with its five rows of shelves, where he walks while dictating "such whimsies as these I present to you here", and where "I am in my kingdom" and tries to be "an absolute monarch" over one corner. This is a rare account of the material setting of the essay's writing, and fits [[studiolo-and-self-cultivation]], though Montaigne himself describes it plainly as a retreat, with elsewhere only "verbal authority". His author is [[michel-de-montaigne]].
