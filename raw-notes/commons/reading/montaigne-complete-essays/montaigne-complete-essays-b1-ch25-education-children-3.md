# Education of children (closing third): conduct over books, things over words, and Montaigne's own schooling

This slice of the essay (the last part; it opens mid-argument and ends on the image of children as asses laden with books) first finishes the pupil's bodily and social training: avoid eccentric aversions, be fit for all nations and companies. It then insists that philosophy is lived, not read, and turns on pedants, rhetoric, sophistry and fine style, preferring a plain, sinewy, speech-like manner. In the second half Montaigne tells the story of his own education: Latin as a mother tongue through his father's scheme, a father's gentleness defeated by the College of Guienne, the accidental gift of Ovid and the stage, and the self-portrait of a slow, idle, but inwardly judging boy. It closes by returning to the thesis that appetite and affection must be won.

## Themes

**Suppleness against singularity.** Montaigne opens by calling "singularity" in manners inconsistent with civil society and holds that natural aversions can mostly be conquered "if he took it in time"; he reports that precept worked on himself "though not without some pains". Young bodies are supple and should be bent "to all fashions and customs". He hedges: there may be "some occult cause" for an aversion, and the limit is that the will stays within bounds: "Let him be able to do everything, but love to do nothing but what is good." Compare [[habit-and-identity]], though here habit is a deliberate training tool, not just invisible machinery.

**Philosophy as conduct.** Montaigne (with Plato, Diogenes, Heraclides, Zeuxidamus as supporting voices and exempla) holds that a lesson is shown in action: prudence, justice, constancy in sickness, modesty in mirth. "The conduct of our lives is the true mirror of our doctrine." The Lacedaemonian answer (Zeuxidamus) is reported as an exemplum he approves. This is the essay's central anti-pedantic claim; compare [[socratic-thinking]], which is likewise practice-oriented, though Montaigne's is not a method of questioning.

**Things before words.** College Latinists spend fifteen or sixteen years learning to speak and to babble; Montaigne uses the pedant anecdote ("He is not a gentleman; he is a grammarian; and I am a logician") to ridicule the guild. Inability to express a conception is, he suspects, a failure of conception itself: their "labour is not to delivery, but about conception". Anecdotes (Cleomenes' reply to the Samian envoys, the two architects, Cato laughing at Cicero, Menander's comedy "all but the verses") are borrowed exempla supporting him. Sophistical syllogisms are to be laughed at rather than refuted, unless they might plant an untruth.

**Plain style and its limits.** He wants a style "natural and plain, the same in writing as in speaking", sinewy, irregular, bold, and a "negligent" carelessness in language like negligent dress; he suits it to courtly ease in a monarchy. He observes that words are imitated easily but invention slowly: "force and sinews are never to be borrowed". He concedes fine speaking is a good quality, only not so necessary as some make it. This is self-description of his own prose as much as pedagogy, and bears on [[michel-de-montaigne]] as a writer.

**Montaigne's own schooling.** His father (reported as seeking the best advice) made Latin the household language, with a German tutor ignorant of French, and Montaigne spoke pure Latin before French; Greek was to come by play, and he was woken by music. He credits the father's prudence and affection and ascribes the poor yield to two causes: his own sterile soil (heavy, idle, slow wit, defective memory) and the father's yielding to common opinion by sending him to the College of Guienne, where his Latin "immediately grew corrupt". He is candid about failing to gain anything he "can honestly brag of". Note the hedge that the Latin-first scheme is not the sole route to ancient excellence: "I do not, however, believe that to be the only cause."

**Self-report and reputation.** Montaigne reports being thought idle, cold in friendship, too disdainful, "too particular", and answers that people unjustly demand supererogation from him, while the gratitude for gifts is greater where nothing was owed. He also claims strong inward judgment that could not be forced, and a talent for acting. These are his own claims, carrying his usual self-irony.

**Appetite, not compulsion.** Reading began in Ovid's fables; a tutor who connived at truantry let appetite carry him to Virgil, Terence, Plautus; otherwise he would have gained "a hatred of books". He defends the stage and public spectacle as civic goods. The unit ends with the image of "asses laden with books": learning must be espoused, not merely lodged.

## Notable passages

**Lived philosophy (c1).** The paragraph from "The lad will not so much get his lesson by heart" to "true mirror of our doctrine" states the practical test of education: look to the pupil's conduct in sickness, mirth, diet and domestic economy.

**Things before words (c2).** The pedant-logician anecdote and the argument that vague expression betrays vague conception is the sharpest anti-scholastic passage in the slice.

**Words are cheap to copy (c3).** Imitating phrases spreads through a people; imitating invention does not. "Most of those I converse with, speak the same language I here write; but whether they think the same thoughts I cannot say." A short reflection on borrowing and style as surface.

**Latin as mother tongue (c4).** The household Latin experiment, with named tutors and Buchanan's plan to base a treatise on it, is the concrete case for learning by immersion without whips or tears.

**Ovid and the tutor's discretion (c5).** The origin of Montaigne's reading and a compact argument that forcing books breeds hatred of them.

**Asses laden with books (c6).** The closing sentence summarising the whole essay's pedagogy.
