# Of the uncertainty of our judgment: every argument has its mirror

This whole essay (Book 1, ch. 47) is a single demonstration built from paired military exempla. It opens with a Latin tag on the liberty of speech and the example of Hannibal failing to exploit victory, then shows how critics of Moncontour or St. Quentin can explain the failure to press home a win as a mind overgorged with success. Montaigne at once turns round and argues the opposite: that pressing on is the mark of an insatiate spirit and that a cornered enemy fights ferociously. The same two-sided move is repeated for rich versus plain soldiers' equipment, insulting the enemy, disguising the general, Pompey's standing still at Pharsalia, and Francis I's choice to await Charles V at home rather than meet him in Italy. Montaigne then draws the conclusion in his own voice: events, especially in war, depend on fortune, which will not submit to human reasons, and our counsels and arguments themselves share in that chance. The essay is short and has no first-person digression; the self-report is minimal, and nearly all of the content is borrowed examples (Plutarch, Xenophon, Roman and French military history) arranged by Montaigne.

## Themes

**Every verdict has a mirror argument.** The essay's engine is a repeated pivot, a version of "But why may not a man also argue, on the contrary". For each commander's decision a persuasive case can be built for the choice made and for the opposite. Montaigne does not say which side is right; the point is that the arguments are symmetrical in quality. He explicitly reasons about the Pompey case that had Caesar lost, the same posture would have been praised. This is judgment as post hoc rationalisation: praise or blame follows the outcome.

**Hindsight and the critic's standing.** The opening sets up armchair critics who explain a leader's failure to exploit victory by psychology (a soul drunk with success, with no appetite left). Montaigne gives the reader the other critic immediately, who reads the same restraint as prudence. The first line, on there being everywhere much liberty of speech, frames judgment as cheap and plentiful. This is reported opinion tested by counter-opinion, not Montaigne endorsing either.

**Examples cut both ways.** Scipio succeeded by invading Africa while Hannibal ruined himself by going home to defend; the Athenians failed in Sicily while Agathocles succeeded in Africa. Identical strategies yield opposite results, so exempla cannot serve as rules. Montaigne grants that we conclude, with some reason, that war depends on fortune.

**Fortune governs deliberation too.** The final move is the sharpest: not only events but our counsels and deliberations depend on fortune, which engages our arguments in her uncertainty and confusion. He backs this with Timaeus in Plato (the participation of discourse in the temerity of chance). Reason is not an external judge of fortune but caught inside it. This is sceptical in spirit and anticipates the Apology's attack on human reason, though here the target is practical prudence, not the senses or theology.

**Attribution note.** The verse tags (Homer, Petrarch, the Latin quotations) are quoted without translation in this text; the poet's lines on prudence failing and a greater power ruling mortals are reported authority that Montaigne brings in to agree with the exempla's lesson. The detailed anecdotes (Sylla and Marius, Pharax, Clodomir, Vitellius, Pyrrhus, Clearchus) are borrowed history, not Montaigne's own experience.

## Notable passages

**The first two-sided argument (c1, c2).** The critics' case against the unexploited victory is given fully, then reversed: pushing a beaten enemy to despair invites disaster, as the examples of Foix at Ravenna and Clodomir show. See the verbatim passage opening "But why may not a man also argue, on the contrary".

**Equipment and morale (c3).** Rich arms either spur courage and give soldiers something to defend, or double their fear and tempt the enemy; Hannibal's retort to Antiochus (the Romans would be satisfied, "were their avarice never so great") turns the argument on its head.

**Pompey at Pharsalia (c4).** Montaigne borrows Plutarch's words explicitly, and then notes that if Caesar had lost, a critic could equally have praised Pompey's steady stance. This is the cleanest statement of how criticism tracks outcome.

**Francis I's dilemma (c5).** A long, balanced pair of lists of advantages for meeting the invader abroad and for waiting at home, ending with the king's choice and the remark that examples exist for both cases. It reads like a decision matrix with no dominant column.

**The conclusion (c6).** Fortune will not be governed by human prudence, and our reasoning shares in the temerity of chance. This resonates loosely with the project's wariness of confident forecasting (WMP's emphasis on uncertainty, closer to climate forecasting than prophecy) and with the sceptical turn in [[michel-de-montaigne]], though Montaigne's claim is about practical judgment in war and is not a theory of historical structure; he offers no machinery behind fortune. See also [[socratic-thinking]] for a looser kinship of method: testing a confident claim by producing its opposite.
