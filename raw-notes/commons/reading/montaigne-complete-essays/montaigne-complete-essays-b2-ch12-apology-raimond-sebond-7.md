# Philosophers' Doubt and the Catalogue of Gods

This slice is the middle of the Apology for Raimond Sebond (7 of 15). It opens by finishing a count of the three philosophical sects: two openly profess doubt, and Montaigne argues the third, the Dogmatists, mostly only assume a confident face. He gathers evidence from Timaeus, Aristotle, Cicero's Academics, Plato and Socrates, then turns to why people keep inquiring when truth is out of reach (Democritus and the honeyed figs, Eudoxus and the sun). He next suggests the philosophers wrote for exercise or for public utility, and moves to religion: pagan worship, an endorsement of an incomprehensible deity, and a long doxographic catalogue of ancient opinions about the gods. He closes by ridiculing gods fashioned in human likeness. The slice is Montaigne reporting and arranging other voices; his own judgments appear mainly in the closing third.

## Themes

**Dogmatists as hidden doubters.** Montaigne claims that most dogmatists show "how far they have proceeded in their search of truth" rather than establish certainty. His evidence is borrowed: Timaeus speaking "as a man to a man", Aristotle (the "prince of all dogmatists") saying the more we know the more room for doubt, and Cicero's account of the Socratic-Academic practice of disputing against everything and concluding nothing. Aristotle's obscurity is called "a Pyrrhonism under a resolutive form". These are exempla and cited opinions in service of his case, not neutral reporting.

**Obscurity as a trade trick.** Philosophers affect difficulty, he says, to hide the vanity of their art. The coin-of-the-learned image is Montaigne's own. A related strand (Cicero, Zeno, Chrysippus, Epicurus, Socrates) lists thinkers who despised most branches of learning except what bears on conduct and life; Socrates examined a person's life before teaching him anything.

**Contradiction as method.** Plato's dialogues, Socrates as midwife who does not himself give birth, and the self-contradictions of Seneca and Plutarch are read as a deliberate way of presenting "the diversity and variety of his own fancies". Montaigne defends treating things variously and compares parliamentary judgments, whose beauty lies in the "dispute and heat of divers and contrary arguments" rather than the common conclusion. This reads as a defence of a many-voiced mode of writing, though he does not apply it to himself here.

**The pleasure of the chase.** Men who despair of reaching truth keep hunting for its own sake. The Democritus figs anecdote and the Plutarch examples (the man who will not let his thirst be quenched) illustrate a "sickly curiosity". The Eudoxus wish to see the sun is reported as an example "they" urge, and Montaigne treats it as vain. Pleasure in knowledge is compared to pleasure in eating that nourishes nothing.

**Truth versus utility, and the civil law.** Montaigne doubts that Epicurus, Plato and Pythagoras seriously meant their atoms, ideas and numbers. Some doctrines were written for public benefit, such as religions, which philosophers did not probe lest they disturb obedience to laws and customs. Plato is said to foist fantastic inventions on the vulgar when he plays the legislator, and the Republic maxim about necessary deception is cited. Even the boldest sects must "submit to the civil law at the end of the account". This touches custom's governing role, though Montaigne frames it as the ancients' practice, not his own policy.

**Religion: the incomprehensible God and the need for images.** Montaigne's own stance: of ancient religious opinions the likeliest and most excusable acknowledges God as an incomprehensible power, and the altar to the "unknown God" seemed most excusable to St. Paul. Pure mental religion would be useless, since the human mind needs a sensible image, and the senses in Christian ritual warm the people. He leans toward sun-worship among those who gave God a body. Note the fideist frame: pagan piety is tolerable as dim natural knowledge, while human-invented gods are "impious and injurious".

**The doxography as reductio.** A long catalogue (Thales to Epicurus) shows ancient writers contradicting each other, and often themselves, on what God is. Montaigne's conclusion is personal: contrary opinions "do not so much displease as instruct me" and "humble" him; reason is no steadier than fortune. Making gods of ourselves, with our desires, fevers and funerals, springs from a "marvellous inebriety of the human understanding", and he would sooner side with worshippers of the serpent, dog or ox, whose nature is less known.

The habit of weighing opposed views resonates with [[socratic-thinking]] and with Montaigne's wider practice described at [[michel-de-montaigne]], though the Apology's scepticism here serves a fideist argument that the essay as a whole still has to complete.

## Notable passages

**Difficulty as a coin (c1).** The juggler image: obscurity is a currency the learned issue to cover the vanity of their art, which fools accept as real. It is Montaigne's own sharp comment on authority and prestige.

**Socrates the midwife (c2).** Socrates as one who helps others deliver and does not himself bring forth, an image of philosophy as assistance and judgment rather than doctrine. Montaigne generalizes it to a class of writers who are inquiring rather than teaching.

**Democritus and the figs (c3).** A story of a philosopher annoyed that a maid explained away the honey; he resolves to seek the cause as if it were natural. Montaigne reads it as the passion for pursuing what we despair of acquiring.

**Plato and useful untruths (c4).** Plato's raillery, the legislator's magisterial style and the claim that it is often necessary to deceive men. The text sets sects that follow truth against those that follow utility, and notes the boldest sects still submit to civil law.

**Contrary opinions instruct (c5).** After the god-catalogue the personal voice breaks in: the diversity of opinions humbles rather than irritates him, and any choice not from God's own hand has "very little privilege".

**Gods made in our image (c6).** Anthropomorphic gods, with our bones, fevers and funerals, are condemned as an intoxication of understanding; the unknown is the more fitting object of deification.
