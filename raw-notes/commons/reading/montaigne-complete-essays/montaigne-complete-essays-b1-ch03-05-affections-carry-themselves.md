# Beyond the Present: Posthumous Concern, Misdirected Passion, and Parley

Three short essays. The first begins from the claim that we are never present to ourselves but always pushed forward by fear, desire and hope, and then spends most of its length on a single case of that forward reach: the concern the living show for what happens to their reputation, corpse and funeral after death. The second observes that a passion in the grip of grief or rage must have an object, and will invent a false one rather than have none. The third asks whether a besieged governor should leave his fort to parley, surveying ancient and modern codes of fair war and concluding with a cautious, qualified answer and a personal confession about trust.

## Themes

**We live beyond ourselves.** Montaigne opens by granting those who tell us to enjoy the present that they have "hit upon the most universal of human errors", then adds that nature herself disposes us to it, "being more jealous of our action than afraid of our knowledge". He then ties this to Plato's "Do thine own work, and know thyself": the wise person acquiesces in the present and is never dissatisfied with herself. The folly of foresight is thus reported as a general human condition, with the Platonic and Epicurean ideal held up beside it, not as a thesis Montaigne simply adopts.

**Judging the dead, and the limits of that judgment.** Montaigne (in his own voice, "I look upon that to be very sound") approves the custom of examining princes' actions after death: obedience is owed to the office of bad kings, but esteem only to virtue, and flattering the memory of a bad prince does "private right at the expense of public justice". The Nero soldiers and the Spartan funeral custom of calling every dead king the best they ever had serve as exempla for and against this. He then reports Aristotle's puzzle about Solon (can a man be happy if he leaves an ill repute?) and answers on his own account that once out of being we have no communication with what is, so Solon might better have said that man is never happy because never so until he is no more.

**Exempla of the dead reaching into the world.** A run of anecdotes (the keys of Rancon laid on Guesclin's corpse, Trivulzio refusing safe-conduct for Alviano's body, Edward I's boiled bones, Zisca's skin drum, the American Indians carrying a captain's bones, Bayard dying face to the enemy) shows a "general practice in all ages" of extending self-concern past the grave. Montaigne distinguishes those who keep only reputation from those who attribute "a certain present and active power" to remains. The Greek rule that asking an enemy for a body to bury concedes victory shows burial as a political act.

**Funerals as vanity, and as custom.** Maximilian's modesty extended to drawers on his corpse; a kinsman died anxious about funeral attendance ("so persistent a vanity"); others fuss over ostentatious frugality. His own view: each should suit the matter to his fortune, and "I should wholly refer the ordering of this ceremony to custom". Funeral rites are consolations of the living more than aid to the dead (a cited saint), and Socrates' "How you will" is the model. He also tempers this with a wry preference, were he to care, for those who enjoy their own monument in advance. The essay ends with the Athenian execution of the Arginusae generals and Chabrias's lost victory at Naxos, both cases of superstition about burying the dead overriding living interest, then a closing image of wine and venison retaining an occult relation to their living origin.

**Passion needs an object.** The gouty gentleman who curses sausages and hams is Montaigne's opening joke, then a general claim: the soul, "being transported and discomposed, turns its violence upon itself, if not supplied with something to oppose it", and prefers a "false and fantastical" subject "even contrary to its own belief" to none. Animals bite the weapon, mourners tear their hair, gamblers chew the cards, Xerxes whips the sea. He escalates from folly to impiety: kings and emperors defying God, Neptune, or Fortune, "as if she had ears that were subject to our batteries". Bion's quip on the bald mourner is borrowed; the closing "we can never enough decry the disorderly sallies of our minds" is Montaigne's.

**Honour and fraud in war; the governor's parley.** The Roman elders, Achaians, the people of Ternate and the Florentines (Martinella bell) are cited as cultures that announce war and refuse deceit, whereas "ourselves", who credit honour to whoever profits, hold with Lysander's fox-skin that a governor should never leave his place to parley. Montaigne does not settle on a rule: it is excusable if safety and advantage are on his side (Rangone), Eumenes demanded a hostage first, and Henry de Vaux did well to go out on the besieger's word (told his ruin was certain, he surrendered to his enemy's discretion and the castle was then blown up). He closes in the first person: he can rely on another's faith readily, but not where it would look like despair or lack of courage rather than free confidence.

## Notable passages

The opening of Chapter III (c1) states the forward-leaning condition that organises the first essay and ties it to fear, desire and hope. The Solon passage (c2) is Montaigne's own reply to Aristotle and fixes the metaphysical limit: the dead are outside communication. The funeral passage (c3) shows his habitual move of deferring to custom ([[habit-and-identity]] is a loose parallel, though here custom is a convenient default for an indifferent matter rather than the invisible machinery of belief), and the Socrates exchange supplies his model. The generalisation about passion (c4) and the Xerxes catalogue (c5) are the clearest psychological claims in the unit, anticipating his later self-observation (compare [[self-construction-through-writing]], though no self-writing appears here beyond a few "I" asides). The parley passage (c6) shows an essay whose conclusion is explicitly conditional, mixing cited precedent with personal temperament.

The unit also shows the essay form at work ([[personal-essay-as-form]]): exempla stacked, a stance tried and qualified, and the self appearing in asides (modesty about bodily functions, the stone, "I should wholly refer the ordering of this ceremony to custom").
