# Cowardice, Cruelty and the Season of Things (Essays II.27-28)

Two short essays. The first, "Cowardice the Mother of Cruelty", starts from a remark Montaigne says he has "often heard" and "found by experience": the cruellest people are the weak and fearful ones. He extends it from massacre by camp-followers, through the revenge-killing and the seconds-in-duels fashion of his own France, to tyrants who kill from fear for their own safety, and ends with a run of grisly tortures. The second, "All Things Have Their Season", compares the two Catos, then turns to old age: studying and desiring should be suited to one's years, and Montaigne reports his own practice of thinking "of nothing now but ending".

## Cowardice the Mother of Cruelty

**Cruelty as cowardice (Montaigne's thesis, offered with experience as its warrant).** Real valour "stops when it sees the enemy at its mercy"; the slaughter after a victory is done by the "rascality and hangers-on" who dared not enter "the first act of danger". The same argument is applied to the lethal quarrels of his own day, where nothing is said but "kill". The anecdote of Alexander of Pheres, who could not bear to be seen weeping at tragedy yet had citizens murdered daily, is borrowed, and serves as the exemplum for the opening claim.

**Revenge defeated by killing.** Montaigne reasons that revenge wants the enemy to feel it, so killing him defeats the purpose: it is "more an act of fear than of bravery; of precaution than of courage; of defence than of enterprise". Bias's remark and the Orchomenian anecdote illustrate that revenge needs a living witness. The same logic condemns Asinius Pollio's posthumous libel, and Aristotle's quip is quoted in support. Note he is reasoning about what the avenger really wants; he does not claim killing is always unjustified (he allows it "to frustrate an offence to come").

**Duels, seconds and the rules of honour.** He blames cowardice (the dread of solitude in danger) for turning duels into group "skirmishes, rencontres, and battles", where the invited second cannot stand idle without seeming wanting in courage or affection. He then argues, against what he thinks is fair play, that once engaged the multiplied combat counts as one hazard. He admits a personal stake: his brother, as a second at Rome, killed his man and then went to help his friend, and was imprisoned until the king interceded. Montaigne says openly that he would like an explanation of the rules of honour "which so often shock and confound those of reason", so his own defence of his brother is hedged and a little uneasy. He turns a joke on the French abroad ("Indiscreet nation!").

**Fencing as a counterfeit of valour.** He holds that skill-based fencing is "not properly valour, because it supports itself upon address"; he gives the example of a friend who chose arms that nullified his skill, and notes that in his youth gentlemen hid their fencing. Drawing on Rutilius, Philopoemen, and Plato's Laches and Laws (all reported authorities), he argues that martial exercise should serve public ends, not private quarrels. He notes himself that he has "strayed from my theme".

**Tyranny, fear and the escalation of cruelty.** Mauricius's inference from Phocas's cowardice and the explanation that tyrants kill because "their faint hearts can furnish them with no other means of securing themselves" make a spiral: first cruelties breed fear of just revenge, which breeds more cruelty (Philip of Macedon's plan to kill the children of his victims). Montaigne openly admits that he drags in the long story of Theoxena (a digression, defended as "fine matter" that "is never impertinent"), an exemplum of maternal courage and a double suicide to escape the king's soldiers.

**Torture beyond death.** He declares that "All that exceeds a simple death appears to me absolute cruelty", questions whether judicial torments (fire, pincers, the wheel) deter anyone whom death does not, and wonders whether his own era keeps "some traces of this barbarity". Josephus, Chalcondylas on Mohammed, Croesus and George Sechel are cited as historical cases, with Montaigne's own observation that the most dreadful to watch are not always the worst to endure.

## All Things Have Their Season

**The Catos and the unseasonable good.** The essay opens with a comparison of the two Catos in which the younger is judged purer and the Censor tainted with envy and ambition. Montaigne reads the elder Cato's late study of Greek as "falling into second childhood". His principle: "All things have their seasons, even good ones", illustrated by Flaminius praying during a battle, Eudemonidas on Xenocrates, and Philopoemen on Ptolemy (all reported anecdotes), with a Horatian tag that the wise set an end even to honourable things.

**Montaigne on his own ageing.** The old-age argument becomes self-report: his longest design reaches a year, he rids himself of hopes, takes leave of every place, and counts it "the only comfort I find in my old age" that it mortifies his cares for the world, riches, grandeur, knowledge, health and himself. He says that a man "may always study, but he must not always go to school". The consistent line is that late study should be what is suitable to one's present condition, "that I may go out better".

**Cato's night.** He ends with the younger Cato reading Plato on the soul on the night he died as part of his ordinary routine, not as preparation, since his certainty already exceeded the philosopher's: loss of life or office was "all one to him". Cato is Montaigne's exemplar of death met without a special performance.

## Notable passages

The opening of ch. 27 states the thesis and its ground in observed behaviour, the cruel who weep. The revenge passage is the sharpest piece of reasoning in the essay: a psychology of what vengeance wants, which undermines the practice of killing. The seconds-and-duels passage shows custom escalating (compare [[habit-and-identity]], though Montaigne here treats it as a fashion that fear produced, not as invisible machinery). The tyrant spiral and the torture paragraph mark his recurrent horror at cruelty in the age of religious wars (the essay does not name those wars in this unit). In ch. 28 the passage on the year-long horizon is a clear piece of self-portrayal, belonging to the essay as a form of self-study ([[self-construction-through-writing]], [[michel-de-montaigne]]); the closing Cato note gives the Stoic's death as a continuation of ordinary life.
