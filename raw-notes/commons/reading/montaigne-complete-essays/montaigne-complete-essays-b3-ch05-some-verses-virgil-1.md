# Ageing, Candour and the Marriage Bed: the opening of "Upon Some Verses of Virgil"

This is the first slice (roughly the first fifth) of the long chapter V of Book III, and it is cut off mid-argument, before the essay reaches its sustained treatment of sex, so it should be read as an opening movement only. Montaigne begins with the weight of "profitable" thoughts (vice, death, poverty, disease) and the need to relieve them with moderate levity. He describes his own ageing: the body now preaches to him, his clear days have become extraordinary, and his mind, which he would like to keep green, is bound to the body's condition. He praises a gay and civil wisdom over austerity, then states his rule of dare-to-say-what-you-dare-to-do, defends confessing his faults publicly, and insists on being truly known rather than falsely praised. He then asks why the act of generation cannot be spoken of, defends the Muses' and Venus's alliance, admires Virgil's Venus (with a mild complaint that she is too passionate for a married one), and argues that marriage is a matter of family, prudence and constancy, not of love, comparing the confusion of love and marriage to the confusion of nobility and virtue, with exempla about Antigonus and Calicut.

## Themes

**The body governs the mind in age.** Montaigne reports (as self-observation, not doctrine) that "‘tis now my body’s turn to guide my mind" and that wisdom, too, "has its excesses". The mind "has contracted so strict a fraternity with the body" that when the body has the stone the mind seems to have it too. He faults "our masters" for leaving health out of the causes of extraordinary states of the soul. This is a sharp empirical rebuke to any picture of the mind as independent of the body; he hedges that the mind should still try to "continue green, and flourish if it can, like mistletoe upon a dead tree".

**Gaiety against severity; philosophy in practice.** He rejects sour, froward spirits ("Virtue is a pleasant and gay quality", citing Socrates's serene face against the never-laughing elder Crassus, with Plato as authority for the link between humour and character). His philosophy "is in action, in natural and present practice, very little in fancy". He wants pleasures "sweet, facile, and ready" rather than magnificent, and mixes folly with counsel by design. Age is licence to retire from the world's opinion; the young "are going towards the world and the world’s opinion; we are retiring from it".

**Confession as a discipline of self-knowledge.** Montaigne enjoins himself "to dare to say all that I dare to do", reasoning that boldness in confessing offsets boldness in doing ill, and that those who hide vice from others hide it from themselves. This is the essay as a technology of self-disclosure ([[personal-essay-as-form]], [[self-construction-through-writing]]). He says he would rather be better known than more commended, using Archelaus ("he did not throw the water upon me, but upon him whom he took me to be") and Socrates as anecdotes borrowed to illustrate the point that praise addresses a mask. Note the hedges: he admits his candour may be "indiscretion", and that he is "greedy of making myself known" yet "hunger for nothing".

**Lying is worse than the sin it hides.** Montaigne reports that he cannot dissemble and judges lying a worse fault than adultery, so he would have disagreed with Thales, who advised swearing to deny it; he calls that counsel "a multiplication of vice", and uses the Origen case and a quip about women's consciences to illustrate hard choices between two vices. These are reported cases he weighs, not rules he derives.

**Silence around sex as a social convention.** The unspeakability of generation is for him a convention: the words least spoken are the best known, and the "franchise of silence" gives the act a perverse immunity. The point has the shape of an analysis of custom as invisible machinery, though here applied to taboo rather than law (compare [[habit-and-identity]]). He sides with "the ancient school" against the modern, and with Aristotle that bashfulness reproaches old age.

**Marriage is not love.** Citing Aristotle and physicians (the latter on health and conception), he argues men marry "for their posterity and family", that arranged matches by a third hand are preferable, and that "furious ardour is worth nothing" in a marriage. A good marriage resembles friendship, "a sweet society of life". This is Montaigne's own view, given with qualifiers ("if there be any such"), and is a different register from his strong claims about love and the Muses elsewhere in the slice.

**Nobility is borrowed, artificial and custom-bound.** By analogy with joining love to marriage, he argues that those who equate nobility with virtue confuse two things. Nobility is a quality "depending upon others", "various in form, according to the country". Exempla (a king's rule for choosing between candidates, Antigonus and the soldier's son, Sparta's hereditary trumpeters and cooks, Calicut's caste rules) illustrate hereditary status as a customary machine of inheritance, closer to a regime of [[Legibility]] by trade and birth than to merit. This is a loose rhyme only; Montaigne is arguing about virtue, not infrastructure.

## Notable passages

**Wisdom has its excesses (c1).** The essay's opening defends moderation even of serious thought: ageing has swung him from sprightliness to severity, and his body now plays the preacher. It sets the register of self-report against the Stoic ideal of constant meditation on death.

**Mind and body in one fate (c2).** The mistletoe-on-a-dead-tree image and the complaint that philosophers omit health from the sources of enthusiasm are the most direct statement here that thought tracks bodily condition.

**The rule of saying what one dares to do (c3).** A programmatic passage on confession and concealed vice, with the claim that the diseases of the soul "keep themselves the most obscure".

**Known truly rather than praised falsely (c4).** The Huguenot jibe, the Archelaus and Socrates anecdotes, and "I am content to be less commended, provided I am better known" together state the ethic of the self-portrait. His complaint that his Essays serve ladies as furniture shows his awareness of readership.

**The franchise of silence (c5).** The unspeakable act, and how suppression makes books and desires more public.

**Marriage and passion (c6).** Marriage as lineage, prudence and moderation, with a cold judgement that marriages made for beauty and desire fail soonest.

Possible connections to the wider project are modest: [[michel-de-montaigne]] as a figure of self-study, and the book-length self-portrait as a distinct early-modern form. The slice contains no treatment of Virgil's verses beyond quotation and praise of their erotic force; much of the essay's sustained argument lies beyond this slice.
