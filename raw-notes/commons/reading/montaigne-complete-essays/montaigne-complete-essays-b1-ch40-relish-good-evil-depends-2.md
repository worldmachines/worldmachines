# Opinion, pain and money: how the soul gives goods and evils their taste

This is the closing part of the essay (the slice starts mid-argument, with Scaevola and the Roman gladiators). Montaigne first assembles a long run of exempla showing that people bear, inflict on themselves, or even court pain and loss when opinion makes them honourable or desirable: Scaevola, a gladiator of Caesar's, women who damage themselves for beauty, lovers' and penitents' self-wounding, bereaved Roman fathers, Cardinal Borromeo. He then generalises that our opinion gives things their value, and turns to a long first-person account of three phases of his own relation to money (dependence on others, anxious hoarding, living at the height of his revenue). He closes that plenty and indigence, like glory or health, are what the possessor makes of them, and that, since many arguments against pain and death exist, each person should apply whichever suits his own humour, with philosophy's last reply being that no one is forced to live in necessity.

## Themes

**Opinion as a "powerful party".** Montaigne cites a Latin sentence (untranslated here) that grief lies "not in natura, sed in opinione", and declares "Opinion is a powerful party, bold, and without measure." His examples run in both directions: people who feel nothing at what the world calls terrible (the three fathers who buried sons with no grief) and people who forsake ease for hardship (Alexander and Caesar hunting difficulty, those who retire to deserts, Borromeo's straw bed). He does not endorse the extreme cases: "I do not follow these monstrous humours", and reports his own grief at losing children at nurse.

**Value is what we add, not what things bring.** "we only repute for value in them, not what they bring to us, but what we add to them." Cost itself becomes part of a thing's apparent substance: price makes the diamond, difficulty virtue, suffering devotion, "griping to physic". The pain-exempla (self-wounding for credit, penance, Good Friday flagellants, women flaying faces) are evidence for this: motive, not the wound, determines what is endured. Montaigne is an observer here, reporting what he has seen or been told, and says he prefers testimonies "nearest to us" from Christendom.

**Money as self-report.** Montaigne narrates three conditions: about twenty years of casual means and dependence on friends, in which he spent cheerfully and prided himself on punctual repayment; a hoarding phase of anxiety, secrecy and fear for the cash-box ("there is more trouble in keeping money than in getting it"), which he partly hedges ("if I did not altogether so much as I say"); and the present life at the height of his revenue, "from hand to mouth", reached when a costly pleasurable journey broke the habit. He reports freely that he hates bargaining and borrows badly. The hoarding passage is also an account of how a prudent habit hardens into compulsion ("men still are intent upon adding to the heap"). Plato, Bion, Epicurus, Thales, Dionysius, Feraulez and an aged prelate appear as supporting voices or exempla, not as Montaigne's own claims.

**Fortune supplies matter, the soul gives it quality.** "Fortune does us neither good nor hurt; she only presents us the matter and the seed, which our soul, more powerful than she, turns and applies as she best pleases." External things take colour from the inner constitution, as clothes warm with the wearer's heat, and the crooked oar in water shows how we see, not just that we see. This is a strong statement of the essay's thesis, but Montaigne frames it through analogies (study for the idle, abstinence for the drunkard), and he never denies that pain and frailty are real.

**A pragmatic, anti-heroic coda.** Having listed so many philosophical arguments against pain and death, he asks why none seems to help, and suggests each person choose the one suited to his own humour, or at least "take a lenitive". Philosophy's blunt fallback is that no one has to remain in necessity. This is a hedge against the Stoic extremes he has just paraded: he treats them as resources to try, not standards to meet.

## Notable passages

**Opinion's power (c1).** The pivot from the exempla to the thesis, with the inverse cases of people choosing hardship.

**Value as what we add (c2).** The clearest general statement: price and difficulty make things valued. This resonates loosely with how [[habit-and-identity]] and belief make the arbitrary feel natural, though Montaigne's focus here is on individual valuation, not collective custom.

**The hoarding phase (c3) and its end (c4).** A self-portrait in the manner of [[self-construction-through-writing]]: unflattering, hedged, and specific, ending in the settled rule that fortune cannot be armed against with accidental weapons, "'tis with our own arms that we are to fight her".

**The soul as mistress of its condition (c5).** The unit's central claim about good and evil.

**Choosing a remedy (c6).** The practical turn: treat consolations as tools suited to temperament.
