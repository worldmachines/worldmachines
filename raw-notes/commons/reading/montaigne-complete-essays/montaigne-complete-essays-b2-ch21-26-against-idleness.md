# Montaigne, Essays II.21-26: Against Idleness, Of Posting, Ill Means to a Good End, Roman Grandeur, Counterfeit Sickness, Thumbs

This unit holds six very short essays of [[michel-de-montaigne]], mostly built from borrowed exempla (Suetonius, Livy, Tacitus, Seneca, Pliny, Froissart) with a few first-person remarks. "Against Idleness" argues, against courtly counsel, that a prince should command his wars in person, and closes on exemplary deaths (Mule Moloch, Cato). "Of Posting" is a catalogue of ancient relay and carrier systems. "Of Ill Means Employed to a Good End" reads states as bodies that need purging and surveys cruel public devices (colonies, foreign wars, drunken helots, vivisection, gladiators). "Of the Roman Grandeur" illustrates Rome's power by what it gave away. "Not to Counterfeit Being Sick" collects stories of feigned ailments becoming real and ends with Seneca on Harpaste. "Of Thumbs" is a short list of customs and laws about thumbs.

## Themes

**The prince must act, not delegate.** Montaigne endorses Vespasian's "An emperor must die standing" and says he reads the office of king as "military throughout". He gives his own judgment ("A fine saying, in my opinion") and backs it with Ottoman and Spanish examples. He concedes that fortune supplies examples both ways, so the claim is partly a matter of what a "virtuous and valiant prince" can bear. He also remarks that the Castilian and Portuguese kings' conquests of the Indies were made from a "lazy abode" and that he will not count them among conquerors. He cites a friend who would rather be beaten than sleep while another fights for him; he does not name him.

**Dying well is partly fortune.** Montaigne tempers the Roman oath of victory-or-death: "the effect lies not so much in our resolution as in our good fortune", and blows seem to avoid those who seek them too eagerly. Mule Moloch, dying, still runs his army from a couch and expires with his finger to his lips, and Montaigne admires him ("whoever died so erect, or more like a man?"). The coda gives Cato's calm as the "most extreme degree" and "most natural" way of facing death: to continue the "wonted course of life even into it". The Moloch story is admiration, while the Cato coda is Montaigne's own ranking of courage.

**States are bodies, and bodies need purging.** In II.23 he presents the body-politic as a given ("kingdoms and republics are founded, flourish, and decay with age as we do") and offers migrations, Roman colonies and deliberate wars as purgations of excess population and idle youth. Roman war-making, Edward III's treaty choices and the French hope for a foreign war are reported as policy, not endorsed. Montaigne's judgment arrives separately: a foreign war is more supportable than civil war, but he does not believe God will favour so unjust a design. He then concedes that "the weakness of our condition" often forces ill means to a good end. This resonates loosely with the lifecycle imagery of [[world-machines]] (rise, flourish, decay), though here the analogy is medical and explanatory, with no thousand-year schedule.

**Spectacle as moral training.** The gladiator passage describes Roman arena culture as "great advantage for the training up the people" in contempt of death, then traces its slide from criminals to innocent slaves, freemen, senators, knights and women. Montaigne's irony is open: he finishes by saying he would find it incredible if modern mercenaries did not likewise stake their lives "in quarrels wherein they have no manner of concern". The essay does not condemn the arena flatly; it tests it as a means and lets its excess speak.

**Power shown by giving.** In II.24 Montaigne treats Roman greatness as the ability to make and unmake kings by letter or gesture. Popilius's cane-circle is the central scene. The closing guess about Solyman (that his gifts of principalities were more likely modelled on Roman custom than on his stated glut of empire) is Montaigne's own conjecture. Read with Tacitus's phrase on kings as "instruments of servitude" (given here in Latin), the essay anticipates a view of empire as network of client dependencies, not just conquest.

**Imagination, habit and the self you cannot see.** II.25 gathers cases where feigning an infirmity brings it on (Caelius, the one-eyed fugitive, the English vow of the bound left eye). Montaigne offers a physiological explanation (the shut eye sends its "virtue" to the other) and hedges: "fortune, I know not how, sometimes seems to delight in taking us at our word". His personal stick-carrying habit may turn to "necessity", and he jokes he would be the first of his family with the gout. He distrusts the dream-blindness story's reliance on imagination, preferring a bodily cause. The Seneca letter turns the theme inward: Harpaste, blind and unaware, complains that the house is dark, and Seneca says we all do likewise about our avarice, ambition and anger; not seeing our own sickness makes it harder to cure, and philosophy is the remedy. This is Seneca's voice, borrowed with approval, and it shows how self-ignorance and excuse-making work as a kind of habit ([[habit-and-identity]]).

**Thumbs.** II.26 is a deadpan catalogue: blood-oath thumb-clasps, Latin thumb idioms and the thumb as "another hand", signs of favour and disfavour in the arena, laws against maiming thumbs to escape military service, and the use of thumb amputation as war policy. Montaigne draws no explicit moral. It is a small specimen of his habit of letting custom and bodily detail speak for themselves.

## Notable passages

**Vespasian and the dying prince (c1).** The opening of II.21 states the thesis (that kingship is labour, not ease) in a single anecdote, with Montaigne's framing judgment, and the argument that subjects cannot be asked to risk themselves for a prince devoted to ease.

**Mule Moloch (c2).** The longest narrative in the unit. A sick king with a failing body uses cunning (drawing the enemy inland, encircling them) and dies concealing his death. Montaigne's closing questions give the essay's emotional centre, and his Cato coda sets the "natural" form of courage next to the heroic one.

**States purged like bodies (c3).** The central metaphor of II.23, with the physicians' fear of too-perfect health and a catalogue of migrations and colonies as evacuations.

**The arena (c4).** The moral-training defence of gladiators and its extension to women, slaves and senators, closing on the contemporary mercenary parallel.

**Popilius's circle (c5).** The sharpest picture of Roman authority in II.24: a king who "possessed all Egypt" gives up a monarchy because of "three lines in writing".

**Harpaste and self-ignorance (c6).** Seneca's letter, quoted by Montaigne, applies physical blindness to moral blindness and ends with philosophy as a medicine that pleases while it heals. Montaigne acknowledges it carried him from his subject, "but there is advantage in the change".
