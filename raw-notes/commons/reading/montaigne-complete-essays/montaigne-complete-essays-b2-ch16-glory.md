# Of Glory: the name is no part of the thing

Montaigne opens by distinguishing the name from the thing it names. Glory belongs to the name, a foreign piece joined from outside, and is therefore fit for God alone; we, being hollow and needy within, should attend to real wants first. He then reports the ancient quarrel (Chrysippus, Diogenes and Epicurus against glory; Carneades, Aristotle and Cicero for it), takes the contempt-of-glory side with his own arguments (virtue that needs witnesses is not virtue; fame is a gift of fortune; the crowd is a poor judge; history forgets nearly everyone), and ends with two concessions: false opinion of glory may be a useful curb on the people, and women should not call mere duty "honour". Throughout he claims only a private good: a life lived in quiet, judged by himself.

## Themes

**Name versus substance.** The essay's governing image is that glory is an exterior addition, "outside" the thing. Montaigne applies it first to God (whose name can be augmented though He cannot) and then to ourselves: seeking a gay garment before a good meal. He admits divinity treats this better and that he is not much versed in it, so the theology is a frame, not his main argument.

**The ancient dispute, reported, then judged.** The contempt-of-glory side (Chrysippus, Diogenes, Epicurus's "Conceal thy life") and the pro-glory side (Carneades, Aristotle, and Cicero, whom he suspects of the passion) are exempla and reported positions. Montaigne endorses the first as "very true and rational" but immediately observes that "we are, I know not how, double in ourselves": even Epicurus's dying letter and will betray a wish for posthumous name. This is his own reading of Epicurus's will (the birthday and monthly feasts), offered as interpretation ("that which makes me interpret"), not as certainty. He also grants glory's incidental commodities (good-will, less exposure to insult), so he does not condemn it flatly.

**Virtue must not depend on witnesses.** If virtue derives its recommendation from glory, men need be virtuous only in public and may do ill "slily". Carneades' serpent example and Roman cases of conscience-versus-law (Sextus Peduceus, Sextilius Rufus, Crassus and Hortensius) are borrowed exempla; Montaigne adds his own report that he has himself often returned a trust. Conscience, "the true seat of virtue", is the sufficient trumpet.

**Fortune governs fame.** Reputation is "purely the work of fortune"; glory is like a shadow that sometimes outruns the body. Caesar and Alexander owe their renown to fortune; countless equal acts of courage are lost in the confusion of battle or done against a henroost. Montaigne draws on his own era's wars: the least lustrous occasions are the most dangerous.

**The crowd as judge, and the gap between face and heart.** A dozen men judge an acre of land, yet we leave our lives to "the rabble, the mother of ignorance, injustice, and inconstancy". Montaigne's self-report: "I care not so much what I am in the opinions of others, as what I am in my own". Strangers see only countenance; old soldiers counterfeit courage (the Platonic ring turned inward). Many covet a name whatever it be (Herostratus, Manlius, as reported by Trogus Pompeius and Livy).

**His own name and oblivion.** He holds that he is not but in himself; his surname is shared by other families, so fame could not even attach to him. Of countless valiant men in France in fifteen hundred years not a hundred are known; ancient writings survive by fortune, and the surviving may be the worst. Three years of a "fantastic and imaginary life" are not worth throwing away our real one.

**Glory as a useful fiction (a counter-current).** Montaigne concedes that if the false opinion keeps men in their duty (Trajan blessed, Nero cursed), let it be cherished. He ascribes to Plato the advice not to despise popular esteem and calls Plato and his tutor bold "artificers" of divine revelations. He then generalizes: every legislator and government has a god or fable at its origin (Numa, Zoroaster, Lycurgus, Solon; Moses is set apart as truly divine), and the Bedouin belief in a better body after death makes men willing to die. He calls it "a very comfortable belief, however erroneous" and defers the subject. This sits uneasily beside the essay's insistence on inner virtue, and he does not fully reconcile the two; it is a utilitarian allowance, not a retreat.

**Honour and duty for women.** In a coda he advises ladies not to call honour what is only duty, since desire and will are offences before God and conscience even when unseen; a woman of honour would rather lose her honour than hurt her conscience.

## Notable passages

**Epicurus's double self.** Montaigne agrees with Epicurus's contempt of glory but shows that even his dying letter carries a touch of the "humour he had decried": "we are, I know not how, double in ourselves, which is the cause that what we believe we do not believe". Montaigne's habit of catching a thinker contradicting his own doctrine.

**Virtue without witnesses.** The attack on the view that virtue is coveted only for the honour that attends it, and the claim that if "impunity pass with us for justice" we shall abandon ourselves to every wickedness.

**Quiet according to myself.** "All the glory that I pretend to derive from my life is that I have lived it in quiet; in quiet, not according to Metrodorus, or Arcesilaus, or Aristippus, but according to myself." Tranquillity is individual because philosophy has found no good common way.

**Face and heart.** Others see countenance, not heart; the mask of courage and the ring that would hide many on the day of danger.

**Oblivion.** Of thousands of French fighters in fifteen hundred years not a hundred are known; the same fortune governs what writing survives.

**Fictions that curb the people.** The legislators' divine patrons and the "counterfeit" added to current money, offered as a practised and defensible expedient.
