# Reason's ignorance of soul and body, and the rules for using the sceptic's last weapon

This is a middle slice of the Apology for Raimond Sebond (it picks up mid-argument and breaks off mid-argument). It opens with the philosophers' own observation that the soul is subject to disease, drugs, wounds and old age, and that Socrates and Cato were unmanned by the bite of a mad dog, so that philosophy's remedies fail just where the reasoning soul itself is overturned. Montaigne then notes that philosophers skip the awkward half of their dilemma on the soul (what if it changes for the worse?), and treats immortality as a doctrine that human reason cannot prove, held only by grace and faith, with the pagan variants (transmigration, Plato's reincarnation, Plutarch's souls turning to demigods) offered as proof of how fabulous reason becomes. He turns to the body: the competing medical and philosophical accounts of the seed and of women's contribution to generation show that man knows his body no better than his soul. He closes the case against reason ("who understands not himself in himself, in what can he?") and addresses the lady for whom the essay was written: this last fencing trick must be used rarely, moderation and the common road are to be preferred, wit needs the curb of laws and custom, and yet, in the final paragraph, he refuses to stop inquiring.

## Themes

**The soul is not exempt from the body's accidents.** Reported through Lucretian verse and the philosophers' own observation, the mind "is capable of alteration", is dosed, drugged, fevered and unhinged. Montaigne's own inference is pointed: philosophy has armed man against other accidents, but not against this one, where "the soul becomes the soul of a madman". The Socrates/Cato exempla are borrowed anecdotes; the use he makes of them (wisdom is one slobber away from annihilation) is his own.

**Immortality: a gift of faith, not a result of reason.** He reports that the doctrine is the most doubted part of human science, that its two supports are the hope of glory and the usefulness of divine punishment for escaped vices (Plato), and that its defenders' arguments are "dreams, not of the teacher, but wisher". Man's anxious soul runs about "to seek out consolations, hopes, and foundations". Montaigne's conclusion is fideist: God alone dictated it, and the more we owe to God "the greater Christianity". The Babel passage frames the plurality of opinions as divine chastisement of pride. This is a theological reading, and the note should not flatten it into secular anti-metaphysics; the sceptical point is deployed on behalf of faith.

**The catalogue of absurd doctrines.** Pythagorean transmigration, Origen, Varro, Chrysippus, Plato's soul-passage through women and beasts, and the Epicurean joke about crowds of souls scuffling for bodies, are listed as comedy. Montaigne judges that the "mysteries of philosophy have many strange things in common with those of poetry".

**Medicine and generation as a second catalogue.** Archelaus, Pythagoras, Plato, Alcmeon, Democritus, Epicurus, Aristotle, Galen disagree on what seed is and whether women have any; even on gestation Montaigne takes the eleven-month side "by the example of myself" (a small self-report). The point: even the lowliest woman has a judgment on these questions, "and yet we cannot agree".

**Man the measure, but unmeasured.** Protagoras's dictum is mocked as "a pretty flam" since man "never knew so much as his own" measure; Thales' admission that self-knowledge is hard implies that all other knowledge is impossible. This is the hinge where the sceptical argument reaches its endpoint.

**The sceptic's weapon is dangerous, use it rarely.** Addressing his patroness, Montaigne calls the argument a "desperate thrust" in which you "quit your own arms to make your adversary abandon his" and gives the exempla of Gobrias and the Turkish prisoners who blew up the ship. The Tuscan proverb and "keep yourselves in the common road" are advice to her, not a retraction of the sceptical case; he adds he is "an enemy to all extravagant ways".

**Wit needs a bridle; custom does the bridling.** Montaigne argues that human wit is "a wandering, dangerous, and temerarious utensil", fettered by religion, laws, customs, sciences and penalties, which it still escapes. Few souls can be trusted with their own judgment; the rest should be "under pupilage", and Epicurus and Plato are cited for the worst law being better than none. He then observes the opposite pathology of his own age: now that arts are received "by civil authority and decree" and schools have "one pattern", knowledge passes at its current rate ("how much it is current for"), with palmistry, enchantments and the philosophers' stone passing as sound coin. The coin metaphor is a striking account of custom and institutional authority replacing assay. It half-fits [[habit-and-identity]] (custom as invisible machinery), though here the machinery is institutional endorsement rather than personal habit, and Montaigne argues both for and against it.

**But he keeps working the wax.** Against Theophrastus' modest limits, Montaigne says wit is "curious and greedy" and will not stop at a thousand paces any more than fifty. Knowledge is cumulative and handled "as bears leisurely lick their cubs into form", and his own attempts make the matter more supple for the next person. The slice ends: difficulty and his own incapacity are no reason to despair. This sits in some tension with the preceding counsel of moderation and with the sceptical argument; Montaigne does not resolve it here.

## Notable passages

**Philosophy disarmed by madness (c1).** The soul in a philosopher turning "the soul of a madman" is the passage where reason's self-sufficiency is most directly undone; the remedies of patience or suicide-by-deprivation of feeling presuppose a working mind.

**Owl's eyes and Babel (c2).** Aristotle's owl image, the reported ancient doubt about immortality, the Pauline text on destroying the wisdom of the wise, and the Babel reading of intellectual diversity as a godly check on pride make up the core fideist argument of the slice.

**Knowing neither soul nor body (c3).** The compact conclusion of the whole sceptical case, with Protagoras and Thales as foils.

**The weapon and its risks (c4).** The addressee is told how, and how seldom, to use the argument. Useful as Montaigne's own self-limiting rule of method; the Gobrias and Turkish-prisoner exempla illustrate self-destruction as a means of victory.

**Wit and custom (c5, c6).** The two passages on bridling the intellect and on knowledge circulating at face value, "we take physic as we do geometry", where custom stands in for verification. Montaigne reports contemporary credulity, with the chiromancy list as comic evidence.

**The wax passage (c7).** Montaigne's self-description as a persistent worker of material who leaves it more tractable for successors; the Lucretian image of wax softened by the sun and thumb gives the slice a constructive turn. Relevant to [[michel-de-montaigne]]'s method of the essay as ongoing handling rather than settled conclusion.
