# Of Moderation: excess in virtue, pleasure and penance

A short essay (Book 1, ch. 29) that opens with a thesis about virtue gone too far and then wanders by association. Montaigne first says that handling corrupts even good things, and that zeal beyond measure in virtue, philosophy or devotion is a fault. He then turns to marriage, where theology and philosophy police even the most private acts, and lectures husbands on sober pleasure with a run of exempla (Zenobia, the Persian kings, Epaminondas, Pericles). A pivot (“is not man a most miserable creature the while?”) shifts the target: the doctrines that curtail the few pure pleasures we have. Penance and medicine, which cure by pain, lead to the opinion that gods are appeased by slaughter, and the essay closes on human sacrifice in the newly discovered Americas, ending with the Mexican envoys’ three-part offering to Cortez.

## Themes

**Excess corrupts the good (Montaigne’s own stance).** He rejects the saying that there is no excess in virtue as a play on words, and reports his own taste: “I love temperate and moderate natures.” Immoderate zeal, even for good, “astonishes me.” The two images are symmetrical: the archer who overshoots misses as much as the one who falls short. The Pausanias’s-mother and Posthumius examples are offered as strange rather than just, a “savage virtue” that “costs so dear.”

**Philosophy’s limits (reported opinion, endorsed).** Callicles in Plato is cited as saying that extreme philosophy makes a man brutish, a contemner of law and incapable of public office. Montaigne concurs (“He says true”) on the ground that excess enslaves natural freedom and leads us off the “fair and beaten way that nature has traced”. This is the same anti-pedantic, nature-trusting line found elsewhere in the Essays, though here it is voiced through a borrowed authority. See [[michel-de-montaigne]] and [[socratic-thinking]] for the contrast: Montaigne’s Socrates is moderate and practical.

**Marriage and the regulation of pleasure.** Theology (Aquinas, on forbidden degrees) and philosophy claim jurisdiction over every private act. Montaigne, speaking for wives, advises husbands that pleasure in marriage should be “a sober and serious delight”, and that licentiousness is as reprovable inside marriage as outside. The exempla (Zenobia, Persian kings, Epaminondas, Pericles, AElius Verus) mostly come from classical writers and are used to show that pleasures suit some persons and offices and not others. He hedges with irony (“if such there still be”; “for my part I always went the plain way to work”) and mocks Plato’s Jupiter story as borrowed from a lascivious poet.

**Against doctrines that multiply misery (a turn in the essay).** Having just endorsed moderation, he now attacks the moralists’ own excess: human wisdom does ill when it cuts down natural pleasures. His preference is a “more natural course”, which he says is both commodious and holy and might have limited pleasure too. Spiritual and bodily physicians agree only that cure comes through pain; the Gallio anecdote (an exile who enjoyed Lesbos and was recalled and confined at home so that punishment matched his feeling) shows that penance and medicine work by being unpleasant. The common rule that contraries cure contraries is said to fail here: one ill cures another. This is an observation about how punitive systems depend on the subject’s feeling, close to the later habit-and-custom analysis ([[habit-and-identity]]) though it is not argued as such.

**Sacrifice as the same logic at full scale.** The belief that pain cures resembles the “ancient” belief that gods and nature are gratified by massacre, once “universally received in all religions”. Amurath’s six hundred young Greeks and the practices of the “new countries” (burning, flaying, hearts torn out, victims singing and dancing) are laid out without comment; Montaigne notes that the victims show “constancy and resolution”. The Mexican king’s annual fifty thousand and his wars waged partly to supply prisoners come from reported accounts of Cortez’s encounters. Montaigne does not here make the cultural-relativist move of “Of Cannibals” (cf. [[noble-savage]]); the new world is described as “pure and virgin”, and the passage sits within a critique of European as well as ancient religious cruelty, since Amurath and the universal “all religions” are included. He leaves the tale unglossed.

## Notable passages

The opening (c1) states the thesis in Montaigne’s own voice. The archer and Callicles passage (c2) carries both the symmetry image and the borrowed critique of philosophy. The pivot passage (c3) is the essay’s hinge, where the exempla of marital restraint give way to the charge that moralists make man more wretched than he is. The Gallio story (c4) is the most concrete expression of the idea that punishment and cure depend on the sufferer’s feeling. The sacrifice passages (c5, c6) are reported matter, not Montaigne’s opinion: the closing envoys’ offer (“if thou art a furious god that feedeth upon flesh and blood”) shows a people uncertain whether their conqueror is god or man, and is left to stand without a moral.
