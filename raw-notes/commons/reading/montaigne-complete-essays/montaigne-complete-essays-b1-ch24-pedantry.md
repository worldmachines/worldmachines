# Of Pedantry: learning that does not become one's own

Montaigne begins with a boyhood puzzle: why do the Italian farces make the pedant the fool, and why do the finest gentlemen despise schoolmasters? With age he concludes the contempt has grounds, though he first tries to explain why a mind full of knowledge should not be quicker for it. He rejects the idea that learning crowds the mind, and then rejects the comparison with the ancient philosophers, who were mocked for being above ordinary life where modern pedants are mocked for being below it. His own diagnosis is that the fault lies in how we are taught: parents and masters aim at stocking the head, not at judgment and virtue. He turns the charge on himself (his book is a patchwork of borrowed sentences), argues that knowledge must be digested and made one's own, and ends with a long contrast between French schooling and the Persian, Spartan and Socratic models, where children were trained in action and in giving reasons for judgments.

## Themes

**Judgment over memory.** The central claim is that education should make a person better, not merely more learned: "We should rather examine, who is better learned, than who is more learned." Learning without judgment is dangerous, and Montaigne says judgment can get along without knowledge but knowledge cannot get along without judgment. Examining magistrates for understanding, and not only learning, is the better method.

**Borrowed knowledge is not knowledge.** Pedants are compared to birds that carry grain home without tasting it, to the man who fetched fire and sat warming himself without taking any home, and to a rich Roman who kept learned men to prompt him. Montaigne says his own habits are open to this charge, since he culls sentences from books "not to keep them" and they are "no more mine than in their first places". He nonetheless insists that a man can be learned by others' learning but "can never be wise but by his own wisdom". This is a hedged self-indictment, not a retraction: he goes on borrowing, but names what it is. It bears on [[michel-de-montaigne]] and the essay's method of citation.

**Philosophers versus pedants.** Montaigne distinguishes the philosophers of old, mocked for lofty disregard of wealth and rank yet capable in action when put to it (Archimedes, Thales, Crates, Heraclitus, Empedocles), from modern pedants, who are despised as incompetent and base. These are exempla he reports, not claims of his own; he also says he does not "well digest" Aristotle's distinction between wise and prudent.

**Nature, disposition and the vessel.** He says knowledge cannot enlighten a soul dark of itself; it is "an excellent drug" that spoils in a tainted vessel. Natures that resist a pedantic education, such as Adrian Turnebus, whom he praises as learned yet without pedantry, are exceptions. He also blames the French system, where learning is left to people of mean condition seeking a living. Here he sounds elitist and fatalistic about natures ("Degenerate and vulgar souls are unworthy of philosophy"); readers should not mistake this for the essay's main pedagogic claim.

**Education as formation by action.** The Persian, Spartan and Socratic examples, all reported from Xenophon, Plato and others, show children trained by habit and example, asked to judge cases and give reasons. The cassock anecdote shows judgment of justice corrected against mere fit, and the pupil whipped for it. Montaigne wants knowledge to be "its complexion and habit: not an acquisition, but a natural possession" (compare [[habit-and-identity]], though here habit is the desired outcome of education rather than the unseen machinery of custom).

**Learning and arms.** In the closing paragraph he claims letters soften courage: the Turks, Scythians and Goths, and Charles VIII's conquest of Naples are offered as evidence. This reverses his earlier remark that great captains were often learned, and he does not reconcile the two; it is an argument by examples, not a settled thesis.

## Notable passages

**Learned versus good (c1).** The cry "O, what a learned man!" draws respect while "O, what a good man!" does not; Montaigne proposes a third crier, "O, the blockheads!" The passage states the essay's thesis about the aim of education.

**The memory-stuffers and Montaigne himself (c2).** Pedants are like birds feeding their young without tasting the grain. Montaigne smiles at himself, since his own book does the same thing; this is a rare moment where the essay admits its own method is open to its charge.

**Taking knowledge on trust (c3).** Fire from a neighbour's house, the stomach full of undigested meat, and the "relative and mendicant understanding". He says he might have found consolation "in myself, had I been trained to make use of my own reason".

**Turnebus and the pedant exception (c4).** A man of pure learning with no pedantry in him is the proof that pedantry is a failure of formation, not of study. The paragraph closes with a standard: education "must, moreover, alter us for the better".

**Dyeing the soul (c5).** Learning must be worked in "not to tincture it only, but to give it a thorough and perfect dye", else it is better left alone, since it is a dangerous weapon in an unskilled hand.

**Cyrus and the cassock (c6).** Cyrus gives a judgment based on fit and is told to judge by justice, and whipped for it, set against French pupils whipped for forgetting a Greek tense. The contrast sets up the Spartan comparison.

**Sparta's pedagogy (c7).** Montaigne reports that the Spartans taught children by action, not hearsay, so that knowledge became "not an acquisition, but a natural possession"; Athens learned to speak well and Sparta to do well. This is the positive model, filtered through Greek sources and idealised.

Compare [[socratic-thinking]] for the Socratic scene with Hippias, which here serves as a jibe at paid teaching and not as a method.
