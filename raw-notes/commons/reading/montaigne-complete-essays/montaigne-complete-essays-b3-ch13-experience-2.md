# Self-knowledge, frank counsel, and the empire of custom

This slice (the second of five of 'Of Experience') begins mid-essay with the Delphic precept to know oneself and moves in four steps. First, Montaigne claims that self-study yields not knowledge but a sense of ignorance and modesty, and that it also sharpens his judgment of others, though he cannot sort human behaviour into tidy categories. Second, he imagines what office he would have been fit for: a nameless, single, silent counsellor who tells a prince the truth. Third, he declares his book 'a register of the essays of my own life', and argues that on health experience is the proper authority and physicians are poor guides. Fourth, he gives his own regimen as a case study of custom, across nations, scholars, Seneca, beggars and his own ageing quirks, and ends on the plain facts of the body. The slice ends mid-argument on his bowels' punctuality.

## Themes

**Self-knowledge as an education in ignorance.** Montaigne recasts the Delphic injunction through a Socratic paradox: only those inside a science can see its difficulty, and a man who is satisfied with himself shows that he knows nothing of the matter. He reports of himself that the 'infinite depth and variety' he finds in self-study has taught him how much he has to learn. He says this is the source of his modesty and his hatred of 'wrangling arrogance'. He supports it with Plato, Socrates, Aristarchus and Antisthenes (reported voices), and offers himself as 'so vain an example'. Compare [[socratic-thinking]] and [[self-construction-through-writing]].

**Unsystematic judgment and the unclassifiable person.** Montaigne says self-study makes him a shrewd reader of friends (some he has 'astonished' with his descriptions). But he refuses to sort their acts 'into certain species and chapters'. He presents his opinions 'by disjointed articles' and 'experimentally', and contrasts this with wisdom, a 'solid and entire building' (a Latin line he quotes). Human inconstancy is so variform that even a single action resists a single label. Perseus of Macedon is cited as a rare case, though he says the description 'seems almost to fit all the world'. This is a method statement for the essay form; compare [[personal-essay-as-form]]. It also rhymes with WMP's concern with [[Legibility]]: Montaigne declines to make persons legible by taxonomy, though he is not hostile to classification as such, only sceptical that it can be done.

**The frank counsellor.** Asked what he would have been fit for, Montaigne says 'for nothing', and then sketches the office he could have filled: an eye-witness who speaks truth to a prince, unflattered and singular. He would want a man content with his fortune, of moderate station, limited to one person, and bound to silence. His reasons are practical. Princes live under public gaze and are surrounded by favourites who look to themselves. Even truth 'has not the privilege to be spoken at all times', so form can matter more than substance. A hedge worth keeping: he says this of an imagined role, not a record of service.

**Experience over physic.** The slice states his book's purpose as 'a register of the essays of my own life'. As exemplary, he says, it is of doubtful use for 'internal soundness' but offers unmatched evidence on bodily health, because he presents it 'pure' of 'art or opinion'. Physicians are attacked with Plato's demand that a doctor must have suffered the diseases he treats, and with images of the painter at table and the town crier describing a lost dog. Tiberius and Socrates are reported as authorities for self-regulation. The scorn is for the profession's promises, not for experience-based medicine: he would 'cry out in good earnest' if physic gave real relief.

**Custom as Circe.** His regimen is the same in sickness and health, since being hurt by long-accustomed things is something he 'believes nothing more certainly' impossible. Custom is 'the potion of Circe'. Examples multiply: night-dew, German stoves, wine dregs in Portugal, the learned man who studies amid noise, Seneca's diet and bedding, the charity boy who preferred sewer mussels to his kitchen. Each is an example of local normality that feels like natural necessity ([[habit-and-identity]]; the invisible-machinery framing is a WMP-style reading, not Montaigne's own phrase). Here the lesson is not just relativism but flexibility: custom can mould us to any form, and 'to change and variation' is 'the most noble and most useful instruction of all she teaches us'. He counsels a young man to 'cross his own rules', and then candidly lists the fussy habits custom has fixed in himself in age. He admits some come from custom and some from nature.

**Against the authority of print.** Montaigne rejects those who trust only what is in a book. Men write as indiscreetly as they speak, truth is 'none the wiser' for being old, and the ordinary things around us, if we could 'find out their light', would yield the greatest examples. He then supplies two contemporary anecdotes, the thirstless gentleman and the scholar who thrives on noise. This links to the printing-press themes in [[eisenstein-printing-revolution]], though Montaigne's own target is the prestige of the quotation, and he is himself a heavy quoter of ancient authors.

## Notable passages

**The infinite depth of the self** (c1). Self-study yields ignorance, modesty and a distrust of arrogant assertion; dogmatists speak 'in the style wherewith men establish religions and laws'. Attribution: Montaigne's own self-report, backed by reported Socratic and Platonic doctrine.

**Opinions 'by disjointed articles'** (c2). The clearest statement here of why he writes unsystematically: 'relation and conformity' are not found in 'low and common souls as ours', whereas wisdom is a unified building. Note the irony and self-deprecation: he does not claim wisdom.

**The register of the essays of my own life** (c3). A self-description of the whole book and its special authority on one subject, the body, which sets up the turn to physic.

**Custom as Circe** (c4). His regimen is unchanged in illness, and custom 'gives a form to a man's life'. This is the slice's pivot from medicine to habit.

**Against print and quotation** (c5). Montaigne attacks those who believe only what is printed, and dignifies a friend's word over Aulus Gellius or Macrobius. Note that this is argued while citing a dozen authors, and that he is not rejecting books, only deference to age and print.

**Custom and the virtue of flexibility** (c6). The beggar's 'magnificences', and custom's capacity for change as the best teaching. In his own voice, he values suppleness over rule-bound discipline, though he concedes (a few lines later) that age has made him rather set.
