# Of Solitude: the backshop of the self

Montaigne sets aside the old debate between the active and the contemplative life and asks instead what solitude is and how it can fail. He begins with the corruption of public ambition and the contagion of crowds, then turns the argument inward: changing place does not cure the mind, since the disease travels with the patient. The true solitude is the mind "called home and confined within itself", and it must be built before leaving society. The middle of the essay recommends detaching one's happiness from wife, children, goods and reputation by keeping a private "backshop", and says retirement suits those who have already served the world and are in decline. Montaigne then speaks for himself, as a person of ordinary soul who relies on bodily comforts and rehearses misfortune. He criticises two false retirements: the ambitious one that still wants glory (Pliny, Cicero) and the bookish one that costs health. The essay closes by endorsing a quoted letter-advice from two philosophers (the text names them only as writing to Idomeneus and Lucilius) on retiring into oneself and keeping a moral inner audience.

## Themes

**Solitude is a property of the mind, not of place.** Montaigne reports Socrates' reply about a traveller ("he took himself along with him") and adds his own images of a ship's lading, a sick man moved from place to place, and a dog dragging its chain. His own conclusion is that the mind "cannot escape from itself" (Horace's line, quoted) and so the true solitude "may be enjoyed even in populous cities and the courts of kings, though more commodiously apart". This is a hedge: place helps, but only after the inward work.

**The backshop: self-possession as insurance.** Through the exempla of Stilpo, Antisthenes and Paulinus of Nola (borrowed anecdotes, used by Montaigne to make his own point) he argues that we should "reserve a backshop, wholly our own and entirely free", so that losing wife, children or goods "may be no new thing". He does not ask us to give them up: they "must be had". The aim is that happiness not depend on them. This is an early statement of the essay as a technology of self-possession, and connects to the self-study at the centre of [[michel-de-montaigne]] and [[self-construction-through-writing]], though here the self is cultivated by withdrawal and rehearsal rather than by writing.

**Ambition and glory are the opposite of solitude.** He says that public men aspire to office for private advantage, that ordinary actions rarely concern ourselves at all (the scrambling soldier, the midnight scholar), and that reputation is "the most useless, frivolous, and false coin that passes current". Pliny and Cicero retire only to write for fame, which he calls "a ridiculous contradiction": they have disengaged "arms and legs" while the soul stays "confined behind more than ever".

**Age and complexion.** Solitude suits those who have given their active years to the world and whose forces are failing; Montaigne places himself among the soft, slow, undriven temperaments for whom it comes easily "both by natural condition and by reflection". He is explicit that he is not a hardliner: he rejects ascetic extremes (putting out one's eyes, throwing wealth in the river) as "excessive virtue", prefers Arcesilaus using gold vessels with moderation, and rehearses death, poverty and sickness by imagining a beggar's patience, a counter-weight of custom against precept. He asks only to be content with himself.

**Against devotional and bookish retreats.** He grants that devout retirement, founded on hope of eternal life, is "much more rationally founded", which is a report rather than a claim that he shares it: he says "neither the end, then, nor the means" of the Pliny advice pleases him. Books are a pleasure that can destroy health like any other; he keeps only books that are "pleasant and easy" or that teach him how to regulate life and death, and he insists on bodily comforts and the pleasures that age leaves him.

**The inner audience.** The closing quoted advice (from philosophers of two different sects, whom Montaigne contrasts with the "boasting and prating" kind) makes the unit's most memorable instruction: do not care how the world talks of you but how you talk to yourself, prepare the self before retiring into it, and keep Cato, Phocion and Aristides as inward witnesses so that you dare not "trip" before yourself. Montaigne presents this as "the precept of the true and natural philosophy", the strongest endorsement in the essay.

## Notable passages

**Taking oneself along (c1).** Socrates' remark and the physical analogies (ship, sick man, stakes, chained dog) establish the essay's pivot: relocation fails because the burden is in the mind. Montaigne then names the cure, calling the mind home.

**The backshop (c2).** The practical doctrine: keep a private room of the self where happiness is settled, so that outer losses are anticipated. Note the balance: loved ones and health are still to be "had".

**Weaning from society (c3).** In decay of nature one should "espouse nothing but ourselves", yet only to the degree that the ties can still be undone without "flaying us". It asserts that "the greatest thing in the world is for a man to know that he is his own".

**Self-report on moderation (c4, c5).** Montaigne's own voice: a modest preparer for fortune's disgrace, a prayer to be "content with myself", and an admission that his ordinary soul needs bodily comforts and that he grips remaining pleasures as age takes the others. This is the unit's best evidence for how he reads himself, and he explicitly hedges that wiser souls may do otherwise.

**Against book-solitude (c6).** A rare place where he turns on his own medium: reading and study are no safe harbour, since the pleasure of books can harm like any other pleasure.

**Retire into yourself (c7).** The long quotation of the two philosophers' advice, including the beasts of chase who "efface the track at the entrance into their den", is the closing statement, and bears on [[socratic-thinking]] only loosely: the inner interrogator here is Stoic-Epicurean moral self-regard rather than Socratic questioning. It also resonates with the world-machines interest in capture resistance in a minimal way (one's inner life as the part that cannot be seized), though Montaigne's concern is moral, not political.
