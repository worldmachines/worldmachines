# Of the Arms of the Parthians: weight, habit and the soft modern soldier

A short essay on armour. Montaigne opens with a complaint about the gentlemen of his day, who buckle on their armour only at the point of extreme necessity and lay it by as soon as danger seems past, so that men are still fastening cuirasses when their companions are already routed. He then turns to the opposite fault: armour so thick and heavy that it hampers its wearer more than it protects him. Exempla from Tacitus, Lucullus, the younger Scipio, Cicero, Caracalla, Marius and a Lacedaemonian soldier (all reported, not his own observations) build to the claim that what makes arms intolerable is only unfamiliarity. The essay closes with Marcellinus's description of the jointed, scaled armour of the Parthians, which Montaigne says closely resembles the French men-at-arms with their barded horses, and a Plutarch anecdote about Demetrius's double-weight suits. The essay has no explicit conclusion; the Parthian passage is offered as a description, not an argument.

## Themes

**Habit as the hidden variable.** Montaigne's central diagnosis is that 'tis nothing but the not being used to wear it that makes the weight of our armour so intolerable. The weight is the same; the difference is training. The Roman infantry carried provisions and stakes, and Cicero (as reported) says arms were to them no more trouble than their own limbs. This is a small, concrete case of the custom-as-invisible-machinery theme (compare [[habit-and-identity]]), though here Montaigne uses it practically, about the body and soldiering, rather than about morals or beliefs.

**Ancients versus moderns, to the moderns' discredit.** He states flatly that the Romans' military discipline was much ruder than ours and accordingly produced much greater effects, with the Spartan sneer at a man seen under a roof as the cap. His closing remark, 'We should not march our people very far at that rate', is his own dry judgment on contemporary softness. The framing is moral and nostalgic: hardship builds capacity. It does not say the ancients were better in every respect, only in this.

**Protection that defeats itself.** Montaigne's own claim is that heavy armour makes the wearer 'rather loaded than secured', so that men contend with their own arms. He gives mocking exempla (Gauls who could neither harm nor be harmed nor rise again; Medes whom Lucullus attacked first because they were as if in prisons of iron) and a prediction that musketeers will lead to inventions to immure us in castles like those carried by elephants. Scipio's rebuke over caltrops and the buckle, trust the right hand over the left, extends this: a defence relied on breeds slackness. The reasoning is by analogy and anecdote, not by measured evidence, and Montaigne hedges with 'I believe'.

**Two opposite errors.** He criticizes both the man who dons armour too late and the man who is smothered by it. He does not sort out which he thinks worse and allows that skipping armour 'does not by that much harm', since armour also kills some through its weight.

**The Parthians as a counter-model.** The Marcellinus passage, spoken by a Roman writer, describes armour that was both resistant and flexible: scales falling over each other like feathers, plates that lent themselves to motion at every joint. Montaigne offers it as a description close to the French men-at-arms; he does not draw out a lesson, so it is best read as the essay's image of armour that has been fitted to the body, set against the clumsy kit of his own day.

## Notable passages

The opening (c1) states the complaint about late arming and the baggage-clutter that follows from soldiers' servants having to carry the arms. The second passage (c2) is Montaigne's own verdict on heavy armour, with the Gauls, Lucullus and the elephant-castles. The Scipio passage (c3) supplies the moral that self-reliance and an aggressive posture matter more than defences. The pivotal c4 asserts the habit thesis and sets the Roman load against the soft modern campaigner. The final passage (c5) is the Parthian description and its comparison to French men-at-arms and the Demetrius suits. Montaigne's Latin and Italian verse quotations are left untranslated in the source and are not relied on here.
