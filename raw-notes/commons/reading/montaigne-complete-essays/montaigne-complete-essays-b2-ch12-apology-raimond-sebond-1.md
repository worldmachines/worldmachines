# Apology for Raimond Sebond, opening: faith, custom and the humbling of reason

This is the first slice of Book 2, chapter 12. It begins with a hedged remark on learning, then tells how Montaigne's unlearned father, who revered scholars, received Sebond's _Theologia naturalis_ from Peter Bunel, and how Montaigne translated it at his father's command. Montaigne then takes up two objections to Sebond. To the first, that Christians should not rest belief on human reason, he grants that faith is a divine gift while defending the use of natural reason in its service. He then shifts to attack how Christians actually hold their faith: as habit, passion and party loyalty, with the French civil wars as his evidence. The slice ends by turning to the second objection (that Sebond's arguments are weak). Montaigne announces that his defence will be to "crush and spurn under foot pride and human arrogance", which opens the sceptical assault on reason that the rest of the Apology pursues. This slice only begins that assault.

## Themes

**Learning respected but not adored.** Montaigne rejects Herillus's claim that learning is the sovereign good, and the claim that all vice comes from ignorance. His father's reverence for scholars ran in proportion to his inability to judge them ("with so much the greater reverence and religion as he was the less able to judge of them"). Montaigne's own position is a middle one: "I love them well, but I do not adore them."

**Reason as servant, not ground, of faith.** In answer to the first objection, Montaigne speaks as a layman and says he "know[s] nothing" of divinity. He holds that faith alone grasps the mysteries and that human means cannot produce it, since the great pagan minds would otherwise have reached it. Yet using our natural "utensils" in God's service is the noblest use of them, as we owe God bodily as well as spiritual reverence. Human reason must accompany faith "with this reservation, not to fancy that it is upon us that it depends." Montaigne is hedged here: he fears that, in fact, "we only have it by this way", meaning by human routes.

**Belief shaped by custom and passion.** Montaigne argues that if faith had a divine basis, novelty, princes' pressure and party success could not shake it. His sharpest claim is that "We are Christians by the same title that we are Perigordians or Germans", since religions are received through birthplace, antiquity, authority, fear or promise. This is an early statement of the custom-as-invisible-machinery theme (cf. [[habit-and-identity]]). It is a claim about how Christians in fact believe, offered as a criticism of them. It is not a claim that the faith is false, and he separates the human supports of belief from "a divine and supernatural constraint."

**Religion as instrument of faction (Wars of Religion).** Justice and religion in the French civil war are "pretended" but not "received, settled and espoused"; each party "draw[s] it like wax into so many contrary figures." Parties swap their doctrines on rebellion as fortune shifts their places. Montaigne says few soldiers fight from pure religious zeal. Zeal "performs wonders" when it serves hatred, cruelty and ambition, and hardly moves toward bounty or temperance. His conclusion is that Christians do not behave as people who believe, and the distinguishing mark of the faith ought to be virtue.

**Fear of death produces belief; real belief would remove it.** Via Plato (in the _Laws_ as Montaigne reports), the atheist who recants in danger, and Bion's deathbed superstition, Montaigne reports and judges that much professed unbelief is vanity and much belief is cowardice. He adds the exempla of Antisthenes and Diogenes (anecdotes borrowed from the ancient sources, not his own claims) to mock priests who promise bliss yet do not hurry to it.

**The world as sacred temple; grace as form.** Montaigne reports Sebond's approach: the universe bears the stamp of its maker, and "this world is a sacred temple". Human argument is "sterile and undigested matter" and grace the form that gives it value, so Sebond's arguments need faith to be "firm and stolid". Montaigne also argues they stand "as solid and firm as any others" even without it. He backs this with a report of an unnamed man brought back from unbelief by Sebond.

**Pride as the target.** In the closing turn Montaigne declares the means he will use against Sebond's critics: to "wrest the wretched arms of their reason out of their hands." He cites Paul, Augustine and Plato ("Understanding is in the gods... and not at all, or very little, in men"). His proposal is to show that reason cannot reach certainty, even in ordinary matters, which is the sceptical fideism the rest of the chapter develops. The slice itself only announces the project.

## Notable passages

The opening on learning and the father's reverence (c1) establishes Montaigne's distance from erudition-worship. His father's awe at scholars is gently ironic. The Luther passage (c2) gives the stated reason for the book, since Bunel thought it fit for a time when the new doctrines threatened to dissolve respect for authority and ancient custom, and it shows how doubt cascades once any belief is questioned. The civil-war passage (c3) is among the most direct political statements in the unit: both sides use religion as a tool. The passage on receiving religion "by our own hands" (c4) is the cultural-relativist core. The "sterile matter / grace is the form" passage (c5) is Sebond's and Montaigne's compact statement of how reason and faith relate. The programme passage (c6) is the pivot into the Apology's attack on human reason, and is rhetorically aggressive where Montaigne elsewhere is conciliatory.

A resonance with World Machines: the claim that belief arrives through custom and place and not through reasons connects with [[habit-and-identity]], and the picture of the cosmos as divinely made "machine" with "marks imprinted upon it by the hand of the mighty architect" is a theological precursor of world-as-legible-artifact thinking (cf. [[Legibility]] in the Modernity Machine frame), though Sebond's legibility is read as revelation, not as a tool of control. The attack on pride and the call to humility is scepticism in the service of faith, so it differs from the later secular sceptical lineage ([[david-hume]]) even where the arguments rhyme.
