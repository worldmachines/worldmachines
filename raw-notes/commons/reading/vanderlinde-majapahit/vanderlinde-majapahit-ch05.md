# Kertanegara: Tantric Kingship, the Pamalayu Expedition, and the Road to War with Kublai Khan

This chapter follows King Kertanegara of Singasari from his accession after the death of his father Wisnuwardhana through roughly two decades of rule: his cultivation of tantric spiritual power through Bhairava worship and ritual sex, a court reshuffle that sidelines the veteran minister Raganata in favor of young favorites, the Pamalayu naval expedition that subdues Srivijaya in Sumatra, the conquest of Bali, a grand 1289 unification ceremony at Wurare, and finally an escalating diplomatic standoff with Kublai Khan's Yuan court that ends with Kertanegara ordering a Chinese envoy's nose cut off — setting the stage for Mongol invasion.

## Themes

**Spiritual power as an instrument of rule.** Kertanegara does not treat magic as private eccentricity but as state technology on par with armies. His nocturnal tantric rituals with Pati-Pati (Master of Divine Weapons), the maithuna sex rite performed to release sakti, and the installation of a fearsome Bhairava idol at the palace's center are described as deliberate displays of "supremacy over the forces of evil." Later, an entire ceremony is staged purely to grant the king spiritual parity with Kublai Khan (who had been declared an enlightened being by a Tibetan lama) before the Sumatra campaign is launched — the text treats this as a precondition for war as real as ships and soldiers. Legitimacy in this world machine runs through consecrated statues (the Joko Dolog, the Mahakala sent to Sumatra, the Prajnaparamita) and ritual cloth (the sinjang kawung) as much as through conquest.

**A quiet purge dressed as a cabinet reshuffle.** The chapter's court-politics thread is a study in how personal favor overturns institutional seniority: Patih Raganata — competent, cautious, embedded in old scriptures, taxation and ceremony — is progressively outranked by Kertanegara's tantric-ritual companions (Pati-Pati, Aragani, Kebo Hijo, Kebo Tengah), who are handed the new privy council and the patih-ship itself. Raganata's forced resignation and demotion to dharmadyaksa, delivered alongside the king's blunt line to Aragani that he is "too ignorant and bigoted to advise on matters of religion," shows a young king converting institutional memory into ornamental status while keeping it (barely) alive rather than executing it outright — a soft transition of authority that nonetheless leaves the old order hollowed out.

**Legibility instruments alongside ritual authority.** The tax exemption granted to religious establishments is not left as verbal grant but "engraved on copper plates" and proclaimed at a public ceremony — an act of documentary legibility (in the [[world-machines]] sense of textual, inscribed, verifiable authority) running in parallel with, not opposed to, the ritual-magical legitimation described above. Kertanegara governs by combining both registers rather than choosing between them.

**Expansion as both military and technological achievement.** The Pamalayu campaign is narrated with real attention to logistics: subduing Madura first to secure the rear (and installing Wiraraja as governor — a demotion at the time that quietly seeds the eventual founding of Majapahit), then the mass shipbuilding at Tuban, and a detailed description of jong construction (outside-in planking, pegged rather than nailed frames, multiple masts). This is Singasari's world machine visibly under construction: the archipelago-wide reach (Sumatra, Bali, Kalimantan, the Malay peninsula) achieved in only a few years is explicitly framed as "the start of the geographical and political concept of Indonesia as a single entity." The Wiraraja detail is a small instance of a [[history-machine]] pattern — a peripheral, seemingly inconsequential appointment quietly assembling the conditions for Majapahit's later, more visible founding.

**Diplomatic brinkmanship collapsing into war.** The Yuan relationship escalates through repeated, almost ritualized demands for tribute and vassalage, met each time by Kertanegara's stalling gifts of jewels and exotic animals. The relationship's breaking point — the mutilation of envoy Meng Qi — reads as the moment ritualized diplomatic patience (on both sides) gives way to open aggression, closing the chapter on the edge of Kublai Khan's 1292 invasion fleet.

## Notable passages

The cemetery ritual scene (the tantric dance, the skull-cup wine, the priest leading in the ritual partner) is the chapter's most vivid establishing image of Kertanegara's spiritual style of kingship, and is quoted at length in the curated passages. The blunt reassignment of Aragani to patih — with its jab about being "too ignorant and bigoted to advise on matters of religion" — crystallizes how personal intimacy, not institutional merit, now governs appointment. The shipbuilding passage documents the material culture of the Pamalayu fleet in specific technical detail (pegged construction, teak from Kalimantan, multi-masted jongs). The Meng Qi scene is the chapter's turning point, dramatizing the shift from tribute diplomacy to violence. The closing passage, describing Kublai Khan's mobilization of a thousand-ship, twenty-thousand-soldier fleet, sets up the direct confrontation the next chapter will presumably narrate.
