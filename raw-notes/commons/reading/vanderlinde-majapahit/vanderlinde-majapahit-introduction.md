# Raffles, Ruins, and the Rediscovery of Majapahit

The introduction opens with Thomas Stamford Raffles, the British Lieutenant-Governor of Java, riding out from Lawang on June 8, 1815 in search of physical evidence of the long-vanished Majapahit empire. It braids three separate acts of recovery across more than a century: Raffles's own hacking through the jungle to find the ruins of Singasari and the temples of Candi Kidal and Candi Jago (with an assist from the earlier, more ethically compromised discoveries of the Dutch official Nicolaus Engelhard); the 1815 Wardenaar survey that mapped Majapahit's capital at Trowulan only to have its findings shelved because the city was smaller than Raffles wanted it to be; and the 1894 seizure of the Nagarakertagama manuscript by the philologist J.L.A. Brandes during the Dutch conquest of Lombok, followed by archaeologist Willem Stutterheim's 1941 reconstruction of the Majapahit palace — a discovery so politically charged amid rising Indonesian nationalism that its publication was delayed until after his death.

## Themes

**Physical erasure as a literal decay sequence.** The chapter is essentially a chronicle of infrastructure decaying past legibility and back again. Candi Kidal is still "regularly frequented by pilgrims, monks and local worshippers" — functional/performative — while the Singasari ruins are unrecognized rubble that farmers can't even attribute to a builder, and Majapahit's capital at Trowulan has been quarried for sugar-factory brick and reduced, in Raffles's own account, to a forest floor where "it is difficult to trace the outline of this former capital." This is the [[decay-sequence]] (Functional → Performative → Allegorical → Ornamental → Invisible → Absent) played out literally in brick and jungle rather than metaphorically in institutions — a useful concrete anchor for the abstract WMP pattern.

**Legibility imposed on the ruins of a dead machine.** Wardenaar's survey — astrolabe, plane table, alidade, sextant, weeks of walking and measuring — is a small, pure instance of the [[legibility-as-machine-core]] impulse: rendering an illegible, overgrown site into a map, a grid, a governable object. Tellingly, the survey is shelved not because it failed but because it succeeded at telling an inconvenient truth (a city of ten thousand, not the sprawling empire Raffles wanted), and the map itself vanishes into an archive for two centuries — legibility produced, then suppressed, then rediscovered.

**A history machine reassembling in the dark, then surfacing at the worst possible moment.** Knowledge of Majapahit accretes from wholly independent, uncoordinated sources — a colonial official's looted garden statues, a soldier's opportunistic grab of manuscripts from a burning treasury, an archaeologist's comparative reading of palace floor plans — decades apart, unconnected to each other, exactly the silent-until-it-lurches-into-visibility pattern of a [[history-machine]]. The lurch, when it comes, lands inside a live [[gramsci-gap]]: Stutterheim finishes his reconstruction of the god-king palace in July 1941, just as Dutch colonial legitimacy is collapsing and Sukarno's nationalist movement is seizing on Majapahit as proof of pre-colonial Javanese greatness — and the book is delayed, then published only posthumously, because a symbol of "Javan supremacy" is now too dangerous for its colonial sponsors to release.

**Colonial extraction wearing the mask of preservation.** Engelhard's claim that he removed the finest statues from Singasari to protect them from being "destroyed" by "the locals" sits alongside his own men's admittedly "heavy-handed and clumsy" damage to the site, and alongside the later stripping of Majapahit's octagonal bricks for Dutch sugar factories. The chapter treats antiquarian interest and resource extraction as continuous with each other rather than opposed.

## Notable passages

The anticipation of Raffles's party as they close in on Singasari — fresh off the Borobudur find — frames the whole introduction as a hunt for a *lost empire*, explicitly analogized to Angkor. The buffalo-and-tigers spectacle at Solo, staged for Raffles's entertainment, doubles as an allegory the English miss entirely: "the buffalo, slow but unbreakable, represented Java, while the savage tigers the English and Dutch. The buffalo always won" — a piece of Javanese political commentary embedded inside a court entertainment for the colonizers. Engelhard's self-serving justification for looting the Singasari statues, and Wardenaar's map being filed away because the city was "hardly the major lost civilization [Raffles] was hoping for," both show the recovery of Majapahit being shaped by what the discoverers wanted to find rather than what was there. Brandes's rescue of the Nagarakertagama manuscript — grabbing "bunches of dried leaves tied together with a string" out of a burning treasury while his comrades stuffed their pockets with gold — is the pivot on which the whole chapter turns, converting Majapahit from rubble into a text-borne civilization. And the account of Stutterheim's suppressed 1941 book makes explicit that Majapahit's revival was inseparable from the terminal crisis of Dutch rule in Java.
