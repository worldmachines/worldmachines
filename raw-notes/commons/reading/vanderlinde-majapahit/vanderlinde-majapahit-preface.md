# Preface: An Empire Asia Forgot

Van der Linde opens not with Majapahit itself but with its precursor state, Singasari, and a single explosive diplomatic incident: in 1290 an envoy of Kublai Khan demanded King Kertanegara submit as a tributary, and Kertanegara answered by having the envoy's nose cut off. The resulting Mongol invasion of Java (1293) found Kertanegara already assassinated and Singasari burned, and ended in a humiliating retreat — but from the wreckage a new power, Majapahit, emerged under Kertanegara's nephew, Prince Widjaya, eventually controlling territory from southern Thailand to Bali and beyond. The preface then pivots from narrative to argument: this is a book written to correct a historiographical asymmetry, introducing an empire the author considers Asia's most powerful yet least known, alongside a candid statement of method and evidentiary limits, and a closing gesture toward Majapahit's afterlife in modern Indonesian politics and popular culture.

## Themes

**A civilizational collision staged as courtroom drama.** The preface's entire narrative hook is the Singasari summit — not a battle but a diplomatic humiliation that triggers one. Van der Linde frames it as a clash between two world-orders: Kublai Khan's vast Sino-Mongol imperium, reaching to the "eastern fringes of Europe," and Kertanegara's freshly unified Indonesian archipelago, a power whose reach was "growing across Southeast Asia." The mutilation of the envoy and the botched invasion that follows is less about individual pique than about two expanding orders discovering each other's limits — a friction between coexisting centers of power that the book will spend its length unpacking.

**Recovering an empire erased from the Anglophone record.** The book's explicit mission — "to introduce the English-speaking world to Majapahit, the most powerful empire in Asia that most people have never heard of" — is posed against the counter-example of Angkor Wat, visited by millions while Majapahit remains obscure outside Javanese-history specialists. This is a historiographical complaint as much as a historical one: physical evidence of Majapahit barely survives, sources are "limited and of doubtful accuracy," and the author admits to filling gaps with informed assumption. The empire's material culture appears to have decayed nearly to invisibility — cities abandoned, temples reclaimed by jungle for centuries before an English expedition rediscovers them — a trajectory that reads close to the WMP [[decay-sequence]] (functional infrastructure receding through neglect into the ornamental, invisible, and finally absent) applied to an entire empire's built legacy rather than a single institution.

**A rise-dominance-decline arc narrated in miniature.** The preface compresses Majapahit's whole life cycle into two sentences: two hundred years of ascendant power, then "rapid decline," abandonment, and three centuries of jungle reclamation before rediscovery. Though far shorter than the millennium-scale cycle of a WMP [[world-machines]] Dawn–Day–Dusk arc, the shape is the same triad — construction/unification, dominance, then collapse into a Gramsci-gap-like interregnum before a new frame (here, an Englishman's expedition and later Indonesian nation-building) rediscovers and reinterprets what was left behind.

**Religious and social pluralism against a contrasting Europe.** Van der Linde stresses that Majapahit hosted Hindu and Buddhist worship simultaneously, senior priests held real court power, and women held influence too — and pointedly contrasts this with contemporaneous Europe's crusades and Catholic–Protestant strife. This comparative move sets up the book's implicit thesis: Majapahit's sophistication (rice-based wealth, naval power impressive even to the Portuguese, political cunning) rivaled or exceeded European contemporaries, making its Anglophone obscurity a matter of historiographical neglect rather than genuine historical insignificance.

**The empire as a living political resource.** The preface closes its argument by tying Majapahit to the present: Indonesia's 278 million people and renewed economic and diplomatic weight are framed as an echo of Majapahit's own era, and modern Indonesian politicians are described using the Majapahit legacy as a unifying myth across the country's many ethnicities and languages — the empire persisting today as story, symbol, and rallying cry rather than as institution.

## Notable passages

The nose-cutting scene is the preface's dramatic core, establishing Kertanegara's character (self-belief in "the dark art of magic" and personal invincibility) and the stakes of the encounter in a single violent gesture. The explicit mission statement — introducing an empire "that most people have never heard of" against the foil of Angkor Wat's fame — is the book's thesis in miniature. The passage on religious diversity and women's court influence, pointedly set against Europe's crusades and sectarian strife, does comparative historiographical work in a few sentences. The author's admission about thin, unreliable sources and necessary interpretive assumptions is a rare piece of methodological candor worth flagging for anyone using this book as a source. Finally, the modern-Indonesia paragraph and the closing rise-and-jungle-reclamation image bookend the preface by turning ancient empire into present-day political resource and setting up the whole book's Dawn-to-Dusk-to-rediscovery structure.
