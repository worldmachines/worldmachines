# Epilogue: Adam Smith's Last Years and His Tribute to Hume

After Hume's death in 1776, Smith finally makes the move to Edinburgh that Hume had long urged, takes up a customs-officer post (an irony not lost on posterity, given his authorship of The Wealth of Nations), becomes the city's reigning intellectual celebrity, and spends his final years revising his two great books. The chapter's real argument is that the sixth edition of The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1790), completed months before Smith's death, is laced with echoes of Hume — in argument, in allusion, and in outright substitution of passages — such that the revisions themselves function as an oblique elegy. The epilogue closes by tracing Smith's death, his instruction to burn his unpublished manuscripts, and the striking disproportion between the fanfare that greeted Hume's death and the near-silence that met Smith's.

## Themes

**A public career built on private disproportion.** Smith is presented as a case of comic irony fully aware of itself: "History's most celebrated champion of free trade... took a day job collecting tariffs for His Majesty's Government." The chapter takes care to show this was not simple hypocrisy — Smith's actual theory of tariffs allowed for moderate, revenue-raising duties, and he treated the job seriously ("requires a good deal of attendance") — but the framing invites the reader to register the gap between doctrine and daily labor. The same disproportion recurs institutionally: within a decade of publication, free trade moved from "a tempting heresy" to government orthodoxy, illustrated by the (possibly apocryphal) story of Pitt and his ministers rising to greet Smith, "for we are all your scholars." An idea assembled in solitude becomes state policy through diffuse, largely invisible uptake among elites before it is ever publicly credited — a pattern of ideas propagating underground before "lurching" into institutional visibility, resonant with [[history-machine]].

**Revision as elegy.** The book's central interpretive move is to read the sixth edition's new material — especially the wholly new Part 6, "Of the Character of Virtue" — not primarily as philosophical development but as tribute. Individual revisions are shown to track Hume's own arguments (on faction and fanaticism, on commercial virtue, on the jealousy of trade, on suicide) and, more pointedly, to substitute overtly Christian language with lines that echo Hume's positions almost verbatim — the replacement of the doctrine of atonement with the Tartarus/Elysium passage being the most conspicuous instance, one contemporaries (the Archbishop of Dublin) read as proof of "the infection of David Hume's society." D. D. Raphael's phrase for this — "a libation to Hume's ghost" — gives the chapter its interpretive key.

**Self-command as the shared virtue.** Part 6's crowning virtue, self-command, is tied back to Hume's own conduct at death: Smith's account of the person who "upon the approach of death, preserves his tranquillity unaltered" reprises, almost point for point, the portrait of Hume Smith had drawn years earlier in the Letter to Strahan, down to the shared invocation of Socrates. The chapter treats this as evidence that Smith is still, at the end of his own life, composing under the influence of his friend's exemplary death.

**Friendship as the book's final subject.** The new discussion of friendship in Part 6 — distinguishing "natural sympathy" from the merely "constrained sympathy" of relatives, colleagues, or "partners in trade" — is read as Smith's last word on his bond with Hume, "the most virtuous... the happiest, as well as the most permanent and secure" of attachments. The chapter treats this passage as effectively unmistakable in its referent, closing the circle opened by the earlier Letter to Strahan.

**Asymmetric deaths.** The epilogue ends by contrasting the two men's ends: similar equanimity in dying, wildly different public reception. Hume's death had produced enormous controversy and commentary; Smith's produced a "mean-spirited" London obituary, a few bland lines in the Scots Magazine, and otherwise near-silence — a final, understated irony for a chapter built on quiet, distributed influence rather than public spectacle.

## Notable passages

The customs-office irony passage sets up Smith's practical relationship to his own doctrine, showing he treated the "day job" as a serious duty rather than a sinecure. The Pitt anecdote ("we are all your scholars") marks the moment free-trade doctrine is dramatized as having captured the state. The "monkish virtues" allusion and the Tartarus/Elysium substitution together form the chapter's evidentiary core for reading the sixth edition as Hume-haunted — the latter is singled out by contemporaries and later scholars alike as the most direct trace of Hume's presence in Smith's final text. The self-command/Socrates passage and the friendship passage are the two moments the chapter treats as unmistakably autobiographical, tying Smith's philosophical last words directly back to the death and friendship of Hume that structured the whole book.
