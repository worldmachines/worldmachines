# Mortally Sick at Sea: The Long-Distance Friendship, 1766–1776

This chapter covers the decade in which Hume and Smith's friendship is tested, and ultimately proven, by physical distance: Hume takes a diplomatic post in London, then retires happily to a newly built house in Edinburgh's New Town, while Smith withdraws to his mother's house in Kirkcaldy for roughly a decade of solitary labor on The Wealth of Nations, venturing out only reluctantly. The two men see each other rarely—sometimes going years with almost no correspondence—yet the letters that survive show a friendship of remarkable warmth, teasing, and mutual investment, one that intensifies in urgency as Hume's health begins to fail near the chapter's close.

## Themes

**Solitude as the price of intellectual production.** Smith's decade in Kirkcaldy is presented as a near-monastic withdrawal—he describes his studies as going forward "like the web of penelope," perpetually unraveling, and blames his poor health on "thinking too much upon one thing." This is the private, invisible labor behind a work that will eventually erupt into public consequence: a paradigm assembling itself quietly for years before it becomes visible to the world, in the mode of a [[history-machine]]. Rasmussen underscores that Smith had been germinating the ideas since his Glasgow professorship, but only now, isolated from society, does the actual writing happen.

**Hume's cultivated contentment versus Smith's compulsive labor.** Hume, by contrast, embraces idleness as an achievement: he retires from public life declaring himself "too old, too fat, too lazy, and too rich" to extend his History of England, and elevates cooking and hosting to a philosophy of life, even applying Smith's own division-of-labor principle to his housekeeper's culinary repertoire. His refusal to keep writing—"Cui bono? Why shoud I forgo Idleness and Sauntering and Society"—reads as a deliberate assertion of sovereignty over his own remaining time, a kind of [[Conatus]]-driven resistance to being captured by public demand ("the Clamours of a stupid, factious Public") even at the cost of further fame or income.

**Friendship maintained through comic irritation.** Much of the chapter's charm lies in Hume's repeated, half-serious campaigns to lure Smith to Edinburgh—offers of apartments, mock legal "Defiance" challenges to meet on a desert island, accusations that Smith's claimed ill health is "only a Subterfuge invented by Indolence and Love of Solitude." Smith, characteristically, resists nearly all of it, deepening the sense that this is a friendship built on affectionate, low-stakes conflict rather than proximity.

**Mortality entering the frame.** The chapter's final movement shifts tone: Hume's letter reporting that he has "fallen five compleat Stones" and might "probably disappear altogether" introduces the intimation of mortality that will dominate the following chapters. The urgency of his pleas for Smith to finally settle in Edinburgh is now inflected with the knowledge, retrospectively supplied by Rasmussen, that Hume's terminal illness is approaching.

**Anecdote as characterization.** The chapter leans on vivid anecdotes—Smith absentmindedly dunking bread and butter into a teapot, Hume being rescued from a bog by fishwives who demand the Lord's Prayer first, Nancy Ord chalking "St. David Street" onto the wall of Hume's unnamed new house—to render both men's personalities: Smith's famous absence of mind, Hume's genial imperturbability in the face of his reputation for impiety.

## Notable passages

The "mortally sick at Sea" letter (from which the chapter's title is drawn) is the emotional center of the unit: Hume, freshly resettled in Edinburgh and able to see Kirkcaldy across the firth, begs Smith to make the short sail rather than force Hume—prone to seasickness—to do so, and jokingly proposes a duel-by-residence on the uninhabited island of Inch-Keith until their philosophical disagreements are resolved. It captures the mix of real longing and comic exaggeration that defines their correspondence.

The Ayr Bank exchange shows the friendship operating as genuine intellectual partnership rather than mere sociability: Hume passes along real-time financial news and asks point-blank whether the bank's collapse affects Smith's "Theory," and Smith later incorporates the episode's lesson—that the failure likely redirected capital toward more productive uses—directly into The Wealth of Nations. It is one of the few moments the reader sees the book actually being built out of their ongoing conversation.

Hume's refusal to extend his History of England, culminating in the blunt "Because I'm too old, too fat, too lazy, and too rich," is played for comedy but doubles as a philosophical statement: having secured his reputation and his fortune, Hume declines to trade further leisure for public approval he holds in low esteem ("Posterity will do me more Justice than the present Age, whose Suffrage indeed coud not have given me great Vanity").

The Nancy Ord anecdote about the naming of St. David Street is a small but telling episode: Hume's untroubled, self-deprecating response to being mock-canonized ("many a better man has been made a saint of before") exemplifies the good humor with which he wore his reputation as "the infidel," a persona that recurs throughout the book.

The chapter's closing lines, in which Hume reports his drastic weight loss and warns "If you delay much longer, I shall probably disappear altogether," function as a hinge into the book's next movement—still lightly comic in tone, but retrospectively ominous, marking the last stretch of ordinary life before Hume's final illness.
