# The Wealth of Nations and the Debts Smith Owed Hume

This chapter traces how much of the argumentative architecture of Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) — published in the final year of Hume and Smith's friendship — was already present, in seed form, in Hume's earlier essays and history. Rasmussen works through Smith's five explicit citations of Hume, his four-paragraph transcription from *The History of England*, and the pivotal passage in Book 3 where Smith names Hume as the only writer to have noticed commerce's role in securing liberty, to argue that Hume's influence on Smith's political economy was real and significant even though it is harder to trace than Hume's influence on *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*. The chapter then surveys where Smith diverged from Hume — most sharply on the dangers of commercial society and on religious establishment — before closing with the correspondence the two exchanged around the book's publication, days before Hume's final illness became acute.

## Themes

**Smith's most important claim is also his most Humean one.** Rasmussen nominates Book 3's claim that commerce "introduced order and good government, and with them, the liberty and security of individuals" as the single most important passage in *The Wealth of Nations* — and notes that Smith immediately credits Hume as the only writer to have noticed this effect. The underlying narrative (feudal lords squandering power on "trinkets and baubles" until they could no longer maintain their armed dependents, thereby ceding ground to strong, law-enforcing kings) is lifted nearly intact from Hume's *Political Discourses* and *History of England*. This is arguably the chapter's strongest [[world-machines]]-adjacent material: it is a compact theory of how one deep institutional order (feudal dependency) is dissolved not by force or design but by the "silent and insensible operation" of a rival infrastructure (commerce and luxury) — a transition Smith and Hume both narrate as gradual, largely unplanned, and only visible in hindsight, which resonates with how a [[history-machine]] assembles silently before lurching into legibility.

**Free trade and anti-mercantilism were already Hume's causes.** Smith's polemic against mercantilism — the idea that national power derives from hoarding gold and silver and that one nation's gain must be another's loss — restates arguments Hume had already made in the *Political Discourses*, especially "Of the Jealousy of Trade" and "Of the Balance of Trade." Both men treat the zero-sum view of commerce as a form of "national prejudice and animosity" rather than a serious theory, and both insist that flourishing trading partners benefit rather than threaten their trading nations. Rasmussen is careful to note real differences of emphasis (Smith grounds the case in the division of labor, which Hume barely mentions) and of scale (Smith's treatment is vastly more systematic), while insisting the core commitments were shared.

**Smith saw the costs of commercial society that Hume mostly waved away.** Where Hume treated commerce's benefits to prosperity, virtue, liberty, and happiness as essentially unmixed goods, Smith is willing to catalogue serious harms: merchants' collusive "mean rapacity"; the mental "torpor" bred by an extreme division of labor, which can render a worker incapable of "any generous, noble, or tender sentiment"; and above all the deep, structural inequality of commercial societies, which Smith argues corrupts moral sentiment itself by teaching people to admire the rich and neglect the poor. This is the chapter's clearest instance of two thinkers building on the same underlying machine while disagreeing sharply about how to weigh its friction and its casualties — a divergence in temperament as much as doctrine.

**On religion, Smith is the more radical of the two.** Hume, despite his skepticism, defended an established church on the grounds that a subsidized, comfortable clergy could be bribed into indolence and thus rendered harmless, whereas Smith — quoting Hume's own case at length before rebutting it — argued that religious pluralism and free competition among sects would defuse fanaticism the same way free markets defuse monopoly. Smith's advocacy of full church-state separation was, per Rasmussen, a genuine outlier position for 1776, existing nowhere outside a few American colonies.

**Both men were early, isolated, and correct about American independence.** Hume's private correspondence shows him urging British withdrawal from America years before most Britons entertained the idea ("I am an American in my Principles"), on pragmatic rather than rights-based grounds; Smith arrived independently at a similar though more equivocal conclusion, proposing an Anglo-American parliamentary union as an alternative to his own preferred outcome, and closing the book by urging Britain to give up its imperial "project" and adjust to "the real mediocrity of her circumstances."

## Notable passages

The Book 3 passage on commerce, liberty, and the fall of the feudal lords is the chapter's anchor, both because Rasmussen calls it possibly the single most important sentence in the entire *Wealth of Nations* and because Smith's own footnote to it is an explicit acknowledgment of debt to Hume. The mercantilism/free-trade passage, culminating in Hume's droll prayer for the prosperity of "even france itself," shows the cosmopolitan logic underlying both men's economics. The division-of-labor passage is Smith at his bluntest about commercial society's human costs, describing a worker rendered as "stupid and ignorant as it is possible for a human creature to become." The inequality passage traces Smith's claim that admiration of wealth is "the great and most universal cause of the corruption of our moral sentiments." Finally, the passage on religious establishment reproduces Hume's own vivid, cynical case for a bribed clergy — the very passage Smith quotes at length before turning it against him.
