# Fêted in France: Hume's Paris Apotheosis and Smith's Grand Tour (1759–1766)

This stretch of the Hume–Smith correspondence tracks two diverging careers that keep intersecting: Smith trades his Glasgow chair for a lucrative post as traveling tutor to the young Duke of Buccleuch, touring Toulouse, Geneva, and Paris over two and a half years, while Hume — sent to Paris as secretary to the Earl of Hertford — is swept into a vogue of adulation from French salon society so extreme it eclipses decades of religious censure back home. Threaded through the narrative is a recurring, practical question the two friends keep putting to each other in letters: where, finally, should they live, and could they ever contrive to live near one another again? The unit closes just as Smith's Paris sojourn ends in a sudden bereavement and Hume, back in London, is about to be drawn into the Rousseau affair.

## Themes

**Patronage converts professor into tutor.** Charles Townshend's offer — relayed first through Hume, who tried and failed to talk Townshend into sending Buccleuch to Glasgow instead — pulls Smith out of the university entirely. The terms (a lifelong pension, travel, and eventually leisure to draft what becomes *The Wealth of Nations* out of sheer boredom in Toulouse) show how aristocratic patronage, not academic institutions, funded the era's major intellectual production; Smith's own line, "I have begun to write a book in order to pass away the time," is the closest thing in the correspondence to an origin story for his masterwork.

**Fame is jurisdictional, not absolute.** Hume's reception in Paris — "I eat nothing but Ambrosia, drink nothing but Nectar" — is deliberately set against the decades of Scottish clerical hostility he endured as "the Great Infidel." The same body of skeptical writing that made him a target for excommunication attempts in Edinburgh makes him the most lionized man in France; Rasmussen's narrative uses this whiplash to underscore how reputation is a function of which public is judging, not a fixed property of the work itself.

**Two grades of irreligion.** Hume's mitigated skepticism turns out to be too pious for the Paris philosophes. The d'Holbach dinner story — counting eighteen guests, "fifteen" confirmed atheists and "the other three haven't yet made up their minds" — dramatizes a real fault line between Hume's suspended-judgment skepticism and the militant, systematizing atheism of Diderot's and d'Holbach's circle. Hume is teased for insufficient radicalism, a small irony given his reputation at home.

**Geography of friendship, negotiated by letter.** Across the unit Hume and Smith repeatedly hash out, entirely in correspondence, whether to settle in Edinburgh, London, or Paris. Hume's "Edinburgh has many Objections and many Allurements" and Smith's blunt rebuttal of Paris — "the cordiality of their friendship is much less to be depended on than that of our own countrymen" — read almost like a negotiation transcript. Smith's counter-proposal, that they make "short excursions" abroad but keep London as their "ordinary residence," is never realized; the two end up on the same side of the Channel but rarely in the same room.

**Sociability as its own infrastructure.** The Select Society's decline and the founding of the Poker Club, the Paris salons of the Comtesse de Boufflers and d'Holbach, and the transatlantic correspondence with Benjamin Franklin all point to an informal, portable infrastructure of clubs, salons, and letters that carried Enlightenment intellectual life across national and confessional boundaries — a loose, self-organizing network operating alongside, and sometimes against, the official strictures (clerical, political) of any one place. Hume's own worry that Edinburgh's religious "narrowness" would "hurt" his friends by association is a reminder that this network survived only where local orthodoxy tolerated it.

## Notable passages

The unit opens with Townshend's offer that turns Smith from professor into tutor — the hinge that sets up his three years abroad and, per Rasmussen's inference, the first stirrings of *The Wealth of Nations*. Hume's own letter about accepting the Hertford secretaryship shows a rare moment of self-doubt beneath his usual jocularity: "I repine at my Loss of Ease and Leizure and Retirement and Independance." The long passage on Hume's Paris reception — contrasted explicitly with Franklin's parallel experience of rustic charm playing well abroad — is the unit's centerpiece, establishing the France/Scotland asymmetry that recurs throughout. The d'Holbach dinner anecdote supplies the unit's sharpest wit while marking a genuine philosophical distinction. And Smith's letter warning Hume off settling permanently in Paris is the clearest statement in the whole correspondence of Smith's own theory of sociability under pressure — real, dependable affection dilutes in "large societies" with too many competing objects, a claim that echoes the impartial-spectator psychology of his published moral philosophy.
