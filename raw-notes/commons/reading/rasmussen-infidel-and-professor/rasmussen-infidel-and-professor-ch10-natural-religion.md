# Hume's Dialogues and the Dispute Over Posthumous Publication

This unit examines the best-known controversy in the Hume-Smith relationship: Hume's deathbed request, embedded in his January 1776 will, that Smith see the *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* through the press, and Smith's persistent refusal to do so. Rasmussen lays out the textual history of the *Dialogues*, traces the correspondence between Hume, Smith, and the publisher William Strahan over the following months, surveys the hostile reception the work received when it finally appeared in 1779 (three years after Hume's death, under his nephew's imprint), and then argues against the standard scholarly reading that this episode significantly damaged the two men's friendship or reveals a substantive philosophical rift between them.

## Themes

**The received view exaggerates the rupture.** Rasmussen opens by cataloguing prominent scholars (Phillipson, Ross, Hanley, Kennedy) who read Smith's refusal as having "cast a shadow," "troubled Hume on his deathbed," or amounted to disloyalty. He then works systematically to undercut this reading: Hume's letters to Smith during the dispute remain as affectionate as ever, closing with pleas for more frequent correspondence and culminating in an invitation for Smith to stay at his house for over a month shortly before his death. The evidentiary record of warmth cuts directly against the "tarnished friendship" narrative.

**The *Dialogues* itself is a synthesis, not a departure.** None of the book's individual arguments — the critique of the design argument, the first-cause argument, the problem of evil, religion's dubious moral and political effects — were new to Hume's corpus; each had appeared piecemeal in the *First Enquiry* or *The Natural History of Religion*. What made the *Dialogues* uniquely dangerous, in Rasmussen's account, was Hume's decision to finally marshal every skeptical weapon in a single "devastating — and entertaining — package," closing off the partial refuges that his earlier, more scattered writings had left open to the pious reader.

**Smith's motive was reputational caution, not disagreement.** Against the view that Smith found the work's content scandalous or philosophically uncongenial, Rasmussen marshals Smith's own religious ambiguity (traceable to his Oxford-era skepticism and *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*), his willingness to privately circulate the manuscript (including sending a copy to the duc de La Rochefoucauld), and his explicit praise of the work as "finely written." The refusal, on this reading, was about *publicity*, not truth-content: Smith wanted the ideas to move among a private circle of the like-minded, not detonate in the public square — especially not weeks after the release of *The Wealth of Nations*.

**Hume held himself to the same standard he asked Smith to violate.** The chapter's sharpest turn is biographical irony: Hume had sat on the manuscript for roughly twenty-five years for exactly the reason Smith now cited — a desire to "live quietly, and keep remote from all Clamour." Every other friend who knew of the work (Elliot, Blair, Ferguson, and eventually even Strahan, who had promised to publish it) also counseled against publication. This turns the puzzle around: the harder question is not why Smith refused, but why the dying Hume suddenly reversed a quarter-century of his own caution and tried to pass the resulting risk on to his closest friend at the worst possible professional moment.

**The reception vindicated the caution.** Contemporary reviews (Critical Review, London Magazine, Monthly Review, and even the Göttingischen gelehrten Anzeigen) treated the *Dialogues* as genuinely scandalous, condemning Hume's "pernicious" principles and lamenting that he had no friend loyal enough to "bury" the work with him — direct empirical confirmation that the clamor Hume and Smith both anticipated was real, not a theoretical worry.

## Notable passages

The will itself is the hinge of the whole episode: Hume left Smith "all my manuscripts without exception," attaching both the publication request and a two-hundred-pound honorarium contingent on completion — a bequest generous enough to make refusal genuinely costly to decline gracefully. Rasmussen's account of the *Dialogues*' argumentative structure — that its novelty lay in combining every prior skeptical thread into one work that "leaves the pious reader no way out, no safe haven" — supplies the interpretive key for why the stakes of publication felt so much higher than for any of Hume's earlier writings. The Rochefoucauld "wind" passage captures Hume manipulating the emotional register even while formally releasing Smith from obligation, and Smith's careful, non-committal reply shows him absorbing the pressure without yielding. Smith's own letter to Strahan — wishing the work "had remained in Manuscript to be communicated only to a few people" and declaring he wanted "no concern in the publication" — is the closest thing to a first-person statement of motive in the record, and it is remarkably free of any objection to the book's content. The closing paragraph delivers Rasmussen's real thesis: that Hume's abrupt reversal of decades of self-restraint, and his choice to offload the risk onto Smith at the very moment *The Wealth of Nations* had just appeared, is a harder historical puzzle than Smith's caution ever was.
