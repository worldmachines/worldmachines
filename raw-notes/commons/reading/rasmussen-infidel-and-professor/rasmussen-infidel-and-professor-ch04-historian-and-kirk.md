# Hume's History of England and the Kirk's Attempt to Excommunicate Him

This stretch of the chapter follows David Hume through the writing and reception of his six-volume History of England (composed out of chronological order between 1754 and 1761, and an immediate commercial sensation despite an unfavorable opening reception), through the Edinburgh Review project that pointedly excluded him, into the Popular Party's mid-1750s campaign to have the Kirk's General Assembly excommunicate him, and on to the publication of the incendiary Natural History of Religion within his 1757 Four Dissertations. Throughout, his letters to Adam Smith — soliciting reviews, reporting on the History's sales, floating a scheme to lure Smith to Edinburgh, complaining that Smith never visits — thread the personal story of their friendship through the public controversies.

## Themes

**History as accident, not design or decline.** The organizing thesis of the History of England, as Rasmussen reconstructs it, is the "blessings of civilization": Hume insists the present is superior to a romanticized past (he catalogues Elizabethan England's smoke-filled, glassless, meatless squalor to prove it), yet he explicitly denies that progress was inevitable. Civilized society, in his telling, emerged from "the great mixture of accident, which commonly concurs with a small ingredient of wisdom and foresight" — unintended consequences, not design, a motif Smith would later echo in The Wealth of Nations. This shows up most strikingly in Hume's account of religion: the medieval Church accidentally held a lawless society together, and — even more surprisingly for so secular a writer — Puritan religious fanaticism, precisely because zealots feared God more than any king, accidentally kept "the precious spark of liberty" alive under Tudor and Stuart absolutism, a spark that would later erupt into civil war before a balanced constitution emerged "only from the resulting ashes." History assembling itself sideways, through actors' unintended byproducts rather than anyone's foresight, resonates with the [[history-machine]] idea that paradigms cohere from distributed, uncoordinated pressure rather than great-man intention; the civil-war interregnum between a dying absolutist legitimacy and an unborn constitutional one is a small-scale instance of a [[gramsci-gap]].

**A historian who refuses to be partisan and is punished for it by both sides.** Hume held that "the first Quality of an Historian is to be true & impartial," and the chapter shows him genuinely straddling Whig and Tory sympathies — enough that Tories called the Stuart volumes a Tory tract while Whigs found him insufficiently partisan for their own cause. Hume's own verdict on this reception ("not displeasd to be abusd by the violent of both Parties") reads as a considered epistemic stance, not just thick skin: a refusal of exclusive claims in favor of provisional plurality of judgment, in keeping with a portrait of him elsewhere as "the reporter of all sides, the doubter of exclusive claims, the distruster of systems."

**The Kirk's excommunication campaign and the friends it really endangered.** The Popular Party, led by George Anderson, tried repeatedly (1755–1757) to have the General Assembly excommunicate Hume over six enumerated heresies, from denying an objective virtue/vice distinction to preferring Catholicism to the Reformation. Rasmussen judges the charges largely fair as a summary of Hume's actual views, and notes the prosecution's real irony: excommunication carried no civil penalty and would only have martyred Hume further, so the true stakes were the reputations of his Moderate minister friends (Blair, Robertson, Carlyle, Jardine, Home) who socialized openly with an avowed skeptic. The threat, though defeated each time, was real enough that Hume later asked Blair to simply stop discussing religion with him altogether so their friendship could continue on other terms — an early, personal instance of the book's larger theme of intimacy sustained across an unbridgeable belief gap.

**Doubling down rather than retreating.** Far from chastened by the threat of excommunication, Hume used the Four Dissertations (1757) to publish his most openly hostile treatment of religion, the Natural History of Religion, arguing that popular religion increases rather than allays fear and anxiety and that the pursuit of special divine favor actively corrodes ordinary morality. He privately acknowledged that a "Tincture of Deism" sells books and that he owed part of his own reputation to it — a frank admission that scandal and sincerity were, for him, not mutually exclusive.

## Notable passages

The long passage on the "blessings of civilization" and the "mixture of accident" is the closest thing to a thesis statement for the entire History, and it sets up the religion-and-liberty argument that follows: Hume's claim that Puritan fanaticism unintentionally preserved "the precious spark of liberty" under Elizabeth is, as Rasmussen notes via another scholar, "the consummate irony of Hume's career." The description of the excommunication campaign's leader, George Anderson — Hume's own mock-encomium calling him "the godly, spiteful, pious, splenetic, charitable, unrelenting, meek, persecuting, Christian, inhuman, peacemaking, furious Anderson" — shows Hume meeting a genuine institutional threat with characteristic irony rather than alarm. The Natural History of Religion's closing dismissal of religious principles as "sick men's dreams" or "the playsome whimsies of monkies in human shape" is quoted as perhaps his most uncompromising anti-religious statement in the whole corpus. Finally, Hume's letter to Hugh Blair proposing that they simply stop discussing religion — "I have, long since, done with all inquiries on such subjects, and am become incapable of instruction" — is the chapter's quietest and most telling passage: proof that the friendships underwriting the Moderate literati's protection of Hume required a deliberate, negotiated silence, not shared belief.
