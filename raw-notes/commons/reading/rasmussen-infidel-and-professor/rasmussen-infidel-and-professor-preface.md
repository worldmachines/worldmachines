# Preface: The Friendship That Shaped Modern Thought

Rasmussen opens by staking out his subject: not a study of Hume's or Smith's ideas in isolation, but a narrative history of the quarter-century friendship between "the greatest philosopher ever to write in the English language" and "history's most famous theorist of commercial society," from their 1749 meeting to Hume's death. He previews the book's shape — mutual literary support, shared social circles, the Rousseau affair, a lifelong and only partly successful wish to see more of each other — and names its organizing tension: the two men's convergent private opinions and divergent public faces on the subject of religion.

## Themes

**Friendship as historiographical method.** Rather than treating Hume and Smith as parallel case studies, Rasmussen uses the friendship itself as the narrative spine, tracking "personal interactions and the impact that each had on the other's outlook." This is a book about influence transmitted through intimacy — commentary on manuscripts, career boosting, consolation in crisis — not just intellectual affinity read off the page.

**The religion problem as running theme.** The preface's most pointed claim is that Hume and Smith "adopt broadly similar views, but very different public postures, with respect to religion and the religious." This sets up the book's title conceit — Hume as open "infidel," Smith as circumspect "professor" — and previews a study of respectability and self-presentation as much as belief.

**Dual address to scholars and general readers.** Rasmussen explicitly positions the book at the border of academic and popular history, hoping to serve both the scholarly literature on Hume and Smith and readers simply curious about "the greatest of all philosophical friendships."

## Notable passages

The preface's opening sentence does double duty as thesis and blurb, pairing Hume's and Smith's superlatives ("greatest philosopher," "most famous theorist of commercial society") to justify why their friendship, not just their ideas, deserves a book. The middle section catalogues the textures of that friendship — shared clubs, shared enemies, mutual literary aid, the aftermath of Hume's break with Rousseau — building toward the pivot on religion, which Rasmussen flags explicitly as a theme that will recur "throughout the book" rather than a passing detail. The closing paragraph is Rasmussen's methodological self-placement: a professor writing peer-reviewable history that is nonetheless meant to be read outside the academy.
