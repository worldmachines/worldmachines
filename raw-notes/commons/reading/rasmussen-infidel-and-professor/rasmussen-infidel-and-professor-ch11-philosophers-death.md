# The Death of David Hume: Dying as a Philosopher Should

This unit covers the final months of David Hume's life, from the onset of his terminal illness (probably colon cancer) in spring 1775 through his death in Edinburgh on 25 August 1776. Because Hume's irreligion was notorious, his approaching death became, in Rasmussen's account, a matter of intense public curiosity: would the famous infidel recant, or at least flinch, at the end? The unit traces how Hume met this expectation head-on—writing a spare, cheerful autobiography (My Own Life), fencing genially with a disappointed James Boswell, joking with Adam Smith about his want of an excuse to give Charon the boatman, and dying, by every account, in perfect composure. It closes with Smith agreeing to complete Hume's story for the public record, setting up the controversy of the following chapter.

## Themes

**A death staged as public philosophical proof.** Rasmussen frames Hume's final year not merely as decline but as performance: Hume "was determined to die as he thought a philosopher should—cheerfully and tranquilly, without undue hopes or fears—and to do it for a public audience." Because he and Smith had both argued that religion answers a psychological need to reconcile people to mortality, Hume's calm, irreligious death was itself an argument—that "this life is enough, if one approaches it in the right way." The stakes were understood by everyone involved: Hume's own account of his final letters shows him treating his composure as evidence, and Smith later wrote to Wedderburn using the same rhetorical frame, insisting Hume died with more genuine resignation "than any Whining Christian ever dyed with pretended resignation to the will of God." This self-conscious, undefended serenity in the face of extinction—needing no external consolation, no futurity, no god—is the kind of unassisted persistence-in-being that resonates with [[Conatus]]: Hume's striving is simply to keep being himself, curious and sociable, right up to the boundary of non-being, without appeal to any framework beyond that.

**My Own Life as curated self-fashioning, not confession.** Hume's ten-page autobiography is analyzed here as a calculated document: it emphasizes his rise from obscurity to fame and fortune, catalogues the poor initial reception of nearly every book he wrote (the Treatise "fell dead-born from the press"), and almost entirely omits both his intimate life (Boufflers, Rousseau) and the real professional costs of his irreligion (failed university candidacies, attempted excommunication). Rasmussen reads this omission as strategic: a reader with no prior knowledge of Hume "would scarcely realize that she was reading the autobiography of a notorious infidel." The closing paragraphs, written in the past tense "almost as if from beyond the grave," overreach into self-flattery (the claim that his friends never had to defend him is simply false) but accomplish their purpose—demonstrating unshaken equanimity before "a disease that he knew was 'mortal and incurable.'"

**Interrogators sent to extract a deathbed recantation, and their failure.** Boswell's visit, Strahan's letter, and Temple's furious reaction to Boswell's report are three variations on the same social ritual: the pious testing whether Hume's skepticism was sincere or merely a pose that death would strip away. Each fails to shake him. Hume tells Boswell flatly that a man's morality has nothing to do with his religion, compares extinction to nonexistence before birth, and shows a mind Boswell describes—against his own hopes—as possessing "a tranquillity of mind and a clearness of head which few men possess at any time." Temple's reaction ("Let him die then & be thrown into the ditch") shows how much cultural pressure was riding on Hume producing a different outcome.

**Humor as the register of Hume's courage.** The Lucian-derived anecdote about Charon is the unit's comic and philosophical centerpiece: unable, unlike Lucian's shades, to invent any unfinished business worth pleading for a delay, Hume tries out excuses about reforming public opinion against superstition, which Charon rudely and correctly waves off ("You loitering rogue... Get into the boat this instant"). Smith preserves two versions—a harsher one in his private letter to Wedderburn (hoping to see "the churches shut up, and the Clergy sent about their business") and a softened one in the eventual public Letter to Strahan—showing Smith already managing how antireligious Hume would appear to posterity.

**Friendship and succession.** The unit's final movement is about trust transferred at the edge of death: Hume's letters to Smith about the Dialogues and about who should extend My Own Life culminate in Hume granting Smith "entire liberty" to add to the account of his life—effectively naming Smith as the author of his public death. Smith's own letters (to Hume and to Wedderburn) show him already rehearsing the account he would publish, aware from the start, whatever he later claimed, that it would provoke exactly the outrage the next chapter describes.

## Notable passages

The Monthly Review's framing of public fascination with Hume's death—wondering whether "new light" would be "let into the soul's dark cottage... through the chinks of its ruins" as he approached his end—sets the stakes Hume was writing against. Rasmussen's own analytical passage on why Hume performed his death ("He was showing, through his example, that this world is enough") states the unit's thesis directly. The Boswell exchange is the fullest dramatization of the public test Hume faced and passed. The Charon anecdote, in its rawer form told to Wedderburn, is Hume's own comic self-portrait of a man with no unfinished business and no need of an afterlife. And the spare final lines recording Hume's actual death—Black's assurance that he "died in such a happy composure of mind that nothing could have made it better"—close the arc the whole unit has been building toward.
