# Young Hume: Life, the Treatise, and the Making of a Skeptic (1711–1749)

This unit narrates the first thirty-eight years of David Hume's life: his birth into a modest Scottish laird's family in 1711, a precocious but unsatisfying university education, an intense period of independent study that cost him his health, the composition and disastrous reception of *A Treatise of Human Nature*, his pivot to essay-writing as a more accessible philosophical genre, his rejected bid for the Edinburgh chair of Ethics and Pneumatical Philosophy, two stints as a military secretary to Lieutenant-General St. Clair, and the writing of the *Philosophical Essays Concerning Human Understanding* (the First Enquiry), whose essays on miracles and on the argument from design are reconstructed in some analytical detail. The chapter closes on the threshold of the decade (1749–1759) that would prove Hume's most productive and would also inaugurate his friendship with Adam Smith.

## Themes

**Hume's affability coexisted with real controversy.** The unit opens by establishing the paradox that structures Hume's reputation: adored personally ("le bon David" in Paris, a favorite even among Edinburgh ministers) yet reviled for his ideas by "some because I am not a Tory, some because I am not a Whig, some because I am not a Christian, and all because I am a Scotsman." Isaiah Berlin's judgment that no philosopher had "influenced the history of philosophical thought to a deeper and more disturbing degree" frames the stakes of the intellectual biography that follows.

**Hume's youthful loss of faith was intellectual, not rebellious.** Raised in a pious Presbyterian household with a minister uncle who conducted heresy trials, Hume describes his adolescent doubt as involuntary and cyclical ("Doubts stole in, dissipated, return'd, were again dissipated, return'd again"). Tellingly, he traces the collapse of his belief not to irreligious authors but to reading the *defenses* of theism by Locke and Clarke — an irony the note preserves via Anthony Collins's quip that no one doubted God's existence until Clarke tried to prove it.

**The Treatise was an audacious, experimental refounding of philosophy that failed on arrival.** Hume's ambition was to "march up directly to the capital or center of" the sciences — human nature itself — using "the experimental method" rather than Cartesian pure reason. The consequence was a radical epistemic humility (we cannot even prove an external world or a persisting self) paired with a defense of custom, habit, and the passions as what actually guides human life. Yet the book "fell dead-born from the press," and Hume spent the rest of his life alternately disowning its "positive Air" and insisting its core principles never changed.

**Hume repeatedly self-censored to manage religious offense, then increasingly refused to.** He describes "castrating" the Treatise's most incendiary material (on miracles and the soul's immortality) as "a Piece of Cowardice," and later, more cynically, counsels a wavering young clergyman that "innocent Dissimulation" is simply the price of passing through the world. This tension — real skepticism paired with strategic concealment — culminates in the Philosophical Essays, which he published under his own name (unlike the anonymous Treatise and early Essays) and left doctrinally uncastrated, including the miracles and design essays reconstructed below.

**The miracles and design arguments apply the Treatise's empiricism directly to theology.** Against miracles, Hume argues that belief should always be proportioned to evidence, and since a miracle by definition violates all of our uniform past experience, no testimony can ever tip the balance in its favor — a conclusion he insists is not dogmatic atheism but simply consistent skepticism. Against the argument from design, he applies the same proportionality rule in reverse: an inferred cause (a designer) cannot be credited with more wisdom, power, or benevolence than its observed effects (a world full of "innumerable disorders and evils") actually display, rendering the argument "essentially a useless hypothesis."

**Hume's public philosophical failures often had material stakes.** His 1745 rejection for the Edinburgh chair over "Scepticism, Heterodoxy & other hard Names" — with even his friend Francis Hutcheson opposing him — pushed him into tutoring the insane Marquess of Annandale and then into two stints as a military secretary, oddly formative detours that gave the future historian firsthand exposure to war and diplomacy (including his comic account of curtsying before Maria Theresa) and eventually the financial independence to write full-time.

**Hume's empiricism did not immunize him against his own era's prejudices.** The chapter registers, without excusing it, Hume's notorious footnote in "Of National Characters" suspecting "the negroes to be naturally inferior to the whites" — a claim built on "personal suspicion rather than actual experience," the very failure mode his own philosophy warned against — and pointedly contrasts it with Adam Smith's opposing remarks on the dignity of enslaved Africans, seeding the book's larger comparison of the two men's characters.

## Notable passages

The passages curated below trace the arc from reputation, to method, to the two great anti-theological arguments, to the costs and limits of Hume's candor. The opening passage (c1) sets up the loved-yet-feared paradox that makes Hume worth a dual biography at all. The Treatise passage (c2) gives Hume's own martial metaphor for his ambition — marching "directly to the capital" of the sciences rather than taking outlying "castles" one by one — which frames everything that follows as an attempt to refound knowledge on experience alone. The miracles passage (c3) and design passage (c4) are the two set-piece arguments that made Hume notorious as "the infidel," rendered here in enough analytical detail to show their shared logical structure (proportioning belief, or an inferred cause, to the actual evidence). The 1764 letter on hypocrisy (c5) is the darkest and most self-aware moment in the unit: Hume freely admits that "innocent Dissimulation" is required to "pass thro the World," a confession that retroactively explains why his failure to obtain the Edinburgh chair stung as much as it did. Finally, the National Characters footnote (c6) is flagged not to indict Hume anachronistically but because Rasmussen uses it, and its Smith counterpart, as an early data point in the book's ongoing comparison of the two friends' characters.
