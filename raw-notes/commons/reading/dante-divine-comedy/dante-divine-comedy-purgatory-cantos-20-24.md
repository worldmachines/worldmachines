# Purgatory, Cantos XX–XXIV: Hugh Capet's Curse, Statius's Conversion, and the Terrace of Gluttony

The unit opens mid-terrace-five (avarice), with Hugh Capet delivering a long dynastic indictment of the greed of the French crown he founded, then covers the mountain's tremor and the Gloria that greets a soul's release, the meeting with Statius (who explains the tremor and later reveals that Virgil's poetry secretly converted him to Christianity), the ascent onto terrace six (gluttony), the first temperance-tree and its recited exemplars, the harrowing encounter with the emaciated Forese Donati and his prophecy against Florence, Bonagiunta of Lucca's recognition of the *dolce stil novo*, a second tree reciting gluttony's punishments, and the terrace's close with the angel of temperance's beatitude. Canto XXV begins only as a bare heading with no text, marking the unit's edge.

## Themes

**Avarice as a force that dissolves natural bonds.** Hugh Capet's speech is less personal confession than dynastic indictment: he traces the Capetian line from his own "gripe" on the "reins of empire" through the Angevin conquests, the humiliation of Boniface VIII at Anagni ("the flower-de-luce / Enters Alagna!"), and a prophesied future Charles who will sell his own daughter "as do / The Corsairs for their slaves." His refrain — each new acquisition made "for amends" but compounding sin — turns the terrace's psalm ("My soul cleaveth to the dust," implied by the earlier sponge-image) into a genealogy of appetite outrunning legitimacy. The nightly antiphon of avarice's punishments (Pygmalion, Midas, Achan, Sapphira, Heliodorus, Crassus) generalizes the point: avarice is figured as a force that overrides kinship, justice, and even self-preservation, subduing "our blood / So wholly to itself, they feel no care / Of their own flesh."

**Purification as release of will, not removal of desire.** Statius's explanation of the mountain's tremor is the unit's clearest statement of Purgatory's mechanics: the mountain is immune to all weather and geological disturbance because it lies above nature's reach, and it shakes only when a soul's will, having exhausted its "propension" toward its besetting sin, freely turns toward ascent. "Purification by the will alone / Is prov'd, that free to change society / Seizes the soul rejoicing in her will." Desire for bliss is present from the first arrival on the mountain; what changes is not the desire but the soul's readiness to act on it — a will-based account of moral change that recurs when Statius describes his own conversion from prodigality and, later, from lukewarm crypto-Christianity.

**Statius's conversion: a hidden cause becoming legible only in retrospect.** Statius reveals that Virgil's own poetry — specifically the Fourth Eclogue's language of a "renovated world," returning Justice, and "a new race descended from above" — planted the seed of his Christian faith long before he understood it, with Virgil cast as "one, / Who, journeying through the darkness, hears a light / Behind, that profits not himself, but makes / His followers wise." Virgil authored a prophecy whose meaning he could not access; Statius lived it out generations later; the connection only becomes visible from Statius's vantage, retrospectively. It is a compact instance of a paradigm assembling silently through independent, unwitting contributions until it lurches into visibility for someone downstream — the kind of pattern the World Machines project calls a [[history-machine]]. His confession that he was baptized but "secretly, through fear, / Remain'd a Christian" while outwardly conforming to "Pagan rites" for five centuries' worth of purgation adds a second layer: a hidden allegiance kept invisible until circumstance forces it into the open.

**Poetic succession recognized from the outside.** On the gluttony terrace, Bonagiunta of Lucca identifies Dante as "the man / Of that new lay th' inventor," and Dante's self-description — "I am the scribe of love; that, when he breathes, / Take up my pen, and, as he dictates, write" — draws from Bonagiunta the admission that the older generation ("the notary," Guittone, and himself) never grasped what "parts one style from other": they lacked the wing-stretch of the new, dictation-driven manner. It is the same shape as Statius's conversion — an old paradigm (courtly rhetoric) recognizing its own supersession only once the new one is already in flight, again legible chiefly in hindsight, another small [[history-machine]] moment, this time in literary rather than religious history.

**Gluttony's punishment as inverted appetite.** The emaciated shades — "eyes... dark and hollow: pale / Their visage, and so lean withal, the bones / Stood staring thro' the skin," compared to Erisicthon's ravenous self-devouring — are starved before a fragrant, unreachable tree and stream, their hunger a magnified mirror of the excess that damned them. Forese Donati's account is the emotional center: he was pulled out of Ante-purgatory early by his widow Nella's prayers and tears, and his gratitude curdles into a scathing prophecy against the immodest "unblushing dames of Florence" and, later, against Corso Donati, dragged to a violent death by "an infuriate beast." The terrace closes with the angel's beatitude rewarding those "whom grace / Doth so illume, that appetite in them / Exhaleth no inordinate desire" — temperance as calibrated, not eliminated, appetite.

## Notable passages

Hugh Capet's "O avarice!" outburst is the terrace's rhetorical peak, cataloguing dynastic crime as an unbroken chain from his own usurpation to the Anagni outrage. The mountain's tremor and the shepherds'-field Gloria mark Statius's release and are staged as a liturgical set-piece the pilgrims can only stand "immovably suspended" through. Statius's account of Virgil's unwitting prophecy ("By thee conducted first, I enter'd the Parnassian grots") is the unit's key conversion narrative. The Bonagiunta exchange names the *dolce stil novo* explicitly and stages a poet's belated recognition of his own obsolescence. Forese's "To drink up the sweet wormwood of affliction" passage grounds the terrace's abstraction in personal grief and marital devotion. The closing beatitude ("As when, to harbinger the dawn, springs up...") reframes temperance as fragrant, gentle discipline rather than deprivation, closing the terrace on a note of grace.
