# Humboldt and Jefferson: Politics and Nature in Washington, 1804

After a hurricane nearly sinks his ship en route from Cuba, Humboldt detours to the United States before returning to Europe, driven by a wish to see a republic built on the 'ideas of 1789' after five years spent seeing nature 'at its best.' In Philadelphia and then Washington, DC he spends an intense week with President Jefferson, Secretary of State Madison, and Treasury Secretary Gallatin — trading his uniquely precise Latin American maps, statistics, and colonial-archive intelligence (especially on the disputed Texas borderlands between the Sabine and the Rio Grande) for American company and, in Jefferson's case, instant political use. The two men discover they are intellectual twins on the question of agrarian republicanism, but the chapter builds steadily toward the one place where their worldviews break: slavery and the equality of races, on which Humboldt is unflinching and Jefferson is not.

## Themes

**Politics and nature as one subject.** The chapter's organizing claim, voiced by Humboldt himself to Madison, is that 'politics and nature belonged together.' Humboldt arrives having witnessed 'the grandeur of the physical world' in the Andes and wants next 'to enjoy the spectacle of a free people' — nature and liberty read as a single continuous inquiry. Jefferson embodies the same fusion in his own person: President, farmer, botanist, inventor, and obsessive measurer (temperature tables, stair-counts, a ruler always in his pocket) whose White House becomes, in Wulf's telling, a 'scientific nexus' where botany and statecraft share a dinner table.

**Science as leverage, not just as ideal.** Humboldt frames science as a 'republic of letters' above national interest — sciences 'always at peace even if their nations may be at war' — and hands over data gathered under exclusive Spanish permission without hesitation. But the exchange is immediately geopolitical: Jefferson uses Humboldt's nineteen pages of extracts and maps in a Cabinet meeting on U.S. strategy toward Spain within a month, weaponizing Humboldt's cartographic and statistical precision (accurate to within 2 degrees where old maps were wrong by up to 140 miles) in the Texas border dispute. This is legibility-making in the service of empire: Humboldt's instruments of scientific measurement double as instruments of territorial claim-making, a small but vivid instance of the world-machine idea that measurement and mapping are core technologies of state control (`[[legibility-as-machine-core]]`).

**Agrarian republicanism as shared political theology.** Both men hold that only a nation of small independent farmers secures liberty — Jefferson believes 'our governments will remain virtuous for many centuries, as long as they are chiefly agricultural,' argues only farmers should be legislators, and drafted (unsuccessfully) a Virginia constitution guaranteeing fifty acres to every free person. Humboldt concurs from direct observation: the small farmers he met in South America had developed 'the sentiment of liberty and independence.' Land, self-sufficiency, and political freedom are treated as causally identical.

**Colonialism read as ecological devastation.** Humboldt is, in Wulf's account, the first to connect empire directly to environmental destruction: Spanish mining turned indigenous laborers into a 'human machine' under the repartimiento system; indigo and sugar monocultures replaced subsistence crops and 'impoverished the soil' until, in Cuba, an entire island could no longer feed itself; dam-building and deforestation on the Rio Apure and the draining of Mexico City's lake system exemplify universal engineering 'follies.' His prescription — subsistence diversity (bananas, quinoa, corn, potatoes) over monoculture and cash-crop dependency — reads as an early articulation of variety-as-resilience against a legibility-maximizing extraction regime, a faint ancestor of the divergence/legibility tension WMP tracks (`[[world-machines]]`), though Humboldt frames it as moral economy rather than machine theory.

**The fracture: slavery and the question of human unity.** The chapter's sharpest turn is the two men's disagreement. Humboldt calls slavery 'the greatest evil' and 'unnatural,' insists all humans 'came from one root' and that 'no race was above another,' and fills his diaries with atrocities he witnessed. Jefferson, by contrast, believed Black people 'inferior... in the endowment both of body and mind,' owned some 200 slaves, and had already abandoned even his half-measure scheme of settling German farmers among them to gradually manumit their children. Wulf does not let the era's celebrated common ground — nature, liberty, agrarian virtue — soften this contradiction; it stands as the visit's one unresolved seam.

## Notable passages

The declaration that 'politics and nature belonged together' to Madison sets the chapter's thesis in Humboldt's own words. Jefferson's introduction as farmer-scientist-president — dressing down, tending roses on the White House windowsills, preferring 'to wade through swamps and climb rocks... rather than attend Cabinet meetings' — establishes just how genuinely the two men mirrored each other. The indigo passage at Lake Valencia crystallizes Humboldt's ecological critique of colonial monoculture, and the mining passage on the repartimiento system shows the human cost beneath the extraction he decries as resting on 'immorality.' The closing passage on slavery and 'one root' — culminating in 'Nature itself was a republic of freedom' — states Humboldt's most radical and enduring conviction, the one place the chapter shows him refusing to trim his principles to fit his host.
