# Across the Andes: Chasing Baudin, Climbing Volcanoes

Having heard a (mistaken) newspaper report that Captain Baudin's round-the-world expedition might pass through Lima, Humboldt and Bonpland abandon their plan to sail directly to Peru and instead choose the far harder overland route across the Andes — through Cartagena, up the Río Magdalena, over the Quindío Pass to Bogotá to consult the botanist José Celestino Mutis, and on to Quito. By the time they arrive in Quito the Baudin lead has evaporated, but Humboldt pivots without complaint into a five-month campaign of systematic volcano-climbing (Pichincha, Cotopaxi, Antisana), driven by a theory about the subterranean connection of volcanoes and the origin of the earth. Wulf interleaves this physical ordeal — smashed instrument cases, shredded shoes, barefoot marches along eight-inch ledges — with an extended treatment of Humboldt's emotional life: his rejection of Rosa Montúfar's attentions, his deep attachment to her brother Carlos, and the pattern of intense, submissive, arguably homoerotic devotion he showed toward male friends throughout his life.

## Themes

**Contingency dressed as plan.** The entire itinerary of this unit is reverse-engineered from a rumor. Humboldt reads a newspaper article, guesses Baudin's likely port of call, and reorganizes a continent-spanning itinerary around a rendezvous that never happens. Wulf frames this not as failure but as characteristic method: Humboldt treats obstacles as accelerants — "the more obstacles that were thrown into his path, 'the more I hastened their executions.'" This restless, almost compulsive persistence — pressing forward under maximal uncertainty rather than waiting for certainty — reads as a vivid instance of conatus, the striving to persist and expand regardless of resistance ([[Conatus]]).

**Instruments as fragile infrastructure of legibility.** The barometer — designed to render mountain heights, and by extension the shape of the globe, measurable and comparable — becomes the chapter's controlling object. Its cost balloons from 12 to 800 thalers once porters' wages are counted, and when an earlier barometer shatters, Humboldt collapses in a town square, unable to imagine measuring the world without his tools. The scene dramatizes how the Enlightenment project of making nature legible — reducible to numbers that travel and compare — depends on absurdly fragile material carriers hauled by hand across the world's most hostile terrain ([[legibility-as-machine-core]]).

**Physical peril as the price of new knowledge.** The Quindío Pass sequence — eight-inch muddy trails, smashed specimen jars, shoes torn to shreds, barefoot marches through blizzards — insists that natural-historical data collection in unmapped regions is inseparable from bodily risk. Humboldt's own diary line, entrusting "one's manuscripts, instruments, [and] collections" to such paths, makes explicit that the fate of the expedition's science and the fate of the men's bodies are the same fate.

**Volcanoes as a systems question.** Humboldt's volcano campaign is not trophy-climbing; it is designed to test whether volcanoes are isolated "local" events or nodes in a subterranean system connected through the earth's core, and by extension to adjudicate between Neptunist (water/sedimentation) and Vulcanist (catastrophic eruption) accounts of the earth's formation. This is an early expression of the holistic, interconnected view of nature that Wulf's larger biography identifies as Humboldt's signature contribution — reading a place only in relation to a whole system, not in isolation.

**Contested intimacy.** Wulf devotes real space to Humboldt's emotional and possibly erotic attachments to men — his declarations of "undying" and "fervent" love, his self-description as having "no sensual needs," his statement that a married man was "a lost man," and contemporaries' pointed remarks (Caroline von Humboldt, Theodor Fontane) about his indifference to women and later insinuations of homosexuality. The arrival of Carlos Montúfar as travelling companion — displacing his sister Rosa's romantic interest — is staged as the latest instance of a lifelong pattern, not an isolated episode.

**Reason bounded by feeling.** The chapter closes on Humboldt's own account of mountains' effect on him: not merely data points but occasions where imagination "soothed the 'deep wounds' that pure 'reason' sometimes created." This is Wulf's thesis in miniature — a scientist whose quantifying, instrument-driven empiricism (the barometer, the altitude measurements exceeding La Condamine and Bouguer's) coexists with, and is completed by, a Romantic surplus that measurement alone cannot capture.

## Notable passages

The Baudin-rumor paragraph shows Humboldt building an entire five-year detour on a single newspaper guess and framing obstruction as fuel rather than deterrent. The Quindío Pass passage is the unit's most visceral image of fieldwork-as-ordeal, with instruments and manuscripts riding barefoot mules along ledges scarcely wider than a boot. The barometer-collapse scene — Humboldt flat on his back in a town square, mourning his broken instrument — is the emotional low point and clearest statement of measurement's material precariousness. The Neptunist/Vulcanist paragraph, capped by Goethe's joking letter about a "female volcano," supplies the intellectual stakes of the climbing campaign. The extended passage on Humboldt's male friendships and Carlos Montúfar's arrival is essential to Wulf's biographical argument and to later scholarly debate about Humboldt's sexuality. And the closing lines on mountains soothing wounds that reason creates crystallize the fusion of measurement and feeling that defines Humboldt's method throughout the book.
