# Chimborazo and the Naturgemälde

Humboldt, Bonpland, Carlos Montúfar, and their guide José leave Quito to attempt Chimborazo, then the tallest mountain known to Europeans. Porters give out at 15,600 feet; the four push on along a knife-edge ridge (the *cuchilla*) in fog, altitude sickness, and bleeding feet, taking barometric and magnetic readings the whole way, until a bottomless crevasse stops them 1,000 feet short of the summit at 19,413 feet — still higher than any human had measured before. Standing there, Humboldt has the synthesizing insight that organizes the rest of his career: every plant, rock, and measurement he has taken from the Alps to Tenerife to the Andes suddenly resolves into one connected system, which he sketches as the Naturgemälde, a cross-sectional "painting of nature" of Chimborazo's vegetation zones. The chapter then follows him south to Lima, north along the coast (where he measures the current later named for him), overland toward an eruption of Cotopaxi he narrowly misses, and finally out of South America across the equator for the last time.

## Themes

**The mountain as instrument of synthesis, not just conquest.** The climb itself is almost incidental to what happens on it — Wulf stages the ascent's physical ordeal (frostbite, bleeding gums, a foot that "completely disappeared" into melting snow) as the precondition for a purely conceptual event. Humboldt does not reach the summit, yet the chapter treats the attempt as his single most consequential act: "everything that Humboldt had seen in the previous years came together." The unreached peak matters less than the vantage it offers.

**Nature as web, not taxonomy.** The chapter's central claim is that Humboldt replaces classificatory science — "tight taxonomic units along a strict hierarchy, filling endless tables" — with a relational one: "Nature is a living whole," not a "dead aggregate." Plants matter not as isolated specimens but "in their relation to the whole." This is presented as a genuine paradigm break: no prior naturalist had shown nature as a global, climate-organized system rather than a catalog. The recurring images — "a web of life," "a thousand threads," "unity in variety" — describe a mode of co-emergent, non-hierarchical relation among phenomena that resembles [[Configurancy]]: nothing here is teleological or centrally ordered, yet everything is mutually constitutive. The realization also reads as a small [[history-machine]] moment — a new way of seeing nature assembling silently across a decade of scattered Alpine, Canarian, and Andean observations before it "lurches into visibility" in a single glance atop Chimborazo.

**The Naturgemälde as a new kind of diagram.** Humboldt's cross-section of Chimborazo, with its side columns cross-referencing altitude, temperature, humidity, and species, is described as an entirely new visual grammar for data — "variety and richness, but also simplicity" combined for the first time. It lets a reader trace one variable (height) across botany, climate, and geology simultaneously, and extends the comparison to other mountains worldwide. This is less a picture than an early instance of infrastructure for legible comparison — a device for making an otherwise chaotic mass of measurements addressable and comparable across the globe, prefiguring later universalizing scientific infrastructures.

**Humility about discovery and attribution.** When the cold Pacific current is later named after him, Humboldt objects that local fishing boys had always known of it; he merely measured it and confirmed it was cold. This small moment complicates the book's own "great man" framing of Humboldt's genius — he himself insists that much of what looks like discovery is really the formalization of existing, distributed local knowledge, a thread relevant to how paradigms are credited versus how they actually assemble.

## Notable passages

The knife-edge ascent passage renders the physical stakes of the climb — altitude sickness so severe it had earlier caused Humboldt to faint on Pichincha, here potentially fatal. The Chimborazo synthesis passage ("everything that Humboldt had seen in the previous years came together") is the chapter's hinge, explicitly naming the "thousand threads" image that becomes central to his later reputation. The two Naturgemälde passages capture the theoretical content directly in Humboldt's own words ("living whole" vs. "dead aggregate"; "unity in variety") and the unprecedented visual form he gave it. The Humboldt Current passage shows his insistence on crediting existing local knowledge over personal discovery.
