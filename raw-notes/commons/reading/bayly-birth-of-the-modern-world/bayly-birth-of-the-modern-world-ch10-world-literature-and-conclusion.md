# World Literature, National Language, and the Fate of the Arts

This unit closes a chapter of *The Birth of the Modern World* by tracing, first, the convergence of the world's literatures on Western-derived forms after 1850 and, second, the parallel transformation of the visual and performing arts by 1914 — arguing in both cases that global uniformity did not simply flatten variety but destroyed some traditions, forced others into adaptation and hybridity, and let popular and vernacular-national forms flourish beneath an increasingly standardized elite culture.

## Themes

**Pre-1850 literary variety was structured by religion and genre, and its erosion is Bayly's own case of contested uniformity.** Before the mid-nineteenth century, the "dominant forms" of world literature — mythological epic, fable, romance, devotional poetry, the Sufi verse of West Asia, the kissas of the Punjab, the Ottoman "city thriller" — were each shaped by the "dominant religious and ideological systems of the societies for which they were written," and this constraint is what "gave world literature its variety." Bayly narrates the post-1850 turn to Western, novel-centered realism as a narrowing of this variety even as it opened new forms of expression — the same uniformity-heightens-difference dynamic he traces elsewhere for states and religions.

**The realist novel became a vehicle for national language-formation, in Europe and far beyond it.** Manzoni's *I promessi sposi* helped fix modern literary Italian; Bankim Chandra Chatterjee did comparable work for a modern Bengali "national sensibility"; Hindi novels of the 1870s–80s were "consciously being formed for the promotion of national integration"; and in Egypt "the emergence of modern Arabic was very much the creation of newspapers," used by nationalist writers like Salama Musa to popularize "a fluid, standardized language" while "claiming to represent a united nation." This is close to the territory [[benedict-anderson]] maps under print-capitalism and [[nationalism]] — though Bayly's emphasis falls on literary and linguistic craft (novelists and journalists self-consciously building a vernacular) rather than on the anonymous simultaneity Anderson stresses.

**Romantic poets aestheticized the nation as a community of sentiment fused to sacred landscape.** Goethe's late writing sought a language for the sublime that let contemporary political conflict appear "reflected in the grandeur of nature"; German romantic poets wrote epics "which lauded the nation as a community of sentiment," anchored to "hallowed terrain" and "sublime mountains and rivers"; Byron fused "restless romantic desire for personal freedom" with "a universal commitment to national freedom," dying at Missolonghi for Greek independence. See [[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]] and [[romanticism-and-political-danger]].

**Imperial conquest was also literary destruction, on a scale Bayly compares to the Spanish conquest of the Americas.** The "grand immolation" he describes — the libraries of Delhi, Jogjakarta, and Sulu destroyed, Napoleonic Egypt's manuscripts dispersed and sold, "the classics of imperial China... looted from Beijing," the oral traditions of "First Nations" erased alongside the peoples who carried them — sits in dark contrast to the chapter's account of literary convergence: uniformity here arrives partly by outright loss, not only by voluntary adaptation. Non-European elites nonetheless produced their own literature of the encounter from within existing genres — Boustany's chronicle of Napoleon, Ghulam Hussain Tabatabai's histories of "the Moderns," Urdu poets lamenting an age "when the face of the heavens was changed," Munshi Abdullah inventing a "new language of politics" in Malay — before full westernization of literary form.

**In the visual arts, the old guild-and-patronage machine was dismantled and a market machine installed in its place.** The "relatively stable, guild-like structure of late-eighteenth-century art and letters" and its "ecclesiastical, aristocratic, and royal patronage... had vanished" by 1914, replaced by "a large, impersonal and increasingly international market" — a shift in *function*, not just style, that Bayly renders in a strikingly violent image: "the decline of patronage, mechanization, and the drive for modernity in education had accomplished what great destroyers such as Genghis Khan and Tamberlaine had failed to do." This is a vivid instance of the [[world-machines]] intuition that legibility and standardization can dissolve deep cultural infrastructure more thoroughly than conquest ever did — though Bayly is describing an art-market transition, not proposing anything like a literal [[modernity-machine]], and the destroyed party here is non-European high art specifically (only Japan, and to a lesser degree China, "largely escaped this fate").

**Popular and hybrid forms proliferated beneath elite convergence rather than disappearing.** Rail travel diffused popular theater; magic lanterns and early cinema reached "country towns"; Chinese, Vietnamese, and Burmese touring opera groups told stories of past heroes while "subtly spreading opposition to colonial governments"; Welsh missionary hymns were "adapted to their new environment" in the Chin Hills of Burma; music hall thrived in Britain's new working-class districts; and cheap chapbooks about "Popular Arab detectives and Xhosa lovers" spread as widely as devotional literature once had. This bifurcation — elite reading matter "rapidly converging in style" while becoming "more distant from" and "less... influenced by" popular forms — has the shape (though not the capture-resistant stakes) of the [[divergence-machine]]'s picture of proliferation continuing beneath a legible surface.

**The closing "Prospect" section warns against a unilinear art history that reads the period only as a runway to modernism.** Bayly notes that "art-historical writing is particularly unilinear, because we know visually 'what happened'" — the outcome being Picasso, Gropius, and Joyce — but insists that around 1900 "there would still have been much to remind us of the old order": Tsars on horseback at the Paris exhibition, Lutyens still building country houses for landed gentry, peasants still buying block prints of "local deities and patrons." This caution against retrospective straight-line narrative rhymes with the [[history-machine]] critique of "great men" historiography, and the coexistence of dying and not-yet-dead cultural orders it describes has the same shape as a [[gramsci-gap]] — though Bayly's frame is art-historical continuity, not legitimacy crisis.

## Notable passages

The unit opens by naming the pre-1850 baseline of genre-bound, religiously constrained literary variety, then narrates that variety's erosion (c1). It turns sharply to the "grand immolation" of non-European literary heritage under early imperialism — libraries, manuscripts, and oral traditions destroyed alongside the peoples who held them (c2). A central passage traces how the realist novel, in Bengal and Egypt alike, became inseparable from the project of forging a standardized national language and "a united nation" (c3). In the arts conclusion, Bayly diagnoses the vanishing of the old guild-like patronage structure as a change of function opening space for experimental, political art (c4), then delivers the chapter's most arresting line: that patronage's decline and educational modernization did to non-European high art what Genghis Khan and Tamberlaine could not (c5). The closing "Prospect" passage resists a unilinear read of the period as mere prelude to Picasso and Joyce, insisting the old order — tsars, gentry, peasant devotional prints — was still visible in 1900 (c6).
