# Defining Native Peoples, the Age of Hiatus, and the Onset of the White Deluge

This unit is the opening of Chapter 12, "The Destruction of Native Peoples and Ecological Depredation." Bayly first defines "native peoples" as a residual, unstable category — pastoralists, nomads, forest-dwellers, and fisher-peoples without a well-defined state structure — and explains why the boundary between them and settled society was porous. He then narrates two phases: a long period before roughly 1820–1840 ("the Age of Hiatus") in which such peoples retained genuine military, economic, and diplomatic leverage over expanding states and empires, and the sharply worse period after 1840 ("the White Deluge") in which railway-, steamship-, and gold-driven mass settlement converted land hunger into systematic expropriation, disease, and extermination. The unit ends partway into this second phase, with the United States reservation policy and its demographic toll.

## Themes

- **"Native peoples" is a definitional residue, not a fixed ethnic category.** Bayly stresses that the label covers groups "without a well-defined state structure" and that its boundary was blurred by constant intermarriage and cultural interchange — Karen, Kachin, and Shan communities assimilating into Burmese peasant society, urbanized Fulani supplying dynasts and clerics to Hausa towns. He contrasts these porous cases with a handful of genuinely isolated peoples (Melanesia, the deep Amazon) for whom nineteenth-century "first contact" was traumatic in a different, more absolute way. He gives a rough demographic anchor: such peoples were perhaps 4–8 percent of the world's population in 1780.

- **The destruction was global and included Europe's own internal frontiers, not only colonial empire.** Bayly is careful to universalize the mechanism: the same drive toward uniformity that displaced the Cree or the Maori also extirpated the Cornish language, gravely damaged Gaelic, Irish, and Welsh, and nearly assimilated Occitan — running in the same years as the destruction of Cham Muslim culture in French Indochina and the decimation of Native Americans on the US east coast. The instruments he names are telling: "the imposition of European-derived definitions of absolute property rights" fencing off hunting and herding lands, and "scientific forestry" imposing uniformity on what had been variety in human-forest relations. He also insists this was not always deliberate policy — population growth and improved communications did as much work as any conscious "policy of standardization, extirpation, or assimilation."

- **Before c. 1820, native and tribal peoples had real leverage — the "Age of Hiatus."** Premodern and early-modern states lacked the reach to police their own frontiers ("a 'barbarian watch'"), so they recruited the very groups they distrusted: mobile cavalry, sappers, and elite corps drawn from tribal and nomadic populations. At the extreme, warrior-nomad groups became ruling dynasties in their own right — the Timurids in India, the Manchus in China, Bedouin Saudi rulers in Arabia, the Fulani founders of the Sokoto caliphate. Concrete instances of this hiatus include British gift-diplomacy with the Iroquois and Mohawk through the Indian Department, Khoikhoi militia service on the Cape frontier, Maori chiefs adapting entrepreneurially to European trade and warfare, Hawaiian chiefly fortunes built on the sandalwood trade, and Gurkha recruitment following the 1814–16 war with Nepal. Bayly also notes that people of the plains did not yet view tribal peoples as "primitive and savage in the way that they came to be viewed after about 1820" — a periodization claim that matters for how the unit's title should be read.

- **After 1840, land hunger converts hiatus into deluge.** The chapter's next section (the beginning of which this unit also covers) marks the "decisive change": emigration to the Americas rose nearly 40 percent in the 1840s, British settlement of Australia accelerated, and (later) Russian settlement of Siberia followed the same pattern after 1890. Bayly identifies "land hunger" — settlers wanting to fence and inherit land they could never have owned in Ireland, Poland, or Russia — as the decisive driver, working through disease, alcohol, dispossession of migratory and forest livelihoods, and outright violence (Tasmania, the Araucanians of Chile, southern Argentina, Florida). He closes the unit on the United States case: reservation policy following Andrew Jackson's earlier removals, and a 50 percent drop in the Native American population between 1850 and 1890.

- **World Machines resonance.** The instruments Bayly names for destroying native life-ways — absolute property title, scientific forestry, census-like reservation classification — are almost a textbook case of what WMP calls legibility: making land and people readable and orderable at the cost of the variety they replace. This resonates with [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and, more specifically, with [[legibility-vs-divergence-the-civilizational-tradeoff]], since the chapter is explicitly a story of divergent, non-state life-styles being extinguished by an expanding legible order. The resonance is only partial, though: Bayly's own explanation resists reducing this to a legibility project — he attributes as much causal weight to raw population growth and "improvements in communications" as to any deliberate "policy of standardization," which is a more contingent, less machine-like story than the WMP framing would suggest on its own.

## Notable passages

- **Defining native peoples.** The chapter's operative definition and its porousness: "Native peoples, as defined here, include groups without a well-defined state structure, pastoralists, nomads, forest-dwellers, and fisher-peoples" — a category whose edges are illustrated by assimilating Karen/Kachin/Shan peasants and isolated Melanesian and Amazonian societies for whom "first contact" was traumatic.

- **The "cultural terms of trade."** Bayly's summary judgment on why small cultural groups lost bargaining power once they were not central to peasant commodity production: the terms moved decisively in favor of settled, surplus-producing societies generally, white or not.

- **Warrior-nomads who became rulers.** A counter-example to simple victimhood: nomadic and tribal groups who, rather than being destroyed, seized state power outright — the Timurids, the Manchus, the Sokoto Fulani among them — illustrating that the pre-1820 relationship between "native" and settled power was two-directional.

- **The chapter's own periodization.** The passage that most directly justifies (and complicates) this unit's framing: societies were "penned in, ravaged, and subjected" gradually, in three explicit phases — first inroads 1780–1820, "the deluge" 1830–1890, and post-1870 pressure toward assimilation or reservation. This is Bayly's own scaffolding for the rest of the chapter.

- **Land hunger as the deluge's motor.** The extended passage on why settlers came and what their claim to "absolute property rights" in land did to migratory and forest-based livelihoods, ending on the disruption of the livestock movements native peoples depended on.

- **The US reservation outcome.** A hard demographic marker for the deluge's severity in one case: reservations and railroads together are credited with a 50 percent drop in the Native American population between 1850 and 1890.
