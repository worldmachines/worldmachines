# Critical Publics, Parallel Modernities, and the Limits of Europe's Lead

This unit reviews the "public sphere" historiography descending from Jurgen Habermas through the case of Benjamin Franklin's Atlantic associational culture, argues that parallel and largely independent "critical publics" were forming across eighteenth-century Islamic, Hindu-Sikh, Confucian, and Japanese societies, and then closes the chapter by explaining — deliberately without reverting to a "backwardness" narrative — why Europe nonetheless secured a real, structural, but only medium-term advantage, before a final "Prospect" section pivots toward the revolutionary, "information-rich state" that the next chapter will show exploding in scale after 1780.

## Themes

**Habermas's public sphere, tested against a longer and wider historiography.** Bayly opens by noting that Habermasian civil-society theory, though influential after 1970, was less novel than it seemed — British historians, he says, had already described the same phenomena "because they were allergic to social science theory... no one had really noticed what they were saying" — before asking whether the West's clubs-and-societies culture, exemplified by Franklin's *Autobiography*, can still ground the traditional started-in-the-West-and-spread-outward story of intellectual modernization once the frame becomes genuinely global.

**Franklin's associational Protestantism, and its exclusions.** Franklin's Junto, his dissenting-Protestant formation (via Puritanism, Deism, and Quaker Philadelphia), and his practical-improvement ethic are read as real evidence of an Atlantic civic republicanism nourished by the "cellular organization and individualistic codes of Protestant churches." But Bayly immediately qualifies this: the same associational world excluded women (beyond aristocratic salons), organizing workers, slaves, Native Americans (Franklin himself imagined drink clearing them away for "honest, industrious white settlers"), religious minorities, and, in colonial Calcutta, Indians kept "at arm's length" from European public space.

**Islamic, Sikh, Confucian, and Japanese "publics" as parallel, not derivative, modernities.** The chapter's central comparative move gathers Wahhabi purification in Arabia, Sufi restatement in Cairo and North Africa, West African Islamic state-building, Shah Wali-Allah's legal and educational reform in Mughal India, and Malay/Indonesian Islamic criticism of rulers into what Bayly explicitly names an "Islamic public sphere," long centered on debate in the mosque square — the *madina* — and only intensified, not invented, by eighteenth-century crisis (a domain long organized around the [[ulama]] and playing out under and against [[mughal-administration]], as with Aurangzeb's assertion of Sunni sovereignty without doctrinal policing). He extends the same logic to North Indian Sikhism, to Qing "practical evidential research" (Gu Yanwu's empirical, anti-metaphysical scholarship, patronized well beyond the official class), and to Tokugawa Japan's "Dutch learning" and samurai criticism of government measured against classical ideals. None of this, Bayly insists, is simply a response to "Western impact": these are societies "grappling with their own forms of modernity." The claim resonates, with real hedges, with the World Machines idea of the [[modernity-machine]] as multi-centered rather than singly authored, and with a [[history-machine]]-like picture of a paradigm assembling from independent, geographically scattered components rather than one "great man" or one civilization diffusing outward — though Bayly's frame is comparative historiography, not a theory of infrastructural paradigms, and his "publics" are explicitly religious idioms rather than the secular technical apparatus a [[legibility-as-machine-core]] reading would expect.

**A real but medium-term and contingent European advantage.** Against both Habermasian triumphalism and [[david-s-landes]]'s civilizational-superiority thesis ("the weakness of Landes's position lies in its reversion to old ideas of Asian stagnation"), Bayly argues the "time lag" has specific structural causes: Europe's fragmented, competitive terrain forced constant military-fiscal innovation, unlike the looser armies of large Asian and African empires, for whom "Asia's relative peace in the seventeenth century was its undoing"; legal codes bound rulers as well as subjects; and an unmatched density of print-and-associational civil society generated public information "at a massive rate" — a point that brushes against [[legibility-as-machine-core]]'s claim that information production at scale is a defining machine capacity, though Bayly locates the driver in civic sociability rather than state apparatus per se. He also credits the eighteenth-century "gentleman-scholar" networks of Europe — figures like [[giambattista-vico]] in Naples and, earlier, [[isaac-newton]] — with a "scientific egotism" that Asian and African scholars, working within Boards of Rituals or madrasa structures, lacked the same public arena to cultivate.

**Explicitly refusing the Victorian "backwardness" verdict.** Bayly closes by insisting each advantage is structural, not moral or civilizational: the Chinese state "guaranteed the welfare of its peasantry more effectively than western Europeans protected their proto-industrial cities," and Indians were largely spared Europe's wars of religion. He credits the Chinese social Darwinist Yan Fu with having understood the real asymmetry — aggression combined with well-defined states and vigorous critique — "rather better than his contemporaries among late-nineteenth-century European liberals." The Chinese case also touches, with hedges Bayly does not draw himself, on [[braudel-china-precocity-puzzle]]'s account of a coherent, supervising state producing technical and scholarly precocity that nonetheless stalled short of a European-style breakthrough — Braudel's puzzle is about technology and capitalism, Bayly's about public critique and associational density, but both trace the outcome to how tightly the state's coherence structured civil space.

**Bridging to "Prospect": the state, not yet industry, drives the next surge.** The coda insists Europe's post-1850 preeminence was still contested by non-European "economic expertise, political ethics, and sociability," and that the sharper widening of the gap between 1780 and 1820 came not from the industrial revolution — which "had not, as yet," kicked in — but from the sudden, gargantuan inflation of the "slowly emerging patriotic and information-rich state" into the revolutionary state and its conservative enemies, opening what Bayly calls an "axial age" for world history.

## Notable passages

The Franklin paragraph (c1) is where Bayly grants the West's associational culture its due: an "extraordinary proclivity to set up clubs and societies for argument and talk" tied to an "unspoken assumption" linking scientific theory to practical improvement, which he says is "more difficult to perceive in other great civilizations." The very next move (c2) undercuts any triumphalist reading of that same culture by cataloguing who the new associational world left out — women, workers, slaves, Native Americans, religious minorities, and colonial Indians alike.

The passage on Shah Wali-Allah and the "Islamic public sphere" (c3) is the chapter's clearest statement that critical, associational publics were not a Western export: debate "in the mosque square, the madina" long predates and runs parallel to the Atlantic clubs, and its eighteenth-century intensification is read as "a response to global change as profound as the rise of nationalism and the centralized state in Europe."

The warfare-and-terrain passage (c4) supplies Bayly's causal mechanism for the "time lag" — competitive, fragmented European geography forcing military-fiscal innovation that the large Asian and African empires, with their "relative peace," did not need. The closing verdict on Yan Fu (c5) is Bayly's explicit refusal of a moral-backwardness reading: Europe's edge was the specific, contingent "trick of combining... aggression with well-defined, medium-sized states, burgeoning commerce, and a culture of vigorous critique," not civilizational superiority. Finally, the "Prospect" passage (c6) is the chapter's hinge to what follows: the "patriotic and information-rich state" is about to be "inflated to a massive degree," not by industrialization but by the revolutionary crisis the next chapter takes up.
