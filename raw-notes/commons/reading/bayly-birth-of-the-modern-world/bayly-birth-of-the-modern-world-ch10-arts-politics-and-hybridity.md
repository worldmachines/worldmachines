# The Global Transformation of Art: Nation, Hybridity, and the Market

Opening Chapter 10, Bayly turns from states and religions to the arts, arguing that between the late eighteenth century and 1914 painting, architecture, literature, and music were simultaneously drawn into nation-building projects, subjected to a homogenizing international art market and museum system, and yet never reduced to passive reflections of politics or capital — hybridization and popular creativity persisted, and sometimes intensified, even as high courtly and sacral traditions collapsed across the world.

## Themes

**Art as an instrument of nation-building.** Bayly opens with artists and writers acting as "active agents" in constructing new polities: Mazzini's declaration that art without "Fatherland and Liberty" is prophecy but not Art itself, Wagner's music dramas as an embodiment of German-ness, Manzoni's fiction generalizing a Tuscan dialect into a national Italian language, Victor Hugo's quasi-Napoleonic state funeral, and Tagore's elevation into a national icon of India. This resonates closely with [[imagined-communities]] — Manzoni manufacturing a shared national language through the novel is close to Anderson's print-capitalism thesis, though Bayly frames it as the deliberate, self-conscious project of individual literary nationalists rather than Anderson's anonymous, technologically-driven process. The pattern also names the mechanism [[nationalism]] describes: art supplying the affective and symbolic content that a legal-political nation-state alone cannot generate.

**Art history itself as a retrospective nationalist and bourgeois project.** Burckhardt's invention of academic Renaissance art history did not simply recover the fifteenth century; in celebrating figures like Cellini and Alberti it also projected nineteenth-century bourgeois individualism backward, creating a usable past. Bayly notes the same dynamic among non-Western scholars who, in dialogue with European and American scholars, began to present their own art and literature as embodiments of "national genius" — canon-formation everywhere in the service of present identity claims, not neutral historical recovery.

**Contested uniformity: destruction and hybridization, not extinction.** Before 1750 most societies had distinctive, coherent artistic traditions; by 1900 these had been transformed at a deep level and pronounced dead by observers like Coomaraswamy. Bayly insists this diagnosis was "probably exaggerated": non-European arts persisted by borrowing and appropriating European techniques into hybrid forms, while the traffic ran both ways — Whistler's Kangxi-style hawthorn jars, Gauguin's Polynesian color sense, and Picasso's African-influenced eclecticism show uniformizing pressure generating new variety rather than simple homogenization. The shape of this argument — a compressing, uniformizing force whose byproduct is excess local variety rather than convergence — rhymes with [[legibility-vs-divergence-the-civilizational-tradeoff]], though Bayly's domain is aesthetic representation and cultural meaning rather than Rao's institutional/political legibility machinery; the resonance is structural, not a claim that Bayly is describing the same mechanism.

**The worldwide dismantling of courtly and sacral art.** Bayly catalogues cases across continents: the Qing imperial porcelain industry and Summer Palace destroyed and looted (1860, 1900), the Benin bronzes taken to the British Museum after the 1897 punitive expedition, the Burmese royal regalia removed to London in 1886, the Ottoman potteries of Iznik closing as the dervish orders and eunuchs were dispersed after the Tanzimat reforms, and Napoleon's stripping of Venice's art treasures. This was as much a story of conquest, revolution, and looting as of gradual market displacement — and it reached into Europe's own ancien régime, where craft guilds were dissolved as "reactionary."

**Commodification and the "demystification" of art.** Invoking Weber, Bayly traces how art across civilizations — Sufi miniatures, Yoruba carving, Persian carpets, Russian icons, Persian and Indian devotional objects — lost its function as religious or totemic practice and became commodity production for lay and expatriate buyers. The irony he flags is double: religion's expansion as formal doctrine (chapter 9) came paired with its withdrawal from art, and the state and market themselves acquired a quasi-religious aura (a patriotic statue of Victor Emmanuel II inspiring "fervour" without claiming to save the soul). This half-rhymes with [[authenticity-and-commodification]]'s question of whether a form can retain non-instrumental value once market logic captures its production — that note concerns modern narrative and the attention economy rather than nineteenth-century devotional objects, but the underlying structural question (does commodification hollow out the thing it commodifies, or merely relocate it) is the same one.

**Mechanization's uneven, Janus-faced effects.** Industrial production destroyed some traditions (Paisley's mass-produced imitation of Indian shawls undercut South Asian artisan textiles) while reviving or sustaining others (Scandinavian woodcraft, the British Arts and Crafts movement). Ruskin's anti-industrial ideology depended on industrially-produced dyes and glass and found its market among the new middle classes that industrialization itself had created — a movement built from the very forces it opposed.

**The quotidian turn and the museum as art's new institutional home.** Painting, opera, and the novel increasingly took modern everyday life as subject (Toulouse-Lautrec's cafe society, Hindi novelists' middle-class protagonists) rather than saints, kings, and myth. Meanwhile the museum — the Louvre, the Metropolitan, and colonial museums in Hanoi, Batavia, Bombay, and Cairo — became the institutional site where displaced and looted "high" art was recollected and classified; Bayly is careful to note "museumization" was not wholly destructive, sometimes stimulating new local craft (Khmer Buddha imitation in French Indochina).

**Bayly's own hedges.** He explicitly warns against reducing all art to political or social reflection: classical revival after 1789 partly grew from the Pompeii excavations rather than politics alone, and early abstraction and atonality represented a genuine liberation of the artistic imagination. He also insists uniformity did not mean declining creativity — popular art and craft survived poverty and mechanization, and the era closed with new globally hybrid popular forms spreading with striking speed: art photography, jazz, and cinema developing almost simultaneously in Paris, Cairo, and Shanghai.

## Notable passages

The Mazzini/Wagner/Manzoni passage (c1) is the clearest statement of art's constitutive role in nation-building, naming the specific mechanism — a manufactured "national language" — that gives the theme its bite. The Coomaraswamy passage (c2) is the pivot of the hybridity argument: Bayly names a contemporary prediction of cultural death in order to overturn it, and the following passage on Whistler, Gauguin, and Picasso (c3) supplies the counter-evidence that uniformizing pressure ran in both directions rather than simply erasing non-European traditions. The catalogue of Benin, Burma, and Ottoman losses (c4) grounds the abstract claim of "decline of high art" in specific, violent episodes of conquest and looting rather than gradual diffusion. The Weber-inflected passage on demystification (c5) carries the chapter's account of art's shift from devotional to commodity production across religious traditions. Finally, the Arts and Crafts Movement passage (c6) is the clearest single statement of mechanization's double effect — a movement that opposed industrialism while depending on it.
