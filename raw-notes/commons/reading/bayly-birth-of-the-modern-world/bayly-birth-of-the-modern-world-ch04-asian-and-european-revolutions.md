# Converging Wars of Legitimacy: Asia's Mid-Century Rebellions and Europe's 1848 Revolutions

Bayly threads together a decade-and-a-half sequence of state-legitimacy crises — Ottoman internal coups and defeat in the Greek war of independence, the Java war of 1825–30, the Persian Babi revolt of 1848, the Taiping Rebellion of 1851–64, and the Indian Rebellion of 1857–9 — as a single escalating "world crisis," then turns to Europe's 1848 revolutions, arguing that the older Marxist reading of 1848 as urban proletarian class war has been displaced by a fuller picture of agrarian jacquerie, artisan crisis, and religious/ethnic conflict. The unit closes by insisting that the Asian and European mid-century convulsions were not merely structurally comparable but directly linked by chains of imperial causation.

## Themes

**Asia's wars of legitimacy form one escalating sequence, not isolated national crises.** Ottoman coups of the 1820s–30s, the humiliating 1827–31 defeat in the Greek war, the Muslim kingdoms of central Java's failed 1825–30 war of liberation against the Dutch, the Persian Babi uprising of 1848, and above all the Taiping Rebellion (from 1851) are presented as accelerating instances of the same phenomenon: old orders whose sacral and administrative legitimacy has been hollowed out by internal renovation and external pressure, with no new settlement yet able to take its place — an interregnum the unit's own language of "wars of legitimacy" makes explicit, and which resonates with [[gramsci-gap]] (the gap between a dying legitimacy and one not yet born), though Bayly's gap is filled with millenarian and dynastic claimants rather than the ideological "morbid symptoms" the term usually names.

**State modernization itself manufactures legitimacy crises.** Russian pressure pushed the Ottomans and Qajar Iran toward "new bureaucracies, more transparent taxation systems, modern armies" — legibility-building reforms undertaken to face down the Western threat — but these very reforms "raised issues of legitimate rule": if the sultan and the Persian padishah were no longer straightforwardly Muslim kings, who would protect the faith? This is a clean case of a state's own legibility project destabilizing the religious-charismatic ground it stood on; it touches [[legibility-as-machine-core]], though Bayly's frame is protector-of-the-faith kingship rather than machine-scale infrastructure as such.

**The Taiping Rebellion as syncretic mass movement with continental consequences.** Hong Xiuquan's movement fused Christian and Buddhist millenarianism, briefly redistributed land and emancipated women, captured Nanjing, and was read by contemporaries as a conventional dynastic-succession revolt (Hong claiming to restore the Ming) before it was crushed — at a cost the unit puts at an estimated 20 million lives. Its knock-on effects were geopolitical, not just Chinese: it drew Britain and France into renewed rivalry in East Asia, gave Russia an opening to expand in the northwest, spilled into Vietnam (prompting French intervention in Cochin China), and left Burma starved of finance and vulnerable to British annexation.

**Three causal threads run under all the Asian revolts, and Bayly resists over-crediting the West.** He names them explicitly: reaction to Western colonial expansion and its new government/commercial forms; internal ethnic-religious tension deepened by new ideologies, especially Christianity and its indigenous forms; and population growth and local economic imbalance only indirectly related to the world system. He is emphatic that the latter threads are not simply colonial effects — most of China's population growth predated 1700, and most agents of Christianity in Asia were themselves indigenous, not European missionaries — so, in his words, the role of European influence in these wars of legitimacy "should not be exaggerated."

**India's 1857 rebellion as a multi-stranded revolt organized around a hollow symbol.** What began as a sepoy mutiny over pay and status widened into peasant risings, dispossessed-ruler revolts, and urban artisan unrest, with the now-powerless Mughal emperor, confined by the British to Delhi, serving as the rebellion's symbolic center despite having no administrative power of his own — the endpoint of the long decline this corpus tracks under [[mughal-administration]]. Bayly stresses the revolt was neither cleanly "Hindu" nor "Muslim" but united by hostility to Christians as a privileged, alien faction, and by British post-1848 impatience with the "self-sufficiency" of Indian courts.

**1848 in Europe: from Marxist "wars of progress" to agrarian jacquerie and artisan crisis.** Historiography before "about 1980" read 1848 as proletarian class conflict prefiguring 1917; Bayly reports the revisionist picture instead — forest-rights invasions and common-land reoccupations against landlords, artisans and putting-out workers (not factory hands) resisting the erosion of guild control, and widespread anti-Semitic violence as Jewish moneylenders became scapegoats for a spreading petty-capitalist economy. This is a rural and religious substrate the classic account had suppressed, though Bayly stops short of erasing the distinctively self-conscious, rights-of-man political language of the urban liberal-nationalist leadership (Italy, Germany, Hungary, Poland), abetted by a burgeoning press and associational culture — a nationalism built through print and club life that half-echoes [[benedict-anderson]]'s account of imagined community, though Bayly's Asian comparanda explicitly lack this apparatus and run instead on "commitment to community and homeland" without it.

**Direct causal chains bind Asia and Europe, not just comparison.** Beyond parallel structures (bania moneylenders, Jewish moneylenders, and Taiping-era Chinese gentry moneylenders as identically-placed scapegoats; the image of weavers' "bones... bleaching" recycled from India to describe Europe's master weavers), Bayly traces literal chains of consequence: the Franco-Russian dispute over Palestine's holy places triggered the Crimean War, which forced Bengal Army reforms that detonated the 1857 Mutiny, which in turn fed Irish radicalism, a new Anglo-racialism, and — via Taiping-era Anglo-French rivalry — the consolidation of British New Zealand. This is the unit's strongest resonance with [[world-machines]]-style crisis contagion, though the mechanism here is diplomatic and military causation across empires rather than a shared infrastructural machine.

**Britain and its settler empire are a partial, not total, exception.** Chartism looked back to eighteenth-century demands against corruption more than forward to class-based socialism, and England avoided 1848-style revolution — but Jamaica, Canada, Australia, South Africa, and above all famine Ireland show the wider British world was not immune to comparable strain; Ireland, tellingly, followed the pattern of India's 1830s famines rather than the violent pattern of 1857.

## Notable passages

The opening survey (**c1**) frames the whole Asian sequence — Ottoman coups, the Java war, the Babi revolt, the Taiping Rebellion — as "forces of dissolution and reconstruction" producing not just political rupture but collective moral crises among people whose "cherished icons" had been uprooted.

Bayly's tripartite causal model (**c2**) — colonial reaction, ideological/religious tension, and population pressure "only very indirectly related to the world system" — is the analytical spine the rest of the unit fills in with cases.

The historiographical pivot on 1848 (**c3**) marks Bayly's revisionist target directly: the older reading of the revolutions as looking forward to 1917, which he replaces with a picture reaching back to "religious riots and peasant jacqueries."

The forest-invasion passage (**c4**) supplies the concrete texture of that revision — peasants breaking landlords' enclosure of once-common woodland — grounding the abstract claim of "agrarian unrest" in a specific repertoire of resistance.

The weavers passage (**c5**) is the unit's most compressed comparative image: E. P. Thompson's "moral economy of the crowd" applied across India and central/eastern Europe in a single sentence, binding two artisan crises usually treated in separate historiographies.

The closing causal-chain passage (**c6**) is the unit's thesis statement in miniature: mid-century Asian and European crises were directly connected by chains of causation, with the Crimean War as the hinge linking a Palestine holy-places dispute to the Indian Mutiny.
