# The Uneven Progress—and Absence—of Global Industrialization

This opening unit of Bayly's chapter 5 first surveys how contemporaries and later historians have understood industrialization and the rise of the modern city, then traces why industrialization took hold rapidly in Britain, the United States, Germany, Belgium, France, and (later) Japan, while remaining marginal in the Ottoman Empire, China, India, Russia, and Latin America; it closes by arguing that this global gulf reflected differences in state capacity, capital markets, and external trade regimes far more than any fixed technical or civilizational aptitude for industry.

## Themes

**Historians have steadily downgraded industrialization's explanatory weight.** Contemporaries such as Marx and Engels, Durkheim, and the new statisticians of France, Britain, and Germany treated industrialization and the metropolis as the defining fact of their age. Later economic historians reversed this: proto-industrialization (Mendels), "industrious revolutions" (de Vries), "gentlemanly capitalism" (Cain and Hopkins, who locate Britain's dominant economic feature in finance rather than manufacturing), and O'Brien's calculation of a sedate ~1.5 percent annual GDP growth 1780–1861 together demoted the "Industrial Revolution" to a mere "industrial evolution." Political history followed suit: 1848 and the Paris Commune are now read as artisan rather than proletarian revolts, 1905 as a peasant-and-soldier affair, and Russian, Chinese, and Indian industrial workers as "peasants in disguise."

**Bayly pushes back: the change was real, if slow to reveal itself.** By the late nineteenth century, industrial output was a substantial share of GNP in the dominant powers, and its effects rippled far past the small industrial workforce—reshaping export agriculture (palm oil and cocoa from West Africa, cotton from Egypt and India, "protein chains" of meat from Australasia and the Americas) and tying non-industrial port cities from Naples to Shanghai to Buenos Aires into a single industrial world system. The resulting gulf in living standards between rich and poor world regions, already opening in 1780–1820, became "enormous" by 1880.

**No single national recipe—multi-centered, contingent origins.** Britain's lead (44 percent of its labor force in industry by 1881, 45 percent of world industrial output in 1840) is explained by competing theories—technology, scarce wartime labor, Braudel's thesis that marginality pushed Britain outward, and Parthasarathi's argument that British industrialization was itself a defensive response to cheaper, high-quality Indian and French artisan textiles. Bayly synthesizes these around the wars of 1793–1815 acting on a pre-existing base of commercialized agriculture and a flexible capital market dating to the 1680s. The United States diverged early toward manager-run, stockholder-owned firms and Ford's 1896 production line. Germany, Austria, Italy, and Russia are usually read as state-driven, but Bayly blunts the state/market contrast—Britain's own imperial state (the Royal Navy protecting the Atlantic slave economy) shaped markets just as surely. Belgium industrialized first on the continent precisely because its weak, insecure state needed the revenue; France's push in the 1850s was openly state-championed, as Louis Napoleon's own rhetoric shows; Russia's industrialization stayed slow, foreign-dependent, and rural, a weakness Bayly links to its defeats in the Crimean War and by Japan in 1905.

**Absence was manufactured, not innate.** Outside the industrial core, being an agricultural or artisan producer was not itself a sentence to poverty (Argentina and parts of Brazil did well); poverty resulted from a *combination* of agricultural export dependence with a trade regime that blocked local protection, expatriate ownership of banks and shipping that exported the value added, and internal constraints like population growth and poor education. Mexico's post-independence textile industry stalled for lack of tariff protection and a free-market ideology among its own leaders, helping set the stage for the 1911 revolution. Britain's own championing of global free trade, backed by great-power tariff impositions such as the Ottoman Tariff Convention of 1838, aborted indigenous industrial efforts from Muhammad Ali's Egypt to the Ottoman provinces; capitulations added a legal handicap on top of a financial one.

**China and Japan as a paired test of diffusion versus endowment.** China, arguably stronger than the Ottoman Empire at the outset (Pomeranz), saw gentry and Qing resistance to industrialization rooted not in "Confucian" conservatism but in a rational anxiety about losing judicial and moral authority over subjects as foreign technology and management entered; Li Hongzhang's self-strengthening arsenals and shipping company show technology was embraced once that control seemed assured, but foreign loans, foreign-collected customs revenue, and the treaty ports starved the state of tariff autonomy. Japan, by contrast, combined internal endowments (a skilled, disciplined labor force, adaptable merchant houses, Tokugawa-era missions abroad) with a comparatively lenient external regime (no full imperialist assault after Perry) and a state that could act where private "animal instincts" were still too fragile—the Meiji government financed shipyards, munitions, and railways from a "development fund" confiscated from the samurai. Later luck (buoyant silk demand, the Chinese indemnity, victory over Russia in 1905) compounded these advantages. Bayly's methodological moral: neither pure diffusionism nor pure endogenous-endowment explanation alone accounts for the divergence.

**Colonial industry survived at the margins, and the artisan/factory binary is "threadbare."** Despite British, Dutch, and French tariff policies designed to shield metropolitan producers, colonial India still grew ordnance and steel production near Calcutta and textile production in Bombay and Ahmedabad, aided by merchant trust networks—though this did little to dent mass rural poverty. The chapter's closing thesis qualifies colonial nationalists' claim that Western imports simply devastated indigenous artisan industry: artisan production instead adapted, as in Lyon's and Japan's silk industries, undercutting any clean "backward"/"advanced" distinction. Bayly's punchline is that the era's vast living-standard gap owed more to external regimes of money and military power than to differences in technical progress, and that the industrial "life-style" served the wealthy's desire to control the poor as much as it served economic efficiency.

## World Machines resonances

The chapter narrates, in miniature, the Dawn-era construction of the [[modernity-machine]]: factories, capital markets, tariff regimes, and above all the "new science of statistics" built "to monitor the number and health of burgeoning urban populations" are exactly the legibility instruments named in [[legibility-as-machine-core]]—though Bayly's insistence on multiple, contingent, state-specific paths (Belgium's weak state, Meiji Japan's samurai fund, Qing anxieties over authority) cuts against any single teleological "prime mover" story the Modernity Machine narrative might otherwise imply, and rhymes more with [[divergence-machine]]'s picture of independent local forces producing radically non-convergent outcomes—even though Bayly's divergence here precedes and interacts with, rather than follows, legibility-building. The global network of cities (Naples to Buenos Aires) tied into an "industrial world system" echoes [[braudel-world-economy]]'s core/periphery hierarchy, with industrial capacity substituting for Braudel's urban/trade hierarchy as the sorting principle. [[fernand-braudel]] and [[adam-smith]] are both named directly in the text as historiographical touchstones for, respectively, Britain's marginal position and the era's first analytic vocabulary of specialization and economies of scale.
