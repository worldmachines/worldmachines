# Pilgrimage, Print, and Building: The Global Standardization of Religion

This unit (the closing stretch of Bayly's chapter on nineteenth-century religion) argues that three infrastructures — pilgrimage networks, the printing press, and sacred architecture — converted the world's major religions from loosely bounded, locally variable traditions into standardized, textually authorized, globally self-aware "world religions" between 1780 and 1914, and shows how this new uniformity became entangled with rising nationalism even as heterodox, syncretic, and post-Christian religious life persisted and even multiplied at the margins.

## Themes

**Pilgrimage turned into a mass, standardizing institution rather than a rare ordeal.** After the Wahhabi sack of Mecca and decades of piratical danger to sea routes, the restoration of Ottoman power, the pacification of Palestine and Syria, and above all steamships, railways, and the telegraph made the Hajj cheap and safe enough for "middle-class people" to undertake for the first time; Snouck Hurgronje's study of Indonesian pilgrims found a whole professional apparatus of "middlemen, preachers, doctors, and pilgrim-mentors" servicing the traffic. The same dynamic elevated Benares over rival Hindu pilgrimage centers and made Rome, Lourdes, Padua, Knock, and Marpingen sites the Catholic Church deliberately built up as popular counterweights to secularism and the Kulturkampf. Bayly's point is that improved communications served pious infrastructure as much as they served states and empires — pilgrimage both required and produced doctrinal uniformity, feeding, for instance, the pan-Islamic consciousness of the later century.

**Print did for religion what Benedict Anderson said it did for the nation.** Bayly explicitly borrows and extends [[benedict-anderson]]'s thesis that print created "imagined national communities," arguing the same mechanism standardized scripture and doctrine: vernacular Qurans in Urdu and Malay, the papal *Osservatore Romano*, the *Christian Science Journal*, and — in the strongest version of the claim — the assertion that Hindu and Buddhist "doctrine" was itself substantially a nineteenth-century print artifact, since print first "allowed Hinduism to be arrayed in textual form in one single bookcase." This is a clean instance of the World Machines idea of [[legibility-as-machine-core]]: a formerly diffuse, orally and locally transmitted tradition rendered legible, bounded, and citable by a new technology, though Bayly frames it as a religious rather than a state-legibility project.

**Sacred architecture converged on a small set of canonical, exportable styles.** The Arabian/Ottoman domed mosque displaced heterogeneous local wood-and-compound styles from West Africa to the Malay archipelago; a revived Gothic became the default idiom for new churches from English suburbs to French Indochina; Hindu temple-builders in India and the diaspora reached back to eleventh-to-thirteenth-century prototypes. Bayly is explicit that this was not simply a story of Western diffusion but of rival faiths borrowing a shared architectural grammar of legibility and dominance from one another — an uneven, multi-centered convergence that resonates (loosely) with the [[modernity-machine]]'s drive to render institutions recognizable and comparable across very different societies, even though the "machine" here is religious self-assertion rather than bureaucratic administration.

**Religion and nation were mutually constitutive, not rivals.** Pan-Slavism fused Orthodoxy with Russian national feeling; new national churches were carved out in Greece (1833) and Bulgaria (1870) as the Ottoman religious ecumene fragmented; Polish and Irish nationalism cast their nations' suffering in explicitly Christian, quasi-messianic terms; the 1879 Bosnian revolt united Muslims and Orthodox Christians against Catholic Austria; and religious anxiety shaped nationalist mobilization in Egypt and Muslim India. This is a direct instance of the [[nationalism]] literature's point that elite secular rhetoric (liberalism, economic justice) coexisted with a popular register in which religion and nationhood were "more closely paired" — Bayly insists religion could as easily fragment a national movement as unify it.

**Uniformity was real but never total — Bayly's "contested uniformity" in miniature.** Against Max Weber's "demystification" thesis, Bayly argues rationalization reached only "the small international middle class"; huge regions (Amazonia, inner Asia, Papua) remained barely touched by any world religion, and the same print-and-travel infrastructure that standardized orthodoxy also let heterodoxy travel and multiply — Babi-Bahai universalism, the Ahmadiyya, theosophy, Kardek-style spiritualism, and messianic revolts (Taiping-adjacent millenarian Buddhism, Boer Great Trek Christian messianism) all spread through print and pilgrimage-like circuits even as they defined themselves against or through the newly standardized "great" religions. This is the book's recurring paradox that global uniformity intensifies rather than dissolves difference; it only half-maps onto the World Machines framing of legibility-versus-divergence, since Bayly's heterodoxies are not resisting legibility so much as adopting its idiom for their own dissenting purposes.

## Notable passages

The Hajj-infrastructure passage (steamships, reduced fares, new middle-class pilgrims) is the unit's clearest empirical demonstration that "archaic" religious travel was retooled by nineteenth-century transport technology into something closer to mass tourism. The Anderson passage is load-bearing because it names Bayly's own analytical debt and extends it explicitly from nation to religion. The Hinduism-as-print-artifact passage is the sharpest, most counterintuitive formulation of the standardization thesis — doctrine itself as an effect of the medium. The religious-building passage is Bayly's own summary statement ("a central concern of this book") tying architecture to the uniformity theme, and is explicit that the West was not the sole center of diffusion. The nationalism passage states plainly that popular nationalism ran on religious solidarity even when elite rhetoric was secular. The closing passage frames the chapter's net verdict: standardizing world religions reached far more people by 1914 than in 1789, but heterodox movements persisted by defining themselves against that same standard, rather than by escaping the world-religious frame altogether.
