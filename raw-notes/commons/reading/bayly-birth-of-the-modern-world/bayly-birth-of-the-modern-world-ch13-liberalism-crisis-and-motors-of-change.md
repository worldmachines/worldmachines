# The Death of Liberalism and the Conclusion: Motors of Change, Power, and Hybrid Resistance

This unit is the concluding chapter of *The Birth of the Modern World*. It opens by tracing a "strange death" of nineteenth-century liberalism across three overlapping worlds in the two decades before 1914 — Western parliamentary states, Europe's authoritarian fringe, and the colonial and semi-colonial world — while insisting this did not make the First World War inevitable. It then turns to the book's own historiographical stock-taking: why global history rose as a discipline, why Bayly rejects both Western exceptionalism and full relativism, why modernity's origins were multi-centred rather than diffused outward from Europe, what actually "moved" the long nineteenth century once no single prime mover will do, and how power operated through co-opted networks that nonetheless produced hybrid ideologies unsettling Western dominance well before 1914.

## Themes

**Liberalism's crisis was simultaneous but not identical across three worlds.** In Western democracies, older upper-class liberalism was "ground to pieces" between urban conservatism and Labour in Britain, split by the Dreyfus case and Jaurès's left in France, and recast as Progressivism in the United States — without repudiating representative government itself. On Europe's fringe the stakes were higher: Karl Lueger's Vienna, the failed but polarizing 1905 Russian Revolution, Milan's shopkeepers turning right, and Mexico's violent agrarian revolution. Outside the West, liberalism retreated into something starker: the Boxer Rebellion, a Japanese conservative turn to "pure" indigenous values, and figures like Aurobindo Ghose, Anagarika Dharmapala, and the pan-Islamists rejecting "liberal [[nationalism]]" for claims staked "on the word of God and the promptings of the blood." Running alongside this, a distinct wave of rights movements — Pan-African Congresses, anti-indentured-labor agitation, Maori associations, and women's public emergence (Bernhardt, Curie) — shows the same decades producing radical universalist claims as well as revivalist particularism. Bayly flags the resulting fork — "the struggle between radical socialism and ethnic and religious revivalism for the allegiance of the masses" — as a hinge for the rest of the twentieth century.

**Contingency, not inevitability, on the road to 1914.** Bayly is careful to resist reading this crisis teleologically: the Ottomans, the Qajars, and the Romanovs might all have "bought off" enough of their subjects to survive; Irish home rule, Indian and Egyptian "extremism," and Balkan/Iranian nationality disputes all looked temporarily manageable in 1912–13. What actually did the damage was the *rapidity* of change outrunning governments' capacity to adjust, compounded by "a series of massive miscalculations." Lenin's diagnosis of the "great acceleration" as rooted in uneven capitalist development is given qualified credit, folded into a wider picture of Anglo-German and Russo-German rivalry radiating outward from a core (Britain, France, Germany, the US) to unstable "outer reaches" (Russia, the Balkans, the Ottoman Empire).

**Against both exceptionalism and relativism: the case for global history.** Bayly recaps why world/global history rose institutionally — the retreat of Western-civilization survey courses, imperial history's growing anachronism, and scholars like Prasenjit Duara working to "rescue history from the nation" — while insisting the genre is a heuristic for cross-checking regional narratives, not a device for dissolving all difference. He affirms that northwestern Europe really was more "dynamic" by 1780, but locates that dynamism in a public "buoyancy" of debate, invention, and (bluntly) killing efficiency that only makes sense set against comparably vigorous Asian and African public debate and commercial acumen — not as free-standing superiority. American exceptionalism gets the same treatment: real, but only legible comparatively against Britain and its settler dominions.

**Multi-centred origins, no prime mover.** The chapter recaps the book's throughline: the first tremors of the "age of revolutions" registering in Mughal India and Safavid Iran before France; Caribbean slave revolts sustaining the European revolutionary cycle by draining British resources; Indian textile competition as a trigger of British industrialization; and a genuinely global history of ideas refusing to sort Benjamin Franklin as secular-modern and Shah Wali-Allah as merely a "religious revivalist." The Taiping Rebellion and the American Civil War are treated as world-historical events on the same scale as 1848. The "new imperialism" is reframed as conjunctural rather than a simple projection of European strength: against Robinson and Gallagher, Bayly argues that the growing "sophistication and coherence" of non-European resistance movements was as much a *cause* of imperial annexation as a response to it — periphery galvanizing center, not the reverse.

**The actual motors: industrious revolutions, domestication, seaborne commerce, and the "second military revolution."** Before industrialization proper (post-1850), Bayly names three deeper drivers: the rise and decline of "industrious revolutions" (disciplined daily life, timekeeping, and a global wave of ascetic "methodism" — Pietism, neo-Catholicism, Krio Anglicanism, Chinese New Sect Buddhism, the Faraizi movement — that built demand for order, drainage, and land rights from below); the "great domestication" of the world to peasant production; and expanding seaborne commerce linking these discrete developments across oceans. Layered on top, autonomous state power — Nadir Shah, the Napoleons, the Zulu kings, King George of Tonga, and Africa's post-1870 conquest-states — is traced to the pressures of a "second military revolution," where the cost of arms and manpower ran ahead of what states could fund, producing the state's "elephantiasis" both after 1780 and again before 1914.

**Power as parasitic networking, not collaboration — and the hybrids that leaked out of it.** Against both reductive world-systems economics and postcolonial cultural dualism (West vs. non-West), Bayly's account of dominance is networked co-optation under subordination: Afghan cavalry served the East India Company and Chinese merchants worked under Dutch protection not from approval but because incorporation into commerce, silver, and protection was their only viable option. Yet the same patronage networks, plus metropolitan political and religious rifts (Catholic-republican France, Liberal-Conservative Britain), leaked skills and rhetorical openings to colonized intellectuals. The result was potent hybrids — Jamal al-din al-Afghani and Muhammad Abduh's pan-Islamism, [[mahatma-gandhi]]'s fusion of Ruskin's aesthetics and Tolstoy's communitarianism with swadeshi, Irish nationalists recruiting Indian and Egyptian allies, and Japan exploiting Anglo-Russian rivalry under "Asia for the Asians" — that "began to subvert European dominance" well before the formal collapse of empire.

## Notable passages

The chapter's grimmest single sentence names the stakes of the "strange death": that "the liberal civilization born of the compromise between revolution and hierarchy in 1815 ... had failed" in the eyes of most political activists, a verdict Bayly measures against critics ranging from Zola and Tolstoy to Gandhi and Aurobindo Ghose, who condemned it not as a perversion of man but "an affront to God." The colonial-world passage on Ghose, Dharmapala, and the pan-Islamists sharpens this: liberal nationalism gave way to claims staked on "the word of God and the promptings of the blood," a register no Western liberal vocabulary could absorb. The chapter's methodological core states flatly that it is "of little use to separate out capital, the state, or rationalistic ideology as the 'prime mover'" and instead names industrious revolutions, "great domestication," and seaborne commerce as the deeper motors before 1850 — a passage that resonates, with real qualification, with World Machines' resistance to naming a single machine-scale driver; see below. The power section's key methodological move — "not so much a theory of collaboration, as one of subordination" — is illustrated concretely through Afghan cavalry and Chinese merchants incorporated into European commercial systems out of necessity rather than consent. Finally, the passage on hybrid ideologies (Afghani, Abduh, Gandhi) shows how the same co-optive networks that built Western dominance also transmitted the intellectual materials of its undoing.

## World Machines resonances

Several moments in this chapter rhyme with WMP concepts, though Bayly is working a historian's causal vocabulary, not a machine-theoretic one:

- The chapter's account of a dying liberal order — "born of the compromise between revolution and hierarchy in 1815" — losing legitimacy across the West and the colonial world simultaneously, with "morbid" reactions (populist right-turns, terrorism, revivalist violence) filling the gap before anything stable replaces it, resonates with [[gramsci-gap]]. The fit is partial: Bayly's interregnum is a specific fin-de-siècle political-economic crisis of one liberal order, not the civilizational-scale Modernity-to-Divergence transition WMP's framework describes.
- The "Summing Up" section's account of world history as a discipline emerging not from a single innovator but from the silent convergence of independent pressures — multicultural university reform, imperial history's growing anachronism, regional historians reinventing themselves as international ones — until it "lurched into visibility" as a field, is close to Lohse's [[history-machine]]: a paradigm assembling distributedly before becoming legible as such. Bayly is narrating his own field's formation, which is a good but not exact match for the concept's usual (institutional/technological) domain.
- Bayly's gloss on "the counting and categorizing project of the modern state" as one candidate driver of change touches [[legibility-as-machine-core]] — but he explicitly refuses to grant it primacy, treating it as one interacting strand among industrious revolutions, war-finance, and ideology rather than the machine's organizing core.
- The chapter's hybrids — Gandhi's Ruskin-Tolstoy-swadeshi synthesis, Afghani and Abduh's pan-Islamism, Irish-Indian-Egyptian cross-recruitment, Japan's "Asia for the Asians" — read as instances of [[Configurancy]]: unlike elements (colonizer doctrine, colonized tradition) co-producing something neither side anticipated or fully controlled. The resonance is with the *shape* of co-emergence rather than Rao's own framing, which is oriented to present and future agency rather than anti-colonial ideological synthesis.
