# Before the Nation-State: Frontier Polities, Archaic Globalization, and the Millenarian Prelude to 1780

Before turning to the emerging Atlantic-centered international order, Bayly surveys what power in the eighteenth-century world actually looked like away from the great agrarian regimes and the future nation-states: porous frontiers run by outsiders, maritime city-states, nomadic and forest polities, and stateless lineage societies built on entirely different logics of hierarchy. Against this decentered picture he then identifies the northwestern European and Atlantic-Caribbean commercial societies as "harbingers" of what comes next — advanced not in kind but in scale and specialization. The unit's second half reaches further back to sketch a genuine "prehistory of globalization": an "archaic globalization," older than any nation-state system, organized by universal kingship, cosmic religion, and humoral bodily practice, which early capitalist trade and a shared global idiom of caste were already beginning to absorb and redirect. It closes by tying a Christian-Muslim "millenarian conjuncture" to the Asian and French revolutionary energies that will drive the book's "world crisis" after 1780.

## Themes

**Sovereignty in 1780 was porous, personalized, and routinely run by outsiders.** Albanians and Romanians ruled the Ottoman lands and founded Egypt's new dynasty; an Armenian dynasty ruled in Iraq; Manchu emperors incorporated Mongol, Uighur, and Tibetan notables; Baltic Germans ruled in Russia and Hanoverian Germans in England. Bayly generalizes this through the Indian triad of deshis (locals), bideshis (foreigners), and pardeshis (familiar outsiders "from just over the fuzzy border") — pardeshi rule, he argues, was closer to the eighteenth-century global norm than any bounded, ethnically self-ruling polity.

**Maritime and stateless societies operated on logics the agrarian-empire lens misses entirely.** Ship-owning "sea-borne supremacies" (the Knights of St John, the Beys of Algiers, Bugis port-princes) taxed and controlled trade until the Royal Navy branded them pirates and destroyed them. Meanwhile much of Africa, native America, and the Pacific had no separate apparatus of "the state" at all: lineage heads mediated with the spirit world rather than exercising resource power, conflict ran along age-groups rather than class or ethnicity, and dependency looked more like servitude within a lineage than like plantation slavery.

**Nomadic and forest polities still met agrarian states on equal terms — the last generation of a much older cycle.** Manchurian herdsmen, Arabian camel tribes underwriting Wahhabi resistance, Zand and Qajar tribal dynasts in Persia, and forest-dwelling chieftain-marauders across India, Burma, and Siberia all dealt with settled states as near-equals. Bayly explicitly frames this as the closing round of the process the medieval historian [[ibn-khaldun]] described: tough frontier peoples periodically breaking in to revive and purge settled governments — a cycle 1780 was about to foreclose for good.

**Northwestern Europe's commercial societies were harbingers, distinguished by degree rather than by kind.** Bayly rejects strong exceptionalism: Dutch and English regional specialization, transnational Dutch finance, and the "flexible, financially sophisticated, consumer-oriented, technologically innovative" slave-plantation complex all surpassed comparable commercialization in the Yangzi valley, Bengal, or Istanbul's hinterland in scale and style, not in some categorical difference — and even these advanced economies coexisted with patchwork jurisdictions (Holland's status anachronisms, Germany's principalities) rather than uniform sovereign states.

**"Archaic globalization" ran on three engines: universal kingship, cosmic religion, and humoral bodily practice.** Kings prized "men from afar" and honorific goods (Kashmiri shawls, Chinese silks, Arab horses) as tools of legitimacy; the Alexander legend and Aristotelian ethics circulated as shared moral currency from Christendom to the Indo-Islamic world; pilgrimage (to Jerusalem, Rome, Mecca) and Sufi expansion moved people across vast distances for reasons of faith; and a shared logic of humoral medicine drove the movement of spices, precious substances, and eventually tea, tobacco, and opium (first medicines, later mass commodities). Bayly's summary contrast is sharp: archaic globalization strategically consumed diversity, where the modern world's globalization is market-driven uniformity.

**Proto-capitalist globalization did not replace archaic globalization so much as feed on it.** Borrowing Appadurai's word, Bayly describes the new chartered-company trade in tea, tobacco, opium, and slaves as "cannibalizing" the older kingship-religion-body networks — for instance turning slave capture, once a strategy for building the archaic African or Ottoman household, into a brutal proto-capitalist industry. That image of a new order assembling itself out of redirected, pre-existing components rather than displacing them outright is a loose but genuine rhyme with [[history-machine]]'s account of a paradigm accreting silently from independent parts before it becomes visible — though Bayly's builders are chartered companies and slavers, not a silent historiographic process, and the parts being cannibalized are moral-religious rather than purely institutional.

**Caste — in the original Portuguese sense — was the era's shared grid of embodied status, prior to race or nation.** Neither race nor nationality "as understood at the end of the nineteenth century" yet dominated; instead an interlocking, purity-and-lineage-based ranking (crystallized in the Mexican pedigree manual Las Castas Mexicanas) proved portable enough that Indian, Islamic, and Chinese observers could each map their own status idioms onto it. This bio-moral classification of whole populations, later to be displaced by statistical race and nationality categories, reads as a rough forerunner-in-miniature of the classificatory ambitions [[legibility-as-machine-core]] names — though it works through honor, blood, and purity rather than census and statistics, and Bayly is careful to keep it a "flexible and indistinct" idiom, not yet a state technology.

**A shared millenarian anxiety, not only material shock, primed the post-1780 "world crisis."** Following Subrahmanyam and Aubin, Bayly traces a sixteenth-century "millenarian conjuncture" in which Christian anxiety over the first Christian millennium-and-a-half and Muslim anticipation of the Prophetic millennium reinforced each other; he argues an analogous convergence recurred after 1720, when Buddhist, Muslim, and Sikh millennial currents met the secular millenarianism of the French Revolution — this time inside larger, more embattled states and empires. The porous, decentered political world described earlier in the unit is exactly the medium through which such ideological shocks could travel "backward and forward" across an already-linked world.

## Notable passages

The pardeshi passage (c1) is Bayly's cleanest single statement of outsider rule as the eighteenth-century default, moving from Ottoman Albanians and Manchu tribal incorporation to the Indian deshi/bideshi/pardeshi vocabulary he adopts as his own analytic term. The stateless-societies passage (c2) does the corresponding work for Africa, the Americas, and the Pacific, insisting that "the state" is not a cross-culturally neutral unit of analysis. The slave-plantation sentence (c3) is a compact, deliberately provocative thesis-statement: the most "advanced" capitalism of the period was also the most violent. The three-engines passage (c4) is the section's load-bearing claim, naming kingship, religion, and bodily practice as parallel and mutually reinforcing drivers of "archaic globalization" — everything else in the second half of the unit elaborates one of these three. The caste passage (c5) shows how a single embodied-status idiom crossed Iberian, Islamic, and Chinese worlds well before race or nationality hardened into their nineteenth-century forms. The millenarian-conjuncture passage (c6) is the unit's forward hinge, explicitly binding a sixteenth-century Christian-Muslim apocalyptic pattern to the Asian and French revolutionary energies the rest of the book will treat as linked, converging crises — the bridge into the "great divergence" the next chapter examines.
