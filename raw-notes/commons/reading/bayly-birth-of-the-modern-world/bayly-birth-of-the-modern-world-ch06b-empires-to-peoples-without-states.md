# Nationalisms and the Peoples Left Outside Them

This unit closes Bayly's account of nineteenth-century nationalism by first surveying its uneven rise across the old agrarian empires — Habsburg, Romanov, Ottoman, Qing, and the colonized or semi-colonized zones of Egypt, North Africa, sub-Saharan Africa, India, and China — and then turning to the underside of that process: the "peoples without states" whom nation-building left stranded, protected, assimilated, or marked for persecution.

## Themes

**Nationalism's emergence follows three distinct timelines, not a Europe/non-Europe split.** Bayly states an explicit three-stage proposition: (1) in regions such as Vietnam, Korea, Japan, and Ethiopia, "old patriotisms" tied to an early history of statehood transformed slowly, over long periods, into exclusive nationality under the pressure of war and communications; (2) in the "complex large polities of eastern Eurasia and North Africa" — Ottoman lands, Egypt, India, but also Hungary, Poland, and the Russian and Austrian empires — the change was abrupt, concentrated after 1860, and driven by international war, colonialism, and state-led modernization; (3) some regions, including the central Ottoman lands, much of Austria-Hungary and Russia, and southern Ireland, had still not produced a coherent nationalism by 1914. Bayly insists these tracks cut across, rather than track, an "inside/outside Europe" boundary — Hungarian and Polish nationalism after 1860 belong to the same story as Indian and Egyptian nationalism. [[nationalism]]

**Continuity with an old patriotism (a "navel") is not required, but it matters where it exists.** Bayly borrows Gellner's image from a debate with A. D. Smith: nations need not be born from prior patriotic solidarity, but where such continuity exists it shapes what leaderships and populations can later do with the nation — his examples are the United States' retained eighteenth-century constitution (a young, "constructed" nation too fragile to amend) against England's unwritten one (too old to need writing down). Zionism is his instance of a nation imagined with no navel at all.

**Old agrarian empires managed cultural difference by segregating and protecting it, not assimilating it.** Where a universal monarch, not "the people," was sovereign, questions of collective belonging mattered less. The Ottoman millet system — semi-autonomous religious communities under bishops or rabbinic councils, alongside parallel arrangements for Muslim minorities and Bedouin tribes — is Bayly's paradigm case, and he draws the same principle across the Mughals, Safavids, and Qing [[mughal-administration]]: these regimes "made a virtue of cultural difference" rather than erasing it.

**The "nationality problem" is manufactured by economic differentiation, European patronage, and internal state reform, not a natural awakening.** Bayly names three simultaneous causes inside the Ottoman lands: Atlantic-economy trade advantages accruing unevenly to Greeks, Armenians, Jews, and Coptic and Assyrian Christians; European powers cultivating minorities as commercial and diplomatic intermediaries (honorary consulships, missionary outreach, consular pressure at Istanbul); and Tanzimat-era internal reforms that, by conceding a hint of popular representation, forced the question of who counted as "the people." The same three-part mechanism — economic advantage, external patronage, internal reform — recurs across his other cases.

**Colonial administration reifies the very ethnic and religious categories it claims only to describe.** Indirect rule, "divide and rule," and the segregation of hill, forest, and desert "minority peoples" (Shan and Karen in Burma, Berbers set against Arabs in French North Africa, Java against the "outer islands" for the Dutch, Hindu/Muslim/Sikh in British India) were driven as much by unspoken political calculation — minority armies less likely to turn on their colonizers, minority elites as a counterweight to nationalist leaderships — as by deliberate policy. Crucially, the colonial legal system, census, and emerging "science" of anthropology did not just record difference; they built it, feeding back into how "so-called minorities saw themselves as mini-nations." This is Bayly's version of the argument that census-taking and comparable state technologies of legibility manufacture the ethnic categories they purport only to enumerate. **The Jewish case runs through every trajectory Bayly names at once.** By the 1870s most European Jews were formally assimilated (Bayly cites the maxim "Be a Jew in your home and a man outside"), yet endogamous, geographically dispersed traditional communities persisted from Algeria to Cochin and southwestern China; a revivalist current pushed back against assimilation; and the Zionist movement (Basle, 1897) proposed an entirely new, navel-less nation. Meanwhile the same nationalist consolidation that assimilated Jews elsewhere also produced modern anti-Semitism as a "touchstone of nationalism," most virulent not where Jewish populations were largest (Budapest, 23 percent) but where ideology ran hottest (Vienna, 7 percent) — and it hardened further after military humiliations (Russia's 1905 defeat, France's 1871 defeat feeding the Dreyfus affair).

**American nation-building defused European-style ethnic conflict for European immigrants but hardened a racial line instead.** A weak federal government, no established church, and abundant land meant that neither religion nor land conflict became nationally politicized battlegrounds for the roughly 20 million Europeans who immigrated between 1850 and 1914 — ethnicity shaped voting without derailing assimilation. But this applied only to people of European descent: Chinese, Japanese, and Indian immigration was restricted, Black Americans were resegregated after emancipation, and Native nations were pushed onto reservations — a pattern Bayly explicitly parallels to the treatment of Aboriginal Australians, Maori, and Canadian First Nations across the British "white dominions."

**Nation-building and minority-making are the same historical act seen from opposite sides.** Bayly's closing thesis is that the creation of harder boundaries between "majority" and "minority" populations was the mirror image of the creation of the nation itself — and minority leaderships were not merely passive victims of this process but active co-authors, constructing their own histories, legends, and languages to mirror the larger projects of the nation. This is Bayly's version of the book's master paradox of contested uniformity: the same forces that made the world's polities more alike (nation-statehood, legibility, representation) simultaneously sharpened antagonisms between the groups now newly sorted inside and outside each nation — a dynamic that rhymes with the [[world-machines]] account of legibility as a machine that scales control precisely by making variety visible and then unbearable, though Bayly's own frame stays historical and comparative rather than treating the state as a single infrastructure with its own life-cycle.

## Notable passages

Bayly's own three-stage summary (c1) is the unit's organizing device and should anchor any downstream synthesis of how nationalism actually spread. The Gellner "navels" passage (c2) is his clearest statement on the constructedness debate, complete with the memorable image of "designer-babies with no navels at all" for wholly invented nations like Zionism. The millet-system passage (c3) is Bayly's model of pre-national difference-management, useful as a baseline against which the Ottoman "nationality problem" described one paragraph earlier is measured. The India passage (c4) is the clearest single statement of how colonial legibility technologies — census, law, ethnology — manufacture the differences they claim only to observe. The closing passage (c5) states outright that nation-making and minority-making are one process, not two, and that minorities were agents in their own ethnic self-fashioning. Blyden's own words (c6) are the unit's only first-person nationalist voice, and Bayly uses them to show pan-Africanism drawing, like every other case in the unit, on borrowed languages of race, progress, and Christian redemption to construct a "shared nationality" from scratch.
