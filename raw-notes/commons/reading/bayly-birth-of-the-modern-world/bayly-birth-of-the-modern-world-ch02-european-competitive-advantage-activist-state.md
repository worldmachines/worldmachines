# European Competitive Advantages: Trade, War-Finance, and the Fitful Making of Patriotic States

This unit closes Bayly's account of why western Europe pulled ahead economically and politically by the early nineteenth century, then pivots to trace the piecemeal, uneven emergence of 'patriotic' identity before the French Revolution — arguing throughout that Europe's edge was late, contingent, and only ever a temporary 'wasting asset,' not proof of inherent superiority, since comparable economic, institutional, and identity-forming processes were also under way in Asia, Africa, and the Americas.

## Themes

**A late, ecological, and partly borrowed economic edge.** Following Kenneth Pomeranz, Bayly credits Europe's economic dynamism to contingent ecological windfalls rather than innate industriousness: a “massive hinterland of underused resources” at home and in the Americas, slave-plantation agriculture supplying cheap provisions, imported Caribbean sugar and Atlantic fish that let northwestern Europe feed a fast-growing urban population beyond what its own farms could support, and — crucially — the efficient long-distance exploitation of coal, which set off a chain of innovation (pumps, iron-casting, vacuum theory, steam power) that only began paying a military dividend in the 1820s–30s. China, by contrast, had reached a “high level equilibrium trap”: efficient enough to meet demand, but not a springboard to breakthrough.

**Secure property, insecure property: a comparative institutional divergence.** Bayly argues the sharper edge lay less in economics than in law: stable legal institutions (English common law, Roman law) protected family and personal property well enough that innovators could get rich and families could invest across generations, and even the Revolution confiscated land from only the Church and a small noble minority. He contrasts this with the Asante (punitive death duties, exclusion of slaves and commoners from advancement), the Ottomans (sultans who saddled their greatest commercial families with impoverishing state contracts), China (multiple inheritance splitting fortunes generation by generation, plus periodic forced 'donations' from merchants), and Russia (state-selected head merchants vulnerable to political change) — while noting Japan's relative stability (primogeniture, a fixed and rooted political leadership) let it later plan its own industrialization on a preexisting industrious revolution.

**Financial institutions as a compounding advantage.** The Dutch joint-stock company pioneered pooling risk and separating ownership from management; the Bank of England and a publicly funded national debt gave British public accounts a “transparency unattainable elsewhere” and became “something like a national icon”; paper money and regional banks eased credit. Chinese firms, however sophisticated, kept management inside the family. Bayly notes the irony that the nineteenth-century colonial state, otherwise indifferent to indigenous economic welfare, nonetheless extended indigenous merchants the same legal guarantees that had helped Europe.

**War-finance and the “brute advantage.”** Europe's edge was also martial: the amphibious cost of fighting by land and sea simultaneously forced sophisticated systems for financing and provisioning both, and Britain's permanent home fleet doubled as a reserve deployable to the Caribbean or the East. Naval endurance-at-sea fell behind in the Ottoman and Asian world after 1700 (“superb navies… for fighting seventeenth-century wars”). The result, per Niels Steensgaard, was that Europeans captured the greatest “value added” in world trade by controlling transport, sale, and the sale of protection itself — even while their actual goods remained less prized than Asian ones.

**The activist, patriotic state — fuzzy, piecemeal, and not uniquely European.** Bayly insists 1790s European states were still as jurisdictionally 'fuzzy' as Asian or African ones: Russia was barely 44% ethnically Russian; Germany and Italy were fragmented; Britain and France were linguistically plural (Gaelic, Welsh, Cornish; a third of France not French-speaking) with surviving local power centers (the Irish Ascendancy Parliament, provincial parlements). What built a slow sense of common patria instead was integrated markets (London's and Paris's commercial dominance, the 'patriotic economy' that eased the 1707 union with Scotland), religious and military antagonism against dangerous 'others' (English/British anti-Catholic Protestantism, French Catholic universalism, German cultural self-definition against a French 'other'), a shift from dynastic to territorial politics, and — notably — state-sponsored education. Enlightened despots' schools (Maria Theresa's Austria: “well over 6,000 schools and 200,000 students”) standardized language and literacy well before nationalist ideology caught up with it; Bayly explicitly ties this to [[benedict-anderson]]'s print-capitalism thesis, adding that the schoolroom mattered as much as the newspaper. This standardizing, uniformity-producing activity of the pre-revolutionary state resonates with [[modernity-machine]] and [[legibility-as-machine-core]] — though Bayly's state is still fumbling toward uniformity rather than executing it at scale, and the identity it manufactures is elite and urban long before it is popular.

**Against diffusionism: patriotic identity was not a European export.** Bayly directly attacks the 'cargo' narrative in which nationalism sailed from Europe to India (c.1880), China (1900s), the Ottoman world (post-WWI), and, latest and worst-used, sub-Saharan Africa. He marshals counter-examples of pre-colonial identity-formation: Qing-era Han Chinese identity (sustained partly through resistance to Manchu-legislated dress codes and hairstyles), Sri Lankan, Burmese, Korean, and northern Vietnamese identities forged against neighbors, Tokugawa Japan's self-definition against outer 'barbarians' and the internally colonized Ainu, new fringe kingdoms (Gurkha Nepal, Rama I's Thailand, Qajar Iran), and African cases from Buganda (“a strong sense of its own history, centralised government… horizontality of social consciousness”) to Shaka's Zulu kingdom. This is Bayly's multi-centred-origins argument applied to nationhood itself: nineteenth-century national identity did not appear from nothing in 1789 and radiate outward — it condensed, unevenly, out of processes already active worldwide. [[benedict-anderson]]'s account of the nation as an imagined community produced by print capitalism is the direct citation here; Bayly's comparative, multi-sited emphasis sits in a different register from [[nationalism]]'s counter-Enlightenment/Herderian genealogy of nationalist doctrine — compatible readings of adjacent questions (how national feeling forms, versus how nationalist ideology was theorized) rather than the same claim.

**Conclusion: exceptionalism as contingent, temporary convergence.** Bayly's closing move is explicitly anti-monocausal: Europe's advantage lay “not in one factor, but in an unpredictable accumulation of many characteristics seen separately in other parts of the world” — economic dynamism, weaponry, medium-sized-state rivalry, and fluid-but-real patriotic identity all had to coincide. He flags mainland Southeast Asia's comparably sharp precolonial identities as a partial parallel, and notes Japan had already “glimpsed their own way to a parallel modernity” by 1870 — Europe's exceptionalism was always a 'wasting asset.' The unit ends by gesturing toward the next topic: the 'texture of society' that let people 'congregate, debate, and adjust their institutions' — civil society and the public sphere.

## Notable passages

The Pomeranz-derived opening (c1) states the ecological/resource argument for European dynamism in its starkest form — hinterlands, plantations, timber, surplus-population export — before Bayly complicates it with institutional and war-finance arguments. The property-rights passage (c2) is the hinge to his comparative-institutions claim: legal security, not superior enterprise, let European capital accumulate across generations. The war-finance passage (c3) states baldly that Europeans 'became much better at killing people' and traces that capacity to the same wars that built European fiscal and commercial institutions — Bayly's most concise statement of the thesis named in the chapter's title. The '50 years' passage (c4) is the load-bearing qualifier for the entire second half: European state-patriotism is real but recent and temporary, not a civilizational given. The print-culture/schooling passage (c5) is where Bayly's argument and [[benedict-anderson]]'s thesis converge most explicitly, naming the schoolroom as a second engine of nationalist consciousness alongside the press. The diffusionism passage (c6) states in full the imperial 'cargo' narrative Bayly is refuting, immediately before he supplies counter-examples from Qing China, Sri Lanka, Japan, and Africa.
