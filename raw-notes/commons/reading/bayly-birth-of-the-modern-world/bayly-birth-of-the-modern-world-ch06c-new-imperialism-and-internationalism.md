# New Imperialism and the Nationalization of the Global Order

This unit closes Bayly's chapter on nineteenth-century nation-states by arguing that the "new imperialism" of the 1870s-1900s cannot be reduced to any single cause -- not capital, not "men on the spot," not the "official mind" reacting to peripheral crises -- because it was above all the overseas face of a more competitive, prestige-hungry European (and Japanese) nationalism; new firepower, medicine, and telegraphy supplied the means but not the motive. The unit then widens its lens: the nation-state's defining feature was that it operated among other, similar nation-states, and this rivalry pressed all international connections -- trade, religion, migration, marriage, dress -- into national form, eroding "archaic globalization" (named in chapter 1) even as older cosmopolitan links persisted beneath the surface and both fed and subverted the new order. Three case studies -- the passport, the Red Cross, and Vivekananda's appearance at the 1893 World Parliament of Religions -- show archaic or universalist institutions being renationalized into an internationalism of formally equal but jealously bounded states.

## Themes

**Imperialism and nationalism as one phenomenon, not cause and effect.** Bayly surveys and subordinates the standard monocausal accounts of the new imperialism -- Marxist-Leninist super-profits, financiers seeking safe investment, "men on the spot" like cotton-traders and mine-owners, Robinson and Gallagher's crises of the "periphery" undermining the "official mind," diplomatic maneuvering among the Concert of Europe -- arguing instead that "all these arguments seem to have some force at different places and different times" but that the deeper, often overlooked driver was the tightening bond between imperial expansion and the "more strident European nationalism" surveyed earlier in the chapter: national governments used empire to pursue prestige and preempt rivals (Bismarck, Crispi's Italy, Meiji Japan, Leopold's Congo), and existing empires (British India, Egypt, South Africa) provoked and were provoked by colonial nationalism in turn (Curzon, Cromer, Milner, Sarrault).

**Technology supplied the means, not the motive.** Rapid-fire guns, new medicines, and the telegraph gave Europeans unprecedented military and administrative reach, but Bayly insists this only explains capacity, not the "velocity and ferocity" of the land-grab: the desire for it was national-competitive, not technological.

**The nation-state's defining trait is existing among rival nation-states.** Bayly relocates the essence of the nation-state away from its internal claims on its own population and toward its position within a system of similar, competing units -- a structural condition that pressed the world toward political uniformity and eroded the older "archaic globalization" of ecumenes, honorific exchange, and trade diasporas named in chapter 1. The imperial state's spread after 1815 forced rigid territorial, linguistic, and religious boundaries onto every international network -- a legibility-and-standardization dynamic that resonates with [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and the [[modernity-machine]]'s pull toward uniform, measurable order, though here uniformity is produced by inter-state rivalry rather than a single administrative apparatus, and [[nationalism]] is the vector rather than bureaucratic technique alone.

**Archaic globalization persisted underneath, and both fed and subverted the new order.** Common-law lineages, evangelical church networks, transatlantic literary culture, coolie and slave-descended labor migration, the "charismatic" non-market demand for gold in India and the Middle East, cross-racial hypogamy, temporary Muslim marriages linking the "Arab, Indian, and Muslim East," and European royal and aristocratic intermarriage (American heiresses solvent-izing declining estates) all continued much as before 1780 -- but were increasingly represented in national terms and controlled by nation-states. Bayly's point is paradoxical rather than declensionist: the old networks did not vanish, they were captured and relabeled by the new national frame.

**Ideology, religion, migration, trade, and the body were all renationalized -- producing "contested uniformity."** Rights theories (adapted by figures like Ram Mohun Roy, and by China after the Opium Wars) turned universal empires into juridically equal states; universal religions (Catholicism, pan-Islamism) kept global aspirations but organized bureaucratically like nation-states; migration control shifted from local headmen and "guest"-merchant status to state surveillance (passports, quarantine, the US restriction of "Asiatics" and Jews culminating in the 1924 National Origins Act); trade shifted from honorary consuls to formal treaties even as capital flows grew more international; and bodily practice -- Europeans abandoning native wet-nurses, dress, and commensality, matched by Hindu nationalist campaigns to expel Muslim body servants -- nationalized old purity boundaries while simultaneously internationalizing elite male dress (English topcoats, French manners). Bayly's own formula names the paradox directly: growing international uniformity went alongside growing conflict between closely defined nation-states -- a close analogue to the World Machines idea of legibility and standardization heightening rather than dissolving difference, though here the friction is inter-national rather than inter-machine.

**Case studies: the passport, the Red Cross, and the World Parliament of Religions.** The passport (following Torpey) moved from a royal letter for elite travelers to a universal instrument of state surveillance, forged in Europe by revolutionary fear and in Asia by East India Company monopoly-jealousy and later British-Indian political fear of itinerant Muslim teachers -- in every case "nationality had become the point of reference," even when the same document was used to evade national control (Chinese and Arab merchants securing foreign nationality). The Red Cross, founded on a putatively universal humanitarian ideal, became "the obverse of the Swiss flag," Swiss-dominated, split into national committees, and eventually forced to fracture into the Red Crescent (after Ottoman attacks on volunteers wearing a "Christian symbol") and later the Israeli Star of David -- internationalism built out of, not against, national units. The 1893 World Parliament of Religions in Chicago was convened to celebrate a universal religious humanity, but nationality controlled who could "represent" a religion; the uninvited Swami Vivekananda seized the platform anyway and, presenting Hinduism as a civilizational and diasporic force while denouncing Western "materialism," welded Hindu identity to Indian nationhood so thoroughly that his reception back home was remembered as the moment Indian nationhood was recognized as a force in its own right -- a data point for Bayly's "empires of religion" thesis, and one that this unit itself connects to [[mahatma-gandhi]]'s later linkage of bodily discipline (vegetarianism, abstinence) to national selfhood.

**Conclusion: nationalism and imperialism as mutually generative, and the paradox of the nation-state.** War -- the mid-century "wars of legitimacy" and their aftershocks -- rather than simply the state, urbanization, or print capitalism (cf. [[benedict-anderson]]'s [[imagined-communities]]), is named as the deepest cause of hardened nationalisms: "war was the origin of nationalisms, just as nationalisms caused wars." Bayly closes on a structural paradox: statesmen conceived nations as monolithic and authoritative, but ordinary people treated the nation as a guarantor of rights and claims on resources, and when states failed to deliver, popular demand intensified -- fueling a proliferation of voluntary associations, reform societies, and moral crusades (antislavery bodies, and new organizations speaking for Indians, Irishmen, socialists, women, indigenous peoples), of which the socialist First International was only the most radical. This is Bayly's bridge into the book's next chapters on the state itself and on political thought.

## Notable passages

**"Imperialism and nationalism were part of the same phenomenon"** is the unit's thesis sentence, generalizing from the partition of Africa to the whole "new imperialism" and offered as a corrective to purely economic (Marxist-Leninist, "men on the spot," Robinson-Gallagher) explanations.

The passage on **the nation-state's defining condition** relocates its essence to its position among rival nation-states, naming this as the source of the pressure toward political uniformity that eroded the "archaic globalization" of chapter 1 -- the pivot on which the rest of the unit turns.

The passage on **archaic globalization's persistence** (common law, evangelical missions, gold's "charismatic" non-market demand, exotic-goods trade) shows the old networks continuing much as before, complicating any clean rupture narrative between the archaic and the modern.

**"Growing international uniformity went alongside growing conflict between closely defined nation-states"** is Bayly's most direct statement of contested uniformity, reached through the renationalization of racial and bodily boundaries.

The **Vivekananda at the 1893 Parliament** passage shows a universalist religious platform captured and redirected into an assertion of Indian nationhood -- Bayly's clearest single illustration of "empires of religion" fusing with nationalism.

The **closing paradox** passage marks the gap between statesmen's monolithic nation and ordinary people's transactional nation, generating the voluntary-association upsurge that bridges into the book's next chapters.
