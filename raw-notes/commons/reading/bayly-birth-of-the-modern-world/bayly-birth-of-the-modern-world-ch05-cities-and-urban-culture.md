# Cities, Class, and the Making of a Worldwide Urban Culture, 1780–1914

This unit closes Bayly's chapter on cities by tracking the transition from the religiously and dynastically organized cities of the eighteenth-century world to a recognizably modern, worldwide urban order: it covers the demographic pace of urbanization, the rupture wrought by the revolutionary wars and commercial expansion of 1780–1820, the racial and class hierarchies that deepened even as cities converged in form, the historiographical debate over whether a unified working-class consciousness ever existed, and the emergence of a genuinely global repertoire of urban leisure and associational life — cafés, department stores, wrestling clubs, football — that both disciplined populations and, in colonial cities, incubated anti-colonial politics.

## Themes

**Urbanization was demographically slow but culturally decisive.** Bayly separates the narrow, statistical sense of urbanization (the share of population in cities over 10,000 — rising only from roughly 9 percent in 1600 to 20 percent by 1900) from urbanization as a *cultural* process, the spread of an "associational culture" from the eighteenth-century middle class down to workers and women. The world's great agrarian societies (China, India, the Middle East) saw population grow without proportionate urban growth, while settler societies (North America, Australia, sub-Saharan Africa) urbanized rapidly from near-zero bases — Victoria's goldfields alone housing 146,000 people by 1871.

**The pre-1780 city was a cosmological, not merely economic, order.** Cities before the revolutionary era were organized around a recurring triad of religious and dynastic power — church-basilica opposite royal fortress in the Christian and Latin American world, mosque/palace/madina in Islamic cities, Brahmin quarters around the temple in South Asia, Beijing's "pivot of the four quarters," the sankin-kotai hostage system binding Japanese lords to Edo. This baseline of "archaic" spatial order (echoing the world economy Braudel traces from the sixteenth century, [[braudel-world-economy]]) is what the revolutionary wars, colonial conquest, and commercial growth after 1780 begin to dissolve — old Mughal capitals like Delhi and Agra ([[mughal-administration]]) declining as new commercial ports (Calcutta, Bombay, Shanghai, Alexandria) took over cultural leadership decades before formal colonial rule.

**Contested uniformity: disciplined, homogeneous cities bred sharper antagonism, not less.** Grid streets, broad highways, and demarcated public space let states police demonstrations, contain fire, and quarantine epidemics — but the same homogenizing pressure that flattened old "urban villages" into uniform middle-class/working-class, white/non-white blocs also "foster[ed] the growth of exclusive and sometimes antagonistic identities." Racial segregation (vagrancy laws against Black Americans, Aborigines, Maori, American Indians), anti-Irish, anti-Slav, and anti-Semitic violence, and eugenic race ideology all intensified specifically in the 1880s–1890s, precisely as cities became more alike — Bayly's chapter-defining paradox, and a close cousin of the WMP claim that legibility and difference escalate together rather than trading off (worth naming as only a partial fit: [[divergence-machine]] names variety as a *flourishing* force, where here uniform conditions generate antagonism and hierarchy, not capture-resistant plurality).

**States performed modernity through urban form.** Beyond policing, cities became stages where states announced legitimacy: khedival Egypt and post-Tanzimat Ottoman rulers built boulevards, opera houses, and statuary-lined squares; Madrid's art nouveau center supplanted Habsburg proximity to the cathedral; Shanghai's neoclassical Bund replaced the "pivot of the four quarters." This is close to the Modernity Machine's core move of legibility-as-control ([[legibility-as-machine-core]], [[modernity-machine]]) — grid patterns and broad avenues as literal technologies of state visibility and order, dressed as aesthetic progress; Bayly, unlike a machine theorist, treats it as often "wasteful expenditure" rather than efficient administration.

**Colonial urban inequality was structural, not just a market outcome.** Life expectancy for the urban poor in Shanghai, Bombay, or Batavia could fall to 28 years — below the surrounding peasantry — because casual colonial workers lacked the food-security networks available in villages even in ordinary years. Bayly insists this reflects institutional exclusion (indigenous workers' lack of "clout" with alien or Creole employers) compounding the terms-of-trade gap between industrial and raw-material economies.

**The unified working-class consciousness of socialist myth did not exist.** Bayly's central historiographical move is to dismantle the turn-of-the-century belief — shared by contemporaries and inherited by social historians into the 1970s — that industrial workers everywhere were converging on a single revolutionary consciousness. Strikes in Russia (1905), Bombay (the Tilak strikes), Chile (Antofagasta), and Buenos Aires were each driven by local wage squeezes, price cycles, and political crises of state legitimacy, not a shared class ideology; "nationalism was a consequence, more than a cause, of European wars, so working-class consciousness was a consequence of turmoil and revolution, rather than its cause."

**A genuinely worldwide urban culture still emerged — and doubled as anti-colonial infrastructure.** Cafés, bistros, department stores (Sears Roebuck, Harrods), wrestling akharas, and football clubs spread as a common repertoire of leisure and association across continents. In colonial cities, where public space was racially divided, indigenous middle classes built parallel institutions (Calcutta University's Coffee House, the Shanghai YMCA) that became "forcing houses of political discussion" — and the same uniform professional-training circuit that produced this worldwide culture carried Mahatma Gandhi ([[mahatma-gandhi]]) and Jan Smuts to London, Sun Yat-sen to Honolulu, and Ho Chi Minh to a Paris restaurant, seeding the anti-colonial politics of the next generation.

## Notable passages

The cosmological-order passage (mosque/palace/madina, Brahmin quarters, Beijing's "pivot of the four quarters") sets the pre-1780 baseline against which everything else in the unit is a rupture. The "contested uniformity" passage states Bayly's paradox in its purest form — homogenizing urban discipline manufacturing antagonistic identity. The state-modernity passage (khedives, Ottoman boulevards, the Shanghai Bund) is the clearest evidence of cities as deliberate performances of legible statehood. The colonial-mortality passage supplies the unit's starkest number (a 28-year life expectancy below the surrounding peasantry) for structural urban inequality. The "myth of social development" passage is Bayly's historiographical thesis statement against a unified working-class consciousness. The closing colonial-associational passage names the actual itinerary — coffee houses, YMCAs, Paris restaurants — through which a shared global urban culture became the connective tissue of anti-colonial leadership.
