# Climacteric: Reexporting the Revolution and Sapping the Legitimacy of the State, 1789-1820

This unit joins the back half of Bayly's chapter on the "climacteric": first, how the wars that grew out of the French Revolution exported revolutionary energy and financial extraction across Europe and, by ricochet, across the whole world; second, a comparative argument about why states as different as France, Britain, China, Japan, Mughal India, and the Ottoman Empire experienced such unequal degrees of legitimacy collapse during the world crisis of 1780-1820, despite facing broadly similar fiscal-military pressures.

## Themes

- **Invasion radicalized the Revolution, and the Revolution became an extraction machine.** The presence of hostile foreign armies on French soil, not the Revolution's ideals alone, is what "released a wave of resistance" that carried revolutionary and then Napoleonic armies across Europe. But the empire this produced quickly became a fiscal-military engine in its own right: indemnities stripped from defeated states (Venice alone lost six million francs), streamlined Egyptian land revenues, and a system of "donations" and "entails" supporting officers and administrators (5,000 people drawing over 30 million francs a month by 1814) that Bayly pointedly compares to Ottoman, Russian, and Mughal patrimonial finance. Enlightened French rule promised to sweep away the old order's corruption and instead reproduced it in new form.
- **The world crisis was a feedback loop, not a one-way European export.** Bayly's central move is to insist the crisis "bounced back" to Europe rather than radiating outward passively — his own butterfly-effect image. Concretely: Napoleon's reversal of Caribbean slave emancipation could not undo Toussaint L'Ouverture's revolutionary army, which helped preserve the Revolution in Europe by absorbing and destroying nearly 100,000 British troops; the fall of Spain triggered Latin American independence wars, which disrupted Mexican and Peruvian silver output, which rippled into West Africa and into a China newly dependent on that silver; British "sub-imperialism" against India and the Dutch East Indies, missionary and whaling incursions into the Pacific, and Maori acquisition of European muskets are all folded into the same connective chain.
- **A "culture of opposition" is the missing middle term between economic distress and political collapse.** Bayly argues poverty and fiscal strain alone cannot explain the "universal wreck of nations": the same pressures produced wildly different outcomes depending on locally available discourses of delegitimization. In France, decades of libelous, often sexualized pamphleteering (Darnton, Ozouf) — especially the gendered slander of Marie Antoinette — had already taught people that the court, aristocracy, and Church were corrupt, so that revolutionary rupture followed the collapse of monarchy's symbolic legitimacy as much as its finances. Britain had comparable satire and rioting but channeled it through Parliament and a wide sense of legal/property order; Japan's Tokugawa elite retained leadership by capturing religious and educational reform movements; China's popular culture of apocalyptic rumor and millenarian Buddhism (via Philip Kuhn's *Soulstealers*) eroded Confucian scholar-gentry legitimacy in frontier zones but, until the nineteenth century, mostly indicted corrupt local officials rather than the Qing throne itself.
- **The same pattern recurs outside the Christian and Confucian worlds.** In India, the Mughal court's Indo-Muslim elite could neither militarily nor ideologically counter Sikh claims to *dharma* (righteous rule) that partly appropriated old Mughal charisma while implicitly excluding Muslims. In the Ottoman world, the Wahhabi purist movement — a return to the "godly old Medina" preached to the poor and to Arab nomads — functioned as "the Islamic world's 'revolution'": turned back militarily by Ottoman-Egyptian arms but never extinguished, later resurfacing from central India to West Africa. The Ottoman core itself survived by channeling reform through the Tanzimat after 1823, a middle position between France's total rupture and Japan's elite-managed containment.
- **Bayly reads this as reconciling economic and discursive historiography.** He explicitly wants to "heal the breach" between materialist histories of economic/political process and the newer, representation-focused historiography (Furet, Baker, Hunt), arguing legitimacy always rested on rulers' perceived equity in managing a moral economy that was itself buffeted by unintended shifts in trade, labor, and money.

## Notable passages

The curated passages trace both halves of the argument: Napoleonic extraction and its Ottoman/Mughal analogue; the butterfly-effect chain from Haiti through silver to China; the French "culture of opposition" built on gendered libel; Kuhn's account of Chinese popular apocalypticism eroding scholar-gentry legitimacy; and the Wahhabi movement framed as an Islamic-world parallel to the European revolutions. Together they show Bayly moving fluidly between hard fiscal-military mechanics and soft, discursive delegitimization as co-equal drivers of the world crisis — resisting a single "prime mover" explanation, consistent with his multi-centred account of modernity's origins. Bayly's picture of decaying royal and imperial legitimacy — moral economies that lose their claim to rule before any new settlement (Tanzimat, Sikh dharma, republican calendar) is fully secured — reads as a state-scale instance of what World Machines calls the [[gramsci-gap]], though Bayly's interregna are measured in years or decades of court-by-court collapse rather than a civilizational machine transition. The Indo-Muslim elite's failure to counter Sikh claims that partly absorbed "old Mughal charisma" also connects directly to [[mughal-administration]].
