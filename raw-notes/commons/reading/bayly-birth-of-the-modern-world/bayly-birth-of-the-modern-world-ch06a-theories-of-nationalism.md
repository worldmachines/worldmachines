# The Global Making of Nationalism, c.1860–1900: Theories, Timing, and Contested Forms

This unit opens Bayly's chapter 6 ("Nation, Empire, and Ethnicity, c.1860–1900") by working through the competing academic "theories of nationalism" — ethnic-continuity, modernization, state-formation, and imagined-communities accounts — not as rival laws but as partial tools of interpretation; it then asks *when* nationalism arose at a world scale, rejecting a Europe-to-periphery diffusion story in favor of near-simultaneous global emergence tied to the era's wars and crises; it surveys *whose* nation was being built, arranging cases along a spectrum from old-patriotism-rooted nations to purely state-fabricated ones; and it closes by examining how memory, national associations, and an expanding print/telegraph public sphere both entrenched and complicated national belonging.

## Themes

**Theories of nationalism are tools, not predictive laws.** Bayly's throughline is methodological: he insists that no single theory "taken separately can possibly explain the nature, still less the timing, of the emergence of nationalism." He lines up four accounts — the "naturalistic"/ethnie-continuity view associated with nineteenth-century patriots and revived by A. D. Smith and Adrian Hastings, which holds that nations grew out of pre-existing linguistic and religious communities; Ernest Gellner's modernization thesis, which ties nationalism to urbanization and industrialization; Eric Hobsbawm and John Breuilly's state-formation thesis, which makes nationalism a deliberate product of state power; and Benedict Anderson's "imagined communities" thesis, which locates nationalism's origin in print capitalism and the diffusion of shared reading. Bayly's move is synthetic: each theory illuminates particular cases and all should be treated as preconditions to be weighted differently case by case, not as a single master mechanism.

**A genuinely global, not diffused, timing.** Against the standard picture of nationalism passing "from supposedly advanced white people to less advanced Asians and Africans," Bayly insists the contrast between Europe and the rest "should not... be overdrawn": Japan was arguably a nation-state before 1868, India and Egypt had vigorous independence movements by the 1880s, and even in Europe most 1848 nationalist movements had negligible popular support. He proposes two linked "world crises" (1780–1815 and 1848–65) as the shared accelerants across Germany, Italy, Russia, the Ottoman lands, Tokugawa Japan, and India, alongside inter-regional circulation of ideas (Ram Mohun Roy reading of Europe's revolutions in Calcutta newspapers, Ho Chi Minh reading Jefferson in French colonial schoolbooks) that moved in more than one direction — Japan itself became a model for other Asian and African nationalists by the 1880s.

**A contested spectrum: old patriotisms, state fabrications, and uncertain empires.** Bayly resists reading today's settled nations back into the period, classifying nationalisms along a spectrum: at one pole, nations rooted in "old patriotisms" of shared language and religion long before the nineteenth century (England, France, Japan, arguably Maharashtra, Sri Lanka, Ireland, northern Vietnam/Annam); at the other, nationalisms manufactured by newly created states (Britain-as-opposed-to-England forged in war with France per Linda Colley, Belgium after 1831, the Latin American republics after independence, and a United States nationalism hardened chiefly by the Civil War); and in between, multinational empires (Russia, Austria-Hungary, the Ottomans, Qing China) whose rulers were caught between fostering and suppressing the nationalisms stirring within their own borders. Categories like Irish, Egyptian, or Chinese nationalism themselves concealed competing loyalties (Ottoman patriotism, British-imperial loyalty) that only later hardened into exclusive identities.

**Perpetuation through memory, parties, and print — and its control.** The second half of the unit turns from origins to reproduction: war memorials, renamed streets, school textbooks, and paramilitary drilling built a "sacred landscape of nationalism" generation to generation; national political parties (Gladstone's Liberals, Germany's confessional and socialist parties, even factions in an autocratic Russia) projected local grievances onto a national stage well before full suffrage existed; and the post-1860 boom in mass-circulation, syndicated newspapers realized Anderson's print-capitalism mechanism in practice, from Stead and Hearst to the Persian constitutional revolution's telegraph network. But this same infrastructure was captured by the very states and press barons it might have made accountable: governments monopolized telegraph lines, news agencies like Reuters filtered what elites read, and a jingoistic press (Churchill's war correspondence, anti-Spanish and anti-Boer coverage) manufactured the belligerent nationalism it claimed only to report — even as colonized readers (Indian nationalists reading Stead, North African nationalists cabling European authorities) turned the same channels to their own ends. The chapter's key paradox — hardening national and imperial boundaries after 1860 coinciding with intensified cross-border linking, communicating, and influencing — rhymes with the World Machines framing of uniformity-versus-divergence as a machine-scale dynamic (see [[world-machines]]), though here the tension plays out through diplomats, journalists, and emigrants rather than a single infrastructural apparatus. The Hobsbawm/Breuilly state-formation account, in particular, describes exactly the census-taking, passport-issuing, education-mandating machinery that WMP calls out under [[legibility-as-machine-core]] — nationalism here is partly a side-effect of the state rendering its population legible and countable. And the passing aside that the "decline of royal and aristocratic legitimacy after 1789... was merely halted, but not reversed after 1815" gestures at something like WMP's [[gramsci-gap]] — an old legitimacy dying without full replacement — though Bayly's successor is competitive electoral/national legitimacy rather than a fully formed new order.

## Notable passages

The opening statement of the chapter's animating paradox (c1) frames everything that follows: nation-states and empires were drawing harder boundaries after 1860 at the very moment people found new ways to link and influence one another across them. Bayly's account of Gellner (c2) shows him crediting a theory's fit for central/eastern Europe while immediately noting its limits elsewhere — a pattern he repeats for each theory in turn. The Hobsbawm/Breuilly passage (c3) is the unit's clearest statement that concrete state technologies (education, citizenship law, census, passport) manufactured national sentiment rather than merely expressing it. Bayly's gloss on Anderson (c4) isolates what he sees as that theory's distinctive power: explaining nationalism in places untouched by capitalism or strong states. The passage on "the other end of the spectrum" (c5) supplies the comparative cases — Britain, Belgium, Latin America, the United States — for wholly state-created nationalism, in explicit contrast to the "old patriotisms" pole. Finally, the passage on the political effects of communications media (c6) captures the chapter's late twist: the same print and telegraph networks that spread national feeling were rapidly re-captured by governments and press barons, so that the free flow of information the theory of imagined communities presupposes was itself contested territory.
