# Picturing the Nation: Art and Architecture from Revolution to Empire, 1760–1914

Nineteenth-century painting, sculpture, and architecture built a shared, contested visual vocabulary of nation, empire, and modernity: the French Revolution's classical-republican iconography (David, Girodet, Canova) displaced dynastic portraiture and was re-exported to Bolivar's South America, Qajar Persia, and the Ottoman Tanzimat; after 1850 official "history painting" gave way to Impressionism and eventually cubism, art forms more critical of bourgeois and industrial order; outside Europe the fate of indigenous visual traditions was "literally, mixed" — Japan adapted on its own strengths while other traditions declined into tourist-dependent "ethnic survivals"; and architecture underwent the same "contested global uniformity," from neoclassical capitols and neo-Gothic parliaments to Haussmann's Paris and colonial re-engineering of Cairo, Alexandria, and New Delhi.

## Themes

**Revolutionary iconography becomes the international visual grammar of the nation-state.** The French Revolution replaced images of kings and aristocratic military heroes with abstract embodiments of the people — Marianne, the Cap of Liberty, Brutus as tyrannicide — which Jacques-Louis David, Girodet, and Ingres then adapted to chart the passage from republic to Napoleonic empire. Girodet's portrait of the black Haitian revolutionary Jean-Baptiste Belley (leaning on a bust of the abolitionist abbé Raynal) is read as the school's most "universalizing" statement, transforming classical nude and civic virtue into "a visual grammar for international revolution." That grammar traveled: [[simon-bolivar]] was refigured as the Brutus or Napoleon of Spanish American independence, Santa Anna postured as Bonaparte, and Persia's Fath Ali Shah adopted Ingres's imperial pose for the Qajar "new monarch" portrait while keeping a traditionally Persian beard as a sign of dynastic potency. This is a visual-culture instance of Bayly's national and imperial mythmaking discussed elsewhere in the book, and resonates with [[benedict-anderson]]'s [[imagined-communities]] — the nation made picturable and thereby real to its members — though Bayly's interest is the specific iconographic lineage (Rome, revolution, empire) rather than print-capitalism's role in [[nationalism]].

**Ambivalence and "othering" run through empire's visual culture.** Following Edward Said, Bayly reads Delacroix's and Géricault's noble, horse-mounted North African chieftains and the British India print series as reinforcing non-Europeans' "otherness" and a "negative triumphalism of the West," yet insists the ambivalence cuts both ways: European artists were also mourning their own vanishing preindustrial, chivalric past, projected onto colonized subjects as "the other." The same double movement structures American art — a "creolizing" Native-woman figure for the young republic gives way to a classicized "Columbia," while paintings of slaughtered pioneer women (echoed in British depictions of 1857 and the Zulu wars) coexist with elegiac images of the "noble Amerindian warrior" and the "death of the native" even as reservations were being filled; Australian painters, meanwhile, erased Aboriginal presence altogether to depict an "empty land."

**After 1850, official state iconography gives way to critique and "the people."** History painting declined in favor of art about ordinary industrial and rural lives, opening space for the Impressionists (Manet, Renoir, Degas) to reject academic training and, in van Gogh's words, paint how "ranks of people group themselves" without "meddl[ing] with politics." Gauguin's Tahiti pursued a different, more sympathetic register of the exotic "other" — identifying with his subjects rather than merely displaying them — before cubism after 1907 pushed Western art toward the same iconoclasm as contemporary anarchist and Communist politics. This drift from state-grammar toward proliferating, individually inflected registers of representation is a loose echo of the [[legibility-as-machine-core]] versus-divergence dynamic (uniform civic iconography giving way to heterogeneous, artist-particular visions) — a resonance, not an identity, since Bayly frames it as taste and politics rather than a machine-scale infrastructure shift.

**Outside the West, artistic modernization was uneven: adaptation versus dependency.** Japan's ukiyo-e tradition (Hokusai above all) proved "dynamic and adaptive," absorbing European technique through Nagasaki's "Dutch learning" and achieving international fame (The Great Wave) without external imposition; Chinese, Korean, Vietnamese, and Indian block-printing and lithography adapted more unevenly, and in British India the Arts and Crafts-inspired art schools produced, in Coomaraswamy's words, mere "trinkets for the Western market," leaving only insulated regions (Kutch, Gujarat, "tribal" areas) to sustain what outsiders reclassified as "ethnic survivals" dependent on tourism and state sponsorship.

**Architecture enacts the century's "contested global uniformity" in built form.** Neoclassical capitols (Washington, the Paris Panthéon, St Petersburg) and Barry's neo-Gothic Houses of Parliament were replicated from Bombay to Melbourne to British Columbia; non-European rulers (Istanbul's Gilderim palace, Mysore's "ancient Hindoo constitution" palace, Awadh's hybrid Mughal-Iranian-classical buildings) adopted European forms with only superficial indigenous ornament, aided by the rolled-steel joist. After 1852 Haussmann's rebuilding of Paris — sewers, boulevards, the erasure of "pits of disease and sedition" — became the template for Rome's Risorgimento monument, St Petersburg, and, per Timothy Mitchell, the khedives' remaking of Cairo and Alexandria, which segregated rich from poor as thoroughly as colonial New Delhi (Herbert Baker) and Pretoria segregated colonizer from colonized. This is squarely a [[legibility-as-machine-core]] story — sewerage, boulevards, and urban re-zoning as infrastructure for reading and ordering a population and its poor/rich, colonizer/colonized divisions — and touches Bayly's broader "contested uniformity" thesis that a shared, standardizing repertoire (of buildings as of states) simultaneously produced new lines of exclusion and difference; the connection to [[world-machines]]'s Modernity Machine is suggestive at the level of infrastructure-building rather than argued by Bayly himself, who stays a historian of connections rather than proposing a machine theory.

## Notable passages

The Belley/David passage (c1) is the unit's clearest statement of revolutionary art's self-conscious universalism — a Black revolutionary posed in French Assembly dress against an abolitionist bust, offered as proof that the new visual grammar claimed to transcend nation and race even as it remained recognizably French and classical. The Said-inflected passage (c2) is the unit's most explicit theoretical move, naming Said directly and then complicating him: othering is real, but entangled with Europeans' own nostalgia. The architecture passage (c3) opens with the phrase "contested global uniformity" almost verbatim from the book's thesis, applying it to the specific case of exported neoclassical and neo-Gothic state buildings. The Cairo/Alexandria passage (c4), citing Timothy Mitchell, extends that argument to colonial urban planning as a technology of social sorting. The Hokusai passage (c5) is the unit's one clear counter-case of successful, self-directed adaptation rather than dependency. The van Gogh/Gauguin/cubism passage (c6) marks the pivot from state-facing to people- and self-facing art after 1850.
