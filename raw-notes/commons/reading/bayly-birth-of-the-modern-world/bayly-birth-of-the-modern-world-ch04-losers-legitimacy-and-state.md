# Losers of Early Globalization, Hybrid Legitimacy, and the Overstretched Restoration State

This unit closes out the chapter's account of the post-1815 world along three linked strands: the regions and classes left behind by the new international division of labor and an increasingly militant free-trade ideology (Caribbean sugar, Indian textiles, Southeast Asian spices, famine-prone peasantries); the improvised, often hybrid legitimacy strategies restoration states used to reconstitute authority after the revolutionary crisis (Napoleonic legal and administrative templates, reinvented monarchy, colonial figurehead sovereignty, Islamic and Confucian accommodation); and the growth of state coercive and bureaucratic capacity - armies turned inward, lawyers, secret police - that nonetheless remained incomplete, setting up the convergent mid-century crises of 1848, the Taiping and Indian rebellions, and the American Civil War.

## Themes

- **Free trade as ideology and as force.** Bayly frames the shift from eighteenth-century mercantilism to nineteenth-century free-trade doctrine not as a neutral economic transition but as a new orthodoxy backed by British power: the domestic repeal of the Corn Laws, and Palmerston's willingness to use the fleet abroad (the Bay of Naples in 1840, the Ottoman tariff reductions of 1838, the Opium Wars). Robinson and Gallagher's "informal empire" names the resulting asymmetry - sovereignty nominally intact, autonomy curtailed by military and commercial leverage.
- **Losers of early globalization.** Old producing regions - Caribbean sugar, Indian textiles, Southeast Asian spices - declined as the "terms of trade" turned against the periphery, and peasant communities absorbed volatility (harvest failures, the Irish potato famine, indigo and cotton price collapses) without the information networks or capital access that let European merchants, and a few well-placed intermediaries (Parsis, Levantine Christians, Greeks), profit instead. Adam Smith's [[adam-smith]] vision of a virtuous global division of labor is invoked and undercut: the system Bayly describes increases aggregate wealth while widening inequity between groups.
- **Resistance to uniform free trade.** Friedrich List's "national political economy" is the unit's clearest statement of pushback against British-dictated economic uniformity, an argument that traveled to Hungary, Ireland, and India as those economies were battered by western imports - a globalizing norm provoking its own protectionist antagonists.
- **Hybrid, patched-together legitimacy.** Across very different polities - Bourbon France, a reinvented British monarchy, the Carlist civil war in Spain, Russian autocracy, new Latin American republics invoking Aztec and Inca descent and later a Bolivarian [[simon-bolivar]] cult, the Ottoman Tanzimat, colonial India's figurehead Mughal [[mughal-administration]] and princely courts, and Qing Confucian orthodoxy - restoration authorities survived by fusing older sacred or dynastic legitimacy with new legal-bureaucratic rationalization (the Code Napoleon, prefectures) rather than resolving the post-revolutionary legitimacy crisis outright. This partial, syncretic settlement resonates with the [[gramsci-gap]] idea of an interregnum where old legitimacy cannot simply be restored and a new one is not yet fully born, though Bayly's regimes are actively stitching together a working hybrid rather than sitting in pure morbid-symptom limbo.
- **Religious accommodation over confrontation.** Islamic reformist currents (Shah Abd al-Aziz, Sayyid Ahmed Barelvi) mostly avoided declaring holy war on colonial power and instead pursued moral and social reform "from within," a pattern the unit generalizes to the [[ulama]]'s largely pacific stance across much of the Muslim world before 1857.
- **The state gains strength, but not enough.** War-trained armies, expanding legal professions, and new secret-police bureaucracies (Fouche's model, the post-1830 and post-1848 crackdowns) show restoration states converting war-making capacity into domestic and colonial control - a rationalizing, record-keeping, policing apparatus that anticipates the [[legibility-as-machine-core]] logic of the modern state. Yet Bayly insists this control remained partial: retreating central government spending in Britain, divided federal power in the United States, and recurring "information panics" (garroters, Thugs, Carbonari, freemasons, triads) reveal how much of social life stayed opaque to the authorities.
- **Converging toward 1848 and beyond.** The unit closes by naming the payoff of the whole chapter: the fragile, hybrid stability described here gives way to a cluster of linked mid-century crises - 1848 in Europe, the Taiping Rebellion, the Indian Rebellion, and the American Civil War - treated as a single global climacteric rather than isolated national events.

## Notable passages

- The Caribbean sugar/Indian textile/spice-trade passage names the concrete "losers" of the new trading order and insists their decline, unlike in the eighteenth century, stayed regionally contained rather than destabilizing the whole system.
- Friedrich List's "national political economy" passage is the clearest ideological counter-current to British free-trade universalism, and the unit tracks its spread as an idea from Germany to Hungary, Ireland, and India.
- The Opium War passage stages Bayly's central irony: British "self-interested moralizing" about free trade colliding with a Qing state trying to defend both its silver reserves and its army from opium.
- The Code Napoleon and prefectures passage is the unit's clearest description of the state as a rationalizing technology that survives regime change - restoration monarchs inherit sharper, more "rational" administrative machinery than their eighteenth-century predecessors had.
- The Mughal-figurehead passage shows colonial hybrid legitimacy in miniature: retaining the appearance of "ancient" indigenous constitutional order while leaving the real, Company-imposed political settlement untouched.
- The closing passage ties 1848, the Taiping Rebellion, the Indian Rebellion, and the American Civil War together as a single global crisis, the argument the rest of the chapter goes on to develop.
