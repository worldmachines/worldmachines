# The Muqaddima's Afterlife: Ottomans, Orientalists, French and Germans

Irwin opens by noting that Ibn Khaldun had little influence on his immediate successors in the Mamluk Sultanate, Andalusia and the Maghreb, and created no school of Khaldunian historians. His first serious admirers were Ottoman writers of the seventeenth century who were beginning to doubt the eternity of their empire. Europe discovered him around 1800 through Hammer-Purgstall and Silvestre de Sacy, who founded institutional Orientalism. Quatremere edited the text and De Slane translated it, and the French colonial state in Algeria funded and used the work. The unit closes with the Germans, who read him through Hegel, Herder and Comte. Throughout, Irwin's interest is in how each readership made Ibn Khaldun a forerunner of its own concerns, and he keeps a dry, sceptical tone toward these appropriations.

## Themes

**Neglect at home, an Ottoman reception through decline.** Irwin says the Muqaddima had only "a slight influence" in the Arab lands. The Ottomans took it up when doubts arose about their rule. Katib Celebi's memorandum outlined "the organic life cycle of regimes: rise, stagnation, and decline on the Khaldunian pattern" and suggested the Ottoman Empire showed early symptoms of old age. Irwin adds a contrast: "The Turks, unlike Ibn Khaldun, entertained hopes that the decline of their regime could be arrested." This is a reader borrowing the cyclical frame while rejecting its fatalism. It rhymes with the WMP interest in cyclical history ([[secular-cycles]], [[linear-vs-cyclical-paths]], [[ibn-khaldun]]), though here the cycle is applied to a single dynasty, not to a millennial world machine, and the hope of arrest is the Ottomans' addition.

**The "Arab Montesquieu" label.** Hammer-Purgstall's phrase stuck. Irwin grants "some justice" in it (climate shaping culture, a search for the social causes of rise and fall) and reports al-Tahtawi's possibly ironical symmetry of "European Ibn Khaldun" and "Montesquieu of Islam." This is the first of the modernizing comparisons that Irwin surveys, and he accepts it only partially.

**Philology, empire and mistranslation.** Silvestre de Sacy's interest was "primarily" philological, but the French conquest of Algeria (from 1830) created a demand for what Ibn Khaldun said about Arabs and Berbers. De Slane's translation was commissioned by the French Ministry of War and funded officially, "presumably" because it might help the colonial project. Irwin lists its faults: omitted poetry, a summarized za'iraja, and 'asabiyya rendered as "esprit de corps," which later French commentators gave "Bergsonian overtones of elan vital." A key concept was thus bent toward a vitalist life force. The 'Ibar translation also tended to drop analytical passages, on Hodgson's criticism.

**Colonial appropriation.** Gautier equated Arab with nomad and nomad with "nihilist destroyer," and used De Slane to argue that the Hilali invasion was a locust-like catastrophe with lasting effects. Irwin's charge is that Gautier missed that Ibn Khaldun used "Arab" in two senses. Gautier then made Ibn Khaldun a genius apart from the Arabs, with "a perfume of the Renaissance," a modern Frenchman who would have approved of the French Empire. The badawa/hadara (nomad/settled) contrast is thereby turned into an ethnic and civilizational verdict. Irwin's account is a warning about how a nomad/sedentary dynamic gets racialized (compare [[chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders]] only at a distance: that is a different typology). Marcais is offered as a similar case.

**Monteil, the anti-colonial retranslator.** Vincent Monteil, who opposed French torture in Algeria and later converted to Islam, retranslated the Muqaddima to be closer to the spirit than the letter. Even so, Irwin reports that he presented Ibn Khaldun as precursor of Machiavelli ([[niccolo-machiavelli]]), of the French Enlightenment, of sociology and of Darwin, and cast his horoscope as a "typical Gemini." Irwin's closing question, how much of this came from the stars and how much from the text, is characteristically dry. Irwin's own stance is against such precursor readings.

**The Germans: Hegel, culture, and pessimism unwelcome.** Without colonies to serve, German-language readers used Ibn Khaldun against "fact-driven positivism and scissors-and-paste chronologies," in the new field of cultural history. Irwin offers a hedged suggestion that the Western discovery may not have been possible before the nineteenth century, and that Hegel's influence may explain German enthusiasm ("It is possible that..."). Von Kremer translated 'asabiyya as "civic spirit" or "national idea," claimed Islam was not really important to Ibn Khaldun, and made him a man born out of his time, yet found the cyclical model of history "unattractively pessimistic" because he believed in Hegelian progress. Irwin closes by rejecting the Comte comparison: sociology is secular and inductive, "while Ibn Khaldun's account of society was not." This links to the WMP contrast between cyclical and progress-driven historiography ([[linear-vs-cyclical-paths]], [[modernity-machine]]), though Irwin frames it as one reader's Hegelian bias, not as a claim about Ibn Khaldun's own system.

## Notable passages

The Ottoman passage (c1) shows Katib Celebi, Na'ima and others using the Muqaddima as a diagnostic for imperial ageing, with the Turkish refusal of Khaldunian fatalism. The Montesquieu passage (c2) is the origin of the "Arab Montesquieu" label and Irwin's measured endorsement. The De Slane passage (c3) documents the 'asabiyya mistranslation and its vitalist afterlife. The Gautier passage (c4) is Irwin's clearest case of scholarship serving empire, stripping Ibn Khaldun of his Arab identity. The Monteil passage (c5) shows the precursor habit even in an anti-colonial translator. The Von Kremer and Comte passage (c6) shows German Hegelian reading and why the comparison to positivism fails on Irwin's account.

For WMP the unit is mainly a case study in reception history, how a thinker is retrofitted as a forerunner, and in how a concept ('asabiyya) drifts under translation. It is also a reminder that Irwin's Ibn Khaldun does not fit modern secular social science, so the connections above are resonances only.
