# Ibn Khaldun's Distance from Philosophy, and the Family of Cyclical Histories

Irwin asks what the ideas of the Muqaddima rested on. He first shows that the Muqaddima's claim that history is "rooted in philosophy" turns on the Arabic word hikma (wisdom, moralizing advice literature, nasiha), not falasifa or faylasuf (philosophy proper). He then argues that Ibn Khaldun disowned philosophy, favored the theologian al-Ghazali over Averroes, and rejected the Greek-derived, city-bound political philosophy of Averroes and al-Farabi. What he took from Averroes, on Irwin's account, was a mood (pessimism) rather than a doctrine. The second half places the Khaldunian dynastic cycle among other cyclical theories, each with its own pessimism: the Qur'anic doom of every nation, the semantics of dawla, al-Kindi and the Ikhwan al-Safa, Confucian cycles (via Patricia Crone), Polybius, Vico, Spengler, Mann's Buddenbrooks, Mahfouz's Harafish and Valery. Irwin's frame throughout is that Ibn Khaldun's history is religiously based and moralistic, though woven "more subtly" than ordinary advice literature.

## Themes

**Hikma is not falsafa.** Rosenthal's "philosophy" translates hikma, which covered sciences not derived from the Qur'an and hadith, and a literature of aphorisms and exemplary tales about kings and sages (Anurshirwan, Buzurjmihr, Alexander in the Thousand and One Nights). It overlaps with nasiha advice literature (Ibn al-Muqaffa', al-Turtushi, Tahir ibn al-Husayn's Risala). Ibn Khaldun criticizes these predecessors for offering no proofs. Irwin's point is that the Muqaddima sits in a moralizing tradition, while being more dispassionate and more profound than it. This is the anti-anachronism move: the apparent philosopher-sociologist is a hakim writing in a religious frame.

**Explicit hostility to philosophy.** Ibn Khaldun claims knowledge "without the instruction of Aristotle", says the philosophers' opinion "is wrong in all its aspects", and treats reason as a scale good for gold but useless for mountains. Irwin adds context: philosophy was unwelcome under the fourteenth-century Merinids and off the madrasa syllabus, and Ibn Khaldun's youthful abridgements of Averroes and al-Razi were presumably repented. Irwin hedges on how much al-Abili taught him ("we do not have much idea of what that amounted to").

**Averroes: city versus ‘asabiyya, shared pessimism.** Averroes, following the Greeks, made the city the highest political form and gave prestige to the longest-resident families. Ibn Khaldun rejects this, since prestige needs a group that makes one feared. Irwin says Averroes had little experience of ‘asabiyya, so his political philosophy was "confined within city walls". Irwin also allows a probable unacknowledged debt, through Averroes's commentary on Plato's Republic, for the idea of luxury as a solvent of virtue. Both writers see good government decaying into tyranny through lust for power and wealth. Ibn Khaldun's version is the slide from caliphate to mulk through luxury and indolence, with Mu'awiya admired because he relied on ‘asabiyya. This resonates with [[decay-sequence]] and [[civilization-undone-by-its-own-success]], though the mechanism here is moral and religious (loss of the "simple state of Islam") and only implicitly a structural sequence.

**Pessimism as an age and a genre.** The Qur'an (7:34) gives every nation a term. Following Bernard Lewis, dawla means a "turn", which implies limited duration, and Lewis suggests ("It is possible") that cyclical theories from Greek or Persian sources contributed. Al-Kindi's astrological end-date for the ‘Abbasids and the Ikhwan al-Safa's 240-year rotation show older cyclical calculation, so the Muqaddima's three-to-four-generation cycle is not unprecedented. Irwin speculates ("may have been confirmed") that Ibn Khaldun's outlook was deepened by Maghribi political failure, the murders of his brother and Ibn al-Khatib, the Black Death and the ruins around him.

**Comparative cycles and where they differ.** Crone: Confucian thinkers also had tribal conquest of settled dynasties, but placed tribal conquest at the cycle's rock bottom and saw settling as improvement, while Ibn Khaldun took the tribesmen's viewpoint and made conquest the high point and settled life effeminate. That inverted valuation is the sharpest contrast in the unit. Irwin then runs Polybius, Hesiod and the metal ages, [[giambattista-vico]] (corsi and ricorsi, three ages, luxury destroying empires, a religious impetus and law-based method like Ibn Khaldun's, and a similar neglect by contemporaries), Spengler (seasonal and organic metaphors, hostility to the "world city", influence on Toynbee), Mann's four-generation Buddenbrooks (via Schopenhauer), Mahfouz's Harafish, and Valery's mortal civilizations. Irwin frames several of these as "parallels" or "unrelated" parallels; only for Mahfouz does he merely wonder ("Surely Mahfouz must have read Ibn Khaldun?"). Relevant to [[linear-vs-cyclical-paths]], [[secular-cycles]] and [[ibn-khaldun]]. Cyclical theories "embody prophecies", which WMP would read as forecasting with a decline bias. Note the difference from the WMP Dawn-Day-Dusk frame: these cycles are dynastic or civilizational and mostly generational, not thousand-year infrastructure lifecycles.

**No reform program.** Ibn Khaldun set out no reform and ignored al-Farabi's perfect city. Irwin notes the political philosophers barely affected practice in Islamic lands.

## Notable passages

The distance-from-philosophy opening (c1) gives Ibn Khaldun's own disavowals and Irwin's institutional context. The Averroes passage (c2) is the clearest statement of what separates the Muqaddima from city-bound political philosophy: ‘asabiyya and desert and mountain life. The pessimism paragraph (c3) is Irwin's key claim that the shared inheritance was a mood, and it also gives Ibn Khaldun's account of caliphate degenerating into mulk through luxury. The dawla passage (c4) shows the cyclical assumption carried in the vocabulary itself. The Crone passage (c5) sets the Khaldunian cycle against the Confucian one. The Vico passage (c6) gives a fairly full comparison of a Western cyclical thinker with Ibn Khaldun, with Irwin's own list of similarities.
