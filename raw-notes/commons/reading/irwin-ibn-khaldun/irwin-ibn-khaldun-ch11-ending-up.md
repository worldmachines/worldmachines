# Ending Up: Irwin's Verdict on a Medieval Theorist

Irwin's concluding chapter opens with H.A.L. Fisher's 1934 confession that he could discern in history no plot, rhythm or predetermined pattern, and sets Ibn Khaldun against it: a man who, having studied the bloody contingency of Merinid, Hafsid, ‘Abd al-Wadid and Nasrid politics, still drew from it laws governing the formation and dissolution of communities. Irwin then turns personal (writing the book as a "necromantic pursuit" or séance), argues that modern readings have lopped off the parts of Ibn Khaldun that do not fit, admits his inconsistencies, surveys the afterlife of his ideas (Arab academics, the *Arab Human Development Report 2004*, Zuckerberg), and ends on his austere, conventional piety and his closing appeal to some later scholar.

## Themes

**Laws against contingency.** Fisher's plotless, contingent history is the foil. Irwin credits Ibn Khaldun with a broader causal palette than Fisher (economy, climate, kinship bonds, and the operations of the supernatural) and says Ibn Khaldun believed his laws would explain not only what had happened but what would happen. Irwin also shows that Fisher himself smuggles in a Whig pattern of progress (Romans, Teutonic collapse, slow recovery), so that the disavowal of pattern is itself "a touch disingenuous". This is Irwin's judgment of Fisher, not a claim by Ibn Khaldun. It touches the WMP tension between cyclical and progressive history ([[linear-vs-cyclical-paths]], [[secular-cycles]]), though Irwin frames it as a contrast of temperaments rather than a test of the cycle.

**Against anachronism.** The core methodological warning: previous accounts making Ibn Khaldun the first sociologist, an early Marxist, an Aristotelian philosopher or a forerunner of Leo Strauss "have exaggerated Ibn Khaldun's rationality and posited an essentially secular frame of mind". The discarded parts are his Maliki jurisprudence, occultism, futurology and some bizarre scientific ideas. Irwin admits he himself failed to understand parts (the za’iraja, physiology, dreaming, soothsaying). Readers extending [[ibn-khaldun]] to WMP purposes should carry this hedge: resonances are resonances, not anticipations.

**Unsystematic, private, inconsistent.** Irwin suspects the Muqaddima's ideal audience was Ibn Khaldun himself, not rulers, jurists, merchants or tribesmen, and lists inconsistencies: the use of ‘asabiyya, badawi and "Arab"; whether the Mamluk system escaped the cycle of decay; whether the cyclical theory applied beyond the Maghreb; the causes of the plague. He attributes these partly to composition over a long time in varied places with uneven library access. Any formalization of the dynastic cycle should therefore treat it as a set of models, not a closed system.

**Reception and afterlife of ‘asabiyya.** Cheddadi sees lessons for anthropologists, Himmich finds Marxism in the Muqaddima, others hail an early Arab nationalist. The *Arab Human Development Report 2004* uses "clannism" as a pejorative, urban, stagnation-linked label, whereas Irwin says Ibn Khaldun's sense of ‘asabiyya "always had a positive and dynamic quality". This is a case of the retrofitting of a forerunner that Irwin chronicles throughout, and it bears on how group cohesion is framed as an engine of state formation versus a brake ([[chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders]] is a loose neighbour).

**Medieval premises, powerful models.** Irwin says few today would share Ibn Khaldun's positive vision of tribal loyalty as an engine of social change, yet he can be compared to Darwin, Marx and Durkheim as a builder of theoretical models from medieval data, even though those models "cannot apply to modern societies". The Zuckerberg quotation supplies a wry summary of how a modern reader values it. This is an early "science of society" claim with an explicit limit of applicability, relevant to the project's own ambitions in [[psychohistory-and-world-machines]].

**Piety, austerity, ordinary belief.** Irwin suggests, tentatively ("Perhaps"), that the ultimate purpose of the Muqaddima was to prepare Muslims for the Last Judgment, citing the hadith of the stranger and traveller. Ibn Khaldun's prejudice against luxury is called unreasonable, and Irwin adds that on many issues the Qur’an and Maliki law books "did his thinking for him". The book is thus also a guide to ordinary Muslim beliefs. The corrosive-luxury motif sits near [[civilization-undone-by-its-own-success]], but Irwin treats it as a personal and religious bias as much as a theory.

## Notable passages

The Fisher epigraph frames the whole chapter: a historian who sees only contingency, in contrast to Ibn Khaldun the generalizer. The "seas of thought" passage is Irwin's manifesto against Procrustean modernizing. The audience and inconsistency passages temper any systematic reading. The *Arab Human Development Report* and Zuckerberg passages show the reception history at work, and the Last Judgment passage gives Irwin's closing, hedged interpretation of the book's ultimate purpose.
