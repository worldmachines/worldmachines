# The Muqaddima among the mirrors for princes, and the disappointing ‘Ibar

Irwin makes two linked moves. First, he reminds the reader that the Muqaddima is not a purely historical or proto-scientific treatise: parts of it read as a “mirror for princes” (nasihat al-muluk, andarz), drawing on pre-Islamic Persian wisdom, Tahir ibn al-Husayn's letter of 821, and above all the pseudo-Aristotelian Sirr al-asrar (Secretum Secretorum), which Ibn Khaldun mistook for Aristotle's Politics. Second, he turns to the Kitab al-‘Ibar itself, the long universal history for which the Muqaddima was the prelude, and judges it conventional: organized by tribes and dynasties rather than annals, limited mostly to Arabic-speaking regions, weak on chronology, and only implicitly illustrating the laws set out in the Muqaddima. A closing comparison with Jean Froissart shows what is and is not modern about Ibn Khaldun.

## Themes

**The Muqaddima as wisdom literature.** Irwin insists the Muqaddima “does not restrict itself to strictly historical issues.” Ibn Khaldun acknowledges debts to Persian sages, to Ibn al-Muqaffa‘ and al-Turtushi, and reproduces a whole letter of advice on rulership that stresses the ruler's dependence on God. This is a corrective to reading the work as secular social science: its political thought sits inside an ethical, religious advice genre. Irwin also notes that the Sirr al-asrar mixed politics with physiognomy, astrology, alchemy, magic and medicine, and that Ibn Khaldun, whom Irwin calls “obsessed with predicting the future,” also cited pseudo-Aristotle on letter-number divination (hisab al-nim).

**The Circle of Justice.** The garden metaphor (world, dynasty, proper behaviour, ruler's policy, soldiers, money, subjects, justice, back to the world) is a closed loop of mutual dependence. Irwin says three such circles appear in the Muqaddima and that the Ottoman Turks were much impressed by it. This is a static, normative circle, quite unlike Ibn Khaldun's generational dynastic cycle; it resonates loosely with systems-of-mutual-dependence thinking in [[world-machines]] and with the decay of a functioning arrangement ([[decay-sequence]]), though it is a moral-political doctrine rather than a decline model. Irwin adds that Ibn Khaldun found nothing ideal about cities such as Tunis or Fez, so Aristotle's real Politics of the ideal city could hardly have been his model.

**A hedged biographical guess.** Irwin suggests, and immediately calls it “possible (though unlikely),” that Ibn Khaldun imagined reading the Muqaddima and ‘Ibar to a young ruler as Aristotle did to Alexander. If so he was disappointed; the first draft was dedicated to the Hafsid ruler Abu’l-Abbas in Tunis.

**The ‘Ibar falls short of the Muqaddima.** The ‘Ibar is over three thousand pages, tribe-and-dynasty based rather than year-by-year, and mostly restricted to Arabic-speaking regions, so it lacks the universal scope of al-Mas‘udi. Scholars Irwin quotes (Dunlop, Talbi, Brunschvig, Little) agree that it is conventional, largely abridges predecessors, and commits the faults Ibn Khaldun criticized in them. Note that these are the judgments of those scholars, with Irwin concurring; Talbi adds that no one man could have met the Muqaddima's demands. Irwin also records what the ‘Ibar does contribute: reports of Mamluk cannon use in 1367 and 1369, information on Circassian Mamluk politics from Barquq, Faraj and senior emirs, and a systematically laid out account of Maghribi Berbers and Arabs (territory, genealogy, tribes).

**Weak chronology and an unfinished demonstration.** Working with few books, Ibn Khaldun relied on memory. Irwin floats, explicitly “only a possibility,” that the North African chapters of the ‘Ibar were drafted first and the Muqaddima grew as a series of afterthoughts, so the narrative was never reconfigured to illustrate its laws. The ‘Ibar downplays personalities and anecdote and focuses on power management and ‘asabiyya, but “the reader has to work hard” to find the general laws in it. Compare the [[history-machine]] idea of large impersonal forces behind events, though Irwin does not frame it that way.

**Froissart as foil, and the “delusive appearance of modernity.”** Froissart's Chroniques celebrate chivalry, colour and dialogue and ignore causes; Ibn Khaldun analyzes, theorizes and generalizes, which gives him “the perhaps delusive appearance of modernity.” But he praised little save conformity to religious law, had no use for heroism, and thought the Black Death changed everything. Irwin closes by noting Ibn Khaldun did not treat history as a separate discipline, leaving it out of his chapter 6 classification of the sciences. This fits Irwin's wider stance against reading Ibn Khaldun as an anachronistic modern; see [[ibn-khaldun]].

## Notable passages

The framing passage on mirrors for princes and Tahir's letter (c1) sets the genre context. The Sirr al-asrar and Circle of Justice passage (c2) is the densest statement of Ibn Khaldun's borrowed wisdom sources, including his misidentification of the text. The assessment of the ‘Ibar as conventional (c3) collects the scholarly verdicts. The chronology and afterthoughts passage (c4) gives Irwin's hedged reconstruction of how the two works relate. The passage on the ‘Ibar's suppression of personality (c5) states how laws are only implicit in the narrative. The Froissart contrast (c6) states most sharply Irwin's warning about anachronistic modernity.

On the World Machines side, the nearest hooks are the reception of a cyclical thinker as a forerunner (compare [[giambattista-vico]] and [[niccolo-machiavelli]] as theorists of political cycles and princely advice), but this unit itself argues genre and historiography rather than cycles.
