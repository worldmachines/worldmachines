# Economics before Economics: Ibn Khaldun's Moral Economy and Its Modern Misreadings

Irwin opens with two modern retrofittings of [[ibn-khaldun]]: his appropriation as a precursor of Marx, Engels and Pareto, and Ronald Reagan's 1981 citation of him for supply-side economics and the Laffer curve. Irwin says Reagan (or his speechwriters) misread him and that Ibn Khaldun "was not an economist in the modern sense." The chapter then reconstructs what Ibn Khaldun actually says in chapters 4 and 5 of the Muqaddima: society arises from economic need and the division of labor in ‘umran; value comes chiefly from labor but is qualified by demand, scarcity and prestige (jah); commerce is necessary but morally suspect; density brings prosperity, and prosperity brings luxury, corruption and ruin. Irwin repeatedly shows the ethical, Qur'anic and occult frame behind the apparently modern analysis, notes empirical blind spots (rural migration, the Mansa Musa gold episode, the Mediterranean gold and spice trades), and closes with Engels's Orientalist reading and the Mamluk-era economic writers al-Maqrizi and al-‘Asadi.

## Themes

**Economic need as the root of society, but moral judgment over market observation.** Humans associate to earn a living; cooperation yields a surplus; "Prosperity is the concomitant of density of population." Yet Irwin says his ideas drew on ethics, hikma (the Sirr al-asrar's circle of virtue, with money maintaining soldiers and soldiers the dynasty), Islamic law and, most strongly, al-Ghazali's Ihya’ ‘ulum al-din, and that "Moral judgments played a larger part in his deliberations than did the observed movement of money and goods." He is not a guide to maximizing income, but charts the cyclical rise and fall of urban prosperity.

**A labor theory of value, with hedges.** "Profit is the value realised from labor" seems to anticipate Smith ([[adam-smith]]), Ricardo and Marx, but Irwin stresses that Ibn Khaldun also recognized demand, scarcity and unlabored property income. Irwin also flags what is missing: money lending is not discussed and agriculture and pastoralism are largely neglected in favor of urban commerce, crafts and long-distance trade.

**Suspicion of commerce, capital and salary.** Commerce is lawful but "tricky," gambling-like, and corrosive to virtuousness and manliness (Irwin notes Malik ibn Anas's earlier suspicions). Excess capital is deplored by hadith; land investments invite confiscation; rulers engaged in trade or monopoly damage the economy, as the ancient Persians knew. Salaried religious scholars are poorly paid, prompting Irwin's aside that Ibn Khaldun writes "from bitter personal experience." Jah, prestige, is nearly as economically consequential as ‘asabiyya.

**Luxury as the engine of urban ruin.** Density and division of labor produce prosperity, but for Ibn Khaldun sedentary culture (hadara) leads to "diversification, luxury, immorality, corruption, and death," and the Qur'anic verse on villages destroyed through their luxurious inhabitants is invoked. This resonates with [[civilization-undone-by-its-own-success]] and the corrosive-luxury half of the [[decay-sequence]]-style narratives, though here the mechanism is moral and divinely judged rather than structural, and the ratchet is cited to God's verdict on cities.

**The dynastic fiscal cycle, not the Laffer curve.** Irwin corrects Reagan: Ibn Khaldun did not think high taxes were the main cause of falling revenue. A new dynasty levies only the religiously lawful taxes (zakat, jizya, kharaj) and has desert virtues (badawa); later luxury and the need to pay armed forces in place of tribal warriors push taxes and arbitrary levies up, trade and work fall, revenue falls, and the senile dynasty cannot collect from outlying areas. The revenue distribution itself moves from the tribal following, to the ruler and court, to suppressing rebels. This links fiscal pathology to the dynastic cycle and the loss of ‘asabiyya (see [[secular-cycles]] for a modern quantitative analogue of fiscal-stress-driven decline, though Ibn Khaldun's account is moral and theological), and Irwin's point is that political and moral decline precede the tax spiral.

**Economics slides into occult science.** Hoarders are "persecuted by the combined psychic powers of the people whose money he takes away," and the Fez qadi chooses the wine tax because it is not haunted by the souls of payers. Irwin twice notes in parentheses that economic theory here "slides into occult science," and also that Ibn Khaldun credited astrological conjunctions for Eastern Islamic prosperity. This is the chapter's clearest illustration of Irwin's thesis that the apparently modern is embedded in a medieval frame.

**Empirical limits Irwin identifies.** Rural migrants flock to modern cities despite Ibn Khaldun's claim that cities are too costly; bigger cities are not obviously richer; the Mansa Musa episode of 1324 shows gold is subject to market shifts; and he attributed Maghribi decline to population loss (plausibly the Black Death) while ignoring the decline of the Sijilmasa gold route, European maritime dominance and the Karimi spice merchants. Irwin is careful to say what is plausible and what Ibn Khaldun did not consider.

**Reception and comparison.** Engels turned Ibn Khaldun's nomad/townsman collision into an Orientalist account of Islamic stasis (the Mahdi cycle produces no progress, unlike the Christian West). Irwin retorts that Ibn Khaldun would not have seen Islam as camouflage for economic greed nor grasped economic progress. Among Mamluk-era writers, al-Maqrizi and al-‘Asadi likewise attacked abusive taxation, monopoly and debased coinage and urged a return to gold and silver, but only Ibn Khaldun placed such moralizing within a science of society, ‘ilm al-‘umran. This chapter is a case study in how thinkers become [[terminal-medievals-vs-early-moderns]]-style retrofit figures, with Reagan and Engels as opposite political poles; compare the readings by [[venkatesh-rao-the-modernity-machine-iii]] only in the general sense of reading an older thinker through a modern frame.

## Notable passages

The **Reagan/Laffer opening** (c1) sets Irwin's anti-anachronism stance and names the two retrofittings. The **origin-of-society passage** (c2) gives the economic-need and division-of-labor argument at its most modern-sounding, which Irwin then complicates. The **population-density and luxury passage** (c3) shows prosperity flipping into moral corruption and divine destruction. The **dynastic tax cycle** (c4) is Irwin's corrective to the Laffer reading and the most WMP-relevant piece: a fiscal life cycle tied to the shift from desert virtue to urban luxury. The **qadi and wine tax anecdote** (c5) exemplifies economics sliding into occult science. The **Engels quotation and Irwin's reply** (c6) shows a nineteenth-century reading of the nomad/sedentary cycle as stagnation rather than renewal; the resonance with [[linear-vs-cyclical-paths]] is that Engels is contrasting cyclical Islam with linear Christian progress, a contrast Irwin says Ibn Khaldun himself would not have recognized.
