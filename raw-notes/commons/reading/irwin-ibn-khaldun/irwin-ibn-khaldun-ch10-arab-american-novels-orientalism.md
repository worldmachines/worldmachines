# Remaking Ibn Khaldun: Arab and American readers, novelists, and the Orientalism charge

This unit is the reception-history close of Irwin's biography. It moves through modern Arab scholarly readings (Taha Husayn, Enan, al-Azmeh, Cheddadi), through two recent American books (Fromherz's Sufi-inflected biography and Dale's Aristotelian one), through two novelistic afterlives (Bensalem Himmich's *The Polymath* and Frank Herbert's *Dune*), and ends by contesting Bruce Lawrence's claim that Ibn Khaldun was "a product of Orientalism". Irwin's through-line is that every reader tends to find an Ibn Khaldun in their own image, and that the fourteenth-century Maliki, occult-believing, strictly pious author is a poor fit for the modern-social-scientist mould. Irwin includes himself, parenthetically, among possible offenders.

## Themes

**Early Arab skepticism about "modernity".** Taha Husayn, trained by positivists at the Sorbonne, praised Ibn Khaldun as a genius but denied he was a scientific historian, sociologist or Kulturhistoriker: he relied on oral information, cared only about the state, and his *‘ilm* was "a body of knowledge" rather than science. Husayn read his religiosity as merely precautionary, which is his claim, not Irwin's. Enan, by contrast, called him "the founder of modern sociology"; Irwin allows a case for precursor but doubts the founder claim because the continuity of ideas between a fourteenth-century Arab and nineteenth-century French and German thinkers "is not there".

**The Machiavelli comparison, mostly to Ibn Khaldun's distinction.** Enan (and Cheddadi) parallel Ibn Khaldun and [[niccolo-machiavelli]]: both were political operators who withdrew to write gloomy, generalizing works. Irwin finds the differences "more striking": Machiavelli stresses fortuna, virtu, the psychology of rulers, war and rhetoric, and would keep religion out of politics; Ibn Khaldun seeks underlying laws, writes no guide to statecraft, is moralistic and religious, and has nothing like ‘asabiyya to offset. This fits Irwin's general refusal of anachronistic assimilation, and is a comparison of [[ibn-khaldun]] with a near-contemporary theorist of power that the unit resolves largely in favour of difference.

**Al-Azmeh and Cheddadi.** Al-Azmeh, aiming to free Ibn Khaldun from Orientalists, cuts him down: unoriginal, neither sociologist nor general theorist, with a gap between deduction and events; Brett's review calls this a presentation of a "freak or satyr". Irwin agrees with al-Azmeh that philosophy was a marginal science for Ibn Khaldun. Cheddadi stresses ethnography and anthropology (with the hedge that Ibn Khaldun did not care for kinship systems, rituals or material life), warns that *ta‘rikh* is not "history", and reads race rather than dynasty as the organizing principle, while insisting the message stays relevant.

**American readings and the deduction/induction argument.** Fromherz makes Sufism shape Ibn Khaldun's cyclical theory; Irwin thinks he was probably a Sufi but that the case for Sufi methodology would need "a lot more detail". Dale places him in a Greek-to-Montesquieu-Hume-[[adam-smith]]-Durkheim philosophical lineage (Aristotle, Galen, Avicenna, Averroes). Irwin concedes the argument is careful, but counters on several points: Ibn Khaldun borrowed philosophers' vocabulary without their full meanings (*muqaddima*, *‘ibar*, *burhan*); al-Abili taught as much theology and occult lore as philosophy; Galen appears only a handful of times, perhaps via citations; access to Aristotle was restricted. Irwin adds that deduction and induction cannot be hard-edged, and the dynastic cycle (tribal conquest, sedentarization, weakness, overthrow by a new tribal wave) must have been induced from the Caliphate, Almoravids and Almohads. He concludes that Maliki jurisprudence was a more important methodological model than philosophy. Note that this is a judgment Irwin hedges only lightly ("I believe") and that the count of scholars on each side is explicitly not the decider.

**Khaldunian novels.** Himmich (nonfiction *Khalduniyya*, novel *Al-‘Allama*/*The Polymath*) reads Ibn Khaldun as a depressed man in an age of decline and as a materialist and positivist, with lessons for the "Third World" about tribalism and despotism. Frank Herbert's *Dune* embeds Khaldunian structure: Bedouin-like Fremen, a survival manual called the *Kitab al-Ibar*, prescient spice, and an austere desert people corrupted by conquest and wealth under Mu'ad Dib. Herbert confirmed he had read the Muqaddima. This is a pop-culture case of the [[civilization-undone-by-its-own-success]] pattern, and of the nomad-conquest-luxury-decay cycle Irwin has called the dynastic cycle, refracted through fiction; the unit itself presents it as a debt, not as proof of theory.

**Orientalism, complicity and many agendas.** Lawrence says Orientalism arose from colonial power's need to predict its subjects, and Ibn Khaldun was a unique resource. Irwin thinks this overstates colonial complicity: religious motives (missionary, polemical, biblical philology) dominated until well into the twentieth century; Silvestre de Sacy, Quatremere, Von Kremer, Monteil and Lacoste do not fit; most Ibn Khaldun scholarship is by Arabs. He concedes De Slane's commission from the French Ministry of War and the selective, inaccurate quotation by Gautier and colleagues in Algiers and Oran. His verdict: many agendas, each making an Ibn Khaldun in its own image (Gibb's pious Christian finding a pious Muslim, Lacoste's Marxist, Mahdi's Straussian). He adds that modern disciplines' hunger for an ancestor, and the self-perpetuating gravity of a heavily studied subject, explain the proliferation, and calls the "gold star for modernity" "odd". Brett's line closes it: Ibn Khaldun "continues to mean all things to all men".

**World Machines relevance.** This unit is chiefly of use to WMP as a caution about retrofitted precursors: wanting [[ibn-khaldun]] as a founder of social science is a way of legitimating current frameworks, and WMP's own use of cycle theories ([[secular-cycles]], [[linear-vs-cyclical-paths]]) should note Irwin's warning that the cycle was induced from Maghribi dynastic history and set in a religious frame of ‘ibra. The Dune case is an example of a non-scholarly reception that sees the same cycle, but only as fiction.

## Notable passages

**Taha Husayn's astringent thesis (c1).** The first serious academic reading denies the modern-sociologist label on positivist grounds, and it anticipates Irwin's own emphasis on a medieval Arab bound by the conventions of his time.

**Machiavelli versus Ibn Khaldun (c2).** A compact list of contrasts; the point is that the comparison, though popular, flattens differences in aim, ethics and concepts (fortuna, virtu, ‘asabiyya).

**Deduction and induction (c3) and the limits of Ibn Khaldun's rationality (c4).** These carry Irwin's answer to Dale and his own summary position on Ibn Khaldun and philosophy.

**Dune (c5).** The clearest statement of the Khaldunian pattern in modern fiction, including the Kitab al-Ibar detail.

**Product of Orientalism? (c6).** The core argument that scholarship has many agendas and that readers fashion Ibn Khaldun after themselves, ending in Irwin's self-implicating aside.
