# Ibn Khaldun and Sufism: A Jurist's Mystic, and Not a Sufi Historiography

Irwin sketches the institutional background of Sufism (tariqas, zawiyas versus khanqas, the moderating influence of al-Ghazali), notes Ibn Khaldun's odd silence about the tariqas as social and political forces, and then asks whether Ibn Khaldun was himself a Sufi. Conceding that he never claimed to be one, Irwin assembles circumstantial evidence: his teacher Abu Mahdi Ibn al-Zayyat, the treatise Shifa' al-sa'il, and favourable treatment of mainstream Sufism in the Muqaddima. He then shows Ibn Khaldun's reservations (Shi‘i contamination, shatahat, the monism of Ibn al-‘Arabi and Ibn Sab‘in) hardening into a fatwa calling for books to be burned. The unit closes by weighing Allen Fromherz's thesis that Sufism shaped Ibn Khaldun's philosophy of history and rejecting it. This is the book's general method in miniature: Irwin puts Ibn Khaldun back into his religious and juridical world and resists a tidy interpretive key for the [[ibn-khaldun]] of the Muqaddima.

## Themes

**Sufism as a religious science, and a curious omission.** Ibn Khaldun classed Sufism among the religious sciences, and by the mid-fourteenth century the tariqas were, in Irwin's account, a crucial religious, political and social force, so pervasive in North Africa that Sufism "came close to becoming the Sunni orthodoxy" (Irwin, citing Michael Cook for the point that Sufism often simply was Islam). Irwin finds it "strange" that Ibn Khaldun does not discuss their social and economic weight, and instead concentrates on what was orthodox doctrine and practice. For WMP this matters: the theorist of group cohesion is silent on one of the period's most important cohesive institutions, which is Irwin's observation rather than Ibn Khaldun's own claim.

**Circumstantial case for a Sufi Ibn Khaldun.** Irwin is candid that Ibn Khaldun never explicitly claimed to be a Sufi, described no mystical experience, and was not called one by contemporaries; he nonetheless judges that circumstantial evidence "overwhelmingly" indicates he was. The evidence is threefold: study under the Sufi Ibn al-Zayyat and enthusiasm for Ibn al-Khatib's turn to the Sufi way in 1372; the Shifa', which holds that a beginner needs a murshid for the science of unveiling (mukashafa) or risks self-delusion or heresy; and the favourable Muqaddima passages, where Sufism is a religious science of austerity and devotion and, unlike theology or philosophy, "led to happiness". Irwin adds that Ibn Khaldun here "looked back with approval on a time when things were allegedly simpler and more austere".

**The jurist policing the mystic.** The Shifa' is not original (it draws on Ibn al-Khatib, al-Ghazali and al-Qushayri) and is shaped throughout by fiqh: the shaykh is "a faqih competent in the fiqh of the hearts", and René Pérez is quoted as saying the text reveals a jurist and mufti. Irwin's Ibn Khaldun wants Sufism given quasi-legal supervision, and his judgments of danger are framed by Maliki considerations of general welfare (istislah): the wrong Sufism, mixed with Shi‘i Mahdist preaching, produced uprisings by Fatimid-descended pretenders. This is a social-order concern, but stated in legal-religious rather than sociological vocabulary.

**Belief in the supernatural.** Ibn Khaldun believed in miracles (the early conquests, Muhammad's victories), in Sufi karamat that influence worldly conditions without being sorcery, and in majdhubs (holy fools) with access to supernatural information. Irwin presents this as a real belief in his own frame, not a rhetorical gesture, which fits his broader case against reading the Muqaddima as secular social science.

**Hardening against speculative mysticism.** In the Muqaddima Ibn Khaldun is noncommittal about al-Hallaj and sceptical of a prediction work misattributed to Ibn ‘Arabi (Irwin notes he probably knew little of the genuine writings and that a vast pseudepigrapha existed); Irwin floats, with a hedge ("It may be that"), that his real target may have been rival courtiers at Barquq's court. A fatwa reported from Fez, apparently issued in Egypt, condemns Ibn al-‘Arabi and Ibn Sab‘in for immanentism and monism, wants their treatises and Ibn al-Farid's poems burned, and now states that al-Hallaj was rightly executed; Irwin thinks the hardening probably came from meeting heterodox mystics and charlatans in Egypt.

**Against a Sufi philosophy of history.** Fromherz reads the Muqaddima's cyclical dissolution and rebirth as a macroscopic echo of the disciple's death and rebirth in the master's hands, and the zahir/batin move as Sufi. Irwin's rebuttals: obedience to a shaykh is not rebirth; ritual death and rebirth is a pagan rite foreign to Islam; in Ibn Khaldun a perished dynasty is replaced, not reborn (a pointed correction for anyone reading the dynastic cycle through [[linear-vs-cyclical-paths]]); zahir and batin were common coin (including among the Brethren of Purity); ‘asabiyya was not a Sufi term; seeking hidden meaning behind events also characterizes Spengler and Hobsbawm; and no other Sufi, including the historian Ibn al-Khatib, is credited with a Sufi historiography. Irwin grants Fromherz one point, that Ibn Khaldun was an elitist who, like al-Ghazali, held esoteric knowledge back from the many, and Irwin says the 1389 khanqa appointment carried financial rather than mystical duties.

**Reception.** Nineteenth-century Europeans preferred a rationalist, materialist, positivist Ibn Khaldun, and even later Hamilton Gibb, who disliked Sufism, ignored the possibility. The unit is thus also a small case of reception history: what readers can see in him depends on what they want to find.

## Notable passages

**Tariqas and the missing social analysis (c1).** Irwin sets out the scale of Sufi institutional power and the puzzle of Ibn Khaldun's silence, with Cook's line that Sufism was often simply Islam.

**The Shifa' and the murshid (c2).** The one text where Ibn Khaldun writes directly about Sufi practice; Irwin uses it, hedged, as evidence he was a Sufi ("it seems unlikely" he would have opined otherwise).

**Fiqh of the hearts (c3).** The juridical framing of mysticism, with the warning about tajalli, monism and letter mysticism, and Pérez's diagnosis of a jurist's mind.

**Miracles and holy fools (c4).** Ibn Khaldun's supernaturalism, and the Mahdist and Shi‘i worries in the next paragraphs of the unit.

**The fatwa (c5).** The most vehement of Ibn Khaldun's judgments, calling for burning texts, which Irwin explains as a product of the Egyptian context.

**Irwin against Fromherz (c6).** The compact rebuttal of a Sufi reading of the Muqaddima, worth keeping because it shows Irwin declining a seductive unifying interpretation; note that Fromherz's argument is here reported by Irwin, who says Fromherz has 'perhaps overemphasized' Sufism.
