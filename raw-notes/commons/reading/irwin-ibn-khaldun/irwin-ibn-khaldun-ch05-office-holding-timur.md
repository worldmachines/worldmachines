# Offices, Caliphs, and the Meeting with Timur

Irwin first summarizes chapter 3 of the *Muqaddima*, where [[ibn-khaldun]] compares court offices (vizier, hajib, na’ib, chancellery head, shaykh of mazalim) across the Maghreb and Mamluk Egypt and finds that their powers differ by regime and drift over time. He then turns to caliphal titles: Mamluk puppet caliph versus Hafsid and Merinid claimants, and Ibn Khaldun's view that the true caliphate ended with the Rashidun in 661. A short anecdote shows him holding twenty thousand dinars for a fallen emir in 1396 while staying reticent about it. The unit's second half, under the heading on Timur in Syria, tells of Timur's advance on Damascus at the end of 1400, Ibn Khaldun's decision to stay behind, his thirty-five days of visits to Timur's pavilion, the prophecies he relayed, the hostile and probably embroidered account of Ibn ‘Arabshah, the sack of Damascus, and Ibn Khaldun's robbery on the road home.

## Themes

**Offices are unstable, personal, and regime-specific.** Irwin's Ibn Khaldun does not produce a fixed typology; he records that a post's authority grows or shrinks with its holder and regime. The Tunisian hajib Ibn Tafraghin was more powerful than the sultans, and the office dwindled when he died; in Egypt the vizier lost authority to the na’ib, ustadhdar and dawadar. Irwin also notes that Ibn Khaldun's claim that the Merinids had no hajibs is belied by his own ‘Ibar. This is comparative institutional description rather than a general theory, and Irwin does not claim more. It rhymes loosely with the hollowing of formal offices in [[royalty-administration-and-antimemetics]], though nothing in the text frames it that way.

**The caliphate as an emptied title.** The Mamluks keep a ceremonial ‘Abbasid puppet; the Hafsids and Merinids take ‘Amir al-Mu’minin or the subtly lesser ‘Amir al-Muslimin. Ibn Khaldun, per Irwin, held that the true caliph enforces the Shari‘a as Muhammad's successor, that the Rashidun era ended in 661, that later caliphs were really kings (muluk), and that the Quraysh no longer had enough prestige to command group loyalty. The title lacks sufficient ‘asabiyya (group feeling). The argument is both religious and social: legitimacy is tied to law and to cohesion. A loose resonance with the [[decay-sequence]] (a title surviving as ornament after its function is gone), though Ibn Khaldun's frame is theological and dynastic, not infrastructural.

**Timur as a test case for the nomad theory, with Irwin's hedge.** Irwin says the meeting can be compared to a scientist examining a newly acquired laboratory rat; Ibn Khaldun thought the Chagatai Turks had best preserved Bedouin vigor untouched by luxury, and a new dynasty would presumably fall to sedentarization within three or four generations. This is Irwin's gloss of what Ibn Khaldun would have thought, hedged by "presumably". It touches the badawa/hadara dynamic and the cyclical decay of dynasties ([[secular-cycles]], [[chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders]]) but the unit shows the theory meeting a real conqueror, not a formal test. Irwin also observes that the Muqaddima and ‘Ibar had shown only limited interest in the Mongols, and that al-‘Umari was his main source on them.

**Prophecy, astrology and calculated flattery.** Ibn Khaldun told Timur about Maghribi prophecies (Ibn Badis and the 1364–65 conjunction, al-Abili, Sufi expectations of a Fatimid-descended conqueror). Irwin thinks Ibn Khaldun probably did not believe the conjunction foretold Timur but used it to put him in a good humor, and that he may have stayed in Damascus partly out of curiosity whether Timur was "the man of the century". Rather than a rationalist, this Ibn Khaldun moves among occult expectations, and Irwin keeps both belief and tactical use in view. His own judgment of Timur, as reported, rejects rumors of heresy and sorcery and stresses intelligence and love of debate.

**Source criticism.** Irwin treats Ibn ‘Arabshah's speeches (fulsome flattery, promises to serve Timur, the books in Cairo) as more likely his own literary style than Ibn Khaldun's, noting he was eleven and unlikely to have been present, yet also that Ibn ‘Arabshah elsewhere calls Ibn Khaldun the "pillar of historians". The claims of Ibn Khaldun, Irwin, and the hostile chronicler must be kept apart.

## Notable passages

The opening survey of viziers and their shifting powers (c1) is the plainest statement of Ibn Khaldun's comparative office-holding material. The caliphate paragraph (c2) ties legitimacy to ‘asabiyya. The nomad-as-laboratory paragraph (c3) is Irwin's key link between the theory and Timur. The prophecies paragraph (c4) shows astrology, Sufi expectation and flattery working together, with Irwin's hedge that Ibn Khaldun probably did not believe the conjunction thesis. The Ibn ‘Arabshah critique (c5) is the source-critical hinge, and Ibn Khaldun's own portrait of Timur (c6) is his main first-hand judgment on him in the unit.
