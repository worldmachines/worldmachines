# Foreknowledge, the Mahdi and the Last Days: Ibn Khaldun's futurology

Irwin proposes that the "chief reason" Ibn Khaldun was so drawn to sorcery is that it overlapped with divination and "the power to know the future" (his own judgment, offered with "I believe"). The unit sets out Ibn Khaldun's hierarchy of foreknowledge (prophet, kahin, ‘arraf), his view of where his own world was heading (peak of Islamic sciences, Arab age over, power drifting north to Franks and Turks, no progress and no reform programme, the End of Time near), and then walks through the sources of foreknowledge he discusses in the *Muqaddima*: the Mahdi and jafr prophecy, astrology, the malahim ("slaughterings") genre, pre-Islamic kahins, catoptromancy, near-death states, the za’iraja device, and dreams. It closes with a short excursus on Asimov's *Foundation* as a distant, Toynbee-mediated echo, and a gloss that "medieval futurology was political".

## Themes

**The Muqaddima's laws give no forecast.** Irwin notes that "the laws of history, as formulated in the Muqaddima, provided no exact guidance to what would happen next", in contrast to divination. Ibn Khaldun held that "the future resembles the past more than one drop of water resembles another", had no sense of social or technological progress, and proposed no reform. This is a cyclical, non-progressive picture, which Irwin ties to the belief that he lived near the End of Time. Compare [[linear-vs-cyclical-paths]] and [[secular-cycles]], though Ibn Khaldun's frame is eschatological and closes on God's plan (mashiyyat Allah), not on an open-ended cycle.

**A ranked, partly valid epistemology of the unseen.** Prophets had perfect vision of unseen things; the kahin a partial vision of particulars, sometimes from demons; the ‘arraf inferred from past events. Irwin says Ibn Khaldun reasoned "somewhat strangely" that because divinely inspired prophecy exists, lesser divination must be at least partially valid. He is inconsistent on whether foresight is God's alone or also available, immorally, to the undirected.

**The Mahdi is believed in, but disciplined by statecraft.** Ibn Khaldun reports the near-universal Muslim expectation of a Mahdi, Antichrist and Jesus, yet doubts elaborate Sufi and Shi‘i Mahdi lore, rejects the idea that he would appear in some out-of-the-way place, and insists that even the Mahdi "would need the support of ‘asabiyya". Irwin shows this as the meeting point of the theory of group feeling and the religious frame: politics and occultism as "two sides of a strange coin". Mahdi claimants (Ibn Tumart, ‘Ubaydallah) actually founded dynasties (Almohad, Fatimid); the Little Jafr was, as Ibn Khaldun rightly deduced, retrospective dynastic propaganda. This ties to [[ibn-khaldun]]'s nomad-revolt dynamics, although here the charisma is explicitly supernatural.

**Skepticism mixed with fascination.** On astrology and malahim he objects on grounds of social welfare (istislah) since prophecies incite rebellion, and denies attribution, yet quotes them at length, speculates on astrological causes of the northward shift of civilization and toys with a Jupiter-Saturn conjunction behind Timur. Irwin repeatedly flags inconsistency (astrology accepted as an ancillary of geomancy; the za’iraja denied to be sorcery yet impious for future events and approached with Sufi dhikr). Attribution matters: the skepticism is Ibn Khaldun's, the verdict of inconsistency is Irwin's, and the endorsement of astrology working for the Almohads is Fromherz's reading as quoted.

**Anxious futurology as politics.** Irwin's closing judgment ("perhaps the product of an age of anxiety") is that rulers such as Abu’l-Hassan, Barquq and Timur listened to diviners, and promises of foreordained victory ignited revolts. This is a fourteenth-century instance of prediction feeding back on the history it forecasts, a reflexivity worth setting beside the World Machines forecasting concern with stakes and reflexivity checks.

**Foundation as distant echo.** Irwin says psychohistory "shows the influence of the Muqaddima, as transmitted and popularized in Arnold Toynbee", with challenge-and-renewal and the Mule modeled on Timur, but adds that Gibbon's influence is "more obvious and more influential", and that Gibbon was retrospective where Seldon was prospective. Relevant to [[psychohistory-and-world-machines]]; note the hedge that these are "ghostly" influences. Ibn Khaldun himself offered no prospective mathematical science.

## Notable passages

**No progress, and the End near (c1).** Irwin quotes Ibn Khaldun's drop-of-water line, sets Hume's opposite claim beside it in parentheses, and sums up: preoccupied with the future, yet no idea of progress or reform, and little future left.

**The Mahdi needs ‘asabiyya (c2).** Ibn Khaldun accepts the Mahdi and the world's end but is hostile to specific Sufi and Shi‘i prophecies; the Mahdi must have state-building qualities.

**The Little Jafr as Almohad propaganda (c3).** Ibn Khaldun's critical-historical test: forecasts right for the past, wrong for the later period. A neat early case of checking prophecy against retrospective fit; see also [[history-machine]] for a different treatment of legitimating narratives.

**Malahim (c4).** The genre of prophecies on dynasties and the end of the world; Ibn Khaldun's denial of their attributions coexists with extensive quotation.

**The za’iraja (c5).** A letter-and-zodiac divination device Ibn Khaldun met in Biskra in 1370. He denied sorcery and cheating, suggested the answers might be implicit in the questions, yet limited it to the past and demanded Sufi preparation. Irwin shows the occult-sciences layer that the modernizing reading of Ibn Khaldun leaves out. Compare [[hermetic-magus-yates]] for the Hermes/Idris-Enoch thread in the European tradition.

**History and science fiction (c6).** Irwin's short reception-history excursus on Asimov, Toynbee, Timur and Gibbon.
