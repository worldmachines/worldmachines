# Ibn al-Khatib, Granada, and the Return to a Violent Maghrib

This unit does two things. First it introduces Lisan al-Din ibn al-Khatib (1313-74), the Nasrid vizier of Granada whom Irwin calls "perhaps the single most influential person in Ibn Khaldun's life": more famous and more productive than his younger friend, a devout Sufi, and a historian of dramatic incidents and personalities rather than general laws. Second it narrates Ibn Khaldun's career from 1363 to about 1379: exit from Fez, service to Muhammad V in Granada (including a 1364 embassy to Pedro the Cruel of Castile), suspicion from Ibn al-Khatib, return to Bougie as hajib, a half-hearted retreat to Biskra, renewed intrigue, and expulsion from Granada in 1375. The section ends with the murders of Ibn al-Khatib (strangled in prison in 1375 after a heresy trial) and of Ibn Khaldun's brother Yahya (1379), and with Irwin's reflection that the Muqaddima came out of, yet says little about, this "nightmarish turbulence".

## Themes

**The scholar-politician as a Maghribi type.** Irwin stresses that Ibn Marzuq, Ibn al-Khatib, Ibn Zamrak, Ibn Khaldun and Yahya were all scholars who took part in court politics, and that "It is hard to think of parallels elsewhere in world history." Careers follow one pattern: high office, betrayal, exile, prison, and often a violent death. Ibn Khaldun's intrigues "had been successful only in the sense that, at the end of them all, he was still alive." This is Irwin placing Ibn Khaldun inside his own milieu rather than treating him as a detached theorist.

**Two historiographies.** Ibn al-Khatib believed history supplies good examples (without it "virtue would die with its possessors"), wrote in flowery, poetry-strewn prose, and focused on incidents, personalities and motives. Ibn Khaldun, by contrast, was "preoccupied with the general laws that underlay historical processes." Irwin nonetheless notes that Ibn al-Khatib held that "all dynasties were in the long run doomed by corruption, greed, and ambition" and that history was "a vicious cycle of usurpations and depositions". That is a cyclical, pessimistic view already available in Ibn Khaldun's circle, though moralizing where Ibn Khaldun's is structural. This rhymes with the decline narratives in [[decay-sequence]] and [[civilization-undone-by-its-own-success]] and with the cyclical side of [[linear-vs-cyclical-paths]], but Irwin gives no theory of cycles here; he only reports Ibn al-Khatib's conclusion. See also [[ibn-khaldun]].

**Sufism, orthodoxy and danger.** Ibn al-Khatib drew on Ibn al-'Arabi and Ibn Sab'in, whose orthodoxy was questioned; this "eventually contributed to his violent death." He was tried for "the linked heresies of monism and incarnationism" and strangled at night. Irwin's aside that "There was nothing 'New Age' about fourteenth-century Sufism" belongs to his general resistance to modernizing readings: a Sufi could also be a powerful politician and a murderer. Ibn Khaldun called Ibn al-Khatib "a martyr" and wrote an elegy on mortality.

**Ibn Khaldun's ambivalence toward office.** He refused Pedro the Cruel's offer of a court place and his family's lands on condition of conversion. Irwin reports the suggestion that Ibn Khaldun dreamed of being Aristotle to Muhammad V's Alexander, but says "there are grounds for doubting this." At Bougie he reached what was "perhaps the peak of Ibn Khaldun's political career", collecting taxes from highland Berbers, which foreshadows the tribal-negotiator role the next section examines. At Biskra he claimed to be "cured of the temptation of office", yet was quickly drawn back in; Irwin calls the retreat "half-hearted".

**Failure as the origin of the Muqaddima.** Irwin notes that Muhsin Mahdi and Patricia Crone suggested Ibn Khaldun wrote the Muqaddima to understand why he had failed politically, and comments "there may be something in this" (a hedge, not a settled claim). He compares Machiavelli and Clarendon, who wrote histories once their careers were eclipsed; see [[niccolo-machiavelli]]. Irwin also observes, citing Crone, that government in the region was "both weak and oppressive", and that "curiously little of this nightmarish turbulence featured in the Muqaddima." He adds Ibn Khaldun's own argument (Muqaddima ch. 6) that scholars are unfamiliar with politics because abstraction and analogy detach them from particulars. This is a caution against reading the Muqaddima as a direct transcript of lived political experience.

**The nomad historian.** Irwin's closing irony ("The historian who was to write so much about nomads was himself a nomad", from Bougie to Fez, Granada, Tlemcen, Tunis and Cairo) is a literal itinerary, not a claim of theoretical kinship. The unit ends by pointing forward to Ibn Khaldun's ideas about nomads.

## Notable passages

The passage on Ibn al-Khatib's view of history (c1) is the clearest statement of the contrast between exemplary-moralizing and law-seeking historiography, and includes the odd fact that neither man mentions the other as a historian. The account of his heresy trial and strangling (c2) shows how Sufi heterodoxy served as a weapon in court rivalries. The 1364 mission to Pedro and the Aristotle-and-Alexander speculation (c3) show Irwin's habit of doubting flattering, philosopher-king readings. The Biskra retreat (c4) gives Ibn Khaldun's own words on leaving politics for scholarship and teaching. The Crone passage (c5) sets out the violent political setting and the Muqaddima's silence about it. The final reflection (c6) gives the Mahdi/Crone hypothesis, the Machiavelli comparison and Ibn Khaldun's account of why scholars fail at politics.

The reception theme (Ibn Khaldun retrofitted as a forerunner of modern social science) does not appear here; this unit is biographical and stays within the fourteenth-century Maghribi frame. Possible loose resonance with [[chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders]] and [[history-machine]] is not argued in the text.
