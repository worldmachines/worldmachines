# Irwin's Preface: Reading Ibn Khaldun as a Visitor from Another Planet

The preface does three jobs. It first assembles the modern acclaim for Ibn Khaldun (Toynbee, Trevor-Roper, Hodgson, Gellner) and his own claim to have founded "an entirely original science". It then gives a brisk sketch of his life (1332-1406; service at the courts of Fez, Tunis, Tlemcen and Granada; retreat to a castle in western Algeria and the first draft of the Muqaddima; Mamluk Egypt as Maliki chief qadi; the meeting with Timur outside Damascus) and of the structure of the Muqaddima and the Kitab al-‘Ibar. Finally, and most importantly, it declares that this conventional account is "at best only partially true" and sets out Irwin's programme: to resist the nineteenth- and twentieth-century habit of recasting Ibn Khaldun as a Western-style precursor, and to treat his strangeness as the point.

## Themes

**A thinker praised as a modern.** The opening quotations are all superlatives from twentieth-century scholars. Gellner's description of Ibn Khaldun as "a superb inductive sociologist" who used ideal types "long before the term was invented" is the kind of retrofit Irwin will spend the book questioning. Irwin also notes in advance that Bruce Chatwin, Frank Herbert (Dune) and Naguib Mahfouz drew on him (see [[bruce-chatwin]]), and that Toynbee's cyclical theory of civilizations was contested even by an admirer such as Trevor-Roper. This is the first hint that the reception history is itself a subject.

**Why historians err.** In Irwin's summary, the Muqaddima begins by asking why historians make mistakes, and names three causes: partisanship, gullibility, and ignorance of what is intrinsically possible. Earlier chroniclers compiled the zahir (external) of events and neglected the batin (interior meaning), the general laws governing the formation and dissolution of human societies. Ibn Khaldun assessed reports by plausibility, cause and effect, and comparison of similar and dissimilar situations, which Irwin calls unusual for an Islamic historian. This is a critique of credulous historiography and, loosely, a premodern attempt at a science of history (compare [[giambattista-vico]] and [[niccolo-machiavelli]], both of whom Irwin lists among the Western figures Ibn Khaldun is made to foreshadow); Irwin's claim here is descriptive of the method, not that it equals modern social science.

**An interpretive battlefield.** Irwin lists the questions modern commentators have asked: first sociologist or not, Greek philosophical debts, exaggerated originality, secret rationalist or atheist, Sufi, why so many enemies, whether the cyclical model of dynasties travels outside the Maghreb, whether nomads were good or bad, and whether he was a good prophet. He suggests, half in jest, that so many interpretations may mean Ibn Khaldun "did not express himself very clearly", and that following his fortunes in European scholarship traces "half the history of Orientalism". The cyclical-dynasty theory that WMP readers will recognize (see [[ibn-khaldun]], [[secular-cycles]]) is here only named as a contested question, not yet explained.

**Against modernization.** The core argument. Irwin says the Muqaddima has been selectively sampled and abridged, and its messages "modernized and rationalized", with Ibn Khaldun presented as precursor of Machiavelli, Hobbes, Montesquieu, Vico, Marx, Weber and Durkheim. His world, Irwin says, had more in common with the Qur’an and The Thousand and One Nights than with modern sociology. Two examples: monkeys placed below men in the Great Chain of Being is not Darwin, and a labor-based account of profit is not Marx's labor theory of value. Irwin's judgment, not Ibn Khaldun's, is that the dynastic and tribal-elite material is not "particularly relevant to modern concerns", since few regimes now rely on the military support of nomads.

**Irrelevance as value.** Irwin explicitly refuses to make Ibn Khaldun relevant to present issues, while allowing himself the reverse: using modern writings to clarify Ibn Khaldun by contrast. Reading the Muqaddima feels like meeting "a visitor from another planet". A premodern approach to society, in which causation rests on God's will, social organization aims at religious salvation, and angels and demons affect battles and prophecy, can give a modern reader a "fresher and perhaps more critical eye" on their own world. "The contrasts matter more than the comparisons."

**Editions and housekeeping.** Rosenthal's Bollingen/Princeton translation (1958, 2nd ed. 1967) is the working text, with Cheddadi's French Pleiade edition as the other scholarly translation; there is no properly edited Arabic edition. Irwin also fixes his transliteration (‘ayn and hamza), hijri dating, and regional terms (Maghreb, Ifriqiyya, Andalusia).

## Notable passages

**The chorus of praise and the self-assessment (c1).** Toynbee, Trevor-Roper, Hodgson and Gellner each rate the Muqaddima at the top, and Ibn Khaldun himself claims a new, "entirely original science" inspired by God. The mixture of divine inspiration and claimed novelty already anticipates Irwin's reading: the originality is real to Ibn Khaldun, but the frame is religious.

**Why historians go wrong (c2).** The clearest compact statement of the Muqaddima's method as Irwin understands it: three sources of error, and the zahir/batin distinction between recording externals and seeking underlying laws.

**The catalogue of unresolved questions (c3).** A map of the modern debate over Ibn Khaldun's identity as sociologist, Sufi, rationalist, futurologist.

**The modernization charge (c4).** Irwin's thesis statement: selective abridgement, Westernizing precursor-hunting, and the Darwin and Marx cautions. It carries his hedge ("it seems to me", "I get the impression") and is his interpretation, not a settled consensus.

**Crone and the case for irrelevance (c5).** Patricia Crone's warning that one must think away modernity to understand premodern societies, followed by Irwin's declaration that Ibn Khaldun's irrelevance is his interest. For WMP this is the useful methodological caution: any resonance between Ibn Khaldun and a cyclical theory of history ([[linear-vs-cyclical-paths]], [[world-machines]]) should be stated with the qualification that his cycles are dynastic, tribal and providential, not a general model of civilization.

**A different mode of causation (c6).** God's will, salvation, angels and demons: the premodern frame Irwin wants the reader to enter before comparing.
