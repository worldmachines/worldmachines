# The Enchanted World of the Muqaddima: Sorcery, Alchemy, Letter Magic and the Chain of Being

Irwin opens by conceding that the Muqaddima grew “looser and baggier” as Ibn Khaldun wrote, absorbing material on secretarial skills, dreams, mystical experience, pedagogy and the occult that is “not strictly relevant to the understanding of historical processes”, perhaps as “an advertisement for himself”. He sets this against the fashion for Mamluk-era scribal encyclopedias (al-Nuwayri, al-‘Umari). He then insists that the Ibn Khaldun sometimes presented as forerunner of Comte, Durkheim and Marx inhabited a “different and darker world” of talismans, spirits and an all-seeing God. The chapter surveys his beliefs: sorcery, the evil eye and dreams are real (Qur’anic warrant, personal experience, the Maliki Risala of Ibn Abi Zayd), yet forbidden; alchemy, astrology and treasure hunting are pseudosciences or frauds harmful to society; letter magic and numerology are credited but found obscure. It closes with the Ikhwan al-Safa’ (Brethren of Purity) as a possible model for his encyclopedism and for the chain of being, and with a speculation about why he attended to the occult at all.

## Themes

**Enchantment as the default frame.** Irwin’s core claim is that Ibn Khaldun’s belief in the supernatural was ordinary for a believing Muslim and shaped by revelation: the Qur’an attests to jinn and knot-blowing witches yet denounces sorcery. Ibn Khaldun claims to have watched a sorcerer at work and to have spoken with North African “rippers”. This is Irwin’s deliberate counterweight to the modernizing reading; note that it is Irwin’s judgment (“obsessed with the occult”), not Ibn Khaldun’s self-description.

**A taxonomy of the occult, with a moral test.** Sorcery works by will, by the celestial spheres or by letters and numbers, or by acting on spectators’ imaginations (perhaps hypnotism, Irwin hedges, though the word used is sha‘badha, “conjuring”). It is infidelity because it venerates stars or jinn. Sufi miracles (karamat) differ from sorcery morally, not phenomenally. Occult sciences are lumped with philosophy among the ‘ulum al-awa’il (sciences of the ancients) and tied to urban life, which counted against them for him.

**Sorcery is real but historically inert.** According to the Muqaddima, sorcery, the evil eye and dream-divination can be effective but cannot interfere with processes of human history. That is why Irwin later asks why he lingers over it, and offers hedged answers (social danger from Berber sorcerers and charlatans; fear of ecstatic mystics and soothsayers at Barquq’s court). This bears on how far the ‘asabiyya-and-dynasty account of history can be read as closed to the supernatural, though the unit does not itself discuss that account.

**Istislah and the social cost of the occult.** Ibn Khaldun, as a Maliki concerned with public interest (istislah), judged alchemy socially damaging: easy gold would destabilize economies, and alchemists were lazy charlatans or self-deluded; forgers and treasure hunters deserved amputation. He also feared that obscure occult texts might carry veiled Shi‘i political programs, as with esoteric Sufism. Here the reasoning is legal and moral rather than empirical, though he also offers a shrewdly practical explanation for vanished treasure (wealth passed to India and Europe, or was melted down).

**Selective credulity, and errors Irwin corrects.** Ibn Khaldun scorns alchemy and treasure maps (“Why should anyone who hoards his money…”) yet accepts letter magic (“cannot be denied”), while admitting the literature is “an unfathomable subject” and finding al-Buni’s distinction of letter magic from sorcery unconvincing. Irwin catalogues mistakes: Harut and Marut were angels in Babel, not Egyptian sorcerers; the Picatrix (Ghayat al-hakim) is probably by Maslama al-Qurtubi, not al-Majriti; Rosenthal mistranslated tullab (treasure hunters) as “students”. Occult authorship was pseudonymous (“Jabir ibn Hayyan” and “al-Buni” worked as genre labels), and Irwin echoes Adorno: occult wisdom is “the metaphysics of dopes”.

**Hierarchy of being and encyclopedism.** The Ikhwan al-Safa’ presented man as microcosm of the cosmos (“As above, so below”) and a graded chain of being. Ibn Khaldun’s Muqaddima passage on an “uninterrupted continuum” from elements through plants, animals and monkeys to man and angels echoes it, and Irwin says the Ikhwan “may have” shaped both his encyclopedism and his occult preoccupation. The chain of being lets some individuals cross to a higher stage and know the future, which leads into his meditations on prophecy.

**Resonances with the World Machines frame (partial).** The pre-modern cosmos of correspondences and hierarchy is a very different legibility regime from the one the [[modernity-machine]] built; compare the sensibility explored in [[hermetic-magus-yates]] and [[frances-yates]] on Hermetic and Renaissance magic, and [[giordano-bruno]]. The point of contact is that a pre-modern “science of society” sat inside an enchanted cosmology, which is what Irwin says separates [[ibn-khaldun]] from later sociological readers. The fit is loose: nothing in this unit concerns cycles or ‘asabiyya directly, and Irwin does not claim the occult content had structural effects on history.

## Notable passages

**The darker world (c1).** Irwin’s programmatic statement against reading Ibn Khaldun as precursor of Comte, Durkheim and Marx, quoting a description of a world of resemblance and talismanic power.

**Sorcery is true, though forbidden (c2).** Ibn Khaldun’s personal experience, the “rippers”, the lion seal “attested by experience”, and the sorcery/karamat distinction.

**Three routes to sorcery and its historical limits (c3).** The typology of will, celestial spheres/letters, and imagination; sorcery as infidelity; the claim that it cannot interfere with history; the death penalty.

**Alchemy as puzzle and social hazard (c4).** Jabir’s seventy treatises “read like puzzles”, the argument from istislah, and treasure hunters’ and forgers’ penalty.

**Letter magic accepted, but obscure (c5).** The one occult science Ibn Khaldun defends outright, with Irwin’s wry aside about his perplexing commentary.

**Why attend to sorcery at all? (c6).** Irwin’s hedged explanation (“perhaps”, “may have feared”): Berber charlatans and the occultists around Sultan Barquq.
