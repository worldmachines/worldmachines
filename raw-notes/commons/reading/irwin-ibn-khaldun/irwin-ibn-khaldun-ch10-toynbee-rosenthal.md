# Toynbee's Ibn Khaldun, the Anglophone Inheritors, and the Rosenthal Translation

Irwin narrates how the English-speaking world came to Ibn Khaldun through Arnold Toynbee, who found in the Muqaddima a "long-lost relative" for his twelve-volume, cyclical, pessimistic *A Study of History*. Irwin sketches Toynbee (classicist, Chatham House director, idiosyncratic Christian, ruin-haunted like Gibbon) and mocks his schema as an array of automata. He then follows Toynbee's influence through Hamilton Gibb, Albert Hourani and Marshall Hodgson, and turns to Franz Rosenthal's annotated translation and the criticisms of it by Gibb, Azmeh and Hodgson. The unit is reception history, not an account of Ibn Khaldun's own arguments.

## Themes

**Toynbee retrofits Ibn Khaldun as an ancestor.** Toynbee, hunting for thinkers willing to generalize about history (Thucydides, Polybius, Augustine, Vico, Spengler and others), praised the Muqaddima extravagantly while patronizing Muslim culture as a foil of darkness. Irwin notes that Toynbee worked from Quatremere's French and was swayed by Gautier's patronizing view of the Arabs, and that he "did not know all that much about Muslim history and culture" (he leaned on the now-discredited Banu Hilal narrative). Irwin treats this as a reading shaped by Toynbee's own scheme. Ibn Khaldun's career was fitted to Toynbee's laws of withdrawal, etheralization and return. Irwin says Toynbee's ideas are "almost entirely discredited now."

**Toynbee's cyclical model, and Irwin's (self-described unkind) caricature.** Civilizations as automata run by creative minorities through challenge and response, decaying into dominant minorities and a universal state, then a "fixed and doomed rhythm." Irwin flags the pessimism common to cyclical theories (Spengler) and E. H. Carr's dismissal of it as "the characteristic ideology of a society in decline." This is a useful foil for [[linear-vs-cyclical-paths]], [[secular-cycles]] and [[civilization-undone-by-its-own-success]]. Toynbee's 21 (later 31) civilizations and his rise-and-fall claims are a rough analogue to WMP's [[world-machines]] lifecycle. The resemblance is loose: Toynbee's engine is religious and moral, and the historians Irwin cites savaged his evidence. Toynbee's explicit debts to [[giambattista-vico]] are also noted.

**Where Toynbee disagrees with Ibn Khaldun on `asabiyya` and nomads.** Toynbee admired the pessimism and the moralizing portrait of decay through luxury and greed, and he approved the refusal to stop with merely human affairs (history needs a believer in God). But he denied that `asabiyya` was a nomadic monopoly ("the basic protoplasm out of which all bodies politic and bodies social are built up"; Renaissance Italians had it), and argued that nomads are a symptom of civilizational decay, not its cause. Most empires, he said, were not founded by nomads, so Ibn Khaldun's base was too narrow. This is a direct counterpoint to the nomad/sedentary engine of state formation (compare [[chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders]]). It is Toynbee's claim about `asabiyya`, not Ibn Khaldun's.

**Gibb: from drab assessment to the Shari`a reading.** Under Toynbee's influence Gibb revised his early view upward. His 1933 essay argued that Ibn Khaldun's primary aim was not proto-sociology but to show that history is an unending cycle of rise and fall because humanity will not follow the Shari`a, and that his thought was modeled on orthodox jurists. Irwin finds it undereferenced and one-sided (it "disregarded the detailed history"), yet thinks the focus on religious law "most plausible." This is consistent with Irwin's own anti-anachronist stance. Gibb also read Saladin's jihad, via Ibn Khaldun, as reviving social cohesion.

**Hourani.** Hourani absorbed a Khaldunesque preoccupation with the cyclical rise and decline of dynasties into *A History of the Arab Peoples*, and Irwin quotes his closing prologue passage on what stayed stable (language, transmitted learning, pilgrimage, belief in God).

**Rosenthal's translation and its critics.** Gibb's 1960 review lambasted Rosenthal's "moronic staccato," though Irwin suspects Gibb was really describing his own aspirational style and notes others find Ibn Khaldun's prose plain and didactic. Irwin praises the translation: it is the best substitute for a critical Arabic edition that does not exist, rests on manuscripts including one in Ibn Khaldun's own hand, and carries enormous annotation. Its uniform rendering of terms like `asabiyya` lacks flexibility but makes the Arabic recoverable. Azmeh disliked its literalism. Hodgson objected to "Arab" (he wanted "camel nomad") and to "supernatural" for `ghayb` (he wanted "invisible"). Irwin defends Rosenthal on both counts and argues Hodgson's real motive was to present Ibn Khaldun as a philosopher (faylasuf) with no truck with the supernatural. He calls Hodgson's Granada philosopher-king story unevidenced, and it may be an anachronistic projection. Hodgson's reading follows Muhsin Mahdi.
