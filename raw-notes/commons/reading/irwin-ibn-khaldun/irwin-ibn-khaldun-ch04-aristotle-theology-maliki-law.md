# Philosophy demoted, theology bounded, Maliki law as Ibn Khaldun's frame

Three sections of Irwin's biography place Ibn Khaldun inside the religious-intellectual world of the fourteenth-century Maghreb. First, Ibn Khaldun treats philosophy (falsafa, mostly Neoplatonized Aristotelianism) as a foreign import, one of the ‘ulum al-awa’il (sciences of the ancients), to be approached only after a grounding in religious law; logic alone is cautiously approved. Irwin adds that Ibn Khaldun's Aristotle was largely pseudo-Aristotle (the Sirr al-asrar, the Theologia). Second, theology (kalam) is valued mainly as a defence against heresy, following al-Ghazali and al-Razi, with an occasionalist view of causation that Ibn Khaldun endorses inconsistently. Third, Maliki jurisprudence is his home discipline, his model for the ‘usul al-ta’rikh (principles of history), and, in Irwin's account, the basis of a strict and austere personal ethic. Irwin closes by endorsing Hamilton Gibb's reading that Ibn Khaldun's pessimism about historical cycles is religious and moral, not sociological.

## Themes

**Philosophy as suspect import, logic as tool.** Ibn Khaldun's hostility to Neoplatonist philosophers resembles his rejection of the logicians' over-reach: they take speculation beyond what can be empirically known, and, he claims, damage religion and thereby society. Irwin notes this is also the background to his denunciations of alchemy, divination and treasure hunting. Logic (mantiq) is discussed separately and approved for use in theology, and his exposition seems to draw on the Organon "at one or more removes". Irwin flags a further limit on the Aristotle story: Ibn Khaldun took the Sirr al-asrar and the Theologia (in fact a digest of Plotinus's Enneads) for Aristotle's own, and Irwin adds that Aristotle's lack of interest in history would probably have made him uninteresting to Ibn Khaldun anyway. This is Irwin's judgment, hedged with "it is questionable whether". Philosophy is also given a Persian origin, taken by the Greeks when Alexander seized Persian books, and Ibn Khaldun laments that "The knowledge that has not come down to us is larger than the knowledge that has."

**He is a philosopher only in a loose sense.** Irwin allows that Ibn Khaldun was a philosopher in the dictionary sense of a lover of wisdom, but not in the fourteenth-century Maghribi sense. What makes him "almost unique among medieval historians" is his capacity to reason abstractly and generalize about social and historical phenomena. This is one of the few places in the unit where Irwin grants something that looks forward-leaning, and he does it in a single measured sentence.

**Theology: Ash‘arite, Ghazalian, occasionalist.** Ibn Khaldun defines kalam as arguing with logical proofs in defence of the articles of faith. He calls al-Ghazali "the first of the moderns" and follows him and Fakhr al-Din al-Razi in using dialectic against the philosophers, handled "like a skilled snake handler who must extract poison for useful purposes". The Ash‘arite doctrines of kasb (acquisition) and occasionalism deny any necessary link between cause and effect; cotton burns because God makes it burn. Irwin says Ibn Khaldun "seems to have agreed" but is not "entirely consistent", and quotes his warning that whoever stops at causes is frustrated and that the Lawgiver forbade the study of causes. Speculative theology has a narrow job (refuting heretics), and in the Maghreb "Heretics and innovators have been destroyed." A short aside says that souls are shaped by habitual activity, so regular prayer conduces to submission.

**Maliki law as home discipline and method.** Malikism dominated the Maghreb and Andalusia. It rested on Malik's Muwatta' and the practice of Medina, was conservative and hostile to theological speculation, honoured taqlid (authority), and gradually admitted istislah (public interest). Ibn Khaldun lectured on Maliki law in Tunis and served repeatedly as chief Maliki qadi in Egypt. Irwin's methodological claim is that the ‘usul al-ta’rikh (principles of history) was modeled on ‘usul al-fiqh: the jurist's tools for weighing transmitters and reasoning by analogy were reapplied to test reports of historical events, though Ibn Khaldun tended to reject long isnads. This matters for readers who see the Muqaddima's critique of credulous historians as a modern empiricism: Irwin locates its template in law.

**Desert simplicity, austerity and a strict Islam.** Ibn Khaldun preferred Malikism because it was the madhhab closest to the simplicity of the Arabian desert dwellers, whereas the other schools grew up in the cities of Iraq and Syria. This ties the law to his idealization of badawa and hostility to urban sophistication. Irwin sets out the strictness of his personal and legal views (options for Christians and Jews, jihad, penalties, austerity, male authority) plainly and without softening. Irwin also notes a tension: devotion to the law "sits oddly with his admiration for the wild Zanata Berbers who refused to submit to it".

**Gibb's thesis: pessimism that is religious, not sociological.** Irwin endorses Gibb ("It is hard to contest") that the Muqaddima's lesson is that history unfolds as it does because of infractions of the Sharia by pride, luxury and greed, so mankind is condemned to "an empty and unending cycle of rise and fall". Irwin adds that Ibn Khaldun did not think full restoration of Shari‘a rule likely, and that he could envisage a politics not based on prophets and holy books while still holding that revealed law served society better. Note the attribution: the Gibb block quote is Gibb's claim, adopted by Irwin, and is not something Ibn Khaldun says in those words.

## Resonances with WMP (carrying the qualifications)

The cycle of rise and fall here is a close cousin of the dynastic-cycle and luxury-as-corrosive themes tied to [[ibn-khaldun]] and to cyclical accounts such as [[secular-cycles]] and [[linear-vs-cyclical-paths]], and Gibb's phrasing (a cycle driven by luxury, pride and greed) rhymes with [[civilization-undone-by-its-own-success]]. But the resonance half-fits: in this unit the engine is moral-theological (violation of the Sharia), not structural, and Irwin explicitly denies a "sociological basis". The reception dynamic (a medieval jurist read as a social scientist) is also an instance of a later age retrofitting a thinker; compare how [[history-machine]] framing stresses assembly from within an era's own components. The occasionalist denial of stable causes is a foil to any forecasting ambition: a psychohistory of the kind sketched in [[psychohistory-and-world-machines]] presupposes regularities that Ash‘arite occasionalism treats as merely God's habit, though Irwin says Ibn Khaldun was not consistent on this.

## Notable passages

**Opening on philosophy (c1).** The framing paragraph: Neoplatonized Aristotelianism was mainstream, Ibn Khaldun distrusted its speculative reach and its damage to religion, but logic was separately and cautiously approved.

**The sciences of the ancients (c2).** Philosophy is classed with astrology and alchemy and must be studied only after saturation in religious law and jurisprudence; the books of the ancients are "avoided, as if they had never been".

**Occasionalism and the limits of causal inquiry (c3).** The Ash‘arite and Ghazalian denial of necessary causation (the cotton example) and Ibn Khaldun's qualified assent, including his warning that the man who stops at causes is frustrated.

**Law as method (c4).** The claim that the principles of history were modeled on the principles of jurisprudence, the paragraph most relevant to the origin of Ibn Khaldun's historical method.

**Desert law and strict Islam (c5).** Why Malikism suited him, and how far his austerity and legal severity went.

**Gibb's verdict (c6).** The block quote that recasts the Muqaddima's cyclical pessimism as moral and religious, with Irwin's endorsement.
