# Departure: Petrarch's Vision of the Venetian Fleet

Crowley opens *City of Fortune* not with a battle or a doge but with a private moment: on the night of 9 April 1363, Francesco Petrarch is woken in his waterfront house by the sound of a merchant galley bound for the River Don (and, for some aboard, onward to India and China) casting off into a storm. His letter describing the scene — awe curdling into moral unease at "this insatiable thirst for wealth" — becomes the frame for Crowley's larger argument: that departure, risk, and the sea were not incidental to Venetian life but constitutive of it, and that the book to follow is the story of the seaborne empire, the Stato da Mar, that this restless departing energy built and eventually lost.

## Themes

**Embarkation as civic ritual and metaphor.** Crowley insists that what struck Petrarch as sublime was, for Venetians, simply Tuesday: rowing was near-universal, and departure — a ferry to Murano, a war fleet under trumpets, the seasonal merchant galleys sailing for Alexandria or Beirut — was "almost as unconscious as crossing the threshold of your house." This experience was ritualized (blessings, prayers to St Nicholas, crowds on the quay) and endlessly restaged in Venetian art, from the mosaic boat in St Mark's to Carpaccio's *St Ursula* to Canaletto. The chapter's real subject is less Petrarch's storm than the fact that an entire civilization organized its self-image around the moment of casting off.

**The sea as spouse: appropriation and propitiation at once.** The Sposalizio del Mare — Venice's annual marriage to the sea, in which the sea's grammatical gender is feminized (*mar*, not *mare*) so it can be wed and thereby possessed — is presented as the emblem of a deeply ambivalent relationship. The sea is simultaneously Venice's defense (the lagoon's mud flats and channels repelled invaders), its opportunity (the route to riches), and its executioner (drowned sailors, cargo lost to storm or arrow). Crowley calls this a "manic-depressive streak in the Venetian soul," and the marriage ritual — appropriation of a dowry, but also placation of a dangerous partner — captures that duality precisely.

**Venice as the first virtual economy.** Unlike any neighboring power, Venice had "no land holdings, no natural resources, no agricultural production or large population" — "literally no solid ground underfoot." Its wealth was entirely relational, built on trade routes and commercial intermediation rather than territorial extraction, which made its prosperity spectacular but existentially precarious: sever the routes and "the whole magnificent edifice might simply collapse." The 1343 appeal to the pope, quoted at length, is the city's own diagnosis of this condition — trade is not one activity among others but declared to be constitutive of life itself ("we cannot live otherwise and know not how except by trade"), a civic-scale statement of the kind of self-perpetuating drive the [[Conatus]] concept names: a polity organizing its entire being around persisting through one specific means.

**A five-hundred-year arc, told from its hinge.** The prologue's structural move is to run time backward: it opens at a single 1363 departure, then closes by reaching back to 1201, when six French knights arrived in Venice "about a crusade" — the event Crowley identifies as the actual origin of the Stato da Mar. The empire's own trajectory, as sketched here, has the shape of a bounded lifecycle: audacious construction seized from the crusades, roughly five centuries as undisputed master of the eastern Mediterranean ("La Dominante"), and finally, when "the sea turned against them," a prolonged rearguard action fought to exhaustion. That construction-dominance-decline shape is worth noting as a smaller-scale echo of the [[world-machines]] Dawn-Day-Dusk pattern, though Crowley's frame here is a single city-state's empire rather than an era-spanning infrastructure — a resonance to hold loosely rather than press.

## Notable passages

Petrarch's letter (the storm, then the moralizing aside about the ship as "a mountain swimming on the surface of the sea" and his confessed "compassion for these unfortunate men") supplies the affective register for the whole prologue — awe inseparable from unease at the enterprise's scale. The description of the sea as Venice's "protection, their opportunity and their fate," running through the Sposalizio to the claim that "Venice shaped the world," is Crowley's clearest statement of thesis. The 1343 papal appeal is the book's primary-source anchor for the virtual-economy argument, and the surrounding paragraph — "no land holdings, no natural resources... the first virtual economy" — is Crowley's own gloss on it. The closing turn to 1201 is the pivot from prologue to narrative proper: departure gives way to the arrival that started everything.
