# The Sack of Constantinople, 1204

The chapter narrates the second and final crusader assault on Constantinople in April 1204 — refined siege engineering on both sides, a failed first attack on Good Friday, a theological propaganda campaign branding the Greeks "worse than the Jews," and a second assault on 12 April in which chained pairs of Venetian ships, a berserk French knight's improbable survival of a death-blow, and a monk's crawl through a bricked-up gate break the defense within a single afternoon. Byzantine political authority disintegrates in days — Murtzuphlus flees, the reluctant Constantine Lascaris reigns for hours, and a Byzantine ceremonial gesture of surrender is catastrophically misread by the crusaders as license for total sack. Niketas Choniates's eyewitness lament frames three days of massacre, rape, and the systematic stripping of Hagia Sophia and the city's thousand-year store of relics and art, followed by the political settlement: the March Pact's prearranged spoils, Baldwin's election as Latin emperor, the grim fates of nearly every protagonist, and Venice's quiet, unlooked-for acquisition of empire.

## Themes

**Contract before conquest.** Before a stone is thrown, the crusade's leaders convene to draft the March Pact — a formal, quantified division of booty (three-quarters to Venice until its 150,000-mark debt is retired, then equal shares), a mechanism for choosing an emperor, and a clause locking out Pisan and Genoese trade. This is Venice's characteristic move: turning the chaos of holy war into an auditable, pre-agreed instrument, wagering everything on the coming disorder being governable by contract. The gap between this legible paperwork and what actually happens — mass looting, promises to spare women and churches broken within hours — is the chapter's central irony.

**Two symbolic systems colliding.** The chapter's hinge is a moment of "tragic misunderstanding": when Murtzuphlus flees, Byzantine clergy stage the customary ritual of a new emperor's acclamation — icons, processions, deference — because in Constantinople's political grammar this is simply how regime change looks after a coup. The Franks, primed by days of sermons calling the Greeks Christ-killers "worse than dogs," read the same gesture as abject capitulation and licence to plunder. Two functioning symbolic orders read the identical scene and produce opposite actions — one expects continuity, the other total war.

**The sack as transfer of world-machine material.** Frankish crusaders hack Hagia Sophia's altar and icons into scrap, melting statuary — some centuries older than the city itself — for coin metal; Venetians, by contrast, loot "like connoisseurs," carrying home intact bronze horses, saint's relics, marble columns, and sculpted reliefs to physically construct St Mark's Square and Venice's own imperial self-image. The city that would rule the seas builds its legitimizing icons out of the plundered legitimizing icons of the empire it just helped destroy — a literal handoff of institutional material from a machine at the end of its Day (or deep Dusk) into the infrastructure of one at its own Dawn. Touches [[world-machines]] directly: infrastructure doesn't vanish at a civilization's collapse, it gets cannibalized into the next one's foundations.

**Contingency amid collapse.** Byzantine institutional authority does not decline gradually — it disintegrates in hours: Murtzuphlus's flight, Lascaris's refusal even to wear the imperial insignia, the Varangian Guard scattering rather than fight. Yet the assault that triggers this collapse turns on identifiable individual acts — Andrew of Durboise's survival of a point-blank sword blow, Aleaumes the monk's crawl through a breach, Peter of Amiens bluffing down the emperor's cavalry charge with nothing but nerve. The narrative openly courts "great men" historiography even as the political aftermath reads as pure structural drift (three emperors dead or blinded, a doge outliving them all by acumen) — a reminder that the individual-contingency register and the machine/structure register operate simultaneously, not as rival explanations.

## Notable passages

The two Durboise/Aleaumes assault vignettes carry the chapter's suspense: single soldiers' survival and nerve, not strategy, open the wall. Choniates's interpolated cry — "O City, City, eye of all cities" — marks the tonal break from siege narrative to elegy, and his admission he "barely knew where to start" licenses the fragmentary, accumulating horror of what follows: the Hagia Sophia altar "hacked to pieces," chandeliers and pulpit "chopped into transportable lots," monks tortured, women raped on the church floor. The "tragic misunderstanding" passage is the chapter's analytic center, naming in one paragraph the entire mismatch between Byzantine ceremonial expectation and crusader intent. Innocent III's verdict — "nothing but an example of affliction and the works of hell" — gives the chapter its title and is treated as a genuine historical hinge: the label that would be "regularly reapplied down the centuries," alongside the Venetians' total contemporary silence about their own role. The closing passage on Venice's empire — gained "overnight," unplanned, while ostensibly bound for Egypt — sets up the book's next turn: what Dandolo's gamble actually built.
