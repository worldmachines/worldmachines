# Return: The Fall of Venetian Crete and the Idea of Homecoming

The epilogue opens at Paleokastro, the ruined Genoese-then-Venetian fort west of Heraklion where, in 1669, Francesco Morosini surrendered Candia after the longest siege in world history. From this single silent ruin Crowley fans outward across the whole arc of Venice's maritime empire — Cyprus lost in 1570, Tinos clinging on until 1715, the muda convoy system already decaying by the 1520s, pirates choking the sea lanes, and finally Napoleon's 1797 entry into St Mark's Square, the burning of the Bucintoro, and the bronze horses carted off to Paris. The epilogue then turns from geopolitics to sentiment: the emotional weight the city held for the tens of thousands of Venetians — merchants, seamen, colonists, administrators — who lived and died across its scattered possessions, and for whom "the idea of return was potent." The book closes not on a battle but on Felix Fabri's 1480 homecoming, arriving by night under the Dolomites to hear all the bells of Venice ringing.

## Themes

**Attrition, not collapse, is how the empire actually ended.** Crowley refuses a single dramatic terminus for the Stato da Mar. Instead the possessions are stripped away piecemeal over two and a half centuries — Cyprus (1570), the muda system (1520s), the Thames galley route, Crete (1669), Tinos (1715) — a long, grinding subtraction rather than a fall. Only the Adriatic core, guarded at Corfu, holds until Napoleon delivers the symbolic coup de grâce. This reads as a textbook Dusk: a world machine whose institutions (the muda convoys, the colonial administration, the fortress network) keep formally operating long after they've lost the capacity to reproduce the order they were built for — a slow-motion instance of what WMP calls the [[decay-sequence]], and the whole two-century unwinding has the shape of a prolonged [[gramsci-gap]], with no new Venetian order arising to replace the dying one.

**Impermanence was Venice's own self-diagnosis.** Crowley notes that "There was always something provisional about the Stato da Mar," quoting the Latin motto on a collapsed Cretan house — 'The world is nothing but smoke and shadows' — as evidence the Venetians half-knew their empire was theatrical, a performance of permanence built on stone that would eventually revert to ruin. This is the [[world-machines]] frame in miniature: an infrastructure (harbours, arsenals, fortresses, the winged-lion iconography) that looked monumental in its Day but was never more than a temporary arrangement, now surviving mostly as scattered, decayed physical trace rather than living function.

**"Return" is the emotional infrastructure binding a dispersed empire together.** Where the first theme is institutional, this one is psychological: Venice, Crowley argues, was "a centrist empire which retained a magnetic hold on its people," and the fantasy of the ship passing back through the lidi bound together everyone posted to its far edges — the fondaco merchant in Alexandria, the consul on the Mongol steppe, the oarsman at sea. The empire's coherence depended less on administrative control than on this shared, potent longing for homecoming.

**Arrival is where the empire's fortunes become visible and personal.** The catalogue of returns — failed admirals in chains, Antonio Grimani surviving disgrace to become doge, Pisani's body packed in salt, Marco Polo unrecognized at his own door — compresses the entire book's cast of fortune and reversal into a single recurring ritual on the Venetian quays, where private and imperial fate are read off the same ship's arrival.

## Notable passages

The Candia-to-Napoleon sequence (Morosini's 1669 surrender through the Perasto flag-burial) delivers the book's final political verdict in miniature: a two-century, colony-by-colony liquidation ending in ritualized mourning rather than battle. The Stato da Mar paragraph, with its inventory of crumbling towers, angled bastions "powerless in the end against Turkish guns," and the *smoke and shadows* motto, makes explicit the theme of built permanence that was always provisional. The "idea of return" passage states directly the psychological thesis of the epilogue — the magnetic pull that held a scattered population to the city. The quay-side anecdote catalogue (Ordelafo Falier, Pisani, Grimani, Marco Polo) dramatizes how individual fates were legible only at the moment of homecoming. And the closing Felix Fabri passage, ending the entire book, replaces geopolitical summation with a single lived arrival — bells ringing, the campanile's angel gilded by sunrise, and Fabri's own words admitting that even joyful return carried "sadness mingled with our joy" at leaving shipboard companionship behind.
