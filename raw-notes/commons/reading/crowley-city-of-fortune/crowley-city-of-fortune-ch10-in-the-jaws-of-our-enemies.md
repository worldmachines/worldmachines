# The Black Sea Emporium: Venice, Genoa, and the Road to the Black Death

Covering 1291 to 1348, this unit traces how the collapse of the crusader ports and the papal embargo on trade with Muslim lands pushed Venice and Genoa's rivalry northward into the Black Sea — a frontier opened by the Pax Mongolica after Genghis Khan's conquests pacified the Eurasian steppe. It follows the uneven contest between Genoese Caffa and Venetian Tana, the wars fought over access to Mongol markets, the routine cruelties of the slave trade that ran alongside silk and grain, and ends with the 1345-46 Mongol siege of Caffa — where plague-ridden corpses were catapulted over the walls — unleashing the Black Death that would kill perhaps two-thirds of Venice's population within a year.

## Themes

**Geography as the engine of rivalry.** Crowley opens with the Bosphorus as a literal lock — a hydraulic anomaly (a surface current running one way, a submarine counter-current running the other) that also functions as the geopolitical hinge between the Mediterranean and the vast, resource-rich, dangerous Black Sea. Once Constantinople's fall in 1204 "sprung the lock," the strait's opening reorganized the shape of the entire medieval trading world, making it the site where Genoa and Venice's long war would actually be fought.

**The Pax Mongolica as integrating infrastructure.** Genghis Khan's unification of "the people with felt tents" and the subsequent Mongol peace across five thousand miles of Eurasia reads almost as an infrastructural event in its own right: banditry suppressed, arbitrary taxation curtailed, a highway opened that let European merchants bypass Arab middlemen entirely. It is a striking case of a conquering, violent order producing — as an unintended byproduct — decades of legible, safe, standardized long-distance exchange; the kind of macro-infrastructure that briefly makes a world's commerce newly *readable* and traversable, echoing how [[world-machines]] treat legibility-producing regimes as historically consequential regardless of their moral character.

**Escalation through improvised violence.** The Genoese-Venetian conflict is shown as a competition that repeatedly outruns rational calculation — from the massacre of merchants and the defenestration of the Venetian bailo, to the disproportionate 1294-99 war, to the catastrophic Battle of Curzola (where the losing side's cultural harvest, ironically, was Marco Polo's dictated memoir). The Peace of Milan settles nothing, and diplomacy at Saray and Tana is shown as a matter of trepidation, appeasement, and "walking on eggshells" rather than strength.

**Fragile forward outposts.** Tana's position — undefended but for a wooden stockade, entirely dependent on Golden Horde goodwill, granted only by the khan's sufferance — makes it a case study in the vulnerability of extended commercial infrastructure: a single merchant's act of private vengeance (Andriolo Civrano's killing of Haji Omar) triggers a massacre and the destruction of the entire colony. The "insatiable thirst for wealth" that Petrarch found baffling drives Venetians to endure homesickness, cold, and existential risk at the edge of the known world — a vivid instance of commercial [[Conatus]], the striving that pushes a mercantile republic past prudent limits.

**Commerce's normalized violence.** The slave trade at Caffa and Tana is narrated with the same procedural, ledger-like tone as the trade in silk or grain — ethnic groups "carefully distinguished" by market value, children stripped and inspected, a sixty-day death guarantee — underscoring how thoroughly human trafficking was woven into the ordinary infrastructure of Black Sea commerce rather than treated as an aberration.

**The same networks that built wealth carried catastrophe.** The chapter's turn is the direct causal chain from the Mongol siege of Caffa (catapulted plague corpses, likely apocryphal in mechanism but historically real in outcome) through fleeing galleys to Constantinople, then Venice. Crowley makes explicit that the very commercial ambition and connectivity that had built Venice's fortune for 150 years — its "rampant materialism" and "optimistic culture of risk-taking" — is what transmitted the plague bacillus back along the same routes, converting the city's defining virtue into its near-destruction and durably shifting Venetian mercantile psychology from expansive risk-taking toward caution (fortuna maris). This is a clean instance of an infrastructure's core strength becoming its lethal exposure — a dynamic [[world-machines]] readers will recognize as a machine's dominant logic curdling into vulnerability at scale.

## Notable passages

The Bosphorus passage establishes the chapter's central geographic conceit — a "lock" whose opening after 1204 determines everything that follows. The Pax Mongolica passage shows how conquest paradoxically produced a corridor of safe, legible transit that Venetian and Genoese merchants raced to exploit. The senate's own description of Tana as sitting "at the limits of the world and in the jaws of our enemies" captures the essential fragility of these forward trading posts, later borne out when a single act of violence collapses the entire colony. The slave-trade passage shows how thoroughly that commerce was integrated into ordinary mercantile bookkeeping, distinct from the drama of war but no less structural. The account of the Tatar army catapulting plague corpses into Caffa is the chapter's hinge point — the (contested but consequential) originating event of the Black Death's westward transmission. Finally, the reflection on the plague's origin in Venice's own "rampant materialism" and "expansion of trade routes" makes explicit the chapter's central irony: the connectivity that built the Venetian world machine also broke it.
