# The Shield of Christendom

Covering 1453 to 1464, this chapter tracks Venice's transformation into the permanent front line against Sultan Mehmet II after the fall of Constantinople: relentless refortification of Crete, Negroponte, Modon and Coron; a running shadow war of spies, bribery and repeated assassination attempts against the sultan; and Venice's failed attempt to convert its strategic position into a genuine pan-Christian crusade, which collapses ignominiously at Ancona alongside the dying Pope Pius II in 1464.

## Themes

**Mehmet II as a force of nature.** The chapter opens with Giacomo de' Languschi's chilling character study of the twenty-six-year-old sultan — polyglot, map-obsessed, modeling himself on Alexander, treating geography and military technology as pure instruments of conquest. Crowley uses this portrait to frame the whole chapter's stakes: a ruler who believes "there must... be only one empire, one faith, and one sovereignty in the world," and who will cause an estimated eight hundred thousand deaths across thirty years of near-continuous war. His unpredictability — bloodless mercy at Athens, massacre at Coron and Modon despite a flag of truce — is itself a strategic weapon, keeping Venice permanently off balance.

**Venice as a permanently mobilized front-line state.** The Stato da Mar's administration after 1453 reads as an unbroken ledger of anxiety: Greek refugees flooding into Corfu, a Cretan revolt suppressed by torture and exile, plague quarantines layered onto Ottoman raids, and constant requisitions of gunpowder, breastplates, masons and galleys for Negroponte, Modon and Coron. The senate's own dictum captures the condition precisely — a state of undeclared, unending war beneath a nominal peace. This is less a single crisis than a permanent operating mode, a garrison-state legibility (ledgers, requisitions, fortification schedules) mobilized against an enemy who refuses to fight on declared terms.

**The shadow war beneath diplomatic ritual.** Behind the formal apparatus of the bailo in Constantinople — a post whose occupants risked Girolamo Minotto's fate (beheading) on one side and the Council of Ten's punitive discipline on the other — Venice ran a parallel campaign of coded messages, bribery, and at least fourteen authorized attempts to poison Mehmet between 1456 and 1479, using operatives from a Dalmatian seaman to the sultan's own physician. The instructions to the would-be assassins ("no witnesses, nothing written down") show a state fully capable of secrecy and covert action when its survival is at stake, alongside the Ottomans' mirroring network of informers within Venetian territory.

**The bankruptcy of the crusading ideal.** Pope Pius II's crusade is the chapter's counter-plot to Venetian pragmatism: rhetorically ardent, institutionally hollow. Pius genuinely believed Christendom might spontaneously rise as in 1201, even drafting fantasy letters urging Mehmet's conversion — but Crowley is blunt that "the pope was hundreds of years too late," with Europe now "too nationalistic, too divided, too materialistic, too secular" for the old unity to reconstitute. Pius's own private cynicism about Venetian motives (suppressed from the printed Commentaries) and the Florentine ambassador's accusation that the whole venture is a Venetian land-grab in disguise both puncture the crusading frame from within — Christendom-as-unity survives mainly as ceremony and tithe-collection, its substance already gone. This reads as a paradigm running on inherited ritual rather than function — the sort of decayed, largely ornamental infrastructure a [[decay-sequence]] framework would recognize, and the interregnum between a dead unifying ideal and any not-yet-existing replacement has the texture of a [[gramsci-gap]].

**Venice's self-interested but structurally correct position.** Every Italian rival — Florence, Milan, Naples — accuses Venice of using the Ottoman threat to expand its own empire, and Crowley does not deny the charge; Venice's own crusade counterblasts are explicitly "creative." Yet the chapter's closing judgment reverses the moral framing: whatever Venice's motives, it alone stood as the actual buffer, and the rest of Italy's refusal to reinforce that buffer would prove catastrophic "in bitter circumstances sixteen years later."

## Notable passages

Languschi's portrait of Mehmet sets the personal register for the whole chapter — an intelligence and ambition rendered in almost novelistic close-up before the narrative widens into institutional history. The senate's paired refortification order captures the chapter's central administrative fact: peace with the Ottomans was never more than a working fiction. The extended account of the assassination plots is the chapter's most striking revelation of realpolitik beneath the diplomatic surface — a state coldly weighing ten-thousand-ducat rewards for regicide while maintaining an official ambassador at the target's court. The Florentine ambassador's outburst and Pius's suppressed private judgment of the Venetians work together to show the crusade project collapsing under the weight of everyone's mutual cynicism, Venice included. The final passage on Venice being strategically right, despite its self-interest, is Crowley's summary verdict, setting up the disaster to come.
