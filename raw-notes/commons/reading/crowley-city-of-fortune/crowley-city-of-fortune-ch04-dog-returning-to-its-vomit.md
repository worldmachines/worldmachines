# 'A Dog Returning to its Vomit'

Covering October 1202 to June 1203, this chapter traces how the Fourth Crusade, ostensibly bound for Egypt or the Holy Land, is twice diverted by financial necessity and Venetian statecraft: first to sack the Christian city of Zara in payment of a crusader debt to Venice, and then — after a suspiciously well-timed offer from the pretender Alexius Angelus — toward Constantinople itself. Pope Innocent III's authority collapses in real time across the episode: his excommunications are suppressed, his absolutions forged, and his letters outpaced by events, culminating in his furious verdict that the reoffending crusaders are like 'a dog returning to its vomit.'

## Themes

**Debt as the crusade's real engine.** Crowley repeatedly frames the outstanding thirty-four thousand marks owed to Venice — and the ticking clock of the one-year maritime lease — as the structural force actually steering the expedition, more than any leader's plan or piety. Dandolo's reasoning at Zara ('we could have a reasonable excuse for going there and taking the provisions') and later at Constantinople shows debt functioning as a mechanism of control that out-argues theology. Venice's precise contractual and ledger-based leverage over a nominally sacred enterprise is an early instance of the accounting logic — deadlines, debts, quantified obligations — that would later become central to the [[modernity-machine]]'s [[legibility-as-machine-core]], though here it operates through a single mercantile state's shrewdness rather than any generalized infrastructure.

**Papal authority losing its grip on events it claims to command.** Innocent III issues escalating threats and excommunications — the Zara interdict, the general excommunication of the Venetians, the 'dog returning to its vomit' letter — and every one is either evaded (a bishops' unauthorized absolution), suppressed by the crusade's own leaders, or simply overtaken by the fleet's progress. His own closing lament, that 'the world ebbs and flows like the sea,' is a rare moment of a nominal authority admitting he cannot steer what he formally rules — a portrait of legitimacy dissolving into performance while material events assemble their own momentum, resonant with how a [[history-machine]] gathers force from distributed pressures rather than a single ruler's design.

**Elite maneuvering versus rank-and-file resistance.** The chapter repeatedly counterpoises the common crusaders — impoverished, betrayed, kept from the Zara spoils, and later dismissed by Robert of Clari's blunt 'Bah! What are we doing with Constantinople?' — against a leadership (Dandolo, Boniface of Montferrat, the inner circle of barons) that overrides objections through suppression of letters, staged consultation, and emotional theater. The Corfu reconciliation scene, where barons weep and kneel before defecting knights to hold the army together, is presented as calculated manipulation dressed as spontaneous feeling.

**Sacred rhetoric weaponized by every party.** Both sides deploy biblical analogy for essentially commercial and political ends: Dandolo defies the abbot of Vaux and 'the pope in Rome' outright; Innocent casts Dandolo as Pharaoh enslaving the 'children of Israel' and the Venetians as the thieves in the Good Samaritan parable; Alexius Angelus's ambassadors dress a bribe ('two hundred thousand silver marks') in the language of restoring rightful inheritance and reuniting the churches. Piety functions throughout as negotiating currency rather than constraint.

## Notable passages

Dandolo's open defiance of papal authority at Zara — 'not even for the pope!' — establishes early that Venetian commercial interest will not yield to religious sanction. The Angelus proposal's extravagant terms show how a single lucrative offer could recast a stalled, indebted crusade's entire trajectory. Innocent's 'dog returning to its vomit' letter is the chapter's title passage and its moral center of gravity, marking the moment papal condemnation becomes rhetorically maximal precisely as it becomes practically powerless. The stage-managed reconciliation at Corfu and Robert of Clari's plainspoken objection frame the recurring rift between manipulated masses and maneuvering elites. Innocent's final image of a world that 'ebbs and flows like the sea' closes the chapter on the note that events have outrun any single actor's intent — Venice's included.
