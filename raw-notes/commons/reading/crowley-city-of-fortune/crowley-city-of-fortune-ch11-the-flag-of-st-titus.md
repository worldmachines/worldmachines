# The Flag of St Titus

This chapter tracks two decades (1348-1368) in which Venice, still reeling from the Black Death, fights the chaotic third Genoese war for control of the Bosphorus and the Black Sea grain and slave routes, loses catastrophically at Porto Longo, watches the Ottomans cross into Gallipoli for good, and then confronts an unprecedented internal crisis when its own colonial nobility on Crete — not the subject Greek population, but Venetians themselves — rebel against taxation and rule from the lagoon in the Revolt of St Titus, a rebellion crushed only after four years of guerrilla war and exemplary terror.

## Themes

**Plague hollows out the machinery of Venetian sea power, not just its numbers.** The Black Death doesn't just shrink the fleets Venice can put to sea (from seventy galleys in 1294 to thirty-five in 1350) — it accelerates a class rift between aristocratic commanders eating good bread and oarsmen eating millet, driving ordinary citizens to hire colonial substitutes rather than row themselves. Crowley frames this explicitly as the fraying of a civic "solidarity" and "discipline" that had underwritten Venetian naval dominance, with consequences that would resurface a generation later — a slow legibility-and-cohesion failure inside the machine of the Stato da Mar itself.

**Three powers converge on a dying Byzantium, and only two of them are watching each other.** Venice and Genoa fight with mounting bitterness over Constantinople's carcass — the 1352 Battle of the Bosphorus is rendered as a night of total chaotic violence, "a version of hell" with no strategy, only luck — while both miss the real long-term threat: the Ottomans, ferried into Gallipoli by Genoa itself for a ducat a head in 1354, a bargain Crowley calls the worst day's work Genoa or Christendom ever did. The republics' zero-sum contest over legible trade routes blinds each to the rising order neither controls.

**Venice's extractive machinery of legibility turns on its own colonists.** The chapter details an obsessively itemized Venetian tax vocabulary — capinicho, acrosticho, zovatico, arico, commerclum, tansa, angariae — applied "impersonally" across the empire, falling hardest on Crete as the empire's fiscal and logistical nerve center. This granular, universalizing apparatus of measurement and extraction (a clear resonance with [[legibility-as-machine-core]]) is what finally breaks: a single new harbor tax at Candia in 1363 triggers the Revolt of St Titus.

**The Revolt of St Titus is a colonial identity coming apart at the seam between two orders.** For 150 years Crete's revolts had been Greek uprisings against Venetian rule; this one is different — Venetian feudatories themselves, families like Gradenigo, Venier, and Dandolo who had "provided doges, admirals and merchant princes," rebel against the mother city. Crowley notes they had by now intermarried with Greek clans, spoke Greek, and leaned toward Orthodoxy: "Crete was starting to conquer the conquerors." The old Venetian ideal of ethnic purity and the new hybrid Cretan-Venetian reality can no longer both hold, and the debate over whether to fly the flag of St Mark or St Titus — followed by a raised flag hung upside down as an ill omen — reads as a textbook [[gramsci-gap]] moment: an old legitimacy visibly dying while a coherent successor order fails to be born, producing exactly the "morbid symptoms" the term names — night-time assassinations of family friends over ideology, factional purges, a proposal to hand the island to the Genoese, mutilated corpses, terror in both directions — before the old order is violently restored.

## Notable passages

The 1352 Battle of the Bosphorus passage is the chapter's set-piece of pure contingency: a hundred galleys jammed into a mile-wide strait in a winter gale, fighting blind in the dark, unable even to distinguish friend from foe — "There was no strategy or control. Outcomes were decided by luck." It exemplifies Crowley's recurring point that these wars were decided as much by weather and chaos as by admiralship.

The taxation catalogue (capinicho, acrosticho, angariae, and the rest) is worth reading closely as a portrait of a genuinely bureaucratic, Byzantine-derived extractive state centuries before industrial modernity — Crete paying for crusades, biscuit, oarsmen, and harbor works it never directly benefited from.

The flag debate — St Mark or St Titus, then the banner raised upside down — is the chapter's clearest symbolic hinge, marking the moment colonial ambivalence becomes open rupture, framed by the chronicler de Monacis as an omen the rebels should have heeded.

The Milletus assassination of Andreas Corner ("friendship gives way to religion, liberty and the eradication of you schismatics from this island, which is our birthright") stages in miniature the ideological violence tearing families and factions apart once the old segregation between Venetian and Greek stopped mapping cleanly onto anyone's actual loyalties.

The chapter closes on the revolt's brutal mopping-up — a four-year scorched-earth campaign, mass executions, desertified rebel highlands kept fallow "for a century" — and the flat observation that Genoa, invited to intervene, held back only because the pope demanded Catholic unity, not from any real change of heart: the underlying rivalry was merely paused, not resolved.
