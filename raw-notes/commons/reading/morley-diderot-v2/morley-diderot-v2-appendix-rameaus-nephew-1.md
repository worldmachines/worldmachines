# Rameau's Nephew, opening scenes: the philosopher meets the parasite

This unit is the first part of the Appendix: John Morley's own English translation of Diderot's dialogue *Le Neveu de Rameau*. The words and positions are Diderot's (as rendered by Morley), not Morley's argument; Morley's only voice here is the bracketed note saying he has omitted pages about forgotten personages and the technical musical discussion. The speakers are `_I._` (the philosopher-narrator, "Moi") and `_He._` (the nephew of the composer Rameau, "Lui"). The narrator first sketches his habit of idle thinking in the Palais Royal and the chess-players of the Regency coffee-house. He then draws a portrait of the nephew as a mixture of elevation and lowness who lives hand to mouth, and explains why such "originals" are useful to a company. After the nephew's ejection from the houses that fed him is recounted, the dialogue begins: it moves through chess, the uncle (the famous composer), and a long debate over whether men of genius are a blessing or a curse, Racine and [[voltaire]] as test cases, and the nephew's confession of envy, ending in a mimed fantasy of fame.

## Themes

**The eccentric as social solvent (the narrator's view).** The narrator says he does not rate these originals highly, yet argues that one in a company is "a piece of leaven" that breaks "that wearisome uniformity which our bringing-up, our social conventions, and our arbitrary fashions have introduced" and unmasks rogues. This is I's defence of the nephew's usefulness, set before He speaks.

**Genius versus ordinary goodness.** He argues that men of genius are "good only for one thing", indifferent to family and citizenship, that they "change the face of the globe" at the cost of infinite disturbance, and that the world is better run by moderate duty and flattery of the prior (the "wisdom of Rabelais"). He also reports a minister's proof that falsehood is more useful than truth. I replies that falsehood may help for a moment but harms in the long run, that truth benefits in the long run, and that society with good laws would not persecute genius (Socrates is the example). He answers with the realist's objection that Socrates was still condemned and defied law. These are the characters' positions, and the dialogue does not settle them.

**Racine and Voltaire: the ledger of vice and posterity.** Offered a choice between a decent tradesman and a spiteful author of great tragedies, He prefers, for the people around the man, the tradesman, who would have funded dinners, wine and a patron's table. I answers with a long-run, species-level calculus: a distant posterity will weep at Racine and envy France, his harm fell on few and dead people, and he is a tree that stunted a few neighbours but shades all comers. I applies the same logic to [[voltaire]] and Greuze: cooling their vanity would extinguish the talent. This is a utilitarian-style trade of private vice for public, long-horizon good, voiced by Diderot's I rather than endorsed by Morley in this unit.

**Optimism, necessity and self-interest.** When He asks why nature, if powerful and wise, did not make the great also good, I says that if all were excellent nothing would be. He replies that the best order of things is the one that includes him, and I generalises: whoever indicts the order of things renounces his own existence. I then suggests the whole is perhaps neither good nor ill "if it is necessary, as so many good folk suppose", a hedge toward determinism that He declines to follow ("I never meddle with" philosophy). The exchange is a miniature of the Enlightenment debate over theodicy and necessity, played comically.

**Envy, mediocrity and the economy of reputation.** He confesses he is full of envy, enjoys hearing of great men's discredit because it "brings us nearer to a level", and is mortified at his own mediocrity. His mimed daydream (fine house, flatterers, being told in the morning that he is a great man) displays reputation as a social machine of praise that manufactures a sense of self, and shows the parasite's dependence on it. A World Machines reader might see in this a small-scale picture of patronage and reputation networks, though that is an interpretive rhyme (the text itself is about one man's psychology, not an institutional system).

## Notable passages

**The Palais Royal opening (c1).** The narrator's self-portrait: he sits "by himself and musing", letting his mind run after "the first idea that offers", comparing thoughts to young beaux following nymphs. It sets the dialogue's register of free, libertine association of ideas.

**On originals (c2).** I's defence of eccentrics as leaven. Note the discipline of the attribution: this is the narrator's view, introduced before the nephew speaks.

**Against genius (c3).** He's speech that genius is good for one thing only, and his dismissal of history and learning, with the minister's claim that falsehood is more useful than truth. A comic inversion of the Enlightenment commitment to truth, which I rebuts shortly after.

**The tree and the ledger (c4).** I's long defence of Racine and Voltaire against domestic virtue: one of the dialogue's most quoted images, with its forward-looking appeal to "centuries to come, and peoples yet unborn".

**The best of worlds (c5).** The exchange on existence, nature's wisdom and necessity.

**Envy confessed (c6).** He's honest account of resentment and the pleasure of others' discredit, ending in "So I was jealous of my uncle."
