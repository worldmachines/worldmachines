# Helvétius's principle of utility and his two paradoxes

Morley (in his own voice, as a Victorian liberal-positivist critic) argues that *L'Esprit* contained the one principle that could have given pre-revolutionary France a direction for reform, public utility, but that Helvétius lost it in "the most repulsive paradox". He sets out the utility passages, credits Bentham with doing what Helvétius failed to do (classify motives, pleasures and pains systematically), then dissects the first paradox (self-love as the sole spring of conduct), explains it as a reaction against theological moralising, and ends with the second paradox (organisation counts least in intellectual inequality; circumstance and education count most), the Godwin line of descent, and a closing verdict that the book unites "most of wholesome truth with most of repellent error".

## Themes

**Utility as the lost key to reform.** Morley says nobody claims Helvétius invented utilitarianism (he cites Hume's name occurring in the book, and Mill on utilitarian schools back to Epicurus), but holds that Bentham took the idea of general utility as the foundation of morality from Helvétius and avowed it. Helvétius's own formulations, quoted at length, make "the Utility of the Public" the single principle of morality and legislation, invariable in principle and pliable in application. This is Morley's judgment, and a strong one; Bentham's acknowledgement is asserted, not documented in this unit.

**Rousseau as the road not taken.** Morley blames the book's discredit for letting [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] "sweep into the field": "the democracy of sentimentalism" displaced "the democracy of utility" in the literature preparing the Revolution, and the Sovereignty of the People is called an "arbitrary and intrinsically sterile" rendering of Helvétius's truth. A partisan Victorian verdict, not a neutral account; it frames the Enlightenment's intellectual machinery as having two possible engines, one sentimental, one utilitarian. Compare the WMP interest in [[history-machine]] style accounts of how ideas assemble into revolutions, though Morley's frame is a decidedly great-book, great-author one.

**Why Bentham succeeded and Helvétius did not.** Bentham came as a practical lawyer and mapped motives and actions in an "objective classification"; Helvétius instead reiterated that self-love fixes our measure of virtue and built a theory of character from "to judge is to feel". Morley names two defects (no root in the march of collective humanity; only egoistic impulses considered) and a classic fallacy: confusing the standard of conduct (utility) with the motive (self-gratification). The legislator problem follows: Helvétius assumes a disinterested legislator while denying disinterest to everyone else, vaulting over "the crucial problem of political constitutions", the selfishness of a governing class. This is a legibility-and-legislation theme in nuce (utility as social machinery), though Helvétius's calculus is psychological and moral rather than the infrastructural sense of [[modernity-machine]].

**Self-love and sympathy.** Morley concedes a true side: deterrent penal law presupposes the doctrine, and Charles Comte is quoted arguing that graded resentment and gratitude are needed for self-preservation. But he insists sympathy deserves a place, and that calling beneficence "self-love in disguise" mistakes the root for the fruit. Helvétius's choice of examples (Brutus, courtly anecdotes, the useful women of disorderly life echoing Mandeville) is condemned as bad rhetoric that drove away Madame Roland and others.

**The theological target.** Morley's charitable reading: Helvétius denied that men love good for good's sake because he was thinking of theologians who held man predisposed to love evil for evil's sake, and he wanted to move morals from "arbitrary and superstitious assumption" to observation. The long quoted passage on fanaticism (Inquisition, Spanish conquests in America, the "charnel-house of intolerance") is Helvétius's words, not Morley's, and supplies the motive: found probity on interest rather than on religion, which has been abused.

**A germ of historical understanding.** A quoted Helvétius passage holds that even bizarre or cruel customs rest on real or seeming public utility. Morley calls this the doctrine Voltaire ([[voltaire]]), Rousseau and Diderot all ignored, that barbarous conduct is intelligibly explicable, and says it approaches "the modern view". The claim is Morley's, hedged by his remark about crude language.

**The second paradox: circumstance over organisation.** Helvétius holds intellectual inequality comes from government, times, education, desire for distinction and the ideas one meditates on; genius is common, the circumstances rare. Morley calls this an "egregious overstatement" of a wholesome half-truth. Diderot (quoted) objects that the author misses the barrier between a man destined by nature for a function and one who brings only industry; Diderot also wrote detailed notes against *De l'Homme* (1774). Morley still judges the error the less mischievous one for France, since it pointed to institutions as the lever of reform; Godwin recast it as a rational half-truth. Resonates with the environment-over-nature side of Enlightenment reformism, less so with any mechanist determinism, since Morley treats it as a social-reform claim.

**A humane closing note.** Morley notes Constant's line on the pain we cause others and Helvétius's remark "In order to love mankind, we must expect little from them" (which impressed Bentham): indulgence and patience, not misanthropy. Final verdict: feeble grasp, no gift for accurate thinking.

## Notable passages

- **Utility handed to Bentham** (c1): the opening claim that Helvétius held the key instrument but could not use it, with the hedge that he did not originate utilitarianism.
- **Rousseau versus utility** (c2): sentimental democracy displaces utilitarian democracy in revolutionary preparation.
- **Why Bentham, not Helvétius** (c3): lawyerly classification of pleasures and pains versus reiterated self-love, and the standard/motive confusion.
- **Reaction against theology** (c4): Morley's charitable reconstruction of the first paradox as a scientific, anti-theological stance.
- **Custom as utility** (c5): the historical-appreciation passage and Morley's claim that it outstrips Voltaire, Rousseau and Diderot.
- **Second paradox** (c6): genius as product of circumstance, and Morley's judgment on its practical value.

Other voices: Hume is mentioned only as a name that appears in the book ([[david-hume]]); Diderot, Mill, Comte, Constant and Godwin are quoted or cited as critics, not as Morley's own thesis.
