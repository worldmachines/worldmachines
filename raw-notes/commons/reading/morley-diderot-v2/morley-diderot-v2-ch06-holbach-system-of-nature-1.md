# Holbach's System of Nature: publication, alarm, and the author

Morley introduces the *System of Nature* (1770), issued under the name of the dead Academy secretary Mirabaud, as the work that "gathered up all the scattered explosives of the criticism of the century into one thundering engine of revolt and destruction." He assigns authorship to Holbach (with a possible Diderot contribution), narrates the terror the book spread among the philosophes' own public, and sets out the reactions of Galiani, Goethe, Voltaire, Frederick and D'Alembert (all quoted or reported contemporaries, not Morley). He then sketches Holbach the man and host through Rousseau's Wolmar, and closes the unit with his own critical verdict: the book neglects the history of belief, but its assault on government makes it the presage of Revolutionary rhetoric.

## Themes

**Authorship and fraud.** Morley's own judgment is that the book is too systematic to be Diderot's, yet there are "good reasons for believing" that some pages were Diderot's. He reports (as fact about Diderot's behaviour) that Diderot fled Paris for Langres and Bourbonne, ready to cross the frontier, when the rumour of his authorship spread. Diderot's own praise, quoted by Morley, is for a philosophy "all of one piece", neither atheist on one page and deist on another.

**A shock to the enlightened.** Morley's framing is that the book revealed to a public that had enjoyed fifty years of intellectual delight the "unfamiliar sky" they had been drifting into: no God, a universe that is "only matter in spontaneous movement", souls that die with the body. This is Morley's rhetoric. Contemporaries respond differently: Galiani calls the author the Abbe Terrai of metaphysics, causing a bankruptcy of knowledge but also a crisis that rotten securities made inevitable; Goethe at Strasburg found the book gray, Cimmerian, corpse-like, not dangerous but repugnant ([[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]]).

**Voltaire's social theism.** [[voltaire]] is alarmed by the peril to the whole Encyclopedic brotherhood, writes a refutation, and makes sure Richelieu and the King hear of it. His stated reason is that society needs a God who punishes and rewards. Morley (his own judgment) says Voltaire never seized the philosophic meaning and prized theism for its "supposed utility as an assistant to the police", illustrated by the anecdote of locking the door against the servants. Frederick of Prussia, offended because the book spared political superstitions as little as theological ones, composed a defence of theism. D'Alembert is reported as a sceptic who nonetheless thinks experience proves a material soul and a limited material deity. The episode shows philosophes and sovereigns tied together by mutual interest, and the Enlightenment network splitting over how far critique should go.

**Holbach the man.** Via Rousseau's Wolmar in the *New Heloise* (Morley infers Wolmar stands for Rousseau's version of Holbach) and Morley's own account, Holbach is benevolent, learned, and hospitable, the maitre d'hotel of philosophy whose house, the Cafe de l'Europe, hosted Hume, Wilkes, Shelburne, Garrick, Franklin and Priestley. Rousseau ([[jean-jacques-rousseau]]), the emotional dreamer, found its atheism unbearable and turned that into Julie's lament over a husband who sees in nature only "a fortuitous combination, the product of blind force." Morley calls this an Ignoratio Elenchi but credits Rousseau with honestly showing that atheists can lead upright lives. The salon is a concrete node in the Encyclopedia's intellectual network.

**Morley's critique: opinions have a history.** Morley (a Victorian positivist) judges Holbach to share the defect of all materialistic speculation, a disregard of historic opinion: he offers reasons for changing convictions without asking what in human character the old ones were rooted in. The *on* in "On lui fit adopter" is an unexplained agent; the school treated mankind as slaves in bondage, which Morley says led, after "a certain amount of good in a bad way" at the Revolution, to a reaction toward Catholicism. This is Morley's view, not Holbach's.

**Politics: revolt against society and against God.** Morley argues the book's more immediate significance is its direct attack on government, unlike Voltaire's abstention from politics and Rousseau's vagueness. He paraphrases Holbach's indictment of sovereigns and a state of society that is a state of war between ruler and ruled, with misery fermenting into the overthrow of a realm. Morley's interpretive claim is that the revolt against the impotence of society and the revolt against the omnipotence of God made a "firm coalition", which ended four-and-twenty years later when Hebert was guillotined as an atheist (Robespierre: atheism is aristocratic). Holbach's premise, as Morley states it, is that social deliverance depends on intellectual deliverance via Naturalism for Theism. He closes on style: diffuse, declamatory, sincere, with Voltaire and Galiani quoted on its faults.

**Resonances with World Machines.** The unit shows the Encyclopedia circle as an idea-machine whose output (a systematic materialism) outran the tolerance of its own patrons, a case for [[history-machine]]-style reading in which a paradigm assembles distributedly before becoming visible; Morley's point that ideas fused revolt-against-throne and revolt-against-altar fits the Modernity Machine's critique of inherited legitimacy ([[modernity-machine]]), though Morley himself is a progressive-positivist and treats the Revolution's atheism ambivalently. Voltaire's utilitarian theism for the police is a small case of religion as social machinery.

## Notable passages

**Publication and Diderot's rumoured hand (c1).** The opening paragraph gives date, pseudonym (Mirabaud), Morley's attribution to Holbach, Diderot's flight and his praise of an unhedged philosophy.

**The shock (c2).** Morley's set-piece on the fear the book produced, and its three verdicts: no God, matter in spontaneous movement, mortal souls. Galiani and Goethe follow.

**Voltaire's alarm (c3).** Voltaire's reaction as courtier-strategist: refutation, letters to Richelieu and Grimm, and the claim that kings and plain men agree.

**Voltaire and the police (c4).** Morley's judgment that Voltaire took the social, not philosophic, view of theism, with the locked-door anecdote and D'Alembert's contrasting scepticism.

**Opinions have a history (c5).** Morley's central methodological critique of Holbach and the Encyclopedic school.

**The assault on government (c6).** Holbach's political indictment as paraphrased by Morley, and the coalition of the two revolts that ended with Robespierre and Hebert.
