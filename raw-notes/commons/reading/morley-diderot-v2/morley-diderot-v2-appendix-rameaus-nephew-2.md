# Rameau's Nephew, translated: the lost patrons, the pantomime, and the music lesson

This is the second appendix unit of Morley's translation of Diderot's *Le Neveu de Rameau*. The words are Diderot's, in Morley's English, not Morley's own argument; Morley says elsewhere that he omitted passages on obscure personages and the technical musical discussion, and the ellipses here mark such cuts. The speakers are `_I._` (the philosopher-narrator, "Moi") and `_He._` (the nephew, "Lui"). He explains that he was expelled from a household of protectors because he had "a little taste, a trifle of wit, a spice of reason"; I advises him to go back and grovel; He recoils, then recites the soliloquy of a man who knows he could prosper by pandering and cannot. He then performs violin and piano in dumb-show, quizzes I about his own shabby youth as a mathematics tutor, and mocks I's plan to educate his eight-year-old daughter in reason and morality, ending with a comic reconstruction of how He actually conducted a music lesson.

## Themes

**Parasitic patronage and the price of being a buffoon.** He's account of his former "people" is of a household that valued him exactly for his vices ("I am a man without consequence"). Their affection is paid in titbits, cuffs and kicks. He's offence was to show reason; the lesson is "the difference between what God made you, and what your protectors wanted you to be." The position is He's, narrated with self-lacerating wit; it is not a thesis Morley or Diderot's I endorses. I's rebuke ("To change your way of talking") and the bracketed stage direction showing I torn between laughter and indignation mark the dialogue's moral instability.

**Dignity and self-contempt, intermittently.** He claims an unexplained "certain dignity attached to human nature that nothing can stifle," yet admits other days when "for a halfpenny there is nothing too dirty for me to do." The long soliloquy imagines himself flattering, lying, procuring, and calls the unused talent "the torture of conscience which comes of the usefulness of the gifts that heaven has bestowed on us." He's equalising argument about Samuel Bernard and Rameau in the grave is a materialist deflation of rank ("To moulder under marble, or to moulder under clay, 'tis still to moulder"), voiced by the figure with most to gain from it.

**Performance as torture and counterfeit.** The bracketed narrator passages describe He's violin and piano pantomime: ecstasy and pain on one face, a false note followed by retuning an imaginary string. I's reaction ("is it not a painful thing to watch the torture of a man who is busy painting pleasure for my benefit?") frames art-as-labour. This is I's reaction, not He's.

**Education, expertise, and the fraud of teaching.** I proposes reasoning "justly", bearing life's troubles "courageously", grammar, fables, history, geography, "a great deal of morality", and little music or dancing. He answers (a) that the masters who could teach such things properly would never teach them, because mastery of elements takes a lifetime; (b) that without the whole of a subject one knows none of it well; (c) that physics is "a drop of water raised by a needle-point from the vast ocean"; and (d) that a teacher who knows nothing at least does not spoil the pupil. Sharp and absurd are mixed on purpose: I answers "all you have told me is more specious than solid," and elsewhere calls him a madman in whose head "such just ideas go pell-mell with such a mass of extravagances."

**The music lesson as a comedy of flattery.** He's reconstruction shows the economy of the pupil-teacher-parent triangle: gossip (including a joke that Voltaire is dead, "so much the better," because he "always dies a fortnight" before a new work, linking to [[voltaire]]), pretended instruction, mutual flattery, a fee. The lesson is theatre staged to satisfy the mother, and the mother herself half-sees it.

**Resonances (tentative).** The patron-and-client machinery and the mother-teacher flattery loop are small social machines of legitimacy-by-performance; this rhymes only loosely with [[modernity-machine]] or [[world-machines]] language, since the passage is satire of Paris manners rather than analysis of institutions. The nephew's frankness about his own motives sits in the Encyclopedist tradition of unmasking, but a dialogue in which both voices are Diderot's, and in which the "philosopher" is gently mocked, resists being read as a doctrine.

## Notable passages

**The sin of common sense (c1).** He describes how his patrons loved him for his vices and discarded him for a moment of reason.

**Dignity versus a halfpenny (c2).** I advises prostrating himself before the mistress of the house; He refuses on grounds of family pride, then undercuts the refusal.

**The soliloquy of the could-be pander (c3).** A full performance of the self-reproach of a man who sees fools prospering and imagines the arts by which he too could.

**The philosopher's curriculum (c4).** I's brief plan for his daughter, a rare statement of I's own educational values: reason, courage, morality.

**Why teachers cannot know (c5).** He argues that real mastery and elementary teaching are in tension, and ends with a nihilistic note on physics.

**A music lesson (c6).** The long comic reconstruction.
