# Helvétius and the Burning of L'Esprit

Morley opens Chapter V by announcing a short account of three books (works of the Encyclopædic school, commonly credited to Diderot's inspiration; Helvétius's comes first, and Holbach is named here only as a bearer of a rival dogma). He then turns to Helvétius's _L'Esprit_: the 1758 prosecution, the burning of the book, and the hostile verdicts of Rousseau, Diderot and Turgot. He quotes Turgot at length on how despotism should be attacked, then gives his own reading that the book's real drift was political and that the church, instinctively, saw this first. The remainder of the unit is a biographical sketch of Helvétius (family, ambition, wealth, the farmer-general post, the conflicting reports of his character), his reception in England, and a first word on the form of the book, ending with Madame Roland's reaction to its doctrine of self-interest.

## Themes

**Persecution as publicity.** Morley says that of all proscribed books of the century _L'Esprit_ "excited the keenest resentment", partly because it "came earliest in the literature of attack". The censor was cashiered, the author lost a post in the Queen's household, and the book was burnt along with Voltaire's poem on Natural Religion. Morley (not a quoted source) adds that this persecution brought Helvétius both public vogue and the sympathy of the Encyclopædic circle, who had previously kept him at a distance.

**Contemporary verdicts, kept distinct.** Rousseau (per Morley) found the book detestable and was spurred by it in the Savoyard Vicar's Profession of Faith (cf. [[jean-jacques-rousseau]]). Diderot, by Morley's account, ranked it with Montesquieu and Buffon yet saw that it rested on paradox. Turgot called it philosophy without logic, literature without taste, morality without goodness. Grimm and Hume are cited on its form (Hume valued its composition, not its philosophy; cf. [[david-hume]], [[adam-smith]]). Morley adjudicates among these, but the verdicts are theirs.

**Turgot on how to attack despotism.** Turgot (quoted) argues that attacks on intolerance must rest on just ideas, that declamation gives the strong a good excuse, that even despotisms contain abuses rulers have no interest in, and that calm, solid treatment of the public good, with "interesting warmth", serves all governments. Morley's gloss is cautious: it is an open question whether the French monarchy could have reformed itself even in 1758, but Turgot could be excused for hoping to avoid the violence to which Helvétius's "diatribes" pointed. This is a reform-versus-revolution frame that sits near [[intellectual-vs-revolutionary]], though here it is a contemporary's policy argument rather than a theory of intellectuals.

**The church as the track of light.** In Morley's own division of labour, the revolutionary host always met the church: Voltaire pierced its dogma, Rousseau shamed its irreligious temper, Diderot exposed its philosophy's "vicious absoluteness" (cf. [[voltaire]]), and then Helvétius and Holbach came "not merely with criticism, but with substitutes": Holbach a dogma of matter and motion, Helvétius a theory of character and morals (interest the basis of justice, pleasure the true meaning of interest, character the creature of education and laws). Morley's own claim, a Victorian-positivist one, is that despite its shallowness the book held the one principle that might have allowed the transition without a Reign of Terror. Here the Enlightenment appears as an apparatus of replacement institutions and doctrines, a loose fit with the [[modernity-machine]] as an infrastructure of assumptions, though Morley speaks only of opinion and social reconstruction.

**Character: ambition, wealth, and contradictory testimony.** Morley derives Helvétius's trajectory (farmer-general, geometer, poet of Happiness, then social theorist after Montesquieu's success) from a charge, made by enemies and friends, of vanity; the seven years of laborious composition are attested by an intimate. Marmontel (quoted) calls him liberal, generous and benevolent, the opposite of what he professes. Diderot's travelling anecdote (reporting a lady's account) paints him fearful, hated by neighbours over his game preserve and guarded by twenty-four men. Morley sets against it well-attested generosity (pensions, hiding the Young Pretender for two years). Morellet is quoted on his salon. Morley also notes the tension of a farmer-general attacking governments, via Turgot's jibe.

**England and the form of the book.** Holbach explained Helvétius's anglophilia as a reaction to persecution; Walpole, Gibbon and Warburton (quoted contemporaries) respond variously to him. Morley says _L'Esprit_ is readable but unscientific, modelled on Fontenelle's elegant popularisation, stuffed with anecdotes. Madame Roland (quoted) found it depressing, showing "repulsive self-interest", yet read it as a portrait of men corrupted by society, and refused its principles.

## Notable passages

**The prosecution and the verdicts (c1).** The indictment's phrase, "the code of the most hateful and infamous passions", the cashiered censor and the shared fire with Voltaire's poem, then the roll of verdicts ending with Turgot's three-part dismissal.

**Turgot's counsel (c2).** The key text for how contemporary reformers thought opinion should be moved: clarity, courage, humanity, not insult.

**Morley's frame (c3).** The church, Holbach and Helvétius "with substitutes", and Morley's counterfactual about a transition without terror: his own judgment, to be held as such.

**Diderot's report of the estate (c4).** A hostile anecdote that undercuts the benevolent Helvétius of Marmontel; Diderot is relaying a third party's report, with his own moralising turn on Madame Geoffrin.

**Form of the book (c5).** Grimm, Fontenelle, Hume and Morley's caution about anecdote as sugar on a depressing doctrine.

**Madame Roland (c6).** A reader's response to self-interest as the explanation of conduct, a useful early reception of utility doctrine as disillusion rather than liberation.
