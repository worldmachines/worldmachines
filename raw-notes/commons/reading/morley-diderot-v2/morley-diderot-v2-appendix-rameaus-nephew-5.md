# Rameau's Nephew, concluding scenes: sublime villainy, the paternal molecule, and the pantomime of beggars

This unit is the end of Morley's appendix translation of Diderot's dialogue *Le Neveu de Rameau*. The words are Diderot's (in Morley's English), not Morley's argument; positions belong to **He** (the nephew, Lui) and **I** (the philosopher-narrator, Moi). Morley's bracketed stage directions and linking notes (for instance the note that musical discussion is omitted) mark his abridgement. He first justifies confessing his own turpitude: great criminals command a respect small rogues do not, and he tells of the Renegade of Avignon, who befriended a Jew, then falsely warned him of an Inquisition denunciation, helped him load his fortune on a ship, robbed him, and had in fact informed against him himself. I recoils from both the deed and the tone. After a musical interlude, I asks why so fine an ear coexists with moral deafness; He answers with fibre, bad company and heredity, then lays out how he is bringing up his young son: show him the louis, teach him gold, avoid being an _espèce_ (a mediocrity) and keep clear of the law. The talk turns to the nephew's failure to write, his inherited name, his refusal to view humanity from the 'epicycle of Mercury', and his 'pantomime' of flatterers, which I generalises to the whole social hierarchy. He leaves for the opera with a last jibe.

## Themes

**Sublimity in evil and unity of character.** He argues that people prize 'unity of character' even in wickedness, so a great criminal earns awe where a petty one earns spit. I counters by finding him 'vacillating', not wholly wicked. The Renegade story is the specimen: the horror is the calculated double game. I's reaction ('I do not know which of the two is most horrible to me--the vileness of your renegade, or the tone in which you speak of it') keeps the moral frame intact while He insists the atrocity is the sincerity.

**Heredity, race and fibre against education.** He explains his deaf heart by 'the paternal molecule', a quasi-materialist physiology (fibre, molecule, blood) in which education merely crosses inclination and produces zigzag, mediocre people. This is the dialogue speaking in the idiom of Diderot's materialist circle, but placed in the mouth of a cynic; the dialogue does not endorse it, and I does not accept He's conclusions about morals.

**Education for a corrupt society.** He's pedagogy is explicitly adapted to Paris, not Lacedaemon: 'If the education is bad, the morals of my country are to blame for that, not I.' A good education is 'one that leads to all sorts of enjoyments without danger and without inconvenience'; I warns that they agree only in appearance and refuses to define 'dangers' and 'inconveniences'. A bracketed passage (the narrator's voice) observes that He is more candid and consistent than most, not more vicious; hypocrisy is the real difference.

**Appetite and self-interest as nature.** He says everything that lives 'seeks its own wellbeing at the expense of any prey', so a child left alone would want wealth and pleasure; I replies that such a child with a man's passions would 'strangle his father and dishonour his own mother'. The exchange stages the self-interest versus socialisation dispute central to Enlightenment moral philosophy, without resolving it.

**The pantomime of dependence.** He's 'postures' (the needy man 'jumps, he crawls, he wriggles') are generalised by I: 'Whoever has need of another is indigent, and assumes a posture.' King, minister, abbe all dance before someone. This is a structural, system-level reading of patronage and court society rather than a personal vice.

**Refusal of the view from above.** He declines to 'perch on the epicycle of Mercury' and classify humanity as Reaumur classifies flies; he stays with appetite and sensation. I teases that he is doing the same. This is a small piece of anti-systematising, bottom-up epistemology.

## Resonances for World Machines (hedged)

The pantomime passage rhymes with WMP's interest in institutions as machinery of dependence, and with the [[modernity-machine]] and its patronage-bound order, though here the focus is individual survival inside court-and-salon Paris rather than infrastructure. The Renegade story and He's candour are a case study of individual opportunism within an [[enlightenment-monism]]-era moral framework that this dialogue explicitly pulls apart (it shows the Enlightenment's own satirical self-critique); the connection is loose. The paternal-molecule talk and the refusal of the 'epicycle' point of view touch the Enlightenment mechanist and anti-systematic strands, but nothing here is argued by Morley himself.
