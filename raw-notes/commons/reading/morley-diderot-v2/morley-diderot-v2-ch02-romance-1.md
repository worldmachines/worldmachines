# Richardson's Influence, the Eloge, and The Nun

Morley opens Chapter II ("Romance") with President de Brosses's remark that Diderot made twenty-five digressions in an afternoon, then argues that Diderot's idolatry of Richardson was a symptom of a social transition (feudal-aristocratic to middle-class, private-life society) that the French literary world felt before it understood it. He sets Voltaire's incomprehension of *Clarissa* against Diderot's passionate *Eloge de Richardson*, quotes Johnson on reading Richardson "for the sentiment", and turns to *The Nun* (*La Religieuse*): the Croismare hoax that produced it, its realism of convent life, and Morley's mixed verdict on its most atrocious portrait ("not art ... but science").

## Themes

**The novel as a social product (Morley's thesis).** Morley holds that the modern novel arose in England "the result of general social causes": it depends on the interest of private life, which required that "the feudal and aristocratic spirit had received its deathblow", and only England's middle class had yet won independence. Scott is cited in support, and Marivaux is called an isolated precursor. This is Morley's own sociological explanation, offered as a causal claim about literary form and social structure. It rhymes with the project's interest in deep infrastructure shaping what a period can imagine (compare [[modernity-machine]]), though Morley's frame is a Victorian progress narrative (feudalism to "industrial democracy") rather than a machine-lifecycle one.

**Sentiment as a revolutionary symptom.** Morley claims a passion for Richardson was "a symbol that a man was truly possessed by the spirit of political revolution". He contrasts [[voltaire]], a rebel against church and superstition who kept monarchic habits and found nine volumes of *Clarissa* empty (Voltaire's words, quoted by Morley), with Diderot, the "half-conscious oracle and unaccepted prophet" of domestic ideals and natural affection. Morley explicitly says this was not deliberate philosophical calculation. He links Richardson-love to the "love of the life of peaceful industry" of the Encyclopaedia. This is a hedged, retrospective reading: Morley's inference, not Diderot's stated programme.

**Diderot's Eloge: fiction as truer than history.** Morley presents the Eloge (after Richardson's death in 1761) as disordered, declamatory, and sincere, and contrasts it with Fontenelle's measured eloges. Diderot's own claims, quoted at length: "the truest history is full of falsehoods, and ... your romance is full of truths"; history paints a few individuals, Richardson paints "the human race"; Richardson's detail is long because real undertakings are long; what is common is "what passes every day before your eyes, and what you never see". Morley notes Diderot's own admission that few readers will feel Richardson's full value, and adds his own jab that most readers know only an abridgment.

**Story versus sentiment; the belle ame.** Morley quotes Johnson (read Richardson "for the sentiment", the story merely "giving occasion to the sentiment") and says this is just what Paris did. He sees a shared adoration of the *belle ame* behind Richardson and Rousseau's *New Heloise*, in a society pairing "stern anti-religious dialectic" with "the most tender and exalted sensibility". [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] adds passion and wild landscape that Richardson lacked. Morley conjectures (explicitly a supposition) that Diderot's silence on the *New Heloise* came from contempt for Rousseau's private perversity and apparent insincerity.

**The Nun: a private mystification becomes a novel.** Written about 1760, published 1796, circulated only among a few friends, so Morley rejects "ingenious historians" who make it the signal of a crusade against the Church, though it expresses the Encyclopaedists' feeling about celibacy and cloistering. It began as a hoax to lure the Marquis de Croismare back to Paris from Normandy; Diderot, caught in his own trap, wept over the story, a capacity Morley traces to Richardson ("Pamela, Clarissa, Grandison ...!").

**Realism, science, and Morley's moral reservation.** Morley says Diderot, "a social destroyer by accident", was in intention "a truly scientific moralist", observing and dissecting; The Nun's convent life is "reduction of the spiritual to the most purely mechanical". Morley's verdict on the depraved abbess is ambivalent: not art but science, powerful, yet "fatally wanting in the elements of tenderness, beauty, and sympathy", read "under protest". These are Morley's judgments, not Diderot's.

## Notable passages

The opening social-causes argument for the English origin of the novel (c1) and the French extension, with the Voltaire contrast (c2), carry Morley's thesis. The Eloge's "history is often a bad novel" claim (c3) is the key Diderot text. Johnson's reading-for-sentiment passage (c4) frames sensibility. The Croismare hoax and Diderot's tears (c5) show the fusion of fiction and lived feeling. The closing verdict on The Nun's portraits (c6) shows Morley's positivist, science-over-art evaluation, with a Victorian moral reserve.
