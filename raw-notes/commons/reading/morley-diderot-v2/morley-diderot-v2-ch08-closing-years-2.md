# Diderot's last years: Voltaire, Naigeon, Romilly, the clergy, and the death of the circle

This unit carries Morley's narrative from the deaths of [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] and [[voltaire]] in 1778 to Diderot's own death on 30 July 1784. It covers the one meeting of Diderot and Voltaire; Morley's complaint that Naigeon, who inherited Diderot's papers, was a poor witness; Samuel Romilly's report of a 1781 visit; the conservative counter-attack (Palissot's satires, the bishops' memorials against Voltaire's Kehl edition and Raynal); the disappearance of the generation of precursors; and finally Diderot's refusal to retract, his removal to the Rue de Richelieu, his death at table, and a closing quotation of his own. Throughout, Morley gives his own verdicts (Naigeon 'good for very little'; the bishops 'blind') alongside quoted testimony from Voltaire, Romilly, and Diderot.

## Themes

**The two patriarchs meet too late.** Morley notes that Voltaire and Diderot met only on Voltaire's 1778 visit to Paris, after 27 years. Their sympathy was 'imperfect' but there was goodwill. Voltaire's letter (quoted) laments dying without having seen him. The reported verdicts are the contemporaries' own: Voltaire says Diderot lacks 'the talent of dialogue'; Diderot likens Voltaire to a ruined haunted castle inhabited by 'some ancient sorcerer'. They quarrelled over Shakespeare. Morley says almost nothing else is known about these conversations, so this is a record of a gap in the evidence.

**The unreliable executor.** Morley's own judgment is that Naigeon, who had Diderot's papers and claimed to be his authoritative expounder, could have been a Boswell and instead was a 'fanatical atheist' and propagandist. Morley borrows Carlyle's complaint that we want the living man, not 'a dull, sulky, snuffling, droning, interminable lecture on Atheistic Philosophy'. Diderot is characterised as a talker (Coleridge-like) rather than an author, which bears on how much of him survives.

**A witness to the turn of opinion.** Romilly's testimony (a contemporary's, not Morley's thesis) shows Diderot ostentatiously atheist, saying theology must be put to the sword, and furious at French tyranny, while D'Alembert, more cautious, observes that the people who were 'drunk with joy' at a Dauphin's birth in 1729 now regard the birth of 'another master' with indifference. Morley reads this as proof that 'the change for which they had worked was at hand'. This fits the [[history-machine]] idea of a paradigm assembling quietly until it becomes visible, though Morley's frame is a Victorian liberal narrative of progress, and the secular-versus-clerical contest is told as a moral one.

**The weakness of the reaction.** Morley's own view is that the conservative side was feeble: Palissot, the best of them, had 'the literary sense' and some fair points about Diderot's chaos and bad drama, but _The Philosophers_ was 'a scandalous misrepresentation' and _Le Satyrique_ (1782) made sport of Diderot's licentious early works. Doggerel was the best retort against Voltaire. The bishops' memorials against Voltaire's Kehl edition and Raynal's _History of the Indies_ ask for the 1757 death penalty and settle for imprisonment, fines, and spying; Morley's point is that 'the only remedy for a moral disease is a moral remedy' and that the old methods were 'impossible for ever'. This is a pointed contrast between a distributed network of print and a state-church apparatus relying on censorship; compare [[eisenstein-printing-revolution]] on print, though Morley does not theorise media.

**The generation disappears.** Morley lists the dead: Voltaire, Rousseau, D'Alembert (tended by [[nicolas-de-condorcet]]), Turgot, Madame du Deffand, Geoffrin, Lespinasse, Madame d'Epinay, Mademoiselle Voland, with Galiani dying at Naples. He praises Galiani's _Dialogues on the Trade in Grain_ and its demolition of 'absolute truths in political economy'. The salons that moved intellectual life from Versailles to Paris are ending. Diderot consoles himself that he will not long survive.

**Premonition of revolution, and a defence.** Morley sees the age at its height but already over: Talleyrand on the sweetness of life before 1789; Figaro's laugh 'pregnant with menace', which he ties to Raynal's saying that the law is a sword levelling what rises. He defends Diderot against the charge of fomenting the Terror via the 'entrails of the priest' couplet: it was published in 1795 and was a dramatic line in the mouth of an 'Eleutheromane'. These are Morley's judgments, offered against a popular accusation. The theme touches [[intellectual-vs-revolutionary]].

**An old man's modesty, and an unyielding death.** Diderot declines to revise his _Letter on the Blind_ for fear of spoiling the young man's page, quoting Horace. In the last illness he refuses the curé of Saint Sulpice's suggested slight retractation as 'an impudent lie', so no retraction was ever made. His last recorded aphorism is that 'the first step towards philosophy is incredulity'. He dies at table after eating an apricot, and Morley closes with Diderot's own meditation on ruins and time, 'I walk between two eternities'.

## Notable passages

The Voltaire passage (c1) is the only first-hand pairing of the two, and its mutual verdicts are short and vivid. Romilly's account (c2) is the 'last authentic glimpse' and carries the testimony about shifting public opinion. The bishops' passage (c3) gives Morley's moral argument against repression. The Figaro-and-couplet passage (c4) shows Morley's reading of the pre-revolutionary mood and his defence of Diderot. The deathbed passages (c5, c6) give the refusal to retract and the final epilogue in Diderot's own words.
