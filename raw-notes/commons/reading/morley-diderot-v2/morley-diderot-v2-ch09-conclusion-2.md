# Diderot's Late Philosophical Pieces: D'Alembert's Dream, the Russian University Plan, the Maréchale, and Falconet

This unit gathers four of Diderot's lesser-known or posthumously published writings, each introduced by Morley with a mixture of exposition and verdict. Section II treats the three dialogues of 1769 (a conversation with D'Alembert, D'Alembert's Dream, and the Lespinasse-Bordeu conclusion), where Morley says Diderot "figures definitely as a materialist". Section III covers the Plan of a University for the Government of Russia, with its science-first curriculum and its deference to Catherine II. Section IV introduces the Conversation with the Maréchale de---- and the parable of the young Mexican. Section V summarises the 1765-67 correspondence with the sculptor Falconet over whether the hope of posterity is a worthy motive.

## Themes

**Materialism as a single-substance monism.** Through the dialogue, the speaker (Diderot's voice in the dream) argues that "There is only one substance in the world", that the thinking man is an instrument with sensibility and memory, and that life arises from matter by heat and movement. Morley's own framing: Diderot did not originate French materialism (Maupertuis, Robinet, La Mettrie came first) and had earlier hesitated over Maupertuis; here, he says, the philosophy is "far enough removed alike from deism and scepticism". This is Morley's reading, supported by Lange, whom he cites. Loosely related to [[enlightenment-monism]] (though that page is about a different, political-philosophical monism, so the fit is partial) and, as an older lineage, to [[baruch-spinoza]].

**Necessity dissolves moral desert into social technique.** Bordeu's replies to Mdlle. Lespinasse recast virtue as beneficence and vice as maleficence, remorse as a product of ignorance or vanity, and rewards and punishments as "means of correcting the modifiable being". This is the view of Bordeu (Diderot's mouthpiece), not a claim Morley endorses; Morley's own attitude to the form is sharply censorious ("odious", "detestable", "the most ugly and disgusting piece in the literature of philosophy") while granting the dialogues' sincerity and dialectical force. He also notes Diderot never meant them for the public and believed he had burned the only copy; they were not published until 1830.

**Education as machinery of state, with science before letters.** The Russian plan reverses the humanist order: arithmetic, algebra, probabilities, geometry, physics, mechanics, astronomy, natural history, chemistry and anatomy come first; Greek and Latin only in the eighth class. Morley reads this as what one "might have been expected from the editor of the Encyclopædia" and praises the concentrated demolition of the classical-language superstition. On religion, Diderot takes no position of his own and merely says the Empress's subjects should conform to her views, which Morley treats as concessions to "her majesty's political religion". Relevant to the theme of philosophers courting [[intellectual-vs-revolutionary]] power and enlightened despotism, though Diderot here is advising rather than governing.

**Catherine's rebuke as a portrait of the courtier-philosopher.** The anecdote (a story, hedged by Morley as such) has Catherine catching Diderot flattering her after he had denounced flatterers of kings.

**Unbelief as sincerity.** In the Maréchale conversation, Morley calls it a terse summary of rationalistic objections to the church's creed, and notes the parable attributed to Rousseau, which Rousseau only echoed in spirit in the Savoyard Vicar's creed (see [[jean-jacques-rousseau]]). The parable's moral: sincere error is forgivable, though the sovereign adds that the rest of the Mexican's conduct is "not equally innocent". Note that the dialogue is between C. (the speaker for unbelief) and the Maréchale; the parable is told by C. and is Diderot's, in Morley's translation.

**Posterity as secular afterlife.** Diderot's side of the Falconet exchange (Falconet's letters were burned, so only fragments survive) defends the "sentiment of immortality" not as a religious dogma but as the desire for lasting reputation. Morley stresses that Diderot's disbelief in the church's dogma gave keenness to this plea. Falconet replies that contemporary glory suffices and posterity is a lottery. Diderot answers: "Posterity is for the philosopher what the other world is for the devout!" Rhymes with the project's interest in historiography and how an era imagines its own legacy ([[history-machine]]), though the match is loose: Diderot's posterity is an audience, not an impersonal infrastructure.

## Notable passages

The clavecin and marble passages (c1) are the core materialist statement: sensibility and memory as properties of an organised instrument, matter flowing through marble, plant, man, and the egg as the refutation of theology. The Bordeu exchange (c2) shows the ethical consequence. The curriculum passage (c3) is Morley's summary of the plan's one "strongly marked idea". The Catherine anecdote (c4) is the unit's sharpest picture of the philosopher-at-court. The parable (c5) and the posterity apostrophe (c6) are the two literary high points, one on unbelief, one on secular transcendence.
