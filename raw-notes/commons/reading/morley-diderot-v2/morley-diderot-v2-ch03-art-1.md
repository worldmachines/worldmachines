# Diderot's Salons and the spirit of French art

Morley opens Chapter III by introducing the nine _Salons_ Diderot wrote for Grimm between 1759 and 1781 (omitting 1773, when he was in Holland and Russia). He first collects praise (Carlyle, Madame Necker, A. W. Schlegel) and gives his own verdict on their freshness and abandon, then offers the Fete Dieu passage as proof of Diderot's range. He next surveys the poor French tradition of art theory and travel-writing on Italy (Dufresnoy, Dubos, Andre, Batteux, Cochin, De Brosses, Dupaty), notes that Diderot never saw Italy, and closes with a portrait of eighteenth-century French painting (Watteau, Boucher, Vernet) as the mirror of its society, its later fall into contempt, and Morley's plea for "historic tolerance". Nearly everything here is Morley's own critical judgment, with quoted contemporaries (Carlyle, Necker, Schlegel, Boucher, De Brosses, Diderot) marked as such.

## Themes

**The Salons as the exception to the philosopher's pose.** Morley (his own judgment, backed by Carlyle's and Schlegel's quoted enthusiasm) says the Salons show "a freshness, a vivacity, a zeal, a sincerity" unique in criticism, partly because Diderot was writing privately for Grimm, free of "literary pose". The sceptic, dogmatist and dialectician "fall back into a secondary place". Diderot is here "capable of forgetting for a season the passion of the great philosophical and ecclesiastical battle", which Morley treats as part of his "deep-lying versatility". This complicates the picture of the Encyclopedie circle as a single polemical machine of ideas: Morley's own claim is that its central figure sometimes stepped out of the machine. Diderot's tears at the _Fete Dieu_ procession are the evidence; the passage is Diderot's words, offered by Morley as an avowal of sensitivity to ritual and collective feeling, not as a change of doctrine.

**France's literary, not philosophic, criticism.** Morley ranks France sovereign in literary criticism and Germany in historical criticism, finds most German aesthetics "clouded with metaphysical speculation", and judges French art-criticism literary rather than philosophic, with Diderot "the only writer who has deliberately brought a vivid spirit and a philosophic judgment" to Beauty (apart from Cousin and Taine, whom he dismisses). These are Morley's verdicts, and are sweeping. His catalogue of predecessors is sharply evaluative: Dubos is "the best contribution" before Lessing and Diderot, while Batteux is quoted via Diderot's quip as a good man of letters "without taste, without criticism, and without philosophy". Diderot's own opinions of these figures are reported, not independently argued.

**Taste as a marker of the age.** De Brosses's contempt for St Mark's as "gothique, du plus mechant gout" is used by Morley to show how the eighteenth century hated Gothic as the symbol of mysticism and of the age it "blindly abhorred" as the source of tyrannical laws and cruel superstitions. Dupaty's rapture before nature's "vast superiority" over art in 1785 marks, for Morley, a shift of spirit before the Revolution. Morley thereby reads art and travel writing as thermometers of changing sensibility. This loosely resonates with the WMP idea of a [[history-machine]] assembling quietly before it becomes visible, though Morley's frame is Victorian progress-and-taste, not infrastructural.

**Art as mirror of a sociable, idle society.** Morley says French art is "the mirror of its time": Rome becomes "a decoration for the scenes of a theatre or the panels of a boudoir", beauty is replaced by prettiness "in season and out of season", and painting serves the court, as in the true story of Pompadour having a famous model introduced into a _Holy Family_ to arouse the king. The long quoted sentence "All is daintiness..." is an unnamed quotation, not Morley's own prose. The Revolution and Empire then bring contempt: pictures rot on the quays, Fragonard dies in beggary, and David's "hideous art of the Empire" effaces what had enraptured Diderot's contemporaries. Morley's own judgments here are strongly moralized ("hideous", "unwholesome and degrading").

**Historic tolerance.** Morley, while reserving "serious admiration" for Titian, Rembrandt and Raphael, asks tolerance for Boucher and his like. He cites Boucher's own self-description ("We others are the painters of our own age: we have not common sense, but we are charming") and says they filled the gap between Le Brun and David as Voltaire's light verse filled the gap between Racine and Chenier (compare [[voltaire]]). He still prefers them to "acres of martyrdoms" in galleries. Diderot himself, he notes, cared little for Watteau ("I would give ten Watteaus for one Teniers"), a taste Morley compares to Louis XIV's dismissal of Teniers's "magots".

**Diderot never saw Italy.** Morley counts it among the "great losses of literature" that Diderot saw only "fifth-rate paintings and sculpture" and never reached Venice, Florence or Rome. A projected trip with Grimm and Rousseau (see [[jean-jacques-rousseau]]) was not realised. Diderot is quoted as acknowledging that artists rarely excel without Italy.

## Notable passages

**The freshness of the Salons (c1).** Morley's central appreciation: sincerity, "overflowing pleasure", a criticism that awakens the reader's imagination, with a biographical explanation (private writing for Grimm) and a characterological one.

**The Fete Dieu avowal (c2).** The most surprising passage: the atheist leader of the encyclopedists moved to tears by a Catholic procession, presented by Morley as completing "the circle of Diderot's immense and deep-lying versatility". Morley's inference about the source of his critical greatness (openness to impression, "profound mobility") is his own reading.

**France and the criticism of art (c3).** Morley's comparative ranking of national traditions and his claim for Diderot's uniqueness, with his Victorian suspicion of metaphysical aesthetics.

**Art as mirror of its time (c4).** The thesis that art reproduces "such imaginative feeling as society could muster" and its fall into contempt after the Revolution; includes the contrast between Roman ruins and boudoir decoration.

**The Holy Family and the Parc-aux-Cerfs (c5).** An anecdote (Morley calls it a true story, without source in this unit) showing painting as an instrument of court appetite, followed by an unattributed quotation about a life of delicate pleasure.

**Boucher and historic tolerance (c6).** Boucher's own verdict on Raphael and Michelangelo, Morley's literary analogy to Voltaire, and his qualified defence of rococo cheerfulness.

A possible WMP rhyme is the "decay" of a style into decoration: art that was once grand becomes ornament for a boudoir, which loosely echoes the [[decay-sequence]] (Functional to Ornamental), though Morley says nothing of the kind and the fit is partial.
