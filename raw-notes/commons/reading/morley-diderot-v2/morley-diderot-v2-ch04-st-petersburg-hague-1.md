# Diderot sets out for Catherine's Russia: Holland, St. Petersburg, and the courting of the philosophers

Morley begins Chapter IV with Diderot, at sixty, resolving in 1773 to visit his imperial benefactress. He introduces the two Russians who prepared the way (Princess Dashkow and Prince Galitzin), recounts the first halt at the Hague, and then digresses to explain what the journey signifies. He contrasts free, commercial Holland with the half-barbarous, Frenchified court of St. Petersburg, and gives a sceptical portrait of Catherine built mostly from diplomats and men of affairs. The bulk of the unit examines the relationship between Catherine and the Encyclopaedists: her flattery of Voltaire, her borrowings from Montesquieu, the Duchesse de Choiseul's verdict that the philosophers were dupes, the Polish Dissidents and the Partition, the Rulhière manuscript, and Falconet's statue of Peter the Great. The unit ends before Diderot reaches the capital.

## Themes

**Holland as the shelter of the literature of emancipation.** Morley notes that Descartes, Bayle and Locke owed security to Dutch valour and tolerance, and that the Encyclopædia's ruling ideas came in direct line from Bayle and Locke. These are Morley's own judgments, and he adds that Diderot himself was not the man for "historic sentimentalism". Diderot's own letters from the Hague are on fish and straw hats, not on this history.

**Russia as an arc of Western progress, run backwards.** Morley says going from Holland to Russia is "to measure an arc of Western progress". He quotes an unnamed onlooker on St. Petersburg as a place where the tenth and eighteenth centuries coexist, and he says that the few Europeanised Russians stand as far from their countrymen "as Englishmen are removed from Iroquois". The framing is a Victorian progress narrative. It treats Russia as a laboratory of forced, artificial modernisation, and it resonates, loosely, with [[modernity-machine]] ideas of imposed legibility. Morley's frame, though, is linear civilisation, not machine lifecycles.

**Philosophy as fashion.** Morley's central claim about Catherine is that "It was the vogue of the philosophers, and not their philosophy that made Catherine their friend." He supports it with Catherine's own confession that Voltaire "made me the fashion" and that the "learned men in _ist_" wearied her. Gunning, the English chargé d'affaires, adds that her motive was "an insatiable and unbounded thirst for fame". He notes that she founded academies and then left them unfinished. This resonates with [[voltaire]] as a manager of reputation. The same Voltaire is shown here as a centre of European intelligence, a role that Catherine's war bulletins fed.

**Enlightened despotism as cultural patronage with a political purpose.** Morley, citing the Reptile-fund comparison, concludes that her European correspondence was "in some measure the result of political calculation". The Duchesse de Choiseul's letter is a contemporary's harsh judgment, which Morley quotes and endorses only in part. It holds that the men of letters were flattered dupes who covered her crimes. Morley adds the contrast with France, where the Sorbonne burned Marmontel's _Belisarius_ while Russian courtiers translated it. Diderot was refused the French Academy and was an honoured guest in Russia. This bears on the philosophers courting power, and on the Encyclopaedists as a network whose prestige could be harnessed by monarchs.

**The code that could not work.** Catherine pillaged Montesquieu for her Instruction, but Morley says Montesquieu's own method would have taught her that absolute power cannot force a free-labour civil system into a society resting on serfdom. He also suggests she privately valued her code less than she told a Parisian philosopher.

**Toleration as a blinding watchword.** When Catherine championed the Polish Dissidents against a Catholic oligarchy, the philosophers saw "that great device on her banners" and "asked no further questions". Morley says that, with the "significant exception of Rousseau" ([[jean-jacques-rousseau]]), they abstained from criticising the Partition or the death of her husband. Voltaire's quoted line is "family affairs with which I cannot possibly think of meddling". The line of argument is Morley's own, a liberal critic's reproach of the philosophers.

**Opinion-management and the Rulhière episode.** Catherine's sensitivity to European opinion is shown by her attempts to buy Rulhière's account of the 1762 coup and then to have him imprisoned. Diderot had warned Rulhière that not everything true is fit to be told. His reply to the Empress, quoted by Morley, offers her two ways to read the book, as a satire on her or as a portrait of a great princess.

**Falconet and the French colony.** Falconet came to cast the Peter the Great statue at Diderot's urging. Morley uses him and the quarrel with Betzki, to show the artistic strain of the French export, and quotes Diderot's complaint that the French at St. Petersburg "rend and hate one another".

## Notable passages

**Holland and the history of emancipation (c1).** Morley's reflection on Descartes, Bayle and Locke in the Dutch republic, which he says Diderot himself did not dwell on.

**Russia as an arc of Western progress (c2).** The comparison between Holland and Russia, with its portrait of a society of two ages at once.

**Catherine and the vogue of the philosophers (c3).** Her own account of how she found the philosophers wearisome and Voltaire useful, with Morley's conclusion that vogue, not philosophy, made her their friend.

**The Choiseul indictment (c4).** The Duchesse de Choiseul's letter on why the Empress needed the men of letters, and why they were pleased to be her dupes. The attribution is to the Duchesse, not to Morley, who introduces it as telling the secret plainly.

**Toleration, Poland and silence on the crimes (c5).** The Partition, the Dissidents, and Voltaire's remark about her husband, showing the philosophers' selective vision.

**Diderot to the Empress on Rulhière (c6).** Diderot's offered readings of the manuscript, pointing to the question of how far the philosophers could give a ruler the history she wanted.
