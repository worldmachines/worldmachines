# Morley on Diderot's Supplement to Bougainville: Orou against monogamy, and Morley against Orou

Morley ends the chapter on Diderot's minor dialogues with a short account of the *Supplement to Bougainville's Travels* (composed 1772, published twenty-four years later), subtitled a dialogue on the disadvantage of attaching moral ideas to certain physical actions which do not really comport with them. He sets the work against Bougainville's 1766-69 circumnavigation and the 1771 narrative, praises its literary grace, quotes Orou's two long speeches against European marriage, and then spends most of the unit on his own verdict: the naturalism is arbitrary and fanciful, the proposed remedy would be retrograde, and Diderot himself, the great celebrant of family feeling, must have written the attacks on constancy and fidelity in a moment of paradox. Morley closes on a partial concession: one interlocutor's diagnosis of how a sweet pleasure became a source of misery contains, on a charitable reading, a real reform programme (wives' property, easy separation, abolition of restitution of conjugal rights), though on an uncharitable reading it is "recklessly immoral". Throughout, the positions voiced are Orou's (that is, Diderot's character's) and the verdicts are Morley's own Victorian-positivist judgments; Morley does not claim Diderot endorsed the extreme reading.

## Themes

**Primitivism and its limits (Morley's judgment).** Morley places the dialogue within the pattern he has traced across this history, where Parisian corruption colours primitive society "with the very hues of paradise". Diderot, he says, was freer of this weakness than his contemporaries and never shared [[voltaire]]'s fancy that China is a land of philosophers, yet for the moment he committed a "sociological anachronism" by turning the people of Otaheite into wise, benevolent patriots and sound reasoners. The affinity with [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] and the [[noble-savage]] idea is explicit in Morley's phrasing (Diderot was touched "almost as deeply as if he had been Rousseau"), though Morley also credits Diderot with more scepticism than Rousseau.

**Orou's argument (Diderot's character).** Orou argues that European monogamy is contrary to nature because it makes a being who thinks, feels and is free into property, and contrary to the general law of things because it forbids "the law of change that is within us" and demands impossible constancy. Prohibition multiplies evil-doers by fear, punishment and remorse. In his second speech, he argues that once men dispose of the ideas of just and unjust at will, a train of suspicion, jealousy, concealment and crime follows. This is an early critique of an institution as legible-by-decree social machinery that manufactures the vice it polices; it loosely rhymes with WMP's concern with how imposed order produces its own pathologies (see [[world-machines]]), though nothing in the text uses such a frame and the argument here is naturalist, not institutional-historical.

**Children as the practical difficulty.** Morley notes that the chaplain falls back on the disposal of children, the standing difficulty of any scheme of free unions, including marriages for terms of years. Orou answers by describing how prized a mother of many children is in Otaheite, and grows contemptuous as he learns that in the west a birth among the bulk of the people is often a sorrow and a hardship.

**Diderot's caveat (quoted by Morley).** Morley stresses that at the close Diderot insists one must obey bad laws until they are reformed ("There are fewer inconveniences in being mad with the mad, than in being wise by oneself") and must be honest with the "fragile beings" who risk the advantages of society by yielding. Morley calls this Diderot's sincerest accent, but still judges the underlying philosophy unsatisfactory.

**Morley's counter-thesis: progress means more self-control, not less.** Morley argues that any future change in the relations of the sexes will move further from, not toward, eighteenth-century naturalism; even an Oneida Creek communism would demand more rigorous discipline of sexual appetite, and Orou would be "a thousand times more unhappy" under Mr. Noyes. His reasoning is that the position and moral worth of women are proportional to the self-control of men, so that the history of civilisation includes the gradual reduction of the violence of a polygamous instinct. This is a Victorian positivist's stadial view of civilisation as progressive restraint, and it is Morley's own thesis, not Diderot's.

**The other side of the ledger.** Morley concedes that the home is "too often a ghastly failure", that the conjugal bond can be cruel to the woman and deadening to the man, and that the family's purity has been bought by a "vast and dolorous host of female outcasts", a cost he sets against Catholic claims about womanhood. He insists, however, that Diderot "does not help us": reducing sexual attraction to its physical elements would be a return to "the nakedness of the brute".

**Diderot against himself.** Morley points to the irony that Diderot was the great exalter of domestic sentiment, and so the dialogue's definitions of constancy, fidelity and jealousy (as vanity, punishment, miserliness and property in a free creature) can only be a moment of "elated paradox", a reaction against ascetic excess that failed to see that the virtue of constancy must have answered to something in human character and society at their best.

**A reformist reading of the closing diagnosis.** The unit ends with an interlocutor blaming the misery of the strongest and sweetest pleasure on man's tyranny of possession, over-burdened usages, civil formalities and religious institutions that attach vice and virtue to non-moral acts. Morley reads this charitably as pointing to the next great step in social improvement (married women's property, separation as natural and blameless, abolition of restitution of conjugal rights), but warns that if it means the formal solemnity of an act that brings human beings into existence is wrong, it is "recklessly immoral" and repugnant to Diderot's better mind.

**Candour about literature.** At the outset Morley defends Diderot as an author "for grown-up people" and criticises English exclusion of unfit subjects as bringing "narrowness and thinness" and "nauseous hypocrisy", while still granting some merit to the practice. He also says that for the present writer the dialogue offers "very little to instruct".

## Notable passages

**Orou's first speech** (c1) is the core of the naturalist case: persons cannot be property, and constancy sworn "in the face of a sky that is not an instant the same" is senseless. **Orou's second speech** (c2) draws the corollary that arbitrary moral codes breed hypocrisy and deceit. **Diderot's obey-until-reformed caveat** (c3) is where Morley locates the author's real seriousness. **Morley's rebuttal** (c4) states the self-control thesis and the Oneida comparison; **the home as ghastly failure** (c5) is his admission of the other side; **the elated-paradox passage** (c6) quotes the dialogue's definitions of Constancy, Fidelity and Jealousy and shows how Morley tries to rescue Diderot's reputation as a family man.
