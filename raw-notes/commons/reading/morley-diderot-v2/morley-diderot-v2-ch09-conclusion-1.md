# Diderot's Interpretation of Nature, read through Comte

Morley opens Chapter IX (the volume's Conclusion) by conceding that an exhaustive survey of Diderot's miscellaneous writings (treatise on harmony, physiology notes, mathematical memoirs, the Russian university plan) is out of the question, and then devotes section I to the *Thoughts on the Interpretation of Nature* (1754), which he counts the most important of the works not yet discussed. He places it against Maupertuis's 1751 *Universal System of Nature* and the Leibnitzian law of least action (the "Law of Thrift"), which Morley says led directly to Holbach. He then walks through Diderot's loosely connected, Baconian aphorisms: the reconciliation of scientists with many instruments and few ideas and philosophers with many ideas and few instruments, a prediction that geometry will stall, Comte-friendly passages on the limits of human knowledge and the role of utility, the rejection of final causes, the unity of nature, and a closing reflection on how little men know of practical things. Throughout, Morley is the Victorian positivist reader: the interpretive frame (Comte, the "subjective synthesis", the Positivist library) is his, while the aphorisms are Diderot's.

## Themes

**Context: Maupertuis and the unity of nature.** Morley (not Diderot) frames the work as a response to Maupertuis's claim, as Morley hedges it ("he seems to have maintained"), that the universe's mechanism is one throughout. Morley says Diderot at this stage resisted that theory, though without his usual vehemence. Morley himself links the Law of Thrift to the road leading to Holbach's *System of Nature*. Diderot's own § 11 nonetheless argues that nature has perhaps produced only one single act, and that without an idea of a Whole there is no Philosophy; Morley says science has been less successful at working out this philosophy than at abandoning final causes.

**Bacon as form; two kinds of thinker.** The work imitates Bacon's *Novum Organum*, with Diderot letting thoughts follow one another as they arise. Morley summarises its scope as reconciling those with many instruments and few ideas and those with few instruments and many ideas, i.e. science without philosophy and philosophy without experiment.

**Geometry's decline and the cost of deductive social science.** Diderot predicts that within a hundred years Europe will not have three great geometers. Morley endorses the broader trend (the century's discoveries lay in the higher sciences) and cites Comte's hostility to algebraic analysis. He then makes his own argument that France's misfortune was that the supremacy of geometry coincided with the age of political discussion: Montesquieu's definitions are said to be those of a geometer, and social truths were treated like Euclid's propositions, with deductions from arbitrary premises taken as accurate descriptions of real circumstance.

**The limits of knowing, and utility as boundary.** In the long passage Morley quotes as the germ of Comte's subjective synthesis, Diderot says that the phenomena of nature so outrun human understanding that we can only hope for a few broken pieces of the great chain; that even a complete divine book of nature would not be comprehensible; that the effort resembles the tower of Shinar; and that "it is utility that circumscribes all", setting bounds to experimental physics as it is about to do to geometry. Morley adds that Comte placed the *Interpretation of Nature* beside Descartes's *Discourse on Method* and Bacon's *Novum Organum* in his Positivist library. This is a Comtean reading; Morley presents it as the text's spirit, not a claim that Diderot was a positivist in name.

**Selection and discipline in inquiry.** § 54 argues against total observation (a complete "insectology") and for assigning great objects to great geniuses. Morley sets against this § 42 on experimental persistence: do not cling to or abandon a system lightly, because "Never is time employed in interrogating nature entirely lost". Morley glosses that this is limited by verifiability, and that Diderot's hypotheses (e.g. the curve analogy in § 44, unifying weight, elasticity, magnetism and electricity) were left to posterity without building any fabric on them.

**Against final causes.** In § 56 Diderot says the physicist abandons the Why for the How, mocks the hymns of defenders of final causes, and argues that physiology advances (Galen to Boerhaave to Haller) by dropping such conjecture; man praises the Eternal for his own poor views. Morley calls this penetrating the hollowness of every system dealing with Final Causes, and notes the world has since moved rapidly along this path.

**Humane priorities.** Diderot closes by posing questions about living versus dead matter, then undercuts them: the world looks old when one surveys human works but young when one sees how ignorant men are of medicine, agriculture and the plough. Morley praises this as showing the difference between a wise and a wasteful use of intelligence.

## Notable passages

**Maupertuis and the Law of Thrift (c1).** Morley's setting of the work: the 1751 dissertation under the name Baumann, the Leibnitzian minimum-action idea turned into the *Loi d'Epargne*, its tendency to make the unity of all forces the goal of inquiry, and its line to Holbach. This is the closest the unit comes to mechanism-as-one-system; it resonates with [[gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz]]'s principle of economy and with monist strands of Enlightenment thought ([[enlightenment-monism]]), though Morley says Diderot's own stance toward Maupertuis was hesitant.

**Geometry and the French political mind (c2).** Diderot's prediction of the end of great geometers and Morley's own claim that deductive geometry corrupted French political thinking by treating social complexity as Euclid. This rhymes with the project's concern with [[Legibility]]-style simplification of complex social reality, though Morley's complaint is about method and Comtean positivism, not about states and scale.

**The Shinar passage (c3).** The unit's most extended Diderot quotation: the infinite phenomena versus the weak organs of man, the unreadable divine book, the tower we insist on building, and utility as limit. It is the key evidence for Morley's Comte connection.

**Final causes (c4).** Why not Why: physiology, not hymns. It shows the mechanistic, non-teleological direction Morley traces towards Holbach, though Diderot here still speaks of the Creator with deference; this is a methodological, not an atheist, argument.

**One act, one Whole (c5).** Diderot's § 11 speculation that nature may have produced a single act, with the independence of any single fact incompatible with an All. Morley notes the world has been slower to work out this philosophy.

**Old world, young earth (c6).** The closing meditation on how little is known of the commonest practical matters, urging that men should devote themselves to inquiries bearing on well-being. Morley frames this as a utilitarian rebuke to metaphysical questions, consistent with the utility theme that [[world-machines]]-style analysis could read as a pragmatic constraint on knowledge-building, though that comparison is the note-taker's, not Morley's.
