# Holbach's System of Nature, part 2: naturalism, man, free will, immortality

Morley expounds the core of Holbach's *System of Nature* and comments on it as he goes. He first characterises the book's tone (an expounder and preacher, not a seeker), then sets out its naturalism: matter and motion are eternal and self-moving, all phenomena are necessary, and order, disorder and chance are merely relative or abstract terms. He then follows Holbach on man: mind or soul is an 'occult force' invented to cover ignorance, and medicine rather than theology holds the key to morals. The long middle of the unit is Holbach on free will and morals, where Morley gives high praise to the determinist argument and to the utilitarian account of praise, blame and punishment, while flagging a verbal shift in the meaning of moral words. The unit ends with the chapter on immortality (a Lucretian argument), Morley's mild regret at Holbach's lack of historic spirit, and Holbach's indictment of kings and social injustice, which Morley reads as a prophecy of the Revolution.

## Themes

**Naturalism as exhortation, not proof.** Morley's own verdict is that Holbach is 'less a seeker than an expounder; less a philosopher than a preacher'. Holbach's programme (as quoted) is to study nature's laws, apply the discoveries to human felicity, and submit to necessity while ignoring first causes behind an 'impenetrable veil'. Experience and physical science are to govern religion, morals and legislation alike.

**Matter, motion and necessity.** Per Holbach, nature is the whole; motion flows from the essence of matter and is eternal; matter is a 'Kind' of existence, not one passive substance. All phenomena are necessary, down to each molecule in a storm and each agent in a revolution. Order is necessity seen as succession, disorder is passage to a new order, and Chance is only 'the limitation of man's knowledge'. This is mechanism and monism in the sense of [[enlightenment-monism]], with a nearer kinship to [[baruch-spinoza]] in the necessitarianism, though Holbach's matter is self-moving, not a substance with attributes, and Morley himself does not draw the Spinoza link here. Morley notes that Holbach defines matter only 'relatively to us' and says nothing definite about its ultimate composition.

**The soul as occult force; medicine and morals.** Holbach (as expounded by Morley) holds that dualism of body and mind explains nothing and that 'mysteries' are a confession of ignorance. Morley adds his own point that humane treatment of insanity and certain crimes came from men like Pinel, trained in eighteenth-century materialism, and could not have come before theology decayed; he also warns that the converse (mental remedies for bodily disease) may now be overlooked. Man is a mere 'insect of a day', and Morley observes that orthodox theologian and atheist both humble human pride, but to opposite effect: the theologian discourages men and waits for Grace, the naturalist arouses them.

**Free will, punishment and the 'machine'.** Morley judges the demolition of free will one of the most effective parts of the book. The man who throws himself from a window and the man thrown differ only in where the impulse originates. Responding to the charge that fatalism undermines justice, Holbach (per Morley) ties praise, blame, reward and punishment to the effect of conduct on common welfare: they are forces acting on motives, as one may dam or divert a stream. Morley adds a qualification of his own: capital punishment aims at other people's minds, not the offender's. Necessarian theory should make us act against bad training and institutions rather than the individual. Against the charge that necessity makes man a machine, Holbach answers that nature is itself a vast machine and the good man one whose springs yield beneficent results. Morley's closing caution is that Holbach does not see how far he has emptied praise and blame of their old content, and he calls for a readjustment of ethical vocabulary to man as an object of science.

**Immortality and the political indictment.** Holbach denies the survival of a soul (a broken clock cannot strike), and Morley finds the argument the familiar one of Lucretius, Book III. Morley regrets Holbach's unhistorical abuse of David, Mahomet and Chrysostom and thinks he overstates in denying all deterrent force to hell, but believes that by 1880 a thousand people would agree with him that non-theological motives can serve. Holbach then turns to education and law and indicts kings; Morley quotes passages on society as a state of war and on whole nations labouring for 'a handful of insensates', and says these make us feel Robespierre, Desmoulins and Danton are already speaking. This is Morley's retrospective framing, not Holbach's.

**Morley's frame.** The comparison to the Darwinian shake to theology, the praise for humane penal reform, and the demand for a new ethical language mark Morley as a Victorian positivist reading Holbach as a precursor. The reading of the book as prophecy of 1789-94 fits [[intellectual-vs-revolutionary]] and the notion of ideas preparing a revolution, though here it is Morley's literary-historical judgment rather than demonstrated causation.

## Notable passages

**Matter and motion from nature herself.** The argument that nature, as the great whole, is the source of its own motion, and that matter is eternal and diverse, is the metaphysical base of the system (c1).

**All phenomena are necessary.** The dust-storm and the 'moral whirlwind' of empires passage universalises necessity to history and revolutions (c2); it also bears on any determinist or mechanistic reading of history, with the caveat that Holbach is making a metaphysical claim, not a forecasting method.

**Mind as an occult force.** Holbach's dissolution of the soul, with the Red Indians and gunpowder analogy, and the claim that calling the union of body and soul a mystery is 'a confession of absolute ignorance' (c3).

**Free will exposed.** Morley's praise of Holbach's argument, the window example, and Holbach's quip that liberty was invented to justify God (c4).

**The good man as a machine.** The reply to the charge of dehumanising necessity (c5).

**The state of war and the valley of tears.** Holbach's pre-revolutionary indictment, with Morley's gloss on the Revolution's leaders (c6).
