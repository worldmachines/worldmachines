# Africans in Charge: Slave-Trade Agency, Anonymized Labor, and the Maroon State of Palmares

Mann opens with the legend of Aqualtune, an Angolan princess-general said to have founded Palmares on the Serra da Barriga in Brazil, and then uses Palmares as a frame for two arguments. The first is an economic-institutional one: Africans, not Europeans, controlled the supply of enslaved people, because in western and central Africa labor rather than land was the alienable, taxable form of property. The second is that the plantation system converted known, kin-linked or war-captive slaves into anonymous units of labor, and that this army of captives, many of them soldiers, repeatedly turned into an enslaved army. He follows revolts and maroon communities in Hispaniola (the Jolof rising of 1521, Enriquillo, Sebastian Lemba) and then gives an extended narrative of Palmares: its structure, its military defenses, its mixed population, the failed Portuguese and Dutch attacks, Zumbi's leadership, and the 1694 fall of Macaco to Domingos Jorge Velho.

## Themes

**African agency and the supply side.** Mann's own claim, leaning on John K. Thornton and Robert Harms, is that except perhaps in the trade's last decades Africans sold captives "in the numbers they chose at prices they negotiated as equals." African states played European buyers against each other; Asante's rise is presented as a calculated exchange of captives for flintlocks, and African demand for guns is treated as a growth driver of the trade. He frames the question of why Africans sold Africans as partly a case of presentism, since slavery was ordinary in both Europe and Africa.

**Property regimes explain the difference.** Following Thornton, Mann says slaves were the only form of private, revenue-producing property in African law, land being state-held and neither easily sold nor taxed. Rulers therefore sought control of people rather than territory (the Napoleon and Egypt contrast). Europeans could own land, Africans could own labor, and the decisive difference was that labor is portable. Mann hedges the comparison of brutality: African slavery was probably less lethal in the long run because surplus slaves had display value and manumission was likelier, yet he notes that captives seized in raids would hardly have celebrated the system's humanity.

**Tiny outposts, large effects, and anonymization.** European posts (Elmina, Gold Coast, Whydah) held only a handful of Europeans and were hemmed in by disease and African armies. Yet they were the catalytic points of a transformation: chattel slavery on colonial plantations, financed and directed by distant owners and financiers, made people "pieces" on a balance sheet. Mann says this transformation "was not understood as it occurred", an unintended, largely illegible shift. It resonates loosely with the legibility theme in WMP ([[Legibility]], [[legibility-as-machine-core]]): captives become standardized, countable inputs. Mann does not use that vocabulary, and the fit is analogical rather than exact. A consequence he draws is that owners did not know that many captives were prisoners of war.

**Slave armies and maroon states.** Revolt is treated as structurally predictable: in Hispaniola, Jolof-led rebellion in 1521, Enriquillo's Taino-African hill village, and Lemba's sixteen-year guerrilla war that shut most sugar mills while officials still asked for thousands more slaves. Mann's inference is that European labor hunger imported people who wanted to destroy the colony, and that maroons helped drive sugar off Hispaniola. Portuguese officials feared Palmares might turn Brazil's colonists into "marine froth" on the coast.

**Palmares as a mixed society.** Led by Angolans, Palmares was neither wholly African nor Angolan: Tupi-speaking Indians, European outcasts, Indian-style housing, a mix of American and African crops (maize, African rice, manioc), African forges, syncretic religion, and African-style military organization. This is Mann's mixed-societies theme within the book's human-exchange thread; it also touches the Columbian Exchange in crops. Planters even bought food from Palmares, trading guns and knives for it. Some details (Aqualtune's biography, Ganga Zumba and Zumbi as titles) Mann explicitly flags as uncertain or legendary.

**Portugal's last resort.** After forty years of failure, the colonial state turned to a bandeirante, Jorge Velho, who had more in common with the maroons than with other Europeans (Indian concubines and networks, poor Portuguese, private army). The defeat of Palmares came through a hybrid Indian-European-African force and a chaotic night assault in the rain, not through metropolitan military superiority. Mann notes that both sides were mainly Indians and Africans, with Europeans not running the show in Palmares.

## Notable passages

**Africans as market actors.** The myth passage (c1) states the thesis directly and is Mann's own argument, backed by Thornton and Harms elsewhere in the unit.

**Land, labor, and portability (c2).** The Thornton-derived account of why kings sought people rather than land; it is the load-bearing economic explanation, with the Napoleon aphorism as Mann's gloss.

**Pieces and anonymity (c3).** The passage on the shift from known slaves to "cans of soup" and "pieces" gives the analytic core of the unit's account of what the Atlantic system changed. The point is Mann's, not a period actor's.

**European labor hunger versus its own maroons (c4).** Links Hispaniola and Palmares and states the fear of a "mosaic of Afro-Indian states", a rare instance of Mann pointing to a counterfactual political geography.

**Palmares as creole polity (c5).** The composition of Palmares in people, crops, buildings, and arms is the unit's clearest example of mixed societies.

**Fall of Macaco (c6).** The night assault, with Mann's own reportage from the Serra da Barriga, shows the hybrid character of both sides and closes with the observation that the destruction of Palmares was far from the end of quilombos and maroons.
