# Out of Money: How Ming China Lost Its Currency and Needed Silver

Mann sets up the demand side of the silk-for-silver trade. He traces Chinese money from Warring-States-era bronze coins through the Song huizi of 1161 (the first nationwide state-printed banknote, which he calls the first modern paper currency), Song and Yuan inflation, and the Hongwu emperor's failed return to coin. By the sixteenth century, when wokou raiders were terrorizing the southeast coast, the empire "had no functioning currency." Merchants improvised with privately certified silver ingots, and the Ming state then adopted silver for taxation (over 90 percent of revenue by the 1570s). China had no silver mines to speak of, so this created a voracious external demand that Japan (via the wokou) and then Spanish Manila could satisfy. Most historical claims here are von Glahn's (Fountain of Fortune, 1996), with Quan Hansheng, the Ming Shi, Gu Yanwu and period officials quoted; the conceptual framing (fiat versus commodity money) and the wry commentary are Mann's own.

## Themes

**Commodity money versus fiat money as a control problem.** Mann's framing is that commodity money leaves the state at the mercy of "random shocks" (his example is the flood of Maldive cowries that wrecked cowry currencies from Burma to Benin), while fiat money gives near-complete control of the supply, which is at once its strength and its defect. The Song, Yuan and Ming histories are then a run of cases in which the power to print became inflation and, for the Yuan, what Mann calls hyperinflation. This is Mann's own synthesis, illustrated with von Glahn's evidence.

**State incapacity in a faction-ridden court.** Mann attributes the currency chaos to policy that was "often accidental by-products of ministerial intrigues, enacted with little regard for their actual effects." Coin bans repeated in 1394, 1397, 1403, 1404, 1419 and 1425 each failed, and each emperor's coins were voided by the next, so merchants faced evaporating capital and a game of hot-potato (Quan Hansheng). Mann hedges himself ("I am oversimplifying"), conceding the currency worked intermittently. The picture is of a state that cannot make its own money legible or reliable, which rhymes loosely with the WMP interest in the limits of legibility ([[Legibility]], [[legibility-as-machine-core]]), though Mann makes no such argument and the failure here is fiscal-monetary rather than an over-reach of measurement.

**Money privatizes from below.** With the state's money untrustworthy, merchants carried silver ingots, weighed with jewelers' scales and clipped with shears, certified by fee-charging kanyinshi. Mann jokes that this was "as if in a libertarian fantasy" the money supply being privatized. Coins were used in fewer than a tenth of transactions by 1570, according to one contemporary writer Mann cites. Later the Ming grudgingly adopted this bottom-up system through tax edicts. That a de facto standard emerged from merchant practice before the state ratified it is the sort of distributed assembly that echoes the [[history-machine]] idea, but only as a loose analogy.

**Silverization creates a paradoxical dependency.** Once taxes were paid in silver, China's huge economy needed a metal it could not mine. The sole nearby source was Japan, with which China was hostile, so merchants bought Japanese silver from wokou with silk and porcelain and used it to pay taxes that funded campaigns against the wokou: "The Ming government was at war with its own money supply." Beijing eventually legalized overseas trade by Fujianese merchants, and the galleons at Manila, carrying Spanish silver, were from the Chinese point of view "ships full of money." This sets up the world-economy formation Mann narrates next (compare [[braudel-world-economy]]; Mann does not use Braudel's frame and the connection is ours).

## Notable passages

**Commodity money's vulnerability (c1).** The cowry-shell example shows how an external commodity shock can dissolve a centuries-old financial system, and sets up the fiat-money contrast.

**Song and Yuan fiat inflation (c2).** Mann's compressed account of the huizi decoupling from bronze, the Song emperor "turn[ing] the printing presses on 'high'" to fight the Jin and Mongols, and the Yuan's hyperinflation and fall.

**Ming coin bans and the empire with no currency (c3).** The Hongwu emperor's copper shortage, then the repeated coin bans, culminating in the claim that "the Chinese empire had no functioning currency."

**The reign-by-reign voiding of coins (c4).** Mann's qualification ("I am oversimplifying") together with the Ming Shi's report of merchants ruined by dynastic succession.

**Silver ingots and privatized money (c5).** The ad hoc silver system, silvermasters and the near-abandonment of coin.

**Silverization and the war with its own money supply (c6).** The hinge of the chapter: the tax reform toward silver, the mismatch with domestic supply, and the wokou paradox.

**Nuance on attribution.** Mann says he has been drawing on von Glahn; the framing of Ming policy as chaotic is a synthesis of von Glahn, Quan Hansheng and contemporaneous complaints (an imperial chancellor, a 1606 gazetteer, Gu Yanwu), while quips such as the libertarian remark are Mann's own.
