# Prologue: a garden, the Columbian Exchange, and the creation of a single world

Mann opens with personal reportage: a college greenhouse of heirloom tomatoes (Black from Tula, bred in Ukraine) leads him to ask why an American crop is bred worldwide, and then to Alfred Crosby's *Ecological Imperialism*. He adopts Crosby's frame, the Columbian Exchange, as his own organizing idea. He then claims that Columbus's voyage marked not the discovery of a New World but its creation, and that this created a single ecological and economic system by the nineteenth century. He treats today's globalization debate as an echo of that origin. The rest of the unit is a guide to the book's four sections (Atlantic, Pacific, the two revolutions, the human exchange), a coda on the garden as a record of exchange, and Ortiz's idea of transculturation.

## Themes

**Ecological rather than purely technological explanation of European expansion (Crosby's argument, adopted by Mann).** Mann reports Crosby's thesis that Europe's long-run edge was "biological, not technological". Ships carried plants, animals and microbes, and ecosystems separated for eons met and mixed. Crosby held that this let Europeans remake much of the Americas, Asia and, to a lesser extent, Africa into ecological versions of Europe. Mann notes that Crosby conceded better troops and weapons. The exchange was "neither fully controlled nor understood by its participants", which is the point where this rhymes with [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and [[Legibility]]. It is an enormous, world-shaping process that nobody could read or steer, though Mann is not a machine theorist and does not frame it that way.

**A new world created, a single system forged (Mann's synthesis of the new research).** Environmental history and Atlantic studies (increasingly Pacific too) are said to be assembling a new picture of the origins of a "world-spanning, interconnected civilization". Mann's claim is that from the collision of two (or three, counting Africa) old worlds a single ecological system emerged "almost instantly, in biological terms". Born of European desires to join the Asian trade sphere, it then gave Europe the political initiative for several centuries. This is a globalization-as-world-system story. It sits near [[braudel-world-economy]] and [[archaic-globalization-bayly]], though Mann himself names the "world-system" only in passing and disclaims a systematic treatment.

**Globalization's two-sided ledger, and the past as template.** Mann declines the free-trade versus anti-globalization polarity and suspects both sides are partly right: enormous economic gains and ecological and social tumult that threatened to offset them, from the outset. He says four-century-old events "set a template" for the present. This is a claim about deep continuity, not a cyclical theory, so any [[modernity-machine]] reading (a c. 1200-1600 construction window) is an outside gloss on Mann, not his own frame.

**Map of the book.** Atlantic (Jamestown, tobacco as the first global commodity craze, malaria and yellow fever, slavery in Virginia, malaria and the founding of the United States); Pacific (silver from Potosi, Manila, Yuegang, sweet potatoes and maize in China, ecological feedback into the Ming collapse and the Communist era); two revolutions (the potato and the Agricultural Revolution, rubber and the Industrial Revolution, both supporting the West's rise); and the human exchange (about 90 percent of Atlantic crossers until around 1700 were African captives, Afro-Indian and Indian-African interactions, Mexico City as the first polyglot world metropolis), with a present-day coda.

**The garden as archive, and transculturation.** Mann's tomato patch, full of species alien to its location, is called "a biological record of past human wandering and exchange". Ortiz's "transculturation" (borrowed things are transformed by the borrower) and Mann's phrase "the crash and chaos of constant connection" as our "home condition" close the unit. This is close in spirit to divergence, since exchange proliferates local variants, and it is the counterweight to homogenization, which the prologue does not itself name. See [[divergence-machine]], with the caveat that Mann does not use that vocabulary.

## Notable passages

**Crosby's thesis (c1).** The compact statement of ecological imperialism: better weapons were real but the decisive advantage was biological, and the Exchange was uncontrolled and not understood by those carrying it out.

**The new picture and "creation" of a world (c2).** Mann's claim that the research adds the role of ecological and economic exchange to the "history of kings and queens", and that Columbus's voyage was the creation, not discovery, of a New World.

**One system from two or three worlds (c3).** The core Mann-own formulation of globalization as a single ecological system by the nineteenth century, which then shaped the political and economic contours of today.

**Both sides may be correct (c4).** Mann sidesteps the globalization polemic and asserts a historical template. Note the hedge: "I came to suspect", and "some muted, some thunderously loud" echoes.

**The human exchange (c5).** The preview of the fourth section, where the slave trade is called the most consequential exchange in human terms and interactions among Africans, Indians and Afro-Indians are described as a heritage just coming to light.

**Transculturation and the home condition (c6).** Ortiz's concept and Mann's conclusion that constant connection has been our normal condition since Columbus.
