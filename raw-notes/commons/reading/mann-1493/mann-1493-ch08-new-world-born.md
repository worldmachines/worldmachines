# Cortés, the Indian-labor dilemma, and the arrival of the Atlantic slave trade

Mann opens with Hernán Cortés as a disappointed man: a rich landowner-entrepreneur (silver, cattle, sugarcane, silkworms) who preferred chasing new kingdoms and was snubbed by Carlos V just as Las Casas's indictment reached court. He then steps back to the Spanish monarchy's unresolved problem, dating from Colón and Isabel: the pope licensed conquest only as a route to conversion, yet the colonies needed labor. The encomienda of 1503 was the compromise, and in Mann's account it failed. The New Laws of 1542 banned Indian slavery (with a loophole large enough to be a license), and within weeks Cortés contracted for 500 Africans. From there the chapter follows Africans across the Spanish and Portuguese Americas: the sugar boom in Mexico and Brazil, roughly 650,000 Africans between 1550 and 1650, cowboys and gauchos, and the long journey of Esteban, whose death at Hawikuh is left deliberately uncertain.

## Themes

**Moral dilemma driving labor policy.** Mann's own argument, built on the royal record: Fernando and Isabel could not justify enslaving people who had never rejected Christianity, but needed Indian labor because Spain lacked indentured servitude or a mass of unemployed to ship out. The encomienda is framed as "an attempt to answer the objections to slavery raised by Adam Smith" ([[adam-smith]]), a protection racket in which Indians paid with labor. Mann reports that it failed in practice: workers vanished into the countryside, and colonists argued that even baptized Indians were less human and could be forced to work.

**From Indian to African labor, with disease and social isolation as selection factors.** Mann says colonists observed that Africans "appeared immune to disease, didn't have local social networks that would help them escape, and possessed useful skills" (ironworking, horsemanship). This is the colonists' perception as Mann reports it, and it hedges nothing about immunity itself here; the fuller epidemiological argument belongs elsewhere in the book. The New Laws are treated as a trend-setter rather than a clean break, since enforcement failed (the viceroy of Peru was decapitated; New Spain suspended them).

**Sugar as engine of the slave trade.** Production in Mexico rose 1550-1600 while price tripled, which Mann reads as surging demand (Indians as well as Europeans wanted sweetness). With Brazil, this opened "the floodgates": about 650,000 Africans in a century, outnumbering European immigrants by more than two to one. Sugar is presented as a demand-driven commodity system nobody planned, an ecological-economic side of the Columbian Exchange more than a designed institution.

**Resistance and escape.** Drawing on James Scott's "weapons of the weak", Mann describes everyday sabotage on plantations and then escape to the heights, where African irregulars roamed Hispaniola for over a century, trading gold for goods and forging iron weapons. Sugar producers moved to Mexico partly to escape these guerrillas. Africans also assimilated into Indian groups on the Rio Grande, revolted at San Miguel de Gualdape, and, as gauchos, reproduced a West African pastoral life on the pampas. This anticipates the maroon material of the next chapter.

**Esteban and the illusion of control.** Mann narrates the Narváez expedition's survivors and Esteban's role as healer, scout and leader, then his second journey and death near Hawikuh. He gives the Spanish and Zuni versions side by side and admits the true fate may never be known. His closing judgment is his own: Esteban, like many Spaniards, "thought that he understood the shook-up world he was creating and that he was in control." This gestures at unintended consequences and the limits of legibility, though Mann is a narrative historian, not a machine theorist, and the link to [[Legibility]] is only an analogy.

## Notable passages

The **encomienda** paragraph (c1) and the **dilemma** paragraph (c2) state the institutional problem: conversion versus labor. They resonate loosely with WMP interest in how institutions are patched over unresolved contradictions (compare the Modernity Machine's scale-through-control theme in [[modernity-machine]]), though Mann does not frame it in those terms.

The **Africans in Hispaniola** passage (c3) records the 1501 attempts to bar Africans, the governor's plea ("They flee to the Indians"), and the colonists' reasons for wanting them anyway. The **weapons of the weak** passage (c4) is the pivot from enslavement to fugitive counter-power. The **floodgates** passage (c5) quantifies the trade after 1550. The closing **Esteban** passage (c6) is the chapter's reflective summary, with Mann's hedge about competing stories.
