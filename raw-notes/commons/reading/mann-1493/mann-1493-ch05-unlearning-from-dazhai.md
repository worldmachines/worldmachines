# Unlearning from Dazhai: erosion, floods and command-style ecology on the Loess Plateau

Mann opens with Dazhai, the Maoist model village that supposedly rebuilt itself after the 1963 floods by terracing its hills, and the slogans ("Destroy Forests, Open Wastelands!") that spread the method nationally. He then turns to the ground itself. The Loess Plateau is a vast heap of wind-blown silt that washes away easily, and the Huang He carries that silt to the North China Plain, where it raises the riverbed and makes floods a permanent threat. The Qing ran an enormous hydraulic system against this, but eighteenth- and nineteenth-century deforestation made floods worse, and the court did little about their cause. Mao's campaigns of the 1960s and 1970s cleared the last steep, scrubby slopes, so erosion rose, harvests fell, farmers migrated and the desert edge began producing dust. Post-Mao reforestation and the newer '3-3-3' scheme (a third of the land replanted with grass and trees, a third in orchards, a third cropped intensively) have been undermined, in Mann's telling, by officials who are rewarded for tree counts and by farmers with no incentive to keep the trees alive. The chapter ends on a line that ties the whole thing to the book's thesis: the government is trying to undo 'the accidental legacy of the global silver trade'. (The closing line is Mann's own framing; the chunk does not itself lay out the silver-to-highlands causal chain, which the surrounding chapter presumably does.)

## Themes

**A vicious circle of extraction from marginal land.** Mann reports Vaclav Smil's description of a "vicious circle": erosion strips nutrients, yields drop, farmers clear and terrace fresh land, which washes away in turn. Mann adds his own on-site observations (crumbling earthen terrace walls at Zuitou, a village that lost half its population, maize grown in near-pure sand where thorny scrub once stood). The Loess Plateau, 'which once caught dust from the desert, was now producing it'.

**Illegible ecological processes versus state-scale action.** Mann's reading of the Qing is that the cause of the flooding was 'well understood' yet Beijing did little, even though much land clearance 'had its roots in Qing policies'. He hedges: the failure 'was not foreordained' and 'nobody will ever know' whether decisive action would have worked, because it was not tried. This is Mann's own qualified argument, not a claim from a cited scholar. It sits alongside the Qing's costly hydraulic infrastructure (perhaps 10 percent or more of the imperial budget, by Mann's estimate), a system of legible control that was 'constantly overwhelmed' by a slow ecological driver it did not govern. The floods, he notes, damaged imperial legitimacy and 'helped to bring about' the dynasty's fall.

**Command campaigns and their failure modes.** Dazhai-style mobilization (thousands of officials bused in, cult-like emulation, slogans) is contrasted with today's planners, who can order whole villages to plant trees without worrying about property rights or protest. Mann argues from field observation that plan targets reward tree counts, not survival or species suited to place, and that farmers who gain nothing from the work let the trees die: 'Every year we plant trees, but no trees survive.' This is his most direct engagement with the question of whether a dictatorship is well suited to ecological repair. His answer is 'things look different on the ground'.

**Political constraint on remediation.** Confronting Dazhai's damage had to be done 'without admitting that Mao had made mistakes'; local officials politely changed the subject when Mann asked. The Green Wall / 'Three Norths' project and the 1981 tree-planting order did not directly address soil degradation.

**Link to the book's argument.** The chapter is titled around 'unlearning', and its last sentence attributes the whole cycle to the 'accidental legacy of the global silver trade'. Within this unit, that link is asserted rather than demonstrated. Note the Qing background (land clearance policy, shack people) is mentioned but not developed here.

## World Machines resonances

The unit is a case of ecological consequences nobody planned or controlled feeding back into state legitimacy, loosely in the register of [[history-machine]] (a process assembling out of distributed components until it becomes visible as a crisis) and of unintended consequences of world-system integration ([[braudel-china-precocity-puzzle]] concerns why China's economy behaved as it did; this unit gives an environmental strand). The top-down plan-by-quota failure, with the metric (trees planted) standing in for the goal (trees alive), rhymes with the legibility critique in [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and [[modernity-machine]], though Mann does not use Scott's vocabulary and is not a machine theorist; the fit is by analogy only.

## Notable passages

**Dazhai as model (c1).** Mao's officials are bused in, learn to terrace every slope, and take home the slogans 'Destroy Forests, Open Wastelands!' The passage shows how a single successful anecdote became national policy.

**The river above the fields (c2).** The silt mechanics: the Huang He rises 1 to 3 inches a year and sits up to forty feet above the plain, so the fertility source is also the flood threat.

**Qing hydraulics and failure to act (c3).** A dozen overflows between 1780 and 1850, a possible million dead in 1887, and Mann's hedged judgment that inaction was not inevitable.

**Mao's second round of clearing (c4).** Terraces of packed earth, nutrient loss, Smil's 'vicious circle', and a one-third rise in erosion into the river during the Dazhai era (Chinese researchers, 2006).

**Smil on waste (c5).** 'One of the greatest wastes of human labor in history' is Smil's judgment, quoted by Mann.

**Fish-scale pits and dead trees (c6).** The dictatorship-versus-incentives argument and the closing link back to the silver trade.
