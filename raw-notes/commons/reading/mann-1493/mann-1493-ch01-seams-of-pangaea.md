# La Isabela and the Reknitting of Pangaea: Globalization as a Biological Event

Mann opens the book with reportage from the ruins of La Isabela, on Hispaniola's north coast, the settlement Colón founded on January 2, 1494 on his second voyage and abandoned within about five years. He recounts its failure (starvation, mutiny under Pedro Margarit, war with a Taino alliance, Colón's humiliated departure in 1496) and then argues that the failed colony nonetheless began "the era of globalization." Mann's central move is to reframe globalization as primarily biological, borrowing Alfred Crosby's Columbian Exchange: the seams of the ancient supercontinent Pangaea were re-stitched as European ships carried species, and pathogens, across the oceans. He then shows the mechanism at small scale in Hispaniola: livestock and African grasses transformed the landscape, a chain of accidental interactions (scale insects, fire ants) wrecked Spanish orchards in 1518-1519, and Old World epidemics, more than Spanish cruelty, erased the native population.

## Themes

**Globalization as a new, continuous, two-hemisphere exchange.** Mann is explicit that long-distance trade (the Silk Road, the Indian Ocean) was old, but claims nothing earlier joined both hemispheres at a scale able to disrupt societies on opposite sides of the planet. His vignettes (African slaves mining silver for China, tobacco in Madrid, Madras, Mecca and Manila, the Edo smoking gangs) foreshadow the book's later chapters. This is Mann's own framing; it resonates with world-system arguments such as [[braudel-world-economy]] and contrasts with [[archaic-globalization-bayly]], which stresses pre-1500 integration, though Mann does concede earlier networks. He locates the origin point in one man and one place, which sits uneasily with structural readings of the period.

**Biology as the deeper layer of history.** Following Crosby, Mann calls the Columbian Exchange "arguably the most important event since the death of the dinosaurs" and an "invisible wave" carrying kings and peasants along unknowing. He notes Crosby's claim was once controversial and that a growing number of researchers now agree. For World Machines this is a striking case of a process that shaped everything yet was illegible to its participants (compare [[Legibility]]); it also fits the idea of a paradigm assembling silently rather than by great-man agency ([[history-machine]]). The fit is partial: Mann is a narrative journalist-historian, not a machine theorist, and the Exchange is ecological rather than institutional.

**Unintended consequences and ecological release.** The fire-ant episode is presented with care about evidential status: Edward O. Wilson's scale-insect explanation is explicitly "informed speculation," while Las Casas's eyewitness account of 1518-1519 is documentary. Mann keeps that hedge. The Spaniards see ants and resort to a lottery-chosen saint, St. Saturninus, a vivid case of actors misreading a systemic, ecological cause.

**Disease, not just cruelty, destroyed the Taino.** Mann concedes Spanish cruelty but says the larger cause was epidemic disease with no prior presence in the Americas; smallpox arrived in 1518, and he cites deaths of three-quarters or more of the hemisphere's people across two centuries. Population estimates for Hispaniola vary from 60,000 to almost 8 million, and Mann reports the range without choosing. The 26,000 counted in 1514 and fewer than 500 by 1548 anchor the collapse, with an economic corollary: the colonists "had wiped out their own labor force." This foreshadows the labor-supply logic behind the later slave trade.

**Colón as failed administrator, world-historical by accident.** Mann's framing of modern critics (a cruel, deluded man who stumbled onto the Caribbean) versus the view that he "inaugurated a new era in the history of life" is his own; he sides with the latter on ecological, not moral, grounds. The colony fails because Colón cannot reconcile governor and explorer roles, colonists want gold not farms, and the Taino stalemate is mutually ruinous.

## Notable passages

**The birth of globalization (c1).** Mann distinguishes the Silk Road and Indian Ocean trade from the post-1494 network on three counts: two hemispheres, speed, and disruptive scale, and concludes that Colón "began the era of globalization." A thesis statement for the whole book.

**Pangaea and the Columbian Exchange (c2).** The core conceptual passage: globalization as biological, Pangaea's breakup, pre-Columbian crossings (gourds, coconuts, sweet potatoes, still debated), and Crosby's phrase about reknitting the seams. Note that Mann calls the ecological importance "arguably" the greatest since the dinosaurs; the hedge is his.

**Remaking the Caribbean (c3).** The hitchhiking menagerie (earthworms, mosquitoes, honeybees, rats), and the sense of species pouring "like eager tourists" into lands that had never seen their like; sets up the Homogenocene concept.

**Scale insects and fire ants (c4).** Wilson's speculative chain from plantains to scale insects to fire ants, joined to Las Casas's account of orchards "scorched and dried out" and colonists sleeping with bed legs in bowls of water.

**Epidemics and demographic catastrophe (c5, c6).** The list of pathogens absent from the Americas, the 1493 and 1518 epidemics, and the claim that "there is no comparable demographic catastrophe," closing with the hint that Taino DNA survives in Dominicans of African or European appearance.
