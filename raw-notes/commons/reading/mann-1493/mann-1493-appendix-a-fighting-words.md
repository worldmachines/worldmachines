# Naming People Without Anachronism: Mann's Terminological Note

This short appendix is Mann's author's note on vocabulary. He names three problems: familiar names are often inaccurate or insulting, different observers perceive things differently, and words shift meaning over time, so an historically accurate term can still convey the wrong thing. Through worked examples (“Asian,” chino and sangley in Manila and Mexico, “European,” negro versus preto, “Indian”) he argues that no consistent period-accurate scheme is available. His solution is to refer to people by modern geographic origin (Spaniard, African, Asian), with local names where regional origin matters, and to use ethnic names for indigenous Americans.

## Themes

**Categories are unstable across place and time.** The same word means different things in different contexts: “Asian” denotes East and Southeast Asia in the United States but South Asia in Britain. Chino in Manila meant roughly people from Asia who are not from the Philippines (perhaps also not Japan), while in Mexico it covered anyone with “Asiatic” features, including Filipinos, and then drifted to mixed descendants of Indians. Mann's own chosen labels are thus a pragmatic compromise, not a claim of accuracy. The china poblana anecdote shows how such drift also produces false folk history, which Mann attributes to what researchers say and not to any single source.

**Collective identities are later constructions.** “European” as a resident-of-Europe word appears in English only in 1639 (per the OED, as Mann reports it); earlier people identified by nation or region, or as Christian. Outsiders in Africa, America and Asia grouped the newcomers together anyway. Mann admits his scheme courts anachronism (he calls the Basque Legazpi a Spaniard, avoids “British” before 1707) and invites correction. This resonates loosely with WMP concern about what categories a machine makes legible versus what actors themselves used; compare [[Legibility]] and [[legibility-as-machine-core]], though Mann is making a plain writer's point, not a theoretical one.

**Race in the modern sense is a later social concept.** At the dawn of globalization Iberians enslaved “Moors,” “infidels” and “idolaters,” and the live question was whether Christians could be enslaved. Negro began as a mostly ethnic descriptor after the 1450s, later coming to mean slave, with Central Africans distinguishing negro (slave) from preto (free), as reported from Heywood and Thornton. Mann hedges: he says he is oversimplifying, since Europeans also believed moral stain was inherited. He separates genetic race, which he says exists but is hard to pin down, from the social concept of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century theorists, and says the two are only loosely connected.

**The indigenous exception.** Indigenous people get ethnic names (Inka, not Peruvians), with exceptions such as “Angolans” in Palmares where ethnicity is unclear. He keeps “Indian” because alternatives are worse for a writer and because, in his experience, indigenous Americans use it themselves; he grants the word is wrong on its face.

## Notable passages

The opening states the three-part terminological problem. The Parián/chino passage is the most concrete demonstration of a label whose meaning shifted by location and speaker, including that Fujianese residents saw “Chinese” as applying mainly to the Han. The method passage gives the rule and admits its flaws. The two race passages explain why Mann avoids modern racial categories for the early period and how slavery's early logic was religious and status-based; readers should treat this as Mann's framing, with his own stated simplifications. The final passage justifies keeping “Indian.”

For World Machines, this is mainly methodological context for reading the rest of the book; it touches the emergence of globalization only indirectly, via the claim that modern race concepts did not yet exist at its dawn.
