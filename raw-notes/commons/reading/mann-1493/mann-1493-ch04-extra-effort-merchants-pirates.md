# Ming Sea Bans, Wokou Pirates, and the Reopening of Trade

Mann opens Part Two (Chapter 4, first half) with the vast treasure fleets of the Yongle emperor and Zheng He (voyages 1405-1433), then asks why China abandoned them and why, a century and a half later, it reversed a long ban on private sea trade. He first sets out two scholarly readings of the shutdown: Landes, Jones and Hall see a fatal Chinese insularity and lack of curiosity, while other scholars (Goldstone is named) say China simply had nothing to gain abroad. He then argues that the deeper question is the Ming's general ban on private trade, its collapse in the face of smuggling and piracy on the Fujian coast (a chronicle of Yuegang, Zhu Wan, the Twenty-four Generals and Shao Pian), and the 1567 reversal, driven by political failure and by a monetary need for silver. The chapter promises that the Pacific economic exchange came first and "greased the skids" for the Columbian Exchange, with ecological consequences deferred to the next chapter.

## Themes

**Two readings of Chinese retreat.** Mann reports, without adopting, the Eurocentric view (Landes, Eric Jones, John A. Hall) that Confucian complacency explains why China did not make "that extra little effort" around Africa. He sets against it the view, reported from other scholars and Jack Goldstone, that China had no economic reason to go, comparing it to the US ending Moon flights. Mann's own position is that "the question remains" at the level of the longer ban trend, which he treats as economics and politics rather than cultural deficiency.

**Control-seeking prohibition backfires.** Mann's own argument: the Ming ban on private trade was less an abhorrence of trade than an attempt to monopolize its profits (the ban-and-tribute scheme, 38 tribute delegations in 1403-04). Cutting the navy because trade was illegal produced "a delirium of smuggling." The attempt to increase state power ended by weakening it. This is a small-scale case of a legibility/control project generating illegible informal networks; it resonates with the theme of [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and [[Legibility]] (state wanting trade readable and channelled), though Mann never uses that vocabulary and this is only a loose rhyme.

**Wokou as a blurred merchant-pirate-elite network.** Mann, drawing on gazetteers and the Xiamen historian Lin Renchuan, describes wokou as mostly Chinese traders, failed scholars, bankrupt merchants and peasants, in multi-ethnic crews, working jointly with Yuegang merchant families. Local elites, and even the navy's officers, were entangled in the smuggling, so enforcement was hollowed from within. The Twenty-four Generals carved Yuegang into fortified neighborhoods and were imitated elsewhere: a self-organizing parallel order that the state could not defeat.

**State incompetence and factional politics.** The late Ming court is portrayed as dysfunctional (an emperor absent for twenty years, a drunk, and the Jiajing emperor's alchemy and grand secretaries; cf. [[ming-dynasty-grand-secretaries]]). Zhu Wan's rigid crackdown, executions, and forced suicide show honest enforcement being destroyed by the coastal elites' court connections. Even Zheng He's voyages ended through bureaucratic infighting.

**Geography and money.** Resource-poor Fujian, dependent on imported rice and its harbors, had "nothing for them on land" once trade was banned. On the economic side, the empire had lost control of its coinage and merchants used lumps of silver; lifting the ban was how China obtained silver, and so the start of the silk-for-silver galleon link between Manila and the Pacific. Mann frames this as the last link of the global network begun by Colon and Legazpi, an early stage of what later becomes globalization as a single world economy (cf. [[braudel-world-economy]] for a comparable world-economy framing, though Mann does not cite Braudel here).

## Notable passages

The Landes/Jones/Hall passage (c1) sets out the cultural-deficiency thesis Mann is complicating, and Goldstone's counter (c2) supplies the moon-landing analogy. The overview of why China opened up (c3) states Mann's thesis: political and economic causes, culminating in silver and the galleon trade. The ban-and-tribute passage (c4) shows the Ming wanted foreign goods, not foreign people. The wokou description (c5) contains Lin Renchuan's line that "Merchants were pirates, pirates were merchants." The closing passage (c6) records Shao Pian's capitulation, the gazetteer's account of losses, the 1567 rescinding of the ban, and the turn to silver.

Caveat for downstream use: the imagery of a Ming state "utterly" losing control comes from official gazetteers and chroniclers quoted by Mann, with formulations he himself flags as face-saving or idiomatic, so casualty figures and rhetoric should be read as period claims, not verified counts.
