# Hong Liangji, Malthus, and the Shack People's Ecological Collapse

Mann opens with Hong Liangji, a volatile Qing scholar-official who around 1793 saw that population grows faster than farmland, and sets him beside Thomas Malthus, who published the same idea in England five years later. Mann then argues, hedging as he goes, that Hong grasped the *mechanism* of the Malthusian trap better than Malthus did. In Mann's reading the trap is an ecological catastrophe that produces social dysfunction, not a simple race between food and mouths. The rest of the unit is a case study. After the silver influx and the arrival of American crops, Chinese smallholders switched to tobacco, and 'shack people' migrants planted maize and sweet potatoes on deforested slopes. Erosion, silted rivers and floods followed, striking the lower Yangzi rice heartland. Officials saw the disaster and did not stop it. Mann, drawing on Anne Osborne, attributes this to a collective-action problem, with a tax-exempt rental loophole as the mechanism. He concludes that American crops helped undermine the Qing, one factor among several.

## Themes

**The Malthusian trap, redefined.** Mann reports Malthus's textbook version (exponential population against linear food supply). He notes that global harvests of wheat, rice and maize tripled between 1961 and 2007 while population roughly doubled, which contradicts Malthus's prediction. He credits Hong, in his own hedged phrase 'apparently', with the subtler prospect that the push for yield produces ecological catastrophe, then social collapse. Mann says this is what researchers now mean by the term, and he frames modern environmental disputes as a question of whether accumulated wealth and knowledge will outrun their environmental costs.

**Crops as a driver of ecological feedback loops.** Mann's own argument is that tobacco (which needs four to six times more fertilizer and twice the labor of rice) and American maize and sweet potatoes pushed cultivation into the hills. Mann, citing historians Tao Weining, Osborne and Li Xiangjun, describes a positive feedback loop. Deforestation eroded the slopes, silt and floods wrecked the valley paddies, rice prices rose, and that spurred more highland maize planting. The flood-rate figures are the quantitative spine: roughly three major floods every two years in the Song, nearly two a year in the Ming, over six a year early in the Qing, and more than thirteen a year in 1841-1911. He also reports the 1970s meteorological-bureau flood maps and his own tally of four lower-Yangzi rice centers. The floods increased even after the Little Ice Age ended, which points to deforestation and not weather.

**Silver, prices, and the state.** Mann reports Quan Hansheng's view that silver inflow raised Chinese food prices as it had European prices, and he notes the Yongzheng emperor's failed 'correct agriculture' policy. Qing efforts to improve transport, meant to move staples, instead helped smallholders shift to cash crops.

**Governance failure as a collective-action problem.** Officials in Zhejiang banned maize in 1802 and 1824 and asked for troops, and nothing happened. Following Osborne, Mann says the chief obstacle was neither squeamishness about deporting people nor confusion. Landowners kept tax-free rental income from upland tenants and spread flood risk across the region. Local interests 'absorbing all of the gain and only a fraction of the pain' blocked every reform. Mann adds that other causes (a rebellion led by a Hakka mystic, weak emperors, two lost wars with Britain) also contributed, and he says 'catastrophe, like success, has many progenitors.'

**Present-day echo.** Mann's own reportage from Yongding, Fujian, finds China Tobacco contracting farmers to convert rice terraces to tobacco and villagers putting maize seedlings into a landslide heap. He offers this as evidence that the process is still going on.

## Resonances with World Machines (tentative)

The story is a case of an illegible, distributed process that no authority controlled or could stop, with unintended consequences of a globalizing exchange. That loosely rhymes with the failure side of [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and [[Legibility]]. The Qing state could see the flood disaster (censors, gazetteers, relief records) but could not act on it. Mann is not a machine theorist, so this is our reading and not his claim. The flow of silver and crops as one interlinked system also touches [[braudel-world-economy]]. The environmental-collapse thread has a loose kinship with the deforestation narratives in [[desiccation-theory-grove]], though Mann's case is about erosion and flooding and not about rainfall decline.

## Notable passages

**Hong's arithmetic of the household (c1).** Mann quotes Hong's farm-and-house thought experiment. It ends with a blunt answer to what nature does about surplus population: 'flood, drought, and plagues.' This is the primary-source seed of the Malthus comparison.

**Hong over Malthus (c2).** Mann's evaluative claim, hedged with 'apparently' because Hong never worked out the details, and the contrast with the failed Malthusian prediction of hunger.

**Tobacco drains the land (c3).** How cash-crop profitability, and imperial neglect of 'correct agriculture', pushed farmers from paddies into the hills.

**Maize into every crevice (c4).** The near tripling of China's cultivated area between 1700 and 1850 and the deforestation cycle it started.

**Flood statistics and the Katrina every month (c5).** The Song-to-Qing rise in floods, and Li Xiangjun's count of 16,384 Qing floods, concentrated in the richest farmland.

**Why nothing was done (c6).** Osborne's collective-action explanation, in which tax-free upland rents let local interests keep the gains and spread the losses.
