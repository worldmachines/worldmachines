# On the Boat: Bulalacao, Externalities, and the Garden

Mann, in first-person reportage, sails to Bulalacao on Mindoro's southern coast, near where Legazpi first met Chinese junks (in Mann's framing, the beginning of today's world-encompassing trade network). Guerrillas of the New People's Army make the road unsafe, so he charters a boat. The town is poor, cut off and waiting for investors; its planning coordinator Chiquita Cabagay-Jano pitches it as about to "swim in the stream of global commerce". At the landing hamlet of Maujao he finds a half-built "farm resort" and hears Rudmar's anger over logging that stripped the hills, an anger Mann says feeds the insurgency. He then generalizes: globalization's costs are local and its benefits diffuse, people want global goods yet resist sameness, and the only durable "home" is a place whose changes one owns, illustrated by a family garden of American-origin maize in a bahay kubo plot.

## Themes

**Globalization's origin site as a peripheral, contested place.** The spot where Spain met China is now a poor, guerrilla-adjacent backwater angling for resorts, golf courses and a webcam. Mann sets a speculative picture of the Mangyan trading with Fujian and Guangzhou junks (he flags it as imagination: "It is tempting to imagine scribes...") against the violent arrival of the Spaniards. The town "was waiting for the world".

**Ecological damage and the insurgency (Mann's own inference).** Rudmar reports that logging companies, after exhausting Luzon, stripped Mindoro's slopes, causing floods and yellow-stained beaches. Mann argues the guerrillas' base in the "ravaged hills" means they "saw all the costs of the great market and none of the benefits". This is his interpretation, not a claim by the guerrillas; he also notes the insurgents target resort-building equipment.

**Externalities and distribution.** Mann grants real benefits from logging (his grandfather's table, jobs for craftsmen and shippers) and says the distribution matters more than the magnitude: gains diffuse worldwide, pain intense and local. He borrows the economists' concept of externalities and their in-theory fix (internalize costs in the price, $125 instead of $100), then concedes "In practice, making those arrangements is not easy."

**Mixed motives: desire for the world and resistance to it.** No one forced Thelma, Dona Rosario, Shaanxi teenagers or Beijing wine buyers to want goods. Yet the same people resist the consequences, wanting to "be aggressively themselves". As the [[divergence-machine]] and [[modernity-machine]] vocabulary of World Machines would put it, this is a tension between convergence and proliferation, though Mann is not a machine theorist and frames it as an open question: "Unity or division ... which will win out? Or is the conflict inevitable?" The Homogenocene produces both identical landscapes and fragmentation into local identities.

**The garden as a counter-model.** Gardeners experiment and "experience the consequences of their own actions", so externalities are rare and change is owned, "which is why they feel like home". The bahay kubo garden holds Mexican-origin maize, squashes and peppers, evidence that the Columbian Exchange was "adapted and remade" by families rather than merely suffered.

## Notable passages

**The town waiting for the world (c1).** Cabagay-Jano's development pitch (investors, golf course, paved road, windsurfing cup, webcam) shows the periphery's own longing to be integrated into the global market.

**Rudmar and the yellow beaches (c2).** The human source of the anger: logging, floods, staining of beaches, and the sentence "They took the color from the earth". The following paragraph links this to the New People's Army.

**Externalities (c3).** Mann's clearest statement of the distributional problem of globalization, with a hedged, practical caveat about the standard economic remedy.

**Wanting and resisting (c4).** The unity-versus-fragmentation passage: the chapter's central reflection on the Homogenocene and identity, resonating loosely with the World Machines question of whether legibility-driven convergence or divergence wins, but posed by Mann as a genuine open question.

**Gardens as owned change (c5) and the remade exchange (c6).** The constructive answer: ownership of consequences makes change feel like home, and the maize with its "American past in its DNA" turned toward the next season shows adaptation of the exchange rather than passive receipt.

Attribution note: the travelogue and the externalities/garden arguments are Mann's own; the logging history and grievance come from Rudmar via translation ("perhaps imperfectly"); the resort clientele explanation comes from a worker.
