# Rolfe's Worms and the Landscape of Tsenacomoco

Mann opens Chapter 2 with a speculation, hedged as "just possible": that John Rolfe, the tobacco pioneer of Jamestown, was inadvertently responsible for introducing European earthworms to North America, carried in the English soil ballast dumped when ships loaded barrels of Virginia leaf. He uses the worms, and researchers' admitted ignorance of what they will do to eastern forests, to frame Jamestown as a case study in unintended consequences and as the English-American opening of the Columbian Exchange, with the colonists "bringing the Homogenocene to North America." The second half ("Strange Land") turns to what the English actually found in 1607: not a pristine wilderness but Tsenacomoco, a expanding Indian empire under Powhatan, whose people farmed, burned and lived with beavers and marshes in ways that made the land look unimproved to English eyes.

## Themes

**Small organisms as world-historical agents.** Mann leans on Darwin's late book on earthworms (Darwin's claim, as Mann reports it, that worms have "played so important a part in the history of the world") and on worm researchers John Reynolds and Cindy Hale. Worms do not travel unless humans carry them; New England and the upper Midwest had none after the Ice Age. Their arrival strips leaf litter, dries and opens the forest and cascades through insects, birds and mammals. Hale's quoted verdict is that this is a "gigantic, unplanned ecological experiment" with unknown long-term outcomes. Mann's own framing is that the arrival of foreigners was "an ecological watershed." The path of the worms is explicitly untraceable; Rolfe's role is conjecture.

**Unintended consequences and early globalization.** Mann's own argument: Jamestown was founded by merchants chasing imagined gold and silver and a short passage to the Pacific and China, in "the anodyne language of economics" an attempt to globalize Virginia. It failed as a business, but tobacco, representative government and the first captive Africans in English America left lasting marks. This rhymes with the WMP interest in world-system formation and in processes nobody controlled (compare [[history-machine]], where a paradigm assembles silently from distributed parts), though Mann is describing ecological rather than institutional assembly.

**Tsenacomoco as a real polity.** Against the "settlers" trope, Mann says the ships landed in the middle of a small, rapidly expanding empire that Powhatan had tripled to about eight thousand square miles and fourteen thousand people. He reports historical geographer Michael Williams's suggestion that the eastern forest may have been more populous in 1600 than densely settled parts of western Europe. The account of Werowocomoco and Powhatan's court draws on John Smith's and William Strachey's reports; the English found the king recognizable and the landscape strange.

**No domesticated animals, different landscape.** Mann's synthesis: only six domesticated species existed in the Americas, so transport and labor rested on human bodies, giving slower communication, no plows, pastures or roads on the English pattern, and larger effective distances. Powhatan farms were also different in form: fire-cleared, hummocked polyculture of maize, beans and squash, with no fences and with fallow land as a communal larder of useful wild plants.

**Legibility gap between two land-management regimes.** English observers, per historian Virginia D. Anderson, took fences as hallmarks of civilization and their absence as proof that Indians did not truly occupy the land. Mann's point is that the English could not read a working landscape they did not know how to see. This partly resonates with [[Legibility]] (the demand that land be demarcated and readable as property), though Mann frames it as cultural misperception rather than a state-building project.

**Engineered nature: beavers and fire.** Beaver dams made the Chesapeake a wet mosaic of ponds, marshes and channels, welcomed by Indians and resented by colonists. Seasonal burning, in Stephen Pyne's phrase, "remade the land into usable forms," producing open, cathedral-like forests and cleared travel corridors. Mann's conclusion: the coast was refashioned by its inhabitants, but the newcomers saw only a snarl and resolved to remake it to suit themselves, which sets up the coming ecological collision.

## Notable passages

**Darwin and the earthworm (c1).** The ballast-swap of English dirt for tobacco leads to the worm passage and Darwin's line that few animals have played so important a part in history as these "lowly organized creatures." It supplies the chapter title and the small-agent-of-large-change thesis.

**Hale on the unplanned experiment (c2).** The worm-free-forest passage describes the cascade from litter loss to understory loss and faunal decline, ending in Hale's admission that "we have no idea what the long-term consequences will be." It is a clear example of an illegible ecological process no one controlled.

**Jamestown as unintended consequence (c3).** Merchants' mistaken beliefs about gold, silver and a short route to China, the commercial failure, and the tag of Jamestown as "the opening salvo" of the Columbian Exchange and a "brushfire in a planetary ecological conflagration."

**Few domesticates, big consequences (c4).** Mann's compact account of why the lack of draft and pasture animals shaped speed, distance and land use in Tsenacomoco.

**Fences, fallow and the "unused" land (c5).** The passage on why the English read Powhatan land as unimproved when fallow plots were a food and medicine larder.

**Fire-made forest (c6).** Regular burning, Pyne's corridors of travel, and the claim that the open forest was as artificial as the clearings.
