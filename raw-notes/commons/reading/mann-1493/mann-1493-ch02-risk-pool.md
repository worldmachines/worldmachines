# Jamestown as a Risk Pool: Joint-Stock Capital, Bad Water and the Starving Time

Mann opens by debunking the John Smith and Pocahontas legend (Henry Adams's 1890s-era skepticism, Helen Rountree's anthropological objection that "None of the story fits the culture") to redirect attention from romance to what the English were actually doing in Virginia. His claim is that Jamestown was "primarily an economic venture" whose fate was decided "by impersonal ecological forces—the Columbian Exchange—that nobody in Virginia was then equipped to understand." He then explains why a private consortium, the Virginia Company, founded it: England's debt-laden crown could not pay for colonies, so risk was pooled among investors in a joint-stock company. The chapter narrates the swampy, brackish site, Smith's trade diplomacy with Powhatan, the winter of 1609-10 "starving time", the near-abandonment of 1610, and the grim statistic that eight of ten of the seven thousand people England shipped to Virginia between 1607 and 1624 died. It closes on two puzzles, why the company kept sending people and why Powhatan let the colony survive, and promises the Columbian Exchange as part of the answer.

## Themes

**Ecology over heroics.** Mann, as narrator, argues that the Smith story is a distraction. He reports historians (Rountree, Adams) who reject the rescue tale, but his own thesis is that impersonal ecological forces decided the colony's fate. The chosen site sat in a saltwater wedge with foul water; Carville Earle (as Mann reports) links it to "typhoid, dysentery, and perhaps salt poisoning." The multiyear drought worsened it.

**Joint-stock company as risk pool.** Because moneylenders treated the monarchy as a bad credit risk, Elizabeth and James delegated colonization to a joint-stock company, in which each investor's loss from failure would be "tolerable." Mann treats this as close to "the capitalist ventures meant in today's discussions of globalization," while noting the difference from Spain's crown-run La Isabela was "anything but absolute."

**Institutional economics, reported and left open.** Mann summarizes Douglass North (property rights, open markets, democratic checks) and David Landes (virtues fostered by European organization) as explaining Europe's rise. This is their argument, presented without endorsement; the chapter's narrative of eighty percent mortality and repeated failure sits uneasily beside it. This rhymes with the [[modernity-machine]] as an institutional construction (joint-stock companies as early corporate infrastructure, cf. [[david-s-landes]]), though Mann is not a machine theorist and the WMP frame is not his.

**Contingent asymmetries with Powhatan.** Powhatan, in Rountree's reading as reported by Mann, judged the starving English a weak, useful trading partner; the two sides' "Confidence borne of ignorance" (Fausz) shaped events. Powhatan behaves as a market actor, raising maize prices as English demand rose, and later withholds food as a weapon.

**Ignorance of the environment.** Colonists starved amid abundance because they were afraid to leave the fort, because many were gentlemen unused to labor (Morgan's count: 92 of 295), and because they did not know the local ecology. Mann concludes: died "of ignorance as much as inanition." This is a case of an unlegible environment defeating actors who assumed the world was readable to them, cf. [[Legibility]]; the fit is loose, since Mann means practical local knowledge rather than state legibility.

**Sunk-cost persistence.** The company tried wine, shipbuilding, iron, silk, salt and glass, and kept sending ships. Mann raises this as an unanswered puzzle.

## Notable passages

The joint-stock paragraph shows the logic of pooling risk. The North and Landes paragraph is Mann's compact statement of the institutional-economics account of Europe's rise, attributed to those scholars. The site description gives the chapter's ecological argument in miniature. The passage on Powhatan's bet shows indigenous strategic agency and the trade-for-food dynamic. The 'starve in the midst of plenty' paragraph names the roles of fear, class habit and environmental ignorance. The mortality passage delivers the number that makes the risk-pool framing chilling: the company's investors diversified their losses while colonists bore theirs in full.
