# Carolina's Indian Slave Trade and the Malarial Logic of African Slavery

Mann follows the English colony of Carolina (founded 1670) through two labor regimes. First comes an Indian slave trade, fed by an arms race among post-Mississippian southeastern confederacies who sold war captives for flintlocks. It was cheap for the English and lucrative for native sellers, and it was gone by about 1715, undone by its own warfare, by slave resistance, and above all by disease. Then, in the section headed "Villa Plasmodia", he explains why African slaves replaced both indentured servants and Indian captives. Duffy-negativity (near-universal in West and Central Africa) gives immunity to vivax, sickle-cell and repeated childhood exposure give partial resistance to falciparum, and falciparum, probably shipped in African bodies, made the Chesapeake and the Carolina low country deadly for Europeans. Mann qualifies the claim: malaria did not cause slavery but strengthened its economic case. He supports this with Massachusetts and Argentina (slaves, little malaria) versus Virginia and Brazil (falciparum, slave societies).

## Themes

**Indian slavery as a short-lived, self-destroying boom.** Mann (drawing on Alan Gallay) reports that captives were cheaper than indentured servants and could be amortized over decades. Carolina outsourced slaving raids to native allies against Spanish- and French-aligned groups. Demand for guns fed raiding, and raiding fed demand for guns, so the Southeast became engulfed in warfare that nearly destroyed the colony. This is a feedback loop nobody controlled, an unintended consequence in the sense that fits the [[history-machine]] idea of a paradigm assembling from uncoordinated components, though Mann himself does not theorize it that way.

**A speculative geographic coincidence, flagged as such.** Mann notes that the Chesapeake boundary between Algonkian societies with few slaves and Muskogean-speaking confederacies with many roughly matches the later slave/free state line. He asks whether native slave-trading traditions eased the way to African slavery in the South and calls the implication "speculative, but not, it seems to me, unreasonable".

**Biology enters history through differential immunity.** The Duffy antigen story (Louis Miller's NIH experiment with seventeen volunteers) and the contrast between inherited and acquired immunity establish that West and Central Africans were, in malarial zones, biologically fitter. Mann then inverts racial-theory logic: the biological advantage became a social deficit, and "Their immunity became a wellspring for their enslavement."

**Quantifying disparity.** Because colonial records are poor, Mann borrows Philip Curtin's data on British soldiers in West Africa (48 to 67 percent annual deaths versus about 3 percent for African troops) and on the Lesser Antilles, concluding cautiously that Englishmen were perhaps three to ten times more likely to die in the first year in falciparum and yellow fever zones. Curtin's conclusion is that a slave was economically preferable at up to three times a European's price. Mann notes that comparing malaria across the Atlantic is itself "a mark of the era we live in, the Homogenocene."

**Malaria, geography and social structure (with hedges).** The Mason-Dixon line roughly tracks the northern limit of endemic falciparum. Mann floats a "Villa Plasmodia" hypothesis that the plantation house on a hill with lawns and open windows is an anti-Anopheles design, and reports, then declines to endorse, claims about a distinctive southern psychology. The better-supported point, following Darrett and Anita Rutman, is that unreliable labor penalized small farmers and rewarded big planters, pushing malarial places toward "exaggerated economic polarization".

**Necessary but not sufficient.** Mann insists slavery would have existed without the parasite (Massachusetts legalized it in 1641; Argentina had it). The claim is comparative: falciparum zones ended up with more slavery, and the imprint was that of a slave society (Virginia, Brazil) versus a society with slaves (Massachusetts, Argentina). Planters did not knowingly exploit African immunity; competitive selection among planters did the work.

## Notable passages

**Cheap captives (c1).** The economic case for Indian slaves, including the 1708 census (four thousand English, almost 1,500 Indian slaves, 160 servants) and the Carolina buyer's boast that one slave brought a gun, ammunition, horse, hatchet and clothes.

**The Chesapeake coincidence (c2).** Mann's explicitly speculative link between native slave-trading cultures and the later slave/free divide.

**Inherited resistance and the inversion of genetic superiority (c3).** The key argument that immunity, converted "through greed and callousness", became a social deficit.

**Curtin's numbers (c4).** The evidentiary core for the mortality gap, with the observation that slavers hired African crews.

**Villa Plasmodia (c5).** The plantation-as-malaria-architecture idea, presented as a possible, not proven, association, and the critique of the older plantation-as-archetype historiography.

**Malaria did not cause slavery (c6).** Mann's careful statement of mechanism: differential survival, economic advantage, imitation of prosperous neighbors, and "its sails filled by the winds of Plasmodium". Unlike [[modernity-machine]] legibility, this is an illegible ecological process that shaped institutions without anyone seeing it; the resonance is loose, since Mann offers no machine theory and stresses contingency.
