# Rubber's Silent Forest and the Long Puzzle of Elastomers

Mann opens Chapter 7 with his own reportage from Longyin Le in southern China, where miles of identical, same-aged Hevea brasiliensis trees have replaced every other plant and, by a local driver's account, the mammals and birds; the villagers are nonetheless grateful for the prosperity rubber brings. He then turns to the history: in 1526 the Venetian ambassador Navagero and other Europeans encounter Aztec ullamaliztli balls and cannot comprehend their bounce; rubber stays a curiosity until the 1820s "rubber fever" and crash, then Charles Goodyear and Thomas Hancock independently arrive at sulfur vulcanization in the 1840s without understanding it. The unit ends with the nineteenth-century chemical establishment dismissing rubber as "grease chemistry" until Hermann Staudinger shows that it consists of very long chain molecules, and with the claim (Susanna Hecht's, as reported by Mann) that steel, fossil fuels and rubber were the three fundamental materials of the Industrial Revolution.

## Themes

**Ecological homogenization as lived landscape.** The Longyin Le plantation is Mann's concrete image of the Homogenocene: one species, one age, everything else cleared, silence of birds and insects. He reports the driver's distress ("There's nothing left") beside the farmers' gratitude, and does not resolve the two. He also relays botanists' warning, in the potato-blight mold, that so wide a monoculture invites an epidemic that could cause ecological calamity and "just possibly, a global economic breakdown." The warning is the botanists' as reported by Mann, not a demonstrated prediction.

**Landscapes as products of distributed, unplanned history.** Mann says the alien-tree landscape "was the creation of countless different hands in many places" and "much older than forty-five". This loosely rhymes with the [[history-machine]] idea of an order assembling silently from independent components, though Mann tells it as a narrative of individual actors, not as a machine theory.

**Encounter with novel matter.** Navagero, d'Anghiera and Oviedo lacked words and categories for bounce; Oviedo's contradictory description (a material that becomes lighter yet stays heavy) shows a European vocabulary failing before an Amerindian material technology. Mann stresses that South American Indians had used rubber for centuries (smoked, stretched, possibly waterproofing cloth, per Susanna Hecht), and that Europeans and Americans only "discovered" it as a product in the 1820s.

**Hype, crash, and blundering invention.** The Salem-centered rubber fever collapsed because early rubber turned brittle in cold and melted in heat. Mann presents Goodyear as obsessive, chemically ignorant and repeatedly imprisoned, and Hancock as more systematic; their patents, three weeks apart in 1844, differ in how well they reflect the actual process (per chemist E. Bryan Coughlin). Invention here runs ahead of understanding.

**Anomalous phenomena the reigning science could not make legible.** Rubber did not fit the crystals, periodic table and small-molecule assumptions of nineteenth-century chemistry, so chemists filed it under colloids and "Schmierenchemie". Staudinger's chain-molecule insight, resisted vehemently, eventually won a Nobel Prize in 1953. This is loosely resonant with [[legibility-as-machine-core]] (a material that resisted the legibility schemes of the period), though the analogy is ours and Mann makes no such argument.

**Rubber as industrial infrastructure.** Vulcanized rubber, Mann argues, underlies tires, electrical insulation, belts, gaskets and O-rings. Hecht's line that the Industrial Revolution required steel, fossil fuels and rubber, and Mann's remark that industrializing nations had steel and fuel but needed a rubber supply, sets up the later chapters on Wickham and plantation Asia.

## Notable passages

**The Asian rubber empire (c1).** Mann's summary that a plant absent from outside the Amazon before 1492 now dominates Southeast Asian ecosystems, with the epidemic-risk caveat attributed to botanists.

**Voices beneath the trees (c2).** The farmers' gratitude, the analogy between potato and Malthusian escape and rubber and the Industrial Revolution, and the turn to the history of hands behind the landscape.

**Confounded Europeans (c3).** Mann's judgment that Navagero, d'Anghiera and Oviedo met "a novel form of matter".

**Nobody knew (c4).** Neither Goodyear nor Hancock knew why sulfur worked or why rubber bounced; Coughlin calls it a huge puzzle that many chemists did not want to study.

**Grease chemistry (c5).** The colloid dismissal and the German label Schmierenchemie, with Coughlin's gloss.

**Long chains and cross-links (c6).** The mechanism explained through Staudinger's result: tangled, extremely long molecules, alignment on stretching, and the reason rubber heats up when stretched.
