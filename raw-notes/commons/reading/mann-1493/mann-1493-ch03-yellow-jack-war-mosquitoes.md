# Yellow Jack, Sugar Despotism, and the Mosquito as Military Actor

The unit has two halves. In "Yellow Jack", Mann follows the 1640s sugar takeoff on Barbados, where Dutch refugees from Brazil taught English planters to grow cane, and traces how the Columbian Exchange (the arrival of *Aedes aegypti* and the yellow fever virus aboard slave ships) changed the economics of labor. He then follows the ecological side-effects (deforestation, coastal marshes, sugar pots as mosquito nurseries, the spread of malaria to the Amazon and the Guianas) to the political outcome: colonial societies in which Europeans could not settle and so governed at a distance. In "War and Mosquitoes", he shows the same logic of seasoning and non-seasoning operating in the American Revolution and the Civil War, where armies entering the malaria zone from the north were disabled by disease. Nearly all the epidemiology is Mann reporting J. R. McNeill (whom he also interviewed); the institutional argument is Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson's.

## Themes

**Disease immunity as a hidden variable in labor systems.** Mann's Barbados account is that indentured servants and slaves were at first about the same price, and that the Columbian Exchange then raised the relative cost of indentured Europeans. Yellow fever is a mild childhood illness in Africa but a plague for Europeans, Indians and island-born slaves, while survivors gain lifelong immunity. The unit implies (Mann does not spell out the full labor-market step here) that this differential immunity is what tilted planters toward African captives. Mann's phrase for the Caribbean, borrowed from James Webb, is "a lethal environment for non-immunes".

**Ecological imperialism creating its own disease habitat.** Planters cleared land quickly; the resulting erosion and coastal marshes favored *Anopheles albimanus*, the local malaria vector, and the clay pots used to crystallize sugar are, on McNeill's reading, ideal breeding vessels for *A. aegypti*. Mann's line that plantations were "factories for producing yellow fever" is a striking case of an unintended, illegible consequence of a production system that nobody designed or controlled (compare the WMP concern with what a legibility-optimizing machine cannot see, [[legibility-as-machine-core]], though Mann is not a machine theorist and does not frame it so).

**Extractive states and offshore ownership.** Where disease blocked settlement, Europeans stayed home and managed a large enslaved workforce through a few onsite managers, so brutality was structural ("sugar despotism"). Booker Brothers in British Guiana and the roughly eighty thousand Frenchmen sent to Guyane (the "dry guillotine") illustrate the extremes. Mann reports Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson: absentee owners had no interest in building schools, hospitals, legal codes or parliaments, locals could not compete or accumulate, and industrialization needs new entrepreneurs and broad investment. Reform efforts (missionaries, the Anti-Slavery Society, "Jock" Campbell) did not change the basic system; Guyana was independent in 1966 with three foreign companies holding 80 percent of export earnings and one night-school university. This is institutional path dependence rather than a machine lifecycle, though it rhymes with WMP interest in how deep infrastructure shapes centuries of outcomes.

**Contingency in wars via seasoning.** Union troops in the Carolinas and Virginia, and Cornwallis's British and Scottish troops, entered malaria territory without immunity, while southern defenders were seasoned. Mann, following McNeill, treats malaria as delaying Union victory by months or years, with a speculative and hedged link to the Emancipation Proclamation, and as a decisive factor in the surrender at Yorktown. Mann and McNeill both credit human leadership and skill too, and Mann's causal claims here are hedged ("likely", "The idea is not impossible"). For WMP this is a vivid case of a non-human, unplanned actor shaping a foundational political event, resonant with the idea of a [[history-machine]] assembling from distributed components, though the mosquito is a contingency here, not a slow-assembling infrastructure.

## Notable passages

**The hitchhikers (c1).** Mann's compact account of yellow fever's biology and its transatlantic move: the virus lives mostly in the mosquito, kills about 43 to 59 percent of victims in six documented episodes McNeill compiled, and leaves survivors immune. The passage sets up the asymmetry between Africans and everyone else on which the rest of the unit depends.

**Sugar pots and marshes (c2).** McNeill's argument as Mann relays it: mill pots full of sugary residue and unused for part of the year were a seventeenth- and eighteenth-century equivalent of discarded tires.

**Offshore ownership (c3, c4).** Mann distinguishes harmless offshore ownership (his wine analogy) from total foreign domination of an economy, then brings in Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson on why extractive states cannot industrialize, including the Booker president's rejection of teaching workers to read.

**The Civil War arithmetic (c5).** Case rates never under 40 percent, 361,968 infected in a single year, and disease killing twice as many Union troops as Confederate fire; Mann's interpretation that malaria "likely delayed" Union victory is his synthesis of McNeill.

**Yorktown (c6).** The culmination: Cornwallis's army, half unfit, sealed in by a French fleet, surrenders. "Anopheles quadrimaculatus stands tall among the Founding Fathers" is McNeill's line, reported by Mann.
