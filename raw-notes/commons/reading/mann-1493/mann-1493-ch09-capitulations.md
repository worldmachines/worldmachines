# Maroon Capitulations: Freedom Won by Treaty, from Panama to Suriname

The unit closes Mann's chapter on maroons (escaped slaves and their Afro-Indian societies). It opens by finishing the Panamanian story: the Spanish crown, frightened of a maroon-pirate alliance after Drake, offers the maroons land, tax exemption and liberty, and calls the resulting treaty a “capitulation.” Mann then runs a survey of comparable cases: Yanga's confederation near Veracruz, the Miskitu of Nicaragua, Great Dismal Swamp and Florida (Seminole and Black Seminole) in the United States, Haiti, and Suriname. The Haiti and Suriname sections bring in disease ecology as decisive: yellow fever and malaria destroy British, French and Dutch forces. Suriname also becomes a case study of a colony without productive institutions and of present-day dispossession of the maroons.

## Themes

**Maroons as agents who bargained from strength.** Mann says he lists examples “if only because slaves’ prospects for autonomy are all too often portrayed as completely dependent on the goodwill of their masters.” His claim is that governments sometimes gave in to raiders who menaced the silver road (Panama, Mexico), and that the resulting settlements were real, if uneasy, freedom. The Crown's word “capitulation” inverts who actually surrendered. Mann hedges the Drake conversion story (“No evidence exists that they actually changed their previous religious practice”).

**Anonymity as the price and prize of freedom.** Mann notes that maroon groups who won freedom also won “the later anonymity that was its concomitant”: Panamanian communities' origins were nearly forgotten by 1819, and Yanga's town is now almost completely “Mexican.” Winning freedom meant becoming ordinary citizens.

**Hybrid, allied and unequal societies.** The Miskitu (African-Indian hybrid people allied with English buccaneers and claiming to be “pure” Indian), the Seminole and Black Seminole (a “strong but carefully delineated alliance” with a form of native bondage Mann says resembled feudalism more than European slavery) show that Indian-African cooperation was not automatic; the Creek hunted maroons. Mann relies on historians (Franklin and Schweninger, Tardieu) for some of these accounts.

**Disease ecology as military and political force.** In Florida the Seminole waited for yellow fever and malaria to kill northern soldiers. In Haiti, following J. R. McNeill, British and then French armies were shredded by the fevers, and Mann concludes that the same diseases that promoted African slavery helped Africans destroy it. The Louisiana Purchase is thereby owed “indirectly to maroons.” This is a clear instance of Mann's ecological-imperialism thread, in which microbes and mosquitoes (Anopheles darlingi, Aedes aegypti) are historical actors nobody controlled, a rhyme with the [[history-machine]] idea of outcomes assembled by distributed, non-designed components, though Mann does not frame it that way.

**Extractive states and absentee institutions.** Haiti is called “a true extractive state.” In Suriname absentee planters living in the Netherlands had “little interest in creating the institutions that underlie a productive society,” leaving a country among the poorest at independence in 1975. Mann's argument is causal-institutional, in the vein of the scholars he cites elsewhere. Haiti's later collapse is attributed to a decades-long European and US embargo on the world's first maroon nation.

**The present-day sequel.** Suriname's post-independence governments leased about 40 percent of the country for logging, built an Alcoa-related dam that flooded maroon villages, and created a nature reserve, all without consulting the Saramaka, Kwinti and others. The Inter-American Court ordered that the Saramaka be given control over their resources, and the nation had not complied at the time of writing. Mann treats the tropical forest as the stake. Loosely, this is the [[divergence-machine]] logic of autonomous local polities persisting against centralizing pressures, though secular and empirical here where the framework is theoretical; Mann is not a machine theorist.

## Notable passages

**The capitulation that was a victory (c1).** Mann dwells on Felipe II's word “capitulation” and explains why maroons could not go home to Africa yet gained tax-free liberty in their own communities.

**Yanga (c2).** A raid destroying a shipment of European fashions finally provokes the colonial expedition; the maroons evacuate, present eleven demands, and get their own “sunset town.”

**Seminole strategy (c3).** Plantation raiding plus waiting for the sickly season; Jesup's offer of liberty in the West eventually splits the alliance.

**Haiti and the Louisiana Purchase (c4).** Toussaint's retreat-and-wait strategy against 65,000 French troops, and Napoleon's resulting sale of Louisiana.

**Suriname's absentee colony (c5, c6).** The accommodation that let slaves escape each year, the institutional poverty it left, and the modern dispute over concessions and the Saramaka case.
