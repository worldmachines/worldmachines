# Tell Me Why

Dennett opens the book with the sentimental childhood hymn "Tell Me Why," whose answer to every mystery — God made it so — he treats as the innocent, pre-Darwinian default his whole book will interrogate. He states his thesis bluntly: Darwin's idea is not just a great scientific theory but a dangerous one, cutting deeper into our fundamental beliefs than either its opponents or its sophisticated defenders usually admit. The chapter surveys why Darwinism provokes fiercer, more anxious reactions than the Copernican revolution did, catalogues the historical gap in Darwin's own account (no unit of heredity until Mendel's forgotten 1865 paper was rediscovered around 1900, no settled Modern Synthesis until the 1940s), and previews the book's three-part structure. It then reaches back before Darwin to reconstruct the "Mind-first" worldview he overturned, walking through Aristotle's four causes and the problem of teleological regress, John Locke's deductive "proof" that a cogitative Mind must be eternal, and David Hume's Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, in which the skeptic Philo dismantles the Argument from Design — and even sketches trial-and-error alternatives eerily close to natural selection — yet cannot bring himself to accept any alternative to a designing Mind.

## Themes

**Darwin's idea is dangerous because it cannot be quarantined.** Dennett rejects the comforting hope that the "safe," empirically bulletproof parts of Darwinism could be walled off from its unsettling implications for religion and meaning while the controversial parts stay merely speculative. He inverts this: the controversies internal to evolutionary biology are "just science" and do not threaten the core idea, while it is precisely the secure core — evolution as a mindless, purposeless, mechanical process — that has the radical implications for meaning, purpose, and the sacred. This framing sets up the book's whole argumentative strategy: face the dangerous idea unflinchingly rather than defang it.

**Aristotle's four causes and the regress of "why."** Dennett revives Aristotle's four *aitia* (material, formal, efficient, final) to isolate the *telos* or final cause as the ancient home of "why" questions. Teleological explanation works cleanly for artifacts (a hammer's purpose is obvious) but collapses into infinite regress once pushed far enough — dramatized in the sawing-a-board dialogue that terminates only in a parent's exasperated "stop asking such silly questions." Aristotle's own answer was God as Prime Mover, the "for-which to end all for-whiches," a move later inherited wholesale by the Abrahamic traditions and by cosmogony-building generally (creation myths, world-eggs, and — Dennett notes dryly — modern Big Bang cosmology, which quietly sidesteps the same "why" question).

**Locke's mind-first "proof" and its collapse into assertion.** Locke's argument in the *Essay* — that "bare incogitative Matter" could no more produce Thought than Matter could arise from nothing — is presented as the high-water mark of pre-Darwinian confidence that Mind must be metaphysically primary, prior to and independent of any material process that could generate it. Dennett stages it as a "proof" whose persuasive force rested entirely on nobody yet having a plausible alternative story of how mindless processes could produce a mind — exactly the gap Darwin would later fill.

**Hume's Dialogues stage the argument from design and its unraveling, without ever finding the replacement.** Cleanthes' opening statement of the Argument from Design (the universe as "one great machine, subdivided into an infinite number of lesser machines") is answered point by point by Philo, who shows the argument's analogical inference from human artifacts to the cosmos is arbitrary, that positing Mind as the first cause only postpones the regress, and who — most strikingly — sketches materialist scenarios of blind, iterative trial-and-error refinement (matter "thrown into any position" and slowly settling into stable, self-perpetuating "economy") that anticipate natural selection by a century. Yet Hume has Philo cave at the Dialogues' close and endorse a designing Mind anyway: not from fear of persecution (he had the work published posthumously precisely to avoid that), but because, in Dennett's reading, he simply could not yet *imagine* a rigorous alternative account of order without a designer. That missing account is exactly what chapter 2 promises to supply.

**A resonance worth naming, carefully.** The chapter's central move — replacing a teleological, Mind-first explanation of order with a non-teleological, blind, algorithmic process that nonetheless generates apparent purpose and design — rhymes with WMP's [[Configurancy]], Rao's non-teleological relational process by which agents and worlds co-emerge; both refuse to smuggle a prior designer or goal into an account of how order arises, though Configurancy is pitched at social/historical co-emergence in the present tense while Darwin's mechanism operates over biological and geological time. There is a second, more oblique rhyme: the chapter's own account of how the Darwinian synthesis actually came together — Mendel's paper ignored for thirty-five years, the Modern Synthesis only cohering in the 1940s through several hands, no single "great man" moment — is a small case study in [[history-machine]] logic, a paradigm assembling silently and distributedly from independent components before lurching into visibility, here doubled by the fact that the *content* of the idea it describes (natural selection) is itself a distributed, undirected process rather than a designed one. Neither link should be pushed further than the chapter supports; Dennett's target here is teleology in nature and metaphysics, not historiography or political economy.

## Notable passages

The chapter's thesis is stated as an unhedged claim early on: Dennett insists the creationists are right that Darwin's idea "cuts much deeper into the fabric of our most fundamental beliefs" than most of its defenders admit — a deliberately provocative alignment with the opposition's sense of stakes, before he goes on to argue the idea should be embraced rather than feared. Later he states his personal stance and method directly, ranking Darwin's idea above Newton's and Einstein's and committing the book to examining it "as unflinchingly, as dispassionately, as possible" rather than settling for reassurance.

The teleology section's sawing-a-board dialogue is the chapter's clearest illustration of the regress problem built into "why" questions — each answer just relocates the question one level up, with no principled stopping point until the questioner runs out of patience.

Locke's proof is quoted for its key deductive move: that if Matter and Motion are supposed eternal, "Thought can never begin to be," since Sense, Perception, and Knowledge would then have to be inseparable properties of matter itself — which Locke treats as self-evidently absurd, and Dennett treats as the premise Darwin would make obsolete.

Hume's Philo gets the chapter's most striking anticipatory passage, imagining a designing God as merely "a stupid mechanic" whose apparent skill is really the residue of endless trial, error, and slow improvement "carried on during infinite ages of world-making" — a near-miss on natural selection that Hume himself could not bring into focus.
