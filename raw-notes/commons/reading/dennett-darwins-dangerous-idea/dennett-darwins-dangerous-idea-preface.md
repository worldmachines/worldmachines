# Preface: Why Dennett Tells a Story Instead of Making an Argument

Dennett opens by noting that a surprising range of thinkers — not only lay people and religious believers but secular philosophers, psychologists, physicists, and even biologists — cannot conceal their discomfort with Darwin's idea, and he states the book's aim: to show why natural selection is powerful and promises a new foundation for our visions of life rather than a threat to them. He then describes his working method: this is not itself a work of science but a work of persuasion built partly from other scientists' own rhetoric, organized for readers through an extensive index rather than a glossary. Most distinctively, he explains why he forgoes the rigorous philosophical argument his discipline favors in favor of storytelling — watertight arguments, he has learned, are simply ignored, so his goal becomes changing what is ignorable by whom. The preface closes with lengthy acknowledgments to colleagues, students, and correspondents at Tufts and elsewhere, and a dedication to his wife, Susan.

## Themes

**The resistance to Darwin's idea is broad and cross-disciplinary, not merely religious.** Dennett is careful to specify that the discomfort he has observed comes "not just lay people and religious thinkers, but secular philosophers, psychologists, physicists, and even biologists" — framing the book's antagonist as an intellectual reflex found across fields rather than a single ideology, and setting up the book's central promise that natural selection is generative rather than corrosive of meaning.

**Persuasion, not proof, is the book's explicit method.** Dennett flags upfront that this is "not itself a work of science," and that when he quotes scientists' rhetoric he is "doing what they are doing: engaging in persuasion." He denies that there is any "sound Argument from Authority" while still granting that authorities "can be persuasive, sometimes rightly and sometimes wrongly" — an unusually candid admission, for a philosopher, that the book's tool is rhetoric rather than proof.

**Formal argument is conceded to be rhetorically weak, so storytelling replaces it.** The preface's most striking claim is that Dennett — self-described as "the author of arguments that I consider rigorous and unanswerable" — has learned that watertight arguments are simply ignored rather than rebutted. His stated goal shifts from winning an argument to "changing what is ignorable by whom," and he chooses to "tell a story" because readers "won't even listen to a formal argument" for his conclusion. This is a meta-methodological move: the book will win converts through narrative and reframing rather than syllogism.

**Method as navigation between extremes.** Dennett describes threading between "the Scylla of glib dismissal and the Charybdis of grindingly detailed infighting," flagging every place he glides past a controversy and pointing the reader to opposing references rather than either ignoring or exhaustively litigating it. This is presented as an ethic of intellectual honesty compatible with his rhetorical aims.

**A loose resonance worth naming:** Dennett's admission that rigorous arguments are simply *ignored* until something changes "what is ignorable by whom" faintly echoes WMP's [[history-machine]] idea that paradigms shift not by winning debates but by infrastructure quietly reorganizing what is visible or sayable. The fit is partial — Dennett's register is one rhetorician deliberately crafting narrative to persuade individual readers, a much smaller and more authored process than the silent, distributed assembly WMP's frame describes at civilizational scale — but the shared insight that direct argument is not what moves settled belief is worth flagging.

## Notable passages

- The opening thesis (c1) states the book's promise in miniature: Darwin's idea "promises — not threatens — to put our most cherished visions of life on a new foundation," setting up the entire volume's argument against those who see evolution as corrosive of meaning.
- The persuasion-vs-authority passage (c2) is Dennett's clearest statement that this is not a work of science but of rhetoric built from other scientists' own rhetoric — an unusual candor about method for a philosophy book.
- The story-vs-argument passage (c3) is the preface's centerpiece: a philosopher admitting that his own "rigorous and unanswerable" arguments are routinely ignored, and choosing narrative instead because it is what will actually move readers.
- The Scylla-and-Charybdis passage (c4) names the balancing act between dismissing controversies and getting lost in them, and commits to flagging every shortcut taken.
