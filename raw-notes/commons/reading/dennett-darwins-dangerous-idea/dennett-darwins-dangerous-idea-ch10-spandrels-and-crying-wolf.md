# Crying Wolf: The Myth of Gould's Revolutions and the Spandrels of San Marco

Chapter 10 opens Dennett's direct reckoning with Stephen Jay Gould, arguing across two sections that Gould's decades-long public campaign against "orthodox" neo-Darwinism has produced an influential myth — Gould as revolutionary refuter of Darwinism — that does not survive close reading of what Gould actually showed. Section 1 catalogs the pattern: repeated declarations that neo-Darwinism is dead or dying (over adaptationism, punctuated equilibrium, mass extinction) that never in fact overturn the modern synthesis, and proposes that Gould's real, underlying target is Darwin's "dangerous idea" that evolution is at bottom an algorithmic, mindless process. Section 2 makes the case in detail against the most famous of these attempted revolutions, the 1979 Gould-and-Lewontin "Spandrels of San Marco" paper, arguing that its own architectural example is historically wrong — San Marco's spandrels were chosen, not architecturally forced — and that the resulting concepts of "spandrel," "exaptation," and "preadaptation" describe positions no orthodox Darwinian ever held in the first place.

## Themes

**A myth more potent than its subject.** Dennett opens with British schoolchildren's charming, wildly inaccurate image of Queen Elizabeth II vacuum-cleaning Buckingham Palace in her crown — an "intentional object" that governs behavior more directly than the real woman ever could. He uses this to introduce his real subject: the mythical "Stephen Jay Gould, Refuter of Orthodox Darwinism," who Dennett says has become more behaviorally potent, among non-biologists who cite Gould against him, than the real (far more orthodox) Gould.

**Crying wolf: a documented pattern of false revolutions.** Dennett lists specific claims widely believed on Gould's authority — that adaptationism has been refuted, that punctuated equilibrium overthrew neo-Darwinism, that mass extinction refutes "extrapolationism" — and asserts flatly that what you believe is a falsehood if you hold any of them. He likens Gould to Manfred Eigen and Stuart Kauffman, fellow self-styled heretics who (unlike Gould) eventually moderated their revolutionary rhetoric, and invokes Aesop directly: Gould keeps defying the moral of the fable about the boy who cried wolf.

**The diagnosis: discomfort with the algorithmic idea itself.** Dennett's key interpretive claim is that Gould's serial failed revolutions are not random noise but successive partial outlines of one underlying target. Rather than adjudicating each Gould claim as an isolated dispute, Dennett reads the pattern itself as evidence that Gould has never made peace with the corrosive, "no-skyhooks" implication of Darwinism — that mind, purpose, and design are themselves products of a blind algorithmic process.

**The architectural error at the root of "pervasive adaptation."** Turning to Gould and Lewontin's celebrated paper, Dennett re-examines its opening example line by line and finds a "crucial misstep." The San Marco spandrels (strictly, pendentives) were not the sole geometrically necessary way to roof a dome on rounded arches — squinches were a well-known Byzantine alternative for centuries before San Marco was built — and the actual pendentive shape was chosen because it approximates a minimal-energy, minimal-tile surface ideal for mounting mosaics. Dennett's conclusion is that the spandrels of San Marco are adaptations, not spandrels, even in Gould's own extended sense.

**Untangling "spandrel," "exaptation," and "preadaptation."** Dennett works through the strict/loose ambiguity in "spandrel" (pendentive vs. any design-problem-to-be-filled), proposes the engineer's "don't-care" (a feature fixed by historical accident, like which side a door's hinges go on, then locally entrenched) as the best steelmanned reading, and then extends the same move to exaptation: since every adaptation is one sort of exaptation or another already under orthodox Darwinism, Gould's targets — panadaptationism, preadaptationism — were never live orthodox positions, so neither "revolution" challenges any orthodox Darwinian tenet.

**A resonance with World Machines historiography.** Dennett's larger point — that the "modern synthesis" absorbs each of Gould's challenges and stays essentially unchanged, never hardening into the brittle orthodoxy Gould hoped to topple — rhymes with the WMP idea of a [[history-machine]]: a paradigm that assembles from many small, distributed components rather than through "great men" and their revolutions. The fit is partial, though: Lohse's history machine still eventually "lurches into visibility," while Dennett's modern synthesis never lurches at all — it just keeps flexibly absorbing punches, arguably a stronger, more capture-resistant case of the same distributed-assembly picture.

## Notable passages

The chapter's opening move (c1) reframes the entire dispute as being about a myth, not a scientific fact — "Stephen Jay Gould, Refuter of Orthodox Darwinism" is the intentional object Dennett must dismantle before he can defend his own use of Darwinian reasoning. The "crying wolf" passage (c2) names the pattern explicitly, pairing Gould with Eigen and Kauffman and citing Robert Wright's warning about a lot of intellectual damage if Gould has misled the public. The thesis statement (c3) is the chapter's hinge: Dennett commits to reading Gould's failed revolutions as outlining the source of Gould's driving discomfort with Darwin's dangerous idea. The Gould-Lewontin definition of "spandrel" (c4), quoted directly from their 1979 paper, is the passage Dennett spends the rest of the section dismantling — note the OCR-preserved slip "elab orate" is the source text's own artifact, not Dennett's. The payoff (c5) delivers his verdict in Dennett's own voice: the spandrels are adaptations, chosen from a set of equipossible alternatives for largely aesthetic reasons. Finally, the left-hinged-door example (c6) is Dennett's own candidate for what a genuine spandrel would look like — a "don't-care" fixed by historical accident — which he uses to show how thin a wedge is left for Gould's alternative to adaptationism.
