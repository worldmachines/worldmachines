# Memes as a Second Replicator: From Ape-Ancestry Anxiety to the Infosphere

Opening with Nietzsche's epigraph on humanity's "conclusive transitoriness," Chapter 12 ("The Cranes of Culture") first catalogues over a century of resistance to placing Homo sapiens fully inside Darwinian theory — the Wilberforce-Huxley debate, the Diamond/Marks fight over "third chimpanzee" DNA evidence — before arguing that what truly sets humans apart, culture, itself needs a Darwinian rather than miraculous explanation. The unit then formally introduces Dawkins' meme as a second replicator running its own selection process, exapting the eukaryotic-endosymbiosis story from earlier in the book as its template, and closes with a tour of memetic phenomena — linked loci, misfiltering, filters as niches, arms races — that make up what Dennett calls the infosphere.

## Themes

**Ancestry anxiety is itself a self-perpetuating meme-cluster.** Dennett traces the "ape or angel" panic across a century-plus — Disraeli's Oxford quip, the Wilberforce/Huxley confrontation, the 1990s Diamond/Marks fight over Sibley-and-Ahlquist DNA hybridization data — and explicitly names the pattern "a phylum of memes, not just a species," using the very concept the chapter is about to formalize to characterize the resistance to Darwinism itself.

**Culture is a second, exogenetic heredity system, and it needs a crane, not a skyhook.** Cultural change (the height of Old Ironsides-era sailors, the gulf between us and Plato's contemporaries) runs far too fast to be genetic, yet Dennett insists it cannot be a miracle either: language, cooperation, and tradition must each be "evolutionarily enforceable," built up from nothing the way the first replicators were.

**The eukaryote story is the template for the birth of "a person."** Dennett retells Margulis' account of prokaryotes invaded by symbiotic parasites to form the first eukaryotic cell, then maps it onto a second invasion: apes "infested with" memes become witting hosts — what we call persons. The parallel is explicit and load-bearing, not decorative.

**A meme is defined structurally, not by content.** Following Dawkins, the three conditions for any evolutionary process are variation, heredity/replication, and differential fitness — a definition that "says nothing specific about organic molecules... or even life." A meme, like a gene, is whatever unit is small enough to be identifiable yet large enough to "replicate with reliability and fecundity" (a single codon is too small to be a gene; a three-note phrase too small to be a meme).

**Whether cultural evolution is genuinely Darwinian remains an open question.** Dennett stakes out the space between two extremes — full structural isomorphism with genetics, and Gould's rejected extreme where cultural evolution "operates according to entirely different principles" — and lands in between: real conceptual transfer, of uncertain scientific rigor.

**The meme's-eye view decenters the author.** "A scholar is just a library's way of making another library" — Mozart, novelists, and painters all report ideas arriving unbidden; Dennett uses this to ask "who's in charge... we or our memes?", undercutting the picture of the mind as sole author and critic of its own thoughts.

**Memes compete for scarce mind-space via phenotypic effects, not truth-value.** Faith, conspiracy theories, chain-letter warnings, and Sir Arthur Sullivan's unusably-titled march ("linked loci") all persist or fail because of how they affect the selective environment around them; filters, critics, and editors are themselves memes competing for niches (McCarthy's poetry-network thought experiment), and positive feedback (bestseller lists) can amplify a meme independent of merit.

## Notable passages

The Wilberforce/Huxley set-piece [c1] shows Dennett using memetic language before he has even defined the term, priming the reader for the formal apparatus to come. The Margulis-to-meme passage [c2] is the unit's structural hinge — where "person" gets a Darwinian, non-miraculous origin story. The three-conditions passage [c3] and the Dawkins etymology/definition passage [c4] are the load-bearing formal content the rest of the chapter builds on. The "library's way of making another library" passage [c5] is the unit's most quotable statement of memetics' unsettling implication for authorship and agency — and the passage that resonates most strongly with WMP's own historiography. The meme-ecology passage [c6] sets up the "infosphere" vocabulary (filters, vehicles, competition for minds) that the rest of the chapter elaborates.

Dennett's decentered picture of cultural change — ideas propagating and recombining through selective pressure with no single controlling author ("a scholar is just a library's way of making another library"; "who's in charge... we or our memes?") — strongly rhymes with [[history-machine]], the WMP concept of a paradigm that assembles silently and distributedly from independent components until it lurches into visibility, explicitly opposed to "great men" historiography. The fit isn't exact: Dennett's unit is the individual idea/meme, operating at the scale of a single mind, while a history machine operates at civilizational scale across institutions — but the underlying move, explaining apparent design as the residue of a selection process with no architect, is the same one.

The chapter's picture of an "infosphere" flooded with promiscuously replicating memes, met by an ecology of filters and critics competing to sort signal from noise, faintly echoes the WMP tension between the [[divergence-machine]]'s proliferation beyond legibility's reach and the [[modernity-machine]]'s drive to make the world orderable — though the fit is loose, since Dennett's filters are themselves memes competing for survival, not an institution imposing order from outside the system.
