# The Emperor's New Mind, and Other Fables

Chapter 15 of *Darwin's Dangerous Idea* defends the possibility of Artificial Intelligence against the claim, pressed most famously by mathematician-physicist Roger Penrose (echoing Godel himself, and earlier attempts by Descartes, J. R. Lucas, and others), that Godel's Incompleteness Theorem proves the human mind cannot be a mere algorithm. Dennett works through the search for a decisive "sword-in-the-stone" test of non-mechanical mindedness, shows where Penrose's argument goes wrong by generalizing a chess-playing parallel, and ends at Penrose's fallback position — an unformulated, noncomputable theory of "quantum gravity" — which Dennett diagnoses as a hunt for a skyhook that has so far turned up nothing but a hunch.

## Themes

**The "sword-in-the-stone" problem: is there a feat only minds can do?** Dennett frames the whole Godel-based tradition — Godel's own conviction, Lucas's 1961 attempt, Penrose's revival — as a search for an empirical test with no plausible lookalikes, the way pulling the sword from the stone unambiguously proves Arthurian legitimacy. He runs through candidate feats: Descartes's two tests for a genuine mind, the Turing Test (imitation game), and chess (via Vaucanson's duck and the von Kempelen/Maelzel chess automaton that Edgar Allan Poe unmasked as a hoax). Each candidate collapses as a decisive test — conversation is too open-ended to adjudicate cleanly, and chess turned out to be "demonstrably within the power of a machine after all."

**Godel's Theorem constrains provers, not all algorithms.** The technical core: no consistent axiomatic system for arithmetic can prove every arithmetical truth; each spawns its own unprovable "Godel sentence." Using the "Library of Toshiba" — the Vast but finite space of all megabyte-length bit strings runnable on his old laptop — Dennett stresses that Godel's result binds only the Vanishingly small subset of algorithms that are consistent arithmetic-truth-provers. It says nothing about algorithms for chess, conversation, or for "producing mathematical insight" imperfectly but impressively, which is the only kind of algorithm AI research has ever needed.

**Penrose's mistake: "algorithm for X" versus "algorithm that yields X."** Dennett's central diagnostic move is a parallel chess argument: there is no *practical* algorithm guaranteed to force checkmate, yet his laptop's chess program still reliably beats him using a merely good algorithm not built to guarantee a win. Penrose's move — there is no algorithm *for* mathematical understanding, therefore understanding cannot be algorithmic at all — commits exactly this fallacy. Penrose takes what Dennett calls the "God's-eye view" of algorithms (provable properties of a whole class) and mistakes it for the separate, empirical question of what some particular, evolved, largely "invisible" heuristic algorithm might actually accomplish.

**Distributed cognition as a crane, not a skyhook.** Against Penrose's fallback that individual rigor rules out unreliable heuristics, Dennett points to the mathematical community itself as a "mind-tool": proofs are presented, checked by others, corrected in public, giving the community as a whole far more truth-detecting power than any single brain — without anything non-algorithmic being invoked. This resonates with the World Machines idea of a [[history-machine]]: a capability assembling silently and distributedly out of many partial, fallible components rather than residing in one "great man" genius — though Dennett's frame is cognitive-institutional (a community of mathematicians) rather than civilizational-paradigmatic, so the rhyme is partial, not identical.

**Natural selection as an algorithmic, not miraculous, designer.** Penrose's deeper objection is that natural selection could not have designed algorithms capable of "conscious judgement," since mutation would normally just break a Turing machine, and since the "ideas" behind algorithms seem to require a conscious mind to conceive them. Dennett answers in the book's recurring skyhook/crane vocabulary: natural selection is "the blind watchmaker, the unconscious watchmaker," an existence proof that good designs — reasons without a reasoner — can be discovered and refined without anyone recognizing them as reasons.

**Quantum gravity as a hunted-for skyhook.** Recounting a week spent with Penrose and physicists at a workshop in Abisko, Sweden, Dennett reports his verdict pointedly: Penrose "has definitely been looking for a skyhook... but I doubt that he's found one." Penrose's only stated reason for positing a noncomputable theory of quantum gravity (potentially seated in neuronal microtubules, per Stuart Hameroff) is that its absence would leave AI possible after all — a circular justification, not evidence from physics itself. The chapter closes by turning Darwin's own rhetorical challenge back on the skeptics: if an algorithmic process can design a nightingale, it is mere hubris to assume an algorithmic process could not write an ode to one.

## Notable passages

- The sword-in-the-stone framing, which sets the chapter's standard for what would count as decisive evidence of a non-mechanical mind.
- The Library of Toshiba passage, extending the book's recurring "Library of Babel"/"Library of Mendel" device to the space of all runnable programs, to pin down exactly what Godel's Theorem does and does not cover.
- The chess-algorithm syllogism and its rebuttal, Dennett's clearest statement of Penrose's fallacy: no algorithm *for* X does not mean X isn't produced by *some* algorithm.
- The mathematical-community-as-mind-tool passage, extending "crane" thinking from individual brains to distributed social/cultural mechanisms of truth-detection.
- The Abisko verdict on Penrose's quantum-gravity hopes, Dennett's bluntest skyhook-versus-crane judgment in the chapter.
- The closing turn of the argumentative burden, echoing Darwin against those who doubt an algorithmic mind could produce art as it produced a nightingale.
