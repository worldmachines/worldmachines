# Threads of Actuality in Design Space

Extending the Library of Babel/Library of Mendel picture from earlier chapters, Dennett distinguishes mere sideways 'drifting' through the space of possible genomes from selection-driven 'lifting' — the accumulation of actual design — and then asks a harder question: of the design features any organism (or artifact) has, which were strictly forced by the laws of physics or logic, and which are historical accidents that merely became 'virtual necessities' once a lineage committed to them? Working the question through extraterrestrial-life thought experiments (autonomous metabolism, vision, arithmetic vs. numerals), Dennett arrives at the chapter's Central Salvo: there is only one Design Space, and everything actual in it — bacteria, eyes, Newton's Principia, Shakespeare's plays, this book's own bibliography — is united within it. The evidence for that unity is the same evidence textual critics use to reconstruct manuscript genealogies: idiosyncratic imperfections that are Vastly unlikely to arise twice by independent invention are the signature of copying and shared history, whether the copies are genomes, Platonic dialogues, or Babylonian ephemerides. The chapter closes by naming memes as the cultural analog of genes and insisting that culture itself — language, art, religion, science — is artifact of artifact of artifact, with no skyhooks anywhere in the chain.

## Themes

**Drift vs. lifting.** Random genetic drift accumulates only 'distance' in Design Space, not design — most mutations are neutral typos, and most non-neutral ones are damaging, so unselected drift is 'inexorably downward.' Natural selection is what converts sideways wandering into upward 'lifting,' the accumulation of actual complexity and fit. Dennett frames this with his running Library of Babel/Library of Mendel analogy: a genome is like a book, and design work is the historical, contingent process of picking a vanishingly rare readable (functional) volume out of a combinatorially Vast space of noise.

**Forced moves and the QWERTY phenomenon.** Some design features are 'forced moves' — solutions so obviously the only viable option (autonomous metabolism, having a boundary) that any lineage anywhere would be driven to them, the way a chess player in a lost position is forced into the one saving move. But Dennett insists reasons for necessity come in two grades: deep reasons (laws of physics, logic, thermodynamics) and shallow reasons, which are merely historical — an ancient accident closes off alternatives until only one path remains 'available,' a 'virtual necessity' rather than a true one. This is the QWERTY phenomenon: a once-contingent choice (five fingers per hand, a keyboard layout, decimal counting) hardens into a necessity for all practical purposes even though nothing in physics required it.

**Extraterrestrial thought experiments as a test of necessity.** Dennett uses hypothetical alien life to separate the deep from the shallow: we would not be surprised if aliens had autonomous metabolisms, locomotion, eyes, or even the same arithmetic (because arithmetic is 'right' — Minsky's Sparseness Principle holds that convergent simple processes tend to be identical, not merely similar). But we would be floored if they used our arabic numerals, because numerals (unlike numbers) are arbitrary symbols with a Vast space of equally good alternatives — a similarity there could only mean shared history, not shared necessity.

**Homology as the signature of history.** The chapter's central evidentiary move, echoing Stephen Jay Gould, is that imperfections and idiosyncrasies — not perfections — are the best evidence of a historical process of descent with modification, because perfect (forced-move) solutions get reinvented independently all the time, while idiosyncratic near-perfection is a 'dead giveaway of copying.' Dennett draws out a sustained parallel to nineteenth-century philology: scholars reconstructing the genealogy of Plato's manuscripts, or Otto Neugebauer deducing that a Greek papyrus fragment was a translation of a Babylonian lunar ephemeris purely from its idiosyncratic numerical pattern, are doing exactly the argument-from-homology that Darwin does with jawbones and ear-bones. Darwin himself noticed and cited this same homology/analogy distinction at work in the genealogy of languages.

**Discovery vs. creation; cranes, not skyhooks.** Following Nicholas Humphrey, Dennett contrasts forced-move 'discoveries' (Newton's Principia — replaceable, since someone else would have found the same forced destination) with idiosyncratic 'creations' (Shakespeare's plays — irreplaceable, because the value lies in the particular trajectory, not just the destination). Good design methods are reusable 'cranes': borrowable, acknowledgeable pieces of prior design work, so that plagiarism is, in this framework, 'theft of crane.' The same logic — that Intelligence itself gets 'broken into bits so tiny and stupid that they didn't count as intelligence at all, and then distributed through space and time in a gigantic, connected network of algorithmic process' — is what lets natural selection do apparently intelligent design work with no author and no skyhook.

**The Unity of Design Space and the introduction of memes.** The chapter's payoff is explicitly programmatic: biological and cultural design are not two domains linked by analogy but literally one Design Space, because human artifacts (books, engineering, mathematics) are themselves 'extended phenotype,' non-miraculously generated by the same cranes-on-cranes process that produced prokaryotes. Dawkins's memes are introduced here (fuller treatment deferred to chapter 12) as the cultural unit of design-transmission parallel to genes — setting up the book's later argument that language, art, religion, and science have no more need of Special Creation or skyhooks than the eye does.

## Notable passages

The drift/lifting passage (c1) is the chapter's mechanical foundation: it establishes that selection, not mere chance, is what makes 'progress' in Design Space non-vacuous — random change alone is 'idle . . . R-and-D work,' analogous to random typographical edits to *Moby Dick*, almost always neutral or harmful, never improving the text.

The forced-move definition (c2) and the deep-vs-shallow-reasons passage (c3) together give Dennett's key analytic tool for the rest of the book: sorting any observed design feature into 'had to be this way' (physics/logic) versus 'happened to become the only option' (QWERTY-style historical lock-in) — a distinction he will keep reusing when he turns to human culture in Part III.

This mechanism resonates with WMP's [[history-machine]]: Lohse's model has a paradigm assemble silently and distributedly from independent components until it lurches into visibility, against 'great man' historiography — precisely Dennett's claim that Intelligence is 'broken into bits so tiny and stupid they didn't count as intelligence at all' and distributed across a network of algorithmic process (c6), with no single genius or skyhook required. It also half-resonates with [[Configurancy]]: the QWERTY/forced-move interplay (c3) is a non-teleological process by which chance and constraint jointly determine which path a lineage's design takes — though Configurancy for Rao names an agent's relational co-emergence with its world, while Dennett's forced moves are blind search-space geometry with no agent presupposed; the rhyme is in the shared refusal of teleology, not in any implied agency.

The Central Salvo (c4) — 'there is only one Design Space, and everything actual in it is united with everything else' — is the chapter's thesis statement in miniature, and the homology passage (c5) is its evidentiary backbone: idiosyncratic imperfection, not perfection, is what proves shared history, whether in genomes, Plato manuscripts, or a German fragment of *Julius Caesar*.
