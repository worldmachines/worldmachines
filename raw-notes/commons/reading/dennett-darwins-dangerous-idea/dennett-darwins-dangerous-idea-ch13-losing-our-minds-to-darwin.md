# Losing Our Minds to Darwin

Chapter 13 argues that language is the pivotal adaptation separating human minds from all other animal minds, builds a four-tier "Tower of Generate-and-Test" model of increasingly sophisticated cognitive control, and then turns polemical: it traces how Noam Chomsky, Stephen Jay Gould, and John Searle — each a serious, non-creationist thinker — have nonetheless resisted extending Darwinian, engineering-style explanation to the human language faculty and mind, making them, in Dennett's framing, unwitting champions of "skyhooks."

## Themes

**The Tower of Generate-and-Test.** Dennett proposes a four-floor model of cognitive design: **Darwinian creatures** (hard-wired phenotypes selected by blind variation and death, no learning within a lifetime), **Skinnerian creatures** (phenotypic plasticity plus operant conditioning — trial, error, and death of a hypothesis rather than the organism), **Popperian creatures** (an inner environment that pre-selects among possible actions before risking them, so "hypotheses die in our stead"), and **Gregorian creatures** (importing externally designed "mind-tools" — above all words — from the surrounding culture, which restructure the inner environment itself). Each floor is cumulative and inherits the constraints of the ones below it: a species that solved a control problem via "need-to-know" specialist modules can only patch, not redesign, its lower floors. Two design philosophies recur throughout: the need-to-know principle (special-purpose, minimal-information subroutines, good in stable/predictable environments — termite mounds, weaverbird nests, beaver dams) versus the commando-team principle (broadly informed, improvising agents, needed as environmental complexity rises).

**Language as the crane that ends cognitive closure.** Dennett argues human minds are not merely quantitatively but qualitatively unlike other animal minds because language lets individual brains draw on the pooled cognitive labors of every other brain in the culture — not one big collective mind, but each brain individually enriched by communicative links to the others. This is used to rebut the "cognitive closure" argument (Chomsky, Fodor, McGinn) that human minds, like all animal minds, must have Darwinian limits on what they can conceive (the example: a monkey can't grasp the concept of an electron). Dennett's counter is that we understand the *questions* about free will and consciousness even where we lack the answers, which is not what cognitive closure looks like; the argument, despite its "biological" framing, actually ignores the biological fact that culturally transmitted design keeps expanding what a human brain can do.

**Chomsky's anti-Darwinism (the "four episodes").** Despite founding modern linguistics (traced to a single day, September 11, 1956, at MIT, alongside the births of AI and cognitive psychology) and delivering the definitive attack on Skinnerian behaviorism, Chomsky has been consistently hostile to evolutionary and engineering (AI) accounts of the "language organ." Dennett narrates this through a personal history: a 1978 Tufts debate where Chomsky and Fodor attacked AI researcher Roger Schank (with Marvin Minsky's aside that only a humanities professor could miss that psychology might turn out to be "like engineering"); Dennett's 1980 BBS commentary on Chomsky's "Rules and Representations," where Chomsky missed that "passing the buck to biology" was meant as a friendly, if disheartening, methodological point; and Chomsky's repeated insistence that the language organ's structure will be explained by unknown laws of physics, not natural selection — an anti-adaptationist ("anti-Spencerian," per Peter Godfrey-Smith's terminology) position endorsed by Stephen Jay Gould's "spandrel" reading of language as an accidental byproduct of brain enlargement. Dennett grants Gould's premise (the brain's growth wasn't initially "for" language) but rejects the conclusion: an exaptation is still an adaptation, so the language organ is as much a product of natural selection as a bird's wing. Steven Pinker and Paul Bloom's 1990 "Natural Language and Natural Selection" is cited as the definitive rebuttal, provoking a hostile, "shocked" reception from Chomsky- and Gould-aligned cognitive scientists that Dennett says convinced him to finish this book.

**Spencerian adaptationism and "nice tries."** Borrowing Godfrey-Smith's term, Dennett labels any view holding that organismic complexity is explained by environmental complexity "Spencerian" (after Herbert Spencer) — adaptationism, behaviorism, and empiricism are all Spencerian; Chomsky's appeal to intrinsic, ahistorical "physics" is explicitly anti-Spencerian. Dennett then reframes the history of cognitive science (Skinner's behaviorism, GOFAI, Connectionism) as a sequence of "nice tries" — deliberately oversimplified models refined rather than refuted by their failures — distinguishing ordinary reductionism (explains without skyhooks) from "greedy" reductionism (tries to explain without cranes, i.e., without enough intervening design-work).

**Searle's Original Intentionality as the purest skyhook.** John Searle is presented as Chomsky's mirror-image ally: where Chomsky won't let the language organ be reverse-engineered, Searle denies that any computational/functional description of the brain is even coherent, and goes further still, denying that biological "function" talk (a heart's function, a wing's function) is a fact about nature at all rather than a human projection — reserving real function, and real ("Original") intentionality, for conscious human artificers alone. Dennett reads this as the clearest instance in the chapter of the belief in skyhooks: minds treated as a source of design that cannot itself be a result of design.

## Notable passages

The chapter's spine is the Tower of Generate-and-Test: the ground-floor "Darwinian creatures," whose behavior is entirely hard-wired by selection (chunk c1), through to the passage that names all four tiers together and ends on the recursive "tower of further internal reflections" that Gregorian, word-equipped creatures make possible (chunk c2) — language is what lets a creature ask what it should think about what it should think about next, without limit.

The cognitive-closure rebuttal (chunk c3) is where the chapter's Darwinian argument does its heaviest lifting against Chomsky/Fodor/McGinn: rather than a wall we will hit, our limits recede under cultural and evolutionary pressure — closer to running out of time before the universe's Heat Death than to a structural ceiling.

Two passages anchor the Chomsky-vs-Darwin case directly in Chomsky's own words and Dennett's rebuttal: Chomsky's claim that the language organ's "discrete infinity" may trace to unknown laws of physics rather than to selection history (chunk c4), and Dennett's answer to Gould's spandrel reading — an exaptation is still an adaptation, so the language organ is "as much an adaptation as the bird's wing" regardless of how suddenly it arrived (chunk c5).

Finally, Searle's "Original Intentionality" passage (chunk c6) gives the chapter's clearest one-line definition of a skyhook — minds as "original and inexplicable sources of design, not results of design" — the belief Dennett spends the whole book arguing against.

## World Machines resonances

The Chomsky/Gould/Searle thread — insisting that language and mind arrived as an inexplicable gift rather than assembling gradually from humbler prior components — rhymes with WMP's [[history-machine]] contrast between silent, distributed assembly (a paradigm that "lurches into visibility" from independent components) and "great men"/miracle historiography that explains a shift by crediting a singular, ungrounded arrival. Dennett's target is a cognitive faculty rather than a civilizational era, and his "crane" is a strictly mechanistic, selectionist one where WMP's history-machine leaves more room for genuinely emergent complexity — but the shared move is refusing an explanation that stops at "and then it just appeared."

The Gregorian-creature picture — a brain that imports externally designed "mind-tools" (words, tools, artifacts) from its culture, which then restructure the brain's own inner environment rather than the other way around — is a loose but genuine echo of [[Configurancy]]'s non-teleological co-emergence of agents and worlds, though Dennett's frame is individual cognitive development across a lifetime, not the civilizational-scale relational process Rao's term describes.
