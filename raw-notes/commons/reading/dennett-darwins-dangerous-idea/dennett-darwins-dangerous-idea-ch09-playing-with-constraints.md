# Playing with Constraints: Game Theory, Cooperation, and Hidden Constraints in Adaptationism

This unit is section 3 of Chapter 9 (plus the chapter's own closing summary and the opening lines of Chapter 10). Dennett first imports game theory — via von Neumann and Morgenstern's original insight that multi-agent rationality cannot be reduced to single-agent maximization — into evolutionary biology, using the Prisoner's Dilemma and Maynard Smith/Dawkins' notion of an evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS) to explain why cooperation among trees, genes, and organisms is rare and must be specially engineered rather than assumed. He then turns to Stephen Jay Gould's objection that adaptationism ignores "hidden constraints" — inherited, historically frozen limits on the space of viable designs — and answers it not by denying that such constraints exist, but by proposing a rigorous method (a thought experiment about chess played under a secret handicap) for discovering them empirically, while warning against both underestimating and overestimating the true space of evolutionary possibility. The unit closes with the book's verdict on the whole Gould/Lewontin critique and pivots into Chapter 10's framing of Gould as the latest in a line of thinkers seeking "skyhooks" to limit Darwin's dangerous idea.

## Themes

**Game theory reframes evolutionary "selfishness" as a formal consequence of multi-agent competition, not a moral indictment of nature.** Von Neumann and Morgenstern's founding realization — that a "guiding principle cannot be formulated by the requirement of maximizing two (or more) functions at once," because rational agents must model each other's rationality rather than treat each other statistically — is, Dennett argues, exactly what makes it apply to "such unconscious, unreflective semi-agents as viruses, trees, and insects": the stakes and payoffs of competition determine which strategies win or lose regardless of whether anyone understands the game.

**The Prisoner's Dilemma and the tragedy of the commons show cooperation is the exception, not the default, absent special design.** The classic PD setup — where mutual cooperation beats mutual defection but individual defection always pays more, whatever the other player does — explains why redwoods can't "get together and agree on some sensible zoning restrictions": defection from any cooperative arrangement always pays off, so trees are stuck building expensive trunks in a race for sunlight, a real-world instance of Hardin's tragedy of the commons. Cooperation, Dennett insists, "must be designed — it do[es] not come for free," citing the eukaryotic revolution's "enforceable truce" between prokaryotic cells as an example of the rare special circumstances under which it can arise.

**Genomic conflict shows the same logic operating beneath conscious agency entirely.** David Haig's analysis of maternal/fetal hormonal conflict during pregnancy — the embryo evolutionarily "favors" strategies that risk the mother's health to protect its own survival, and her body counters with antagonist hormones — is a Prisoner's Dilemma neither party experiences as a dilemma: "such an agreement is evolutionarily unenforceable," in Haig's words, even though both genomes would benefit from a cheaper truce.

**An ESS is defined by stability against invasion by copies of itself, not optimality from any external standpoint — which cuts against both Panglossian and "Pollyannian" readings of nature.** Dawkins' economical restatement — "An ESS is a strategy that does well against copies of itself" — captures why game-theoretic reasoning yields robust, testable predictions without requiring that the winning strategy be best in any absolute sense. Dennett names the naive-optimist error the Pollyannian Paradigm (nature is nice) as the mirror image of the Panglossian Paradigm his adaptationist critics accuse him of; his actual position — Mother Nature isn't nice, "but that isn't the end of the world" — sits between both.

**Gould and Lewontin's "hidden constraints" are real, but Dennett converts detecting them into a research problem for adaptationism rather than a refutation of it.** Gould's image of "railroad tracks lying across the landscape" — inherited developmental pathways that channel evolutionary change regardless of whether selection would otherwise favor a different route — is answered with a chess analogy: a stronger player secretly commits to a handicap (e.g., "my queen must move only in rook fashion, never diagonally"), and the weaker player can only detect it by acting as if all legal moves are available and watching for systematic departures from the best available move. The method generalizes directly to biology: "peeking at the molecules" doesn't bypass this epistemic problem, since DNA is "more machinery, more design ... in need of reverse engineering" — descending a level is an extension of adaptationism, not an alternative to it.

**Calibrating the space of the "actually possible" requires avoiding both actualism and Identikit overreach.** Against the temptation to assume a butterfly's camouflage proves "if the forest floor had any other color pattern ... the butterfly would look like that pattern instead," Dennett insists this could be false — the lineage may have exactly one viable path (actualism, "only the actual was possible"), which would make Darwinism vacuous if it were the rule. But the opposite error — assuming the Vast "Identikit Space" of every imaginable variant is equally reachable through the "Library of Mendel" — equally overestimates real possibility; adaptationists must keep testing at every level rather than resolving the question by fiat in either direction.

**The unit folds this defense into the book's master argument: adaptationism is the load-bearing core of Darwinism, not a corrigible bias, and Gould's recurring "revolutions" are recast as a search for skyhooks.** The chapter-closing verdict — "Gould and Lewontin's fabled refutation of adaptationism is an illusion" — sets up Chapter 10's claim that Gould, "like eminent evolutionary thinkers before him, has been searching for skyhooks to limit the power of Darwin's dangerous idea," the book's recurring shorthand for any move that tries to exempt some phenomenon from cumulative, non-teleological explanation.

**A loose resonance worth naming: Mother Nature's blind, retrospectively-endorsed brilliance rhymes with WMP's [[history-machine]], though the domains differ.** Dennett's adaptationists are praised for spotting "brilliant" designs only after Mother Nature has already stumbled on them — no foresight, only retroactive rationale — which echoes the WMP idea of a history machine (Lohse) assembling silently and distributedly from independent components until it "lurches into visibility," against "great men" historiography. The rhyme is at the level of process (decentralized accumulation without a designing genius, visible only in hindsight); it breaks down in substrate, since one is genetic/developmental and the other institutional/paradigmatic.

**A second, thinner resonance: the selfish trees exemplify something like Spinoza's [[Conatus]] at the sub-agent level.** Each tree "looking out for itself, trying to get as much sunlight as possible" and unable to enter binding collective agreements is a vivid image of striving-to-persist-and-expand that resists collectivization — though WMP's conatus is usually the thing a capture regime suppresses from outside, whereas here it is many small conatus-driven agents whose mutual striving itself forecloses cooperation; the fit is partial and the direction of causation is reversed.

## Notable passages

The von Neumann/Morgenstern passage (c1) supplies the founding game-theoretic premise Dennett extends to unreflective organisms. The Prisoner's Dilemma passage (c2) is the canonical setup underwriting every subsequent claim about cooperation's rarity. Dawkins' ESS definition (c3) is the working criterion — stability against invasion, not optimality — used throughout. The chess-epistemology passage (c4) is Dennett's proposed method for detecting hidden constraints without abandoning adaptationism. Gould's "railroad tracks" quote (c5) is the objection that passage answers. The closing passage (c6) delivers the chapter's verdict and pivots into the book's recurring skyhooks/cranes framing for Chapter 10.
