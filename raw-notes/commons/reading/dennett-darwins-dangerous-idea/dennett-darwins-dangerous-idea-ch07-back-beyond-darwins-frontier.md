# Cranes All the Way Down: Priming Darwin's Pump

Opening Part II ("Darwinian Thinking in Biology"), Chapter 7 asks whether the crane-based style of Darwinian explanation Dennett has spent six chapters defending can be pushed back past the point where Darwin himself stopped — past the origin of heredity and self-replication — and then surveys the emerging science of molecular evolution (Eigen, Cairns-Smith, Maynard Smith, Küppers) that suggests it can.

## Themes

**Darwin's reticence was principled, not evasive.** Dennett reads Darwin's refusal to speculate publicly about the origin of life — the private "warm little pond" letter, the concession to Asa Gray that the laws of nature might themselves be designed — as a scientist's responsible handling of dangerous, under-evidenced ideas, comparable to treating speculative claims as "laboratory wastes" requiring an environmental-impact statement. Dennett's claim is that modern biochemistry has changed the epistemic situation enough that "too many very interesting cats are already out of the bag," so reticence is no longer the right policy.

**Skyhooks postponed, cranes resumed.** The chapter's organizing image, carried over from earlier chapters, is that invoking God to jump-start replication (Asa Gray's hope, Denton's improbability argument, Cairns-Smith's watchmaker-style challenge) is a skyhook that "would at best simply postpone the solution to the problem." Dennett's bet is that Darwinian, crane-style explanation "jumps niftily down a level" instead — the same recursive, no-miracles method that explains the Tree of Life should also explain the origin of the replicators that make the Tree possible. This recursive "cranes all the way down" move is the chapter's thesis and previews the rest of Part II.

**Dawkins' regress argument against a designer of replication.** Dennett imports Dawkins' rebuttal to the "God set up the original replicating machinery" move: any designer capable of engineering something as organized as the DNA/protein system would itself have to be at least as complex and organized as what it purportedly explains, so the move is self-defeating rather than explanatory. Dennett pairs this with his own "intellectual tennis without a net" argument (via Ronald de Sousa) that a defense of faith which appeals to faith itself, mid-argument, forfeits the game — a rules-of-engagement point about when reason can be suspended, load-bearing for why Dennett feels licensed to treat the origin-of-life question scientifically rather than theologically.

**The first-replicator paradox and its solution: "macros."** Section 2 states the puzzle precisely: every living thing descends from a living thing, but the number of living things is finite, so there must have been a first one — yet a whole bacterium (four million ordered nucleotides) is too complex to have arisen by chance. Dennett's resolution is that before bacteria there were much simpler, non-parasitic self-replicating macromolecules — he coins "macros," by analogy with a programmer's macro, to name these bare, meaning-free replicating fragments that met the necessary Darwinian conditions for evolution a billion years before any living cell existed.

**Cairns-Smith's clay scaffold and "naked genes."** One candidate route into the paradox: Graham Cairns-Smith's proposal that self-replicating silicate clay crystals served as a temporary scaffold, attracting protein and RNA fragments as "tools" until those fragments could replicate on their own — "No skyhook — just a ladder that could be thrown away... once it had been climbed." This produces "naked genes": short self-replicating RNA strings that specify nothing but their own copying, which still leaves the harder problem of how such meaningless replicators ever came to specify — and need — proteins (Maynard Smith's vicious circle: no accurate replication without ~2000 base pairs of RNA, and no RNA that long without accurate replication).

**Eigen's hypercycle breaks the vicious circle.** Manfred Eigen's proposed exit is the hypercycle: fragments of type A enhance B, B enhances C, C completes the loop back to A, so that a mutually reinforcing community of elements bootstraps cumulative replication without needing any single element to be independently sufficient. This is a strongly non-teleological, distributed bootstrapping story — no designer, no privileged component, just interlocking local reinforcement that eventually "takes off" — which resonates with [[Configurancy]]'s picture of co-emergence without a governing telos, and with the [[history-machine]] pattern of paradigms assembling silently and distributedly from independent components until they lurch into visibility, in contrast to a "great designer" story (the fit is not exact: Eigen's loop is chemical kinetics, not historical agency, but the shape of the argument — structure from distributed mutual reinforcement, no top-down author — is the same).

**From tournament of luck to tournament of skill: syntactic before semantic fitness.** Before any of this can be about anything, Dennett argues, there must first be purely syntactic selection: in a population of equiprobable self-replicating fragments, whichever "wins" early rounds — for no reason but chance — occupies more slots in later rounds, so "dynasties of sheer replicative prowess" emerge from a coin-tossing tournament with no skill involved at all. This is "the birth of the ultimate QWERTY phenomenon": lock-in from historical accident. Only once a chemical bias (G/C stability) couples syntactic replicative success to particular amino acids does a "semantic" link — code that is a code for something — appear, and the tournament of luck becomes a tournament of skill.

## Notable passages

The **skyhook/crane** passage (c1) states the chapter's organizing bet explicitly: Darwin's own cranes get their booms lifted, in turn, by more cranes, "not just one idea but many, multiplying like the brooms of the sorcerer's apprentice" — rejecting the idea that Darwinian explanation must bottom out in a skyhook.

Dawkins' block quotation (c2) supplies the regress argument Dennett leans on throughout: postulating a designer of the replicating machinery only relocates the very organized complexity that needed explaining.

The **macros** passage (c3) is where Dennett names the pre-cellular replicators that resolve the first-life paradox, deliberately borrowing "macro" from programming to stress that these earliest replicators are bits of program or algorithm before they are biology.

The **Cairns-Smith clay** passage (c4) gives the chapter's most vivid image of a crane operating below the level of DNA itself — self-replicating clay crystals as disposable scaffolding, "naked genes" clinging to them like quasi-parasites.

The **hypercycle** passage (c5) is the chapter's technical payoff: a concrete, non-magical mechanism (Eigen's mutually reinforcing loop of replicating fragments) for how a system can bootstrap complexity with no privileged starting point — the strongest [[history-machine]] / [[Configurancy]] resonance in the unit.

The **tournament of skill** passage (c6) closes out the unit's argument by showing how purely syntactic, meaningless competition among replicators (a coin-tossing tournament) can generate the biased, lock-in populations of near-identical strings that a later semantic layer (the genetic code) builds on.
