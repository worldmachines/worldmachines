# The Future of an Idea

In the closing chapter of *Darwin's Dangerous Idea*, Dennett turns the book's central machinery — Design Space, cranes versus skyhooks, memes — onto the question of value itself: why do we prize a condor over a cow, a redwood over algae, an original Leonardo over a perfect copy, one culture's survival over its assimilation? His answer is that value tracks irreplaceable design work, which lets him argue for a discriminating conservationism toward both biological and cultural diversity — including religious tradition, which he treats as a population of competing memes that, like species, must evolve, be quarantined, or go extinct. He ends the book by reaffirming its title metaphor: Darwin's idea is a universal acid that corrodes comforting illusions but leaves behind sturdier versions of what actually matters, and he closes with a caution against the idea's own seductiveness.

## Themes

- **Cranes all the way down, not skyhooks.** Dennett uses Bach to restate the book's core mechanism at the scale of individual genius: Bach's brain was not a "magic pearl of genius-stuff" but an accumulated, contingent "structure of cranes, made of cranes, made of cranes," a heuristic search engine built by genetics, culture, and luck that let him traverse a vanishingly small, well-chosen slice of Design Space. The same logic that dissolved the appearance of a designing God in earlier chapters here dissolves the appearance of unaccountable individual genius, without diminishing what was actually achieved.

- **Value tracks designedness, not sentiment alone.** A run of intuition pumps — condor versus cow, redwood versus algae, a popsicle-stick model versus the popsicle sticks themselves, the Mona Lisa versus a human life — is used to argue that our sense of what is precious tracks the amount of irreplaceable design work a thing embodies. This doesn't resolve the incommensurabilities (Dennett is explicit that it doesn't), but it explains why we feel them as incommensurable rather than as a simple utilitarian calculation.

- **There is no natural line for when life begins or a soul appears.** Extending the anti-essentialism from the species chapters to bioethics, Dennett argues Darwinism forecloses any hope of finding a principled "saltation" marking the start of a human life or the birth of a soul, just as it forecloses a principled line marking a new species. He is careful to say this doesn't answer the moral questions, only that it dissolves the fantasy that biology or theology can hand us an algorithm that does.

- **Faiths are memes: some to be let evolve, some to be caged.** "A faith, like a species, must evolve or go extinct when the environment changes," and Dennett extends the conservationist logic to religion: benign, self-contained traditions (his example is the Hutterites) are tolerated the way a zoo tolerates a caged lion, while memes that endanger others — forced female circumcision, fatwas, doctrines of racial or religious supremacy — are, in his phrase, "beyond the pale" and must be actively resisted, not respected merely because they wear religious dress.

- **Preserve by scholarship, not by freezing a culture in amber.** Dennett rejects "human game preserves" (ethnic or religious enclaves maintained as living fossils) in favor of scholarship — the way Attic Greek survives through philology rather than through anyone still speaking it. Churches becoming cultural museums, the Welsh language kept alive by policy, zoos as "second-class havens" for endangered species: preservation is honest about the fact that the living context that produced these treasures (slavery, ignorance, oppressive hierarchy, in some cases) is not itself worth preserving.

- **Universal acid, handled with care.** The chapter's second section reasserts the book's title metaphor directly: Darwin's idea is a "universal solvent" that, once it has passed through everything, leaves "stronger, sounder versions of our most important ideas" rather than nihilism. But Dennett pairs this with a warning about the idea's seductiveness — how easy it is to manufacture a plausible-sounding but false Darwinian explanation — and closes on the folk image of the friend mistaken for a monster: he asks the reader to judge whether Darwinism is the Wolf in Sheep's Clothing or the Beast that is, in fact, Beauty's friend.

## Notable passages

The Bach passage is the chapter's clearest restatement of cranes-versus-skyhooks, and it resonates with the World Machines idea of a [[history-machine]] — a paradigm that assembles silently and distributedly out of independent components rather than through "great men"; Dennett's insistence that Bach is "an utterly idiosyncratic structure of cranes, made of cranes" is the individual-genius version of the same anti-heroic historiography. The line a few paragraphs later that the Tree of Life "created itself... not in a miraculous, instantaneous whoosh, but slowly, slowly, over billions of years" likewise rhymes loosely with [[Configurancy]]'s non-teleological co-emergence of agents and worlds, though Dennett's frame stays biological rather than relational-ontological.

The condor/cow/redwood/algae intuition pumps make vivid why the book's design-space metaphor was never just about biology: it is offered as a genuine (if admittedly incomplete) account of aesthetic and moral value.

The passage denying any "natural" line for the birth of a soul or a species is one of the book's more provocative bioethical extensions, and Dennett is careful to flag that dissolving the metaphysics does not itself answer the moral question.

The faith-as-species passage and the church-becoming-a-museum passage both describe religious traditions losing their living function while their forms persist — congregations that can no longer afford both gilded candlesticks and charity, Beefeaters and Cardinals "keeping alive traditions, rituals, liturgies, symbols, that otherwise would fade." This is a strong echo of the World Machines [[decay-sequence]] (Functional → Performative → Allegorical → Ornamental → Invisible → Absent): a "denatured" church kept on as a museum is Dennett's own sketch of a functional institution sliding toward the ornamental, though his unit of analysis is a specific tradition or congregation rather than a civilization-spanning world machine.

The closing universal-acid passage is the chapter's — and the book's — thesis statement, restating the title metaphor in its most compressed form and setting up the closing caution against second-rate, over-eager Darwinian explanation.
