# Some Varieties of Greedy Ethical Reductionism

Dennett tackles the naturalistic fallacy head-on, arguing that the real sin is not deriving "ought" from "is" but doing so too crudely — a diagnosis he tests against Skinner's behaviorism, E. O. Wilson's sociobiology, the Hutterites' communal experiment, and appeals to scripture, before sketching how genuine morality could still have evolved by cumulative, unforesightful steps: parental investment, kin selection, reciprocal altruism, and iterated Prisoner's Dilemma tournaments.

## Themes

**The fallacy is greediness, not naturalism.** Dennett rehabilitates naturalism against the standard Moore/Hume charge: no simple "ought" follows from any simple "is," but that only rules out grounding ethics on facts as *sufficient* axioms, not on facts as relevant evidence. Citing Bernard Williams, he insists ethics must answer to "an appreciation of human nature" — the fallacy is "greedy reductionism of values to facts," a simple-minded rush from fact to value, not the naturalist project itself.

**Skyhook-hunters on both sides.** McGinn and Chomsky's "innate ethics module" is itself a skyhook dressed as humility — Dennett reads their resistance to evolutionary explanation as the same "scientism"-baiting move he attacks elsewhere in the book. The chapter's real targets, though, are the greedy naturalists: Skinner, whose "reinforcing effects" definition of goodness makes heroin good and elder care bad, and E. O. Wilson, whose "cardinal values" (gene-pool survival, gene-pool diversity, demythologized rights) reduce ethics to a single overworked variable.

**The genetic fallacy, twice over.** Dennett accuses Wilson/Ruse and Richard Alexander of conflating a trait's evolutionary origin with its current warrant — the same move Nietzsche warned against. Genes may be culture's historical source without being its "principal beneficiary" now that memes, and the persons they build, are themselves potential beneficiaries. Wilson's own image — "the genes hold culture on a leash" — is quietly subverted: the leash is so long that persons can, per Dawkins, "rebel against the tyranny of the selfish replicators" without doing anything anti-Darwinian.

**The Hutterites: a real experiment, and a warning.** Wilson and Sober's four-century case study of Hutterite communal "organismic" living — no private property, lottery-split colonies modeled on bee-colony fission, relentless preaching against "true community's" difficulty — shows cultural engineering can out-altruize genetic default settings. But Dennett reads it as greedy reductionism's most sincere form: the Hutterites solve the problem of ethics by abolishing the one thing that makes it a human problem, the option to opt out. A totalitarian solidarity, even a benevolent one, is "extremely vulnerable to dissuasion" precisely because it cannot afford free thought.

**Religion is the oldest variety.** Citing "the Bible says so" as grounds for an "ought" commits the identical fallacy — unless one already grants the text literal divine authorship. Dennett's own position, self-labeled secular humanism, treats scripture as an "is" worth mining (like folk medicine) but never as a naturalistic-fallacy-proof axiom; he warns his own camp against the same greediness ("if it's bad for the environment, it's bad") that afflicts Skinner and Wilson.

**Building morality by cranes, not skyhooks.** Dennett closes by sketching a gradualist bootstrap: parental investment (Trivers) enables kin selection (Hamilton) enables reciprocal altruism (Trivers) enables the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma dynamics that Axelrod, Nowak and Sigmund, and Kitcher model computationally — Tit for Tat, discriminating altruists, forgiveness policies. No single mechanism is designed for ethics; morality is a by-product "bonus" of whatever modules or gadgets happen to favor altruistic choices, arrived at by many small, unforesightful steps rather than one grand design. That anti-heroic, distributed-assembly logic is close kin to [[history-machine]]'s account of a paradigm assembling silently out of independent components until it lurches into visibility, though Dennett's "components" here are biological and cultural lineages rather than institutions — the rhyme is in the shared refusal of a single architect, not in the domain.

## Notable passages

The chapter's thesis statement reframes the naturalistic fallacy in one paragraph: ethics is "responsive to... facts about human nature," so "the fallacy is not naturalism but... any simple-minded attempt to rush from facts to values" — greedy reductionism, not reductionism per se.

Skinner supplies the reductio: if goodness just is "reinforcing effects," then, as Dennett needles, "that would mean that heroin is good, apparently, and taking care of elderly parents is bad" — Skinner's own hedge that heroin's reinforcement is merely "anomalous" is no rescue.

The Wilson material turns his famous metaphor against him: the genes may "hold culture on a leash," but a sufficiently long leash lets meme-built persons become "larger, higher entities" whose transcendence — the capacity to "rebel against the tyranny of the selfish replicators" — is fully compatible with Darwinism.

The Hutterite passage carries the chapter's ethical payload: Dennett grants the "organismic organization" its real success, then insists the "option of opting out" is "the last thing we want to destroy in our social engineering" — a value this project names [[capture-resistance]], the capacity to resist a regime that would flatten individual [[Conatus]] by statistical averaging (here, literally: colonies split by lottery into equal halves, on "the genetic rules of meiosis"). WMP's frame is macro-historical — an era's capacity to resist capture — where Dennett's is one commune's suppression of individual dissent; the rhyme is that both diagnose control-by-averaging as the mechanism, and both treat the capacity to exit as what is actually at stake.

The closing passage on Axelrod-style tournaments frames the whole positive program: whether human morality required "an impressive bottleneck" of improbable accidents or sits in a wide "basin of attraction," Dennett is confident that "mutual recognition and the capacity to communicate a promise... are necessary conditions for the evolution of morality" — a falsifiable, evolutionary-mechanistic answer to a question the naturalistic-fallacy debate had treated as unanswerable.
