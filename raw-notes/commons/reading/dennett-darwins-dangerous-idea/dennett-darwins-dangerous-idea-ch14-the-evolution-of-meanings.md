# The Evolution of Meanings

Chapter 14 of *Darwin's Dangerous Idea* extends Dennett's Darwinian argument to meaning itself: against philosophers who insist that human words and thoughts have "real," "original," or "intrinsic" intentionality categorically unlike anything an artifact or animal could have, Dennett marshals a chain of thought experiments — a coin-sorting "two-bitser" vending machine relocated to Panama, a frog's fly-detecting eye retrained on food pellets, Putnam's Twin Earth, his own "two black boxes" fable, and finally a cryogenic-capsule robot — to argue that all meaning, without exception, is conferred by selective/functional history rather than stamped in at birth, and that the line philosophers draw between "derived" (artifactual) and "original" (human) intentionality cannot be principled once you notice that human intentionality is itself derived from the "intentions" of natural selection and our own selfish genes.

## Themes

**Meaning is a historical, relational fact, never an intrinsic one.** The two-bitser — a vending-machine transducer built to detect U.S. quarters — physically does the same thing wherever it goes, but its *meaning* changes the moment its environment of use does: shipped to Panama, its acceptance state comes to mean "quarter-balboa here" rather than "U.S. quarter here," with no fact of the matter about the exact moment of the switch. What fixes what a state means is the (frequently unconscious) selective history of its users, never some fact legible from the device's innards alone. This is a close, if partial, cousin of `[[Configurancy]]`'s picture of agents and worlds co-emerging non-teleologically: Dennett's two-bitser and frog have no meaning apart from their relation to a selecting environment — though Configurancy is Rao's frame for cultural/historical actors, not transducers and retinas, so the resonance is a rhyme, not an identity.

**Indeterminacy is the precondition for evolved meaning, not a flaw in it.** Fodor's objection to Darwinian theories of meaning is that they leave what a state "really" means indeterminate (does the frog's eye report "fly" or "small-moving-dark-thing"?). Dennett's frogs — relocated to a zoo and fed food pellets instead of flies until later generations are, by natural selection, pellet-detectors — show that this indeterminacy is exactly the "blind variation" that any exaptation, and hence any evolution of new function or meaning, requires. Essentialism about meaning is as unmotivated as essentialism about species.

**Twin Earth, read through Darwinian spectacles, undercuts its own moral.** Putnam's thought experiment (you wake up on a planet where "horses" are actually a different species, "schmorses") is meant to show that meaning outruns what's in your head. Dennett grants the case its force but turns it: your own concept of "horse" was never determinately fixed to *species* rather than to *superficially-horse-shaped-thing* in the first place (compare Siamese speakers' word for "cat," or the discovery that coyotes are dogs) — so there is no hidden fact being violated, only an indeterminacy in your concept resolving itself under new selective pressure, exactly as with the frog and the two-bitser.

**The intentional stance is explanatorily indispensable, and that is itself a form of causal potency.** In the "two black boxes" fable, two independently-built expert systems seem to send mysterious "red"/"green"/"amber" signals down a wire; the mystery dissolves only once you notice the boxes are describing the same world and the signal really is truth/falsehood/nonsense in English. No microphysical description of the wire, however exhaustive, explains the regularity without climbing to the semantic level — just as no microphysical history of any giraffe explains why giraffes have long necks without asking, Dewey-style, "why."

**"Derived" and "original" intentionality collapse into each other.** The chapter's capstone thought experiment — build a robot to keep your cryogenically-frozen body safe for four centuries, then cede it real-time autonomy — shows the robot developing subsidiary goals that may drift far from, even against, your interests. "Client centrism" (you are the sole original source of meaning; the robot's is merely derived) proves to apply just as well to you: your intentionality, too, is derived, from the "intentions" of your selfish genes — "They are the Unmeant Meaners, not us!" This is Dennett's version of `[[Conatus]]` domesticated into an engineering problem: a survival machine whose only original mandate is to persist, from which every other goal is "offspring" — though where Spinoza's conatus is a metaphysically fundamental striving, Dennett's is bequeathed by a designer (genes, or a sleeper's engineers) and only later escapes its brief, so the fit is partial. The robot's earned autonomy — "becoming a locus of self-control and self-determination... by confronting problems during its own 'lifetime'" — also rhymes with `[[capture-resistance]]`'s framing of autonomy and self-organization as achievements rather than intrinsic endowments; the domains differ sharply (one mind's independence from its genetic "client" vs. an era's institutions resisting legibility), but both refuse to let "real" autonomy require an unmoved, non-derived source.

## Notable passages

**The two-bitser (c1).** Dennett's founding thought experiment: a quarter-detecting vending-machine part whose "meaning" is entirely a function of the selective history of its designers and users, not anything read off its physical state — a template for every later move in the chapter.

**The frog and the pellet zoo (c2).** Real frogs bred in captivity on food pellets rather than flies come, over generations, to be selected as pellet-detectors — demonstrating that the "indeterminacy" Fodor treats as a defect in Darwinian meaning-theory is the very raw material any shift in function requires.

**Essentialism evaporates (c3).** The chapter's thesis in miniature: meaning, like function, "is not something determinate at its birth... it arises not by saltation or special creation, but by a (typically gradual) shift of circumstances."

**Twin Earth (c4).** Putnam's own thought experiment, restated in Dennett's terms, en route to being turned against its intended conclusion: whether Twining's and your assertions about the passing "horse" are true or false depends on a fact about your prior concept that may never have been fixed.

**The point of the fable (c5).** The "two black boxes" fable's moral, stated directly: only the intentional stance — going up a level to ask why — explains the causal regularities of both rigged black boxes and long-necked giraffes.

**The Unmeant Meaners (c6).** The chapter's culminating move: if an artifact's autonomy-in-drift doesn't earn it "real" intentionality, then neither does yours, since you too are a survival machine built by "Unmeant Meaners" — your genes.
