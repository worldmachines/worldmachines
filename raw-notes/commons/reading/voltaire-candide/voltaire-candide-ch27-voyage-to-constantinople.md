# Voyage to Constantinople: Cunégonde's fall and the galley slaves who turn out to be Pangloss and the Baron

Candide and Martin board the Turkish ship carrying the deposed Sultan Achmet, fresh from the Venice dinner with six dethroned kings. Candide takes the encounter as confirmation of Pangloss's doctrine; Martin deflates it as commonplace. Reunited with Cacambo, Candide learns that Cunégonde is a dishwasher in the household of the exiled prince Ragotsky on the Propontis, and that she has turned horribly ugly, her fortune lost to the Buenos Ayres governor's price and to a pirate. A short exchange with Martin asks whose misfortune is worst. In the galley hired to find her, Candide pities two beaten rowers, recognizes them as the Baron (whom he thought he had killed) and Pangloss (whom he saw hanged), and ransoms them with diamonds sold at a loss to a Jewish dealer, then sets out for Cunégonde in another galley.

## Themes

**Optimism as a reflex that survives its own counter-evidence.** Candide's reasoning (c1) is that because he gave charity to one of six fallen kings, and merely lost a hundred sheep, he must "insist on it that Pangloss was right. All is for the best." The narrator's irony is in how the lesson is drawn from the wrong evidence: other people's greater misery is offered as proof of the system. Martin's reply is that dethroned kings are "a very common thing" and the dinner "a mere accident". This is Candide's voice and Martin's, not a stated thesis of the author; the pattern is the one the vault names [[panglossian-optimism]] and [[the-best-of-all-possible-worlds]].

**Comparative suffering cannot be adjudicated.** Candide asks whose condition is most to be pitied (c3). Martin declines to rank: he would "need to enter the heart of each man", and he asks what "scales" Pangloss would use to weigh sufferings, adding that millions are in conditions a hundred times worse. The passage turns on the unavailability of a common metric for harm; a loosely modest reading is that the satire targets the optimist's confidence in a total ledger, which resonates with [[Legibility]] (making suffering commensurable), though the text itself makes no such claim and Martin is a pessimist with his own ready answer.

**Fortune is a chain of accidents, and money is the world's only mechanism.** Cacambo's account (c2) is a ledger: two million to the governor for permission, the rest taken by a pirate, the pirate's route through a string of Aegean and Marmara ports, and now everyone enslaved. Candide's response, "What a chain of shocking accidents!", is followed at once by the practical remark that he still has diamonds. Honour obliges him to love Cunégonde "ugly or handsome", and the narrative's comic tone is in that clause and in his regret that she has grown ugly. Everything in the unit is bought and sold: Cacambo repurchased, the ransom set at fifty thousand sequins by a captain who reasons from the men's claimed rank, and diamonds sold at half value.

**Resurrections without explanation.** The two rowers are the Baron and Pangloss (c4, c5), the one killed and the other hanged, and the unit shows them alive before it explains how. The comedy is of abruptly reversed expectations; in the narrative logic, titles ("one of the first barons of the empire", "the most profound metaphysician in Germany") meet the captain's contempt ("Christian cur"), who prices them only as rank-bearing property. The metaphysician is found chained to an oar, a juxtaposition left for the reader to read, with no comment from the narrator.

## Notable passages

**The six kings and the reflex (c1).** Candide's confident inference and Martin's drier gloss, in the same paragraph.

**Cunégonde's fall (c2).** Cacambo's reply, his catalogue of losses, and Candide's honour-bound but unromantic reaction.

**Martin on weighing misfortune (c3).** The set-piece in which Candide invokes Pangloss as an authority who would settle the question, and Martin asks what scales he would use.

**The galley recognition (c4).** Candide's pity, the near-resemblance, his hedge "if I had not seen my master Pangloss hanged", and the two men's cry; a recognition scene played for pathos and comedy at once, with the bullwhip continuing in the background.

**Ransom and reunion (c5).** The captain's price, Candide's undervalued diamond, the Baron's gracious nod and Pangloss's tears, and Candide's immediate setting out again. The Baron's promise to repay and his concern for his sister set up what follows, though that is outside this unit.
