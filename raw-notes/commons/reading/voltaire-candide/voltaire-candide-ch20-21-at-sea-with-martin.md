# Candide and Martin at sea: Manichaeism, a sea-battle, and the hawks

Candide and the old philosopher Martin sail from Surinam for Bordeaux. Candide, still hoping for Cunégonde and still holding some money and jewels, leans back toward Pangloss's doctrine; Martin, who has nothing to hope for, declares himself a Manichaean. A Spanish ship sinks a Dutch pirate (the very skipper who robbed Candide) in view of their vessel, and one of Candide's red sheep floats up. Each man reads the event his own way. They argue for fifteen days with no progress. In Chapter XXI, as France comes into sight, Martin gives a bleak sketch of the French and of Paris, and the two argue over whether human nature has ever changed. The chapter ends mid-sentence on Candide's appeal to free will.

## Themes

**Optimism as a mood tied to circumstances.** The narrator says Candide, thinking of what he still has and repeating Cunégonde's name 'especially after meal times', 'leaned toward Pangloss's doctrine'. This is the narrator's ironic voice: Candide's confidence tracks his comfort and his hope, not argument. This fits the satire's recurring target, which can be read as the way a system explains harm away (compare [[panglossian-optimism]] and [[the-best-of-all-possible-worlds]]). The chapter shows the mechanism at the level of one person's digestion and hope.

**Martin's pessimism as a rival system.** Martin's Manichaeism is Martin's own claim, not the narrator's verdict: God 'has abandoned' the globe 'to some evil being', except El Dorado. His evidence is cities and families wishing each other's destruction, the poor hating the rich, 'a million regimented assassins' roaming Europe, and the point that private griefs are worse than public calamities. It is as total an explanatory frame as Pangloss's, and the book lets neither win: after fifteen days they are 'just as far advanced as the first moment they began'. A modest reading is that the satire targets the habit of fitting all evidence to one doctrine, whichever direction it faces (interpretation, not stated in the text).

**One event, two readings.** The sinking of the pirate is the chapter's test case. Candide sees punished vice; Martin grants that but asks why the passengers perished: 'God has punished the knave, and the devil has drowned the rest.' Candide's joy at one recovered sheep exceeds his grief at losing a hundred. The sheep's survival is narrated with ironic flatness.

**The hawk argument and the cut-off.** Martin answers Candide's question about whether humanity has always been vicious by asking whether hawks have always eaten pigeons, and so whether a fixed nature should be expected to change. Candide's reply, 'because free will—', is interrupted by arrival at Bordeaux. Readers can take this as the book declining to settle the question of fixed nature versus agency; that resonates loosely with arguments over whether history has direction (see [[argument-of-progress]]), though the text itself says nothing about progress and the connection is only analogical.

**Paris as chaos.** Martin's account of France (love, slander, nonsense) and of Paris, a 'chaos, a confused multitude, where every one seeks pleasure without being able to find it', with its pickpockets, a week's imprisonment on suspicion, and 'scribblers, malcontents and fanatics', sets up the Paris episode to come. It is Martin's reported experience, delivered with a deadpan last line: 'It is said that the people of that city are very polite: I believe they may be so.'

**Money as the true companion.** Martin follows Candide to Venice because 'I have none, but you have; therefore I will follow you anywhere'. His pessimism has a practical, dependent side.

## Notable passages

**Martin's Manichaean confession (c1).** The longest statement of the pessimist position in the unit, attributed to Martin: the world abandoned to an evil being, cities and families in mutual hatred, mercenary armies, envy within peaceful cities, and private griefs worse than public ones. Candide's only reply is 'there is some good in the world'; Martin: 'it has escaped my knowledge.'

**The sea-battle and the sheep (c2).** Candide and Martin watch through spy-glasses as a hundred men are drowned; Martin: 'you now see how mankind treat each other.' The recovered red sheep and the Dutch skipper's fate prompt the split verdict, 'God has punished the knave, and the devil has drowned the rest.'

**A stalemate (c3).** Fifteen days of dispute with no advance, redeemed only by 'the satisfaction of talking, of communicating their ideas, and of comforting each other.' The pair's friendship is the unit's one positive note, and it comes without agreement.

**Martin on Paris (c4).** A compact satire of the city and its literary hangers-on, told from Martin's own misadventures.

**The hawks and free will (c5).** Candide's list of human vices, Martin's hawk analogy, and the unfinished 'free will—'. The exchange also includes Martin's answer to why the world was formed ('To drive us mad') just before it.
