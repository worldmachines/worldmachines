# Thunder-ten-tronckh and the Bulgarian recruiters

The unit opens in the castle of the Baron of Thunder-ten-tronckh in Westphalia, where the simple-minded youth Candide is raised alongside Cunégonde, the baron's daughter, under the tutor Pangloss, "the oracle of the family". Pangloss's doctrine that this is the best of all possible worlds is shown at work: it flatters the baron's castle and baroness, and justifies swine, stockings and spectacles. Pangloss's own "experimental philosophy" with a chambermaid is watched by Cunégonde, who then tries the experiment with Candide; the baron catches them, notes "the cause and effect", and kicks Candide out. In Chapter II the starving Candide is lured by two recruiting-officers ("the blues") with flattering talk of mutual aid, toasted into being "the hero of the Bulgarians", handcuffed, drilled and beaten. After a failed walk he is court-martialled, chooses flogging over execution, and is pardoned by the King of the Bulgars just before being shot.

## Themes

**Optimism as a system that explains away.** Pangloss's reasoning runs from effect to purpose: noses exist for spectacles, stones for castles, so the greatest baron ought to be the best lodged. This is the narrator's satirical rendering of the doctrine, not a neutral summary. It closely echoes the vault's [[panglossian-optimism]] and [[the-best-of-all-possible-worlds]]; the target is plausibly Leibnizian theodicy ([[gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz]]), but that is interpretation, since the unit never names Leibniz. Note that the doctrine is a social ratifier: the existing hierarchy is declared the best end.

**Cause and effect turned against the pupil.** Pangloss proves "there is no effect without a cause"; Cunégonde grasps "the force of the doctor's reasoning upon causes and effects"; the baron "took note of the cause and effect" and acts on it. Causal vocabulary is applied comically to sex and punishment, and the sage who ran the experiment suffers nothing in this unit while the pupil is exiled.

**Hollow aristocratic pride.** The narrator measures the baron's power by his castle having "not only a gate, but even windows", and Candide's possible father is refused over a shortfall of quarterings. Status is registered by counting, which is a faint rhyme with legibility ([[modernity-machine]]), though nothing in the text develops that.

**Consent manufactured by flattery.** The blues invoke Pangloss-like talk ("Mankind were born to assist one another") and Candide in turn credits it to Pangloss; a toast to a king he has never seen makes him a "hero" and a conscript. The grim comedy lies in the gap between the courteous vocabulary and the handcuffs.

**Free will under compulsion.** Offered flogging or execution, Candide "remonstrate[s] ... that the human will is free" and then chooses by "that divine gift called free-will". The narrator's irony shows a choice with no real alternatives, a small-scale instance of doctrine meeting force.

**War as drill and arbitrary clemency.** Violence is quantified (thirty blows, twenty, ten; 2,000 men, 4,000 strokes) and the king's pardon turns on a casual pass-by; the king is praised in ironic tones as "a prince of great genius", which is the narrator's voice, not a sincere endorsement.

## Notable passages

**Pangloss's demonstration.** The "things cannot be otherwise than they are" speech is the book's founding statement of the doctrine as voiced by Pangloss, with the correction that everything is not merely right but "best". The preceding paragraph introduces his "metaphysico-theologo-cosmolonigology", where the baron's castle is "the most magnificent of all castles".

**The experiment behind the bushes.** Cunégonde's reading of Pangloss's lesson, and her thought that she might be "a sufficing reason for young Candide", is the bawdy parody of the principle of sufficient reason.

**Expulsion.** The baron's kicks and the closing line about "all possible castles" show the best-world rhetoric collapsing into consternation within a single sentence.

**The recruiting scene.** The "you are perfectly right, gentlemen" exchange shows Candide quoting Pangloss to ratify his own entrapment.

**Free-will and the pardon.** The court-martial choice and the king's "clemency" frame the unit's contrast between stated principle and enforced fact. [[voltaire]] is the author behind the narrator, but the unit itself offers only ironic voice, not a stated thesis.
