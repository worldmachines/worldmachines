# Buenos Ayres and Paraguay: the Governor, the flight, and the Jesuit commandant

Cunégonde and Candide, with the old woman, land at Buenos Ayres and wait on the Governor, Don Fernando d'Ibaraa y Figueora y Mascarenes y Lampourdos y Souza, whom the narrator mocks for pride proportional to the number of his names. He asks if Cunégonde is the captain's wife; Candide's scruple about lying leads him to say she will marry him, and the Governor sends him off to drill his company and makes his own declaration. The old woman, consulted, urges Cunégonde to accept the Governor and make Candide's fortune. Then an alcayde arrives in pursuit of the murderers of the Inquisitor, the stolen-jewel trail having been traced through the Cordelier who was hanged; the old woman tells Cunégonde to stay and Candide to flee at once. In Chapter XIV Candide's mixed-blood footman Cacambo (many trades, one-quarter Spanish, born in Tucuman) takes charge, proposes they go and fight *for* the Jesuits of Paraguay, and praises their government. At the frontier they are disarmed and held, but the commandant, hearing Candide is German, receives them in a luxurious arbour while the Paraguayans eat corn in the sun. Candide and the commandant recognize one another: he is Cunégonde's brother, the baron's son, believed killed by the Bulgarians, now a Jesuit.

## Themes

**Aristocratic pride, mocked by the narrator.** The Governor is drawn in the narrator's ironic voice: he "carried his nose so high" that everyone was "violently tempted to kick his excellency." The long name stands for rank as sheer accumulation. The old woman's counsel (her own voice) repeats the point from the other side: Cunégonde's "seventy-two quarterings" are worth little without a penny, so honour yields to prudence. Her speech is pragmatic, not a statement of the narrator's view; the satire lands on a social order in which noble birth and sexual possession are currency.

**Candide's scruple against the world's pragmatism.** Candide cannot lie "because indeed he was not" married to her, and he forthrightly asks the Governor to perform the ceremony, which hands the Governor the opening. The narrator's treatment is affectionate but ironic: the "purity of his heart" is a liability, a motif of innocence repeatedly punished in the book. No Panglossian doctrine is invoked in these chapters beyond Candide's regret that Pangloss was hanged (twice, in Ch XIII and Ch XIV) before he could lecture on evil.

**Cacambo's picture of Jesuit Paraguay (a character's reported speech, not a verdict).** Cacambo calls it "an admirable government" because "Los Padres own everything there, and the people have no money at all", and "the masterpiece of justice and reason". He also notes that the Jesuits fight Spain and Portugal in America while hearing the confessions of the same princes in Europe. The reader is invited to hear this as praise with an edge; on a modest interpretation, the target is the Society of Jesus and the hypocrisy of a church that sanctifies both sides of colonial war. Cacambo is a cheerful opportunist ("If we cannot find our account in one world, we'll find it in another"), so his enthusiasm is itself part of the joke. This is a colonial-Atlantic setting that touches [[grove-green-imperialism]] and [[mann-1493]] only loosely: the text gives no economics, just the stylized image of an ordered, communal mission state.

**Hierarchy visible in the arbour.** At the frontier the sergeant says the provincial has "forbidden any Spaniard to open his mouth except in his presence", and the commandant eats from gold vessels in a bird-filled arbour "while the Paraguayans were eating coarse Indian corn out of wooden dishes in the open air, and exposed to the burning heat of the sun." The narrator does not comment; the juxtaposition does the work. It undercuts Cacambo's 'justice and reason' and the 'communal property' claim, since the property is evidently not shared equally. The commandant's slaves ("negro slaves") are mentioned in passing in the next paragraph.

**Coincidence and recognition as the novel's engine.** The commandant, "a very handsome young man" whose boldness resembles "neither" Spaniard nor Jesuit, turns out to be the Baron's son. The scene parodies romance recognition (tears, embraces, "Like true Germans" lingering at table) and Candide's remark "this is a strange world we live in" sits beside his lament "O Pangloss! Pangloss!" Optimism is not stated, but the pattern of loss followed by miraculous reunion is the kind of 'it all works out' sequence that Pangloss's doctrine would claim as evidence; here it just keeps the plot running. See [[panglossian-optimism]] for the vault's treatment, and note that Candide's invocation of Pangloss is wistful rather than doctrinal.

## Notable passages

**The Governor** (c2). The narrator's ironic voice mocks the Governor's hauteur; "carried his nose so high" is the key image of pride as comic physical posture.

**The old woman's counsel** (c1). Her reduction of Cunégonde's history ("ravished by a Bulgarian soldier; a Jew and an Inquisitor have both tasted of your favours") to bargaining leverage, ending "People take advantage of misfortunes", is the chapter's cold-eyed pragmatism, spoken by a character who has herself suffered.

**Cacambo's pitch** (c5, c3). First the pivot from enmity to alliance ("you were going to fight against the Jesuits of Paraguay; now let's go and fight for them"), then his enthusiastic account of Los Padres, whose irony the reader must supply.

**The arbour** (c4). Gold vessels and a cool arbour for the commandant against corn in the sun for the Paraguayans: the satire is by contrast, with no narratorial gloss.

**The recognition** (c6). Candide and the commandant discover each other as Westphalians; the sequence ends in tears and the line "O Pangloss! Pangloss! what joy would this have given you if you had not been hanged." The comedy of surprise and the story's persistent habit of killing and resurrecting characters meet here. Candide also names the dramatic irony that he was going to fight against the Fathers when the Jesuit is Cunégonde's brother.
