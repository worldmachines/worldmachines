# The Baron Killed and the Oreillons (Chapters XV-XVI)

In Paraguay Candide finds Cunégonde's brother alive. The baron tells how he survived the massacre at Thunder-ten-tronckh, was revived by a Jesuit, and rose to become a colonel and priest in the order's South American colony. His joy at the reunion collapses when Candide says he intends to marry Cunégonde: the baron answers with her 'seventy-two quarterings' and strikes him, and Candide runs him through. Cacambo disguises Candide in the dead man's Jesuit habit and they flee across the frontier. In Chapter XVI they shoot two monkeys that seem to be attacking two naked women, discover the women mourn them as lovers, and are captured by the Oreillons, who want to eat a Jesuit. Cacambo talks them free by arguing, in the Oreillons' own terms, that the captives are their friends, and Candide ends by praising the natural man.

## Themes

**Rank outlasts every shock.** The baron has survived massacre and been remade as a Jesuit officer, but his pride of birth is unchanged. Candide answers with Pangloss's teaching that 'all people are by nature equal', so the doctrine appears here as a lesson in equality; the narrator's irony lies in how swiftly it ends in a sword-thrust. Read modestly, this is a satire on aristocratic pride, not a stated thesis.

**Violence and its comic bookkeeping.** Candide weeps while pulling out the sword and then tallies: 'I am the best man in the world, and yet I have already killed three men; and of these three two were priests.' Self-regard and killing sit side by side. The baron's own speech ('they will be well excommunicated and beaten', 'Providence has sent you hither') shows the Jesuit colony as a military and ecclesiastical machine, though the unit only gives the baron's account of it.

**Misreading what one sees.** Candide shoots the monkeys for the girls' sake and is told he has killed their lovers. Cacambo's 'one-quarter human' reply and Candide's recollection of Pangloss on centaurs and satyrs make the episode a joke about the limits of confident inference, with Pangloss's authority again recalled and again unreliable.

**Optimism under the cauldron.** Facing the spit, Candide asks 'what would Pangloss say if he could see how a state of nature is formed? Everything is right. It may be so: but I must confess it seems harsh'. This is a small, direct test of [[panglossian-optimism]]: the formula is repeated and then hedged by lived experience. Compare [[the-best-of-all-possible-worlds]].

**Nature as argument, and the noble-savage reversal.** Cacambo's speech turns cannibalism into a matter of 'the law of nature' and 'justice' and wins by appeal to the Oreillons' sense of law. Candide's closing verdict, 'pure nature is an excellent thing', is his own reaction to being spared, and it sits ironically beside the cauldron he had just feared. It resonates with the [[noble-savage]] debate and [[jean-jacques-rousseau]], though here the satire cuts both ways (the Oreillons are both threatening and courteous). It is interpretation, not an authorial position.

## Notable passages

**The baron's career (c1).** The Jesuit baron's account of his rescue by holy water, his rise to colonel and priest, and the claim that Paraguay's sovereigns prefer non-Spanish Jesuits as more obedient.

**The quarterings quarrel (c2).** Candide's appeal to Pangloss's equality against 'seventy-two quarterings', ending in the killing and the tally of three men, two priests.

**Monkeys and centaurs (c3).** Cacambo's 'one-quarter human' explanation and Candide's recalled Pangloss; a comic episode about unreliable inference.

**The cauldron and the state of nature (c4).** Candide's hedge on 'Everything is right'.

**Cacambo's speech (c5).** An argument built on the law of nature, enemies and friends, and the offered proof of the robe; it persuades the Oreillons by speaking in their own idiom of justice.

**Pure nature (c6).** Candide's ironic conclusion that his killing of the baron saved him, and that nature is excellent because it did not eat him.
