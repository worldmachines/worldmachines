# Candide's two killings and the flight to Cadiz

The unit runs from Cunégonde's apartment through the road to Cadiz. Issachar, a choleric Jew who resents the Inquisitor and Candide alike, attacks Candide and is run through with a sword the old woman had given him. The Inquisitor walks in on the scene and Candide, reasoning in an instant, stabs him too. The old woman organizes the escape on three Andalusian horses; the Holy Brotherhood arrives to find the bodies, the Inquisitor is buried magnificently and Issachar is thrown on a dunghill. At an inn Cunégonde discovers that her gold and jewels are gone, the old woman suspects a Father Cordelier, a horse is sold cheaply to a Benedictine, and the three reach Cadiz, where a fleet is assembling against the Jesuits of Paraguay (the sentence breaks off in the text). During the voyage they argue over Pangloss's philosophy and over who has suffered most, and the old woman promises to reveal her own story.

## Themes

**Rationalization of violence by a gentle character.** Candide, described as "the most gentle, sweet-tempered young man breathing", kills twice. The narrator calls his reasoning "clear and instantaneous", and it is a self-interested calculus (burning, rivalry, "I have now begun to dip my hands in blood"). His excuse is passion, not principle: "when a man is in love, is jealous, and has been whipped by the Inquisition, he is no longer himself." The satire is on the ease with which reasoning, here the narrator's ironic voice, supplies a justification after the fact. It is a small, human-scale echo of the way a system of belief rationalizes harm; compare [[panglossian-optimism]], though here the rationalizer is Candide, not Pangloss.

**Pangloss's doctrine invoked, and misapplied, at the moment it fails.** Candide twice reaches for his tutor: wishing for his "most excellent advice" (though Pangloss, he says, was hanged), and citing Pangloss's proof that "the goods of this world are common to all men" to complain that the thieving Cordelier should have left them enough. The doctrine is quoted as a lever for a personal grievance, which sits oddly beside the optimism it belongs to. This is Candide's reported use of Pangloss's claim, not a statement of the narrator's thesis. See [[the-best-of-all-possible-worlds]] and [[gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz]] for the target; the text here only shows the doctrine bending to convenience.

**Clergy and institutions as predators and beneficiaries.** The Inquisitor holds Sundays by agreement, the Cordelier is suspected thief, the Benedictine buys the horse "very cheap", and the Inquisitor receives a "magnificent" burial while the Jew's body is thrown "upon a dunghill". Cunégonde's own line, "where will I find Inquisitors and Jews who can give me more?", is deadpan about how she has been financed. The satiric target is plausibly the Church and its hypocrisy, though the text itself only shows the facts through the narrator's flat tone, a modest interpretation.

**Hope displaced onto the New World.** At sea Cunégonde reasons that "it must be there that everything is best", and Candide says the new sea "is already better than our European seas; it is smoother, and the winds blow more regularly." This is optimism by relocation, tested immediately by Cunégonde's admission that she has "almost lost all hopes of a better." The Paraguay expedition against the Jesuits, glimpsed in the truncated sentence, belongs to the colonial Atlantic that later chapters develop ([[columbian-exchange-mann]], [[grove-green-imperialism]]), but this unit only names it.

**Competitive suffering.** Cunégonde catalogues her woes in pairs (two Bulgarians, two wounds, two castles, two fathers and two mothers, two whipped lovers) and the old woman answers that she would "suspend your judgment" if she knew her birth and rank. Misery becomes a contest of rank and quantity, a comic structure that sets up the old woman's tale and later Martin's pessimism; it shows a character's claim about her own suffering, not an authorial verdict.

## Notable passages

The first killing (c1) is quick and almost slapstick: Issachar's fury, the sword from the old woman, and the body "sprawling" at Cunégonde's feet. The second (c2) is the key passage: the narrator's "whole train of reasoning" and Candide's explanation to Cunégonde. The theft-and-Pangloss exchange (c3) shows the doctrine reduced to a complaint and the old woman's practical, one-buttock pragmatism. The burial and dunghill (c4) delivers the unit's institutional irony in one clause. The voyage conversation (c5) is where hope in the New World, Pangloss's philosophy, and the contest of misfortunes meet.
