# Reunion on the galley and the ugly Cunégonde

In the last stretch of the chain of reunions, the ransomed galley slaves tell how they survived. The Baron explains how he was cured, imprisoned by Spanish troops, made chaplain at Constantinople, and then flogged and sent to the galleys for swimming naked with a young Turk, an event he calls "an act of more flagrant injustice." Pangloss tells of his botched hanging and near dissection, then of a second sentence for putting a fallen bouquet back in a woman's bosom in a mosque. Asked whether he still thinks all is for the best, he says he holds his first opinion. In Chapter XXIX the party reaches the Transylvanian prince's house on the Propontis and finds Cunégonde and the old woman hanging out tablecloths. Cunégonde is now sun-burnt and wrinkled. Candide, out of good manners and an old promise, still intends to marry her, and the Baron refuses on grounds of rank.

## Themes

**Optimism survives by refusing evidence.** Pangloss is hanged, cut open, whipped and chained to an oar. His answer is that "I am a philosopher, and it would not become me to retract my sentiments, especially since Leibniz could not be wrong." His reason is loyalty to a system, not experience. The jargon he adds (pre-established harmony, plenum, materia subtilis) is an ironic mockery of Leibnizian vocabulary in Pangloss's mouth, and it is satire of a doctrine, not a neutral summary of Leibniz. It echoes [[panglossian-optimism]], and the question of whether any suffering could falsify the doctrine fits the vault's [[the-best-of-all-possible-worlds]], though Pangloss here is a comic figure and not a policy analyst. See also [[gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz]].

**Institutional absurdity, told in the victims' own matter-of-fact voice.** Rain defeats the burning, a novice executioner ruins the hanging, and a surgeon and his wife take the living Pangloss for a devil and a heretic. The Inquisition's machinery fails through incompetence and superstition, yet the survivors are still punished by a cadi for trivial acts. Interpretively, the satire's target is institutional cruelty and arbitrary justice, which the narrator treats with deadpan, though the text does not state a thesis.

**Rival grievances and vanity.** The Baron and Pangloss argue over whose offence was worse. The Baron insists that the Icoglan swim was more wrong than a bouquet. The Baron's complaint of injustice from the cadi sits beside his own pride later in Chapter XXIX, where he refuses to let his sister marry below a baron of the empire. Interpretation: aristocratic pride survives the galley unchanged, in parallel with Pangloss's creed.

**Philosophy as idle consolation.** The company discuss "moral and physical evil, free-will and necessity, and the consolation available to a slave on a Turkish galley" while being rowed. The narrator's list makes the abstract debate sit comically beside the slavery.

**Romance deflated.** The narrator notes that Cunégonde, "not knowing that she had become ugly, because no one had informed her of it," holds Candide to his promise. Candide recoils "with horror" but proceeds "out of good manners." The long-sought beloved is reduced to a kitchen-maid and the marriage is propelled by obligation and then by quarrel with the Baron.

## Notable passages

**The Baron's account (c1).** A short, brisk narrative of misfortune ending in a claim of injustice; note that the Baron accepts the sentence's cruelty while resenting its application to himself.

**Pangloss's near-dissection (c2).** The comic centrepiece: failed burning, failed hanging, the surgeon's flight, the wife's fear that the devil is in heretics. The passage shows how superstition both nearly kills and then saves him.

**Pangloss's creed intact (c3).** After the galley comparisons, Candide asks the pointed question and Pangloss's reply is the chapter's thesis-by-irony.

**Cunégonde at the washing line (c4).** The reunion opens in domestic labour, in contrast to the earlier romance.

**The Baron refuses (c5).** Candide's promise, the old woman's farm, and the Baron's rank argument, and Candide's answer that he would kill the Baron again.
