# The farm, the dervish, the old Turk, and the garden

The last chapter first disposes of the Baron: Candide, who no longer much wants to marry Cunégonde, is goaded by the Baron's impertinence and Cunégonde's insistence into the match. Pangloss writes a treatise on the Baron's lack of rights, Martin proposes drowning him, and Cacambo's plan (hand him to the Turkish captain, then ship him to the Jesuit Father-General) is the one adopted. The household then settles into disappointment: the diamonds are gone, Cunégonde grows ugly and headstrong, the old woman is ill-natured, Cacambo is worn down by garden labour, and Pangloss cannot get a university post. Boredom prompts the old woman's question whether suffering or idleness is worse. Pacquette and Brother Giroflée turn up ruined, a famous dervish refuses to discuss the origin of evil, and a modest old Turk who cultivates twenty acres with his children offers an alternative. Candide's "we must cultivate our garden" closes the argument; the household takes up useful work, and Pangloss still recites his chain of events.

## Themes

**Boredom as the new test of optimism.** After war, earthquake, and Inquisition, the narrator's irony notes it would be "natural to suppose" Candide would now "lead the most agreeable life in the world". Instead the old woman's list of every misery they have suffered, set against "remain here doing nothing", turns the book's question from suffering to idleness. Martin's answer (convulsions of misery or lethargy of boredom) is the pessimist's version; Candide "did not provide any other opinion". Pangloss, notably, "maintained it, and at the same time didn't believe it all": the narrator shows his [[panglossian-optimism]] persisting as habit after belief has gone, a system that keeps explaining without conviction.

**Refusal of metaphysics.** The dervish's reply, in his own words, is "Be quiet", with the image of the ruler's ship and its rats. Pangloss wanted "causes and effects, on the best of possible worlds, the origin of evil, the nature of the soul, and a pre-established harmony", the vocabulary of Leibnizian system-building (compare [[gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz]], [[the-best-of-all-possible-worlds]]). The door is shut on him. This is one character's rebuff, not necessarily the book's verdict; the dervish's indifference to the rats is itself hard to read as comforting.

**Local work against remote politics.** The old Turk says he never learned the name of any mufti or vizier and sends the produce of his garden to Constantinople; labour "keeps us from three great evils—boredom, vice, and want". Set against the exiles and impaled heads Candide's party watch from their window and the strangled viziers, the Turk stands for a bounded, practical life. The narrator's irony still reaches him: he says those who meddle in public affairs "deserve" a miserable end, a cheerful callousness. Martin contrasts him with the six kings, and Pangloss answers with a list of murdered monarchs from scripture and history.

**Who says what about work.** Candide's "we must cultivate our garden" is a brief, undefended remark, not a stated programme. Pangloss absorbs it into his own system (Adam was put in Eden to work the land), and Martin takes it as the "only way to make life bearable". All three read the same line differently. It is reasonable, as interpretation only, to see the satire turning from system-building to modest practice, but the text leaves the garden's meaning open; compare the lens on tending-the-garden in [[the-best-of-all-possible-worlds]], noting that here it is a rural farm of cooks, carpenters and embroiderers, not a technological programme.

**The persistence of the system.** Even in the final lines Pangloss recites that all events are linked, listing Candide's catastrophes as the cause of "preserved citrons and pistachio-nuts", and Candide replies "Excellently observed… but we must cultivate our garden." Candide does not rebut him; he neither accepts nor refutes the chain of events. The book ends in a courteous stalemate between a system that explains everything and a practice that does not try. Cf. [[voltaire]] only as the author of a satire, not as a stated thesis-holder here.

## Notable passages

(c1) The old woman's question about whether misery or idleness is worse, with the three men's boredom and the sight of exiled and impaled officials, sets the problem.

(c2) Martin's verdict on boredom and misery, Candide's silence, and Pangloss's optimism held without belief.

(c3) The dervish's rebuff of the party's metaphysical questions.

(c4) The old Turk's disinterest in the strangled mufti and his three evils kept off by labour.

(c5) Candide's "we must cultivate our garden" and the responses of Pangloss and Martin.

(c6) The household's distribution of tasks, ending in Pangloss's last recitation and Candide's reply.

Resonance with World Machines should stay modest: the chapter is a satire of [[panglossian-optimism]] and a picture of small-scale work, not a theory of institutions or world machines.
