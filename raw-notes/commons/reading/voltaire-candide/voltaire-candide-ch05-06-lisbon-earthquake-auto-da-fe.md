# Tempest, Earthquake and Auto-da-fé: Optimism Meets Disaster and the Inquisition

The unit covers the end of Chapter V and all of Chapter VI. A storm wrecks the ship and the good Anabaptist James drowns while saving the sailor who struck him; Pangloss stops Candide from rescuing him by proving the Lisbon coast was made for that drowning. In Lisbon an earthquake kills thirty thousand; the same sailor loots, Pangloss lectures him on "universal reason", and Candide lies wounded asking for wine and oil while Pangloss insists the cause is demonstrable. A familiar of the Inquisition catches Pangloss in his own system (original sin, free will) and breaks off the conversation. In Chapter VI the Portuguese sages respond to the disaster with an auto-da-fé, on the University of Coimbra's finding that slow burning prevents earthquakes. Pangloss is hanged for speaking his mind, Candide flogged for seeming to approve, and a second earthquake follows. Candide's closing lament is the first moment he turns the doctrine on itself, and an old woman then appears.

## Themes

**Optimism as a system that explains away harm.** Every event is absorbed by Pangloss's reasoning: the drowning was destined ("proving his argument à priori"), the earthquake has a "sufficient reason" and a sulphur train from Lima, and a volcano at Lisbon "could be in no other spot". These are Pangloss's words, voiced in the narrator's ironic register. The target, modestly read, is Leibnizian-style optimism ([[gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz]]); the pattern matches what the vault calls [[panglossian-optimism]] and [[the-best-of-all-possible-worlds]], where explanation substitutes for help. The text does not give a theory of machines.

**Explanation versus care.** Candide asks for wine and oil; Pangloss answers with an argument about probability versus demonstration and only then fetches water. The two characters sit at different registers: one is dying, the other classifying. Candide's own response is practical: they help the wounded the next day.

**Counter-example embodied in the sailor.** The sailor, who let James drown and loots amid the dead, dismisses Pangloss's "universal reason" and says he has "trampled four times upon the crucifix". Pangloss's charge that this behaviour is "untimely" (his word, not the project's sense) shows how the system turns a moral failing into a failure to fit the order of things.

**Institutional absurdity as a second theodicy.** The Inquisition's familiar probes Pangloss's optimism for doctrinal consequences, and the Portuguese answer to a natural disaster is to burn people. The victims are chosen for trivial or arbitrary reasons (marrying a godmother, setting aside bacon, speaking, seeming to approve). The narrator's irony: a ritual with an "infallible" rationale is the Church-state's counterpart to Pangloss's reasoning, and it is dated by a second earthquake the same day. This reads as satire of dogma and persecution, an interpretation, in the spirit of [[enlightenment-monism]]'s opposition between reason and institutions only loosely: Voltaire's target here is superstition, not Pangloss's rationalism alone.

**The first crack.** Candide's "If this is the best of all possible worlds, what are the others like?" is the character's own reaction, not a thesis. It remains a question; he laments Pangloss, the Anabaptist and Cunégonde (whom he believes dead) but does not yet abandon the doctrine.

## Notable passages

- **The drowning and the a priori proof (c1).** Candide, "was preparing to jump in" and is held back by Pangloss; the sailor who caused the death swims ashore. Rescue is replaced by argument, and the good man perishes while the villain survives.
- **The earthquake and the looter (c2).** The scene sets "sufficient reason" against plunder and "universal reason" against a sailor's contempt, with the dead and dying around them.
- **Pangloss at the dinner of mourners (c3).** Comfort offered as necessity: the volcano "could be in no other spot". The consolation is a restatement of the system.
- **The familiar of the Inquisition (c4).** Pangloss tries to square optimism with original sin and free will and is interrupted by port wine, a comic emblem of power ending the argument.
- **The auto-da-fé (c5).** The Coimbra decree and the costumed procession, with Candide's painted flames upside down and Pangloss's upright, make the ritual grotesquely precise while arbitrary.
- **Candide's lament (c6).** The scene after the whipping, where doubt arises through personal suffering and the list of lost loved ones.
