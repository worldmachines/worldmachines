# Neutralizing amour-propre: the Augustinian background to Rousseau's passion

Douglass argues that Rousseau's claim that amour-propre is 'naturally neutral' can only be understood against a strong French Augustinian tradition in which the passion was the vicious root of man's depravity. He sketches that tradition (Augustine, Senault, Pascal, Esprit, La Rochefoucauld, Nicole), then the gradual neutralization of the passion (Malebranche, with Descartes in the background, then Saint-Pierre, Vauvenargues, Duclos), and finally reads Rousseau's amour de soi-même / amour-propre distinction as a move against an Augustinian-Hobbesian picture of man. This is Douglass's own interpretive thesis, offered as context for Rousseau's later attempt to harmonize the two loves through love of fatherland.

## Themes

**Augustine and the problem of the right love.** For Augustine a good will is a good love, and self-love or pride caused Original Sin and the split between the earthly and heavenly cities. Douglass notes that this shaped much seventeenth-century French thought (Port-Royal, Senault, La Rochefoucauld, even Malebranche), and that Rousseau read widely in it in the 1730s and called himself 'half Jansenist'. Around the 1640s, following Stiker-Métral, amour-propre became the pejorative French rendering of amor sui.

**Amour-propre evil only through the Fall.** In Senault and Pascal the passion was natural and righteous in Adam and became disordered only through Original Sin; in fallen man only grace plus reason can direct the passions. The tradition also held that vice imitates virtue (Esprit, La Rochefoucauld, La Bruyère), with Nicole pushing this furthest: amour-propre, enlightened, can reproduce the effects of charity, foreshadowing Mandeville, though a society built on it stays corrupt. Douglass stresses that Nicole combined this Augustinian anthropology with a Hobbesian account of civil society, showing how the two were complementary.

**Neutralization.** Malebranche went further than Nicole: amour-propre is 'neither virtue nor vice', equated with the desire to be happy, and can genuinely accord with love of order and so of God. Douglass adds that Descartes's view that the passions are 'all in their nature good' may have influenced him, and that Malebranche still kept Original Sin while shifting attention from the origin of the disorder to its misuse. By mid-century Vauvenargues and Duclos openly attacked the neo-Augustinians, though the negative associations persisted and Malebranche himself, followed by Barbeyrac's Pufendorf, blamed Hobbes for grounding justice on amour-propre, feeding the Hobbes-Epicureanism association.

**Rousseau's secularized Fall.** Rousseau's distinction had precedents (Abbadie, Dubos, Mandeville's self-love versus self-liking), but Douglass emphasizes that it first appears in a note criticizing Hobbes for making man naturally evil through an inflamed amour-propre. Hobbes is thereby read as sharing the Augustinian account that Rousseau repudiates. Original Sin is recast as entrenched inequality and luxury; if these can be avoided, amour-propre can be turned toward political virtue. In Emile amour-propre is the extension of amour de soi-même once man is social, and 'becomes good or bad only by the application made of it', so the two loves are not by nature in contradiction. Douglass ties the viability of Rousseau's republicanism to this natural goodness.

## Notable passages

The Augustinian premise (c1) frames the whole problem as one of love's object. The Senault and Pascal passage (c2) supplies the key structural idea, that amour-propre is evil only through the Fall, which Rousseau secularizes. Malebranche (c3) is the decisive step to moral neutrality. The passage on the note to the Discours sur l'inégalité (c4) is the hinge of the unit: Hobbes and Augustine converge, and the political stake is whether institutions must counteract a natural vice. The last two passages (c5, c6) give Douglass's reading of the secularized Fall and Rousseau's positive account in Emile.
