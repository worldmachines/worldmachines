# Free Will and Man’s Moral Nature

Douglass turns from the physical side of natural man (needed to show the state of nature was not a state of war) to what Rousseau called the ‘Metaphysical and Moral side’ of man. He reconstructs the natural law background: Pufendorf defined the will as spontaneity plus freedom, explicitly against Hobbes, so as to separate moral from purely physical actions, and Burlamaqui, following Barbeyrac, passed this on with added stress on inward, spiritual consciousness of freedom. He then reads Rousseau's free-agent passage in the Discours sur l’inégalité against that background, weighs the alternative that La Mettrie's l’Homme machine was the target, and traces how free will grounds the inalienability of freedom in part two of the Discours and, later, Du contrat social.

## Themes

**Physical versus moral side of man.** Rousseau's ‘moral side’ is not the same as natural man being amoral: the moral capacities are largely dormant in the most primitive state and take a long time to develop, but they are what eventually lift man above the beasts. Douglass says this distinction would ‘permeate’ Rousseau's thought.

**Free will in the natural law tradition.** For Pufendorf, free will marks off moral (voluntary, imputable) actions from ‘purely Physical operations’; his account was formulated against Hobbes (De homine, Leviathan), and Burlamaqui, following Barbeyrac, took it to be the foundation of all morality. Douglass thinks Burlamaqui's language and spiritual emphasis make him a plausible source for Rousseau.

**Contested significance and possible targets.** Douglass reports the Strauss–Masters view (with Hobbesian readers generally) that Rousseau repudiated free will in favour of perfectibility, notes that O'Hagan goes some way towards refuting it while granting that the rest of the Discours leans on perfectibility, and mentions the suggestion that La Mettrie was in view. His own view is cautious: the wording is closer to Pufendorf and Burlamaqui, Rousseau's definition is ‘neither particularly original nor controversial’ in that context, and whether he meant it against Hobbes ‘would be hard to prove either way’. The tentative conclusion is an anti-Hobbesian natural law setting, not a quarrel with philosophe materialism.

**Freedom as inalienable gift of nature.** Free will elevates man above instinct-driven animals; freedom is renounced when one becomes subject to another's will. Rousseau's part-two claim that freedom cannot be alienated is set against Pufendorf (who allows divesting oneself of freedom by contract) and possibly implicitly Hobbes; Douglass says it returns with full force in Du contrat social. Diderot and Burlamaqui are cited as contemporaries making the same point.

**Two principles versus one.** Hobbes's man is matter in motion, with self-preservation as the single principle of the laws of nature and civil association; Rousseau's man is physical and moral, so civil association rests on two inalienable gifts of nature, life and freedom. A pact that stripped either would be illegitimate. This is Douglass's own synthesis of why Rousseau never abandoned nature.

**Possible resonance with WMP.** The Hobbesian man as a purely mechanical being versus Rousseau's insistence that man is more than a machine touches, only loosely, the machine idiom of [[world-machines]] and the [[modernity-machine]]. Douglass is not a machine theorist and the ‘machine’ here is a materialist theory of the individual, not deep social infrastructure. [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] is the central figure; the appeal to spirit against mechanism sits within what later readers such as [[isaiah-berlin]] treated as the Enlightenment's internal disputes, though Douglass says nothing of that.

## Notable passages

The opening (c1) frames the physical/moral split and heads off the misreading that natural man is amoral. The Pufendorf block quotation (c2) is the tradition's definition of the will, which Douglass says ‘closely foreshadows’ Rousseau. The Rousseau passage (c3) gives the free-agent and ‘Laws of Mechanics explain nothing’ claim. The methodological hedge (c4) is the heart of Douglass's argument and keeps its tentativeness. The inalienability passage (c5) links free will to the illegitimacy of renouncing freedom. The closing paragraph (c6) states the one-principle versus two-principle contrast with Hobbes.
