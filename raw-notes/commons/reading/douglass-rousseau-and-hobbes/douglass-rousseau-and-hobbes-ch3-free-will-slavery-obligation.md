# Free Will, Slavery, and Obligation: Rousseau's Contrat social against Hobbes and his natural-law critics

Douglass opens by contrasting the two problems. Hobbes, having ridiculed free will, could treat a covenant extracted by fear of violent death as just as valid as any other, and could hold that protection implies consent and hence obedience (Douglass notes that Hobbes's implied consent appears in De cive even if Rousseau never read Leviathan). Rousseau, committed to man's free will, could accept only certain contracts as legitimate. Douglass then reads the first chapters of Du contrat social (family, right of the strongest, slavery) as a strategy: Rousseau fuses Grotius with the reputation of Hobbes, and grounds his refutation of force-based right in the distinction between physical and moral power. Douglass shows that this distinction was already a standard criticism of Hobbes within the natural law tradition (Pufendorf, Barbeyrac, Burlamaqui, Diderot's Encyclopédie article 'Citoyen', and Hübner in 1758), so the chapter 'On the Right of the Strongest' adds little to what Hobbes's critics were saying. The second half turns on what Rousseau saw in those same critics: Pufendorf's two pacts and a decree, and Burlamaqui's transfer of sovereignty, leave the people dependent on a superior will, so that they look little better than Hobbes. Rousseau's problem is thereby set: an association in which each 'obeys only himself and remains as free as before'.

## Themes

**Free will as the hinge of Rousseau's response to Hobbes.** Douglass's thesis is that Rousseau's criticisms of Hobbes 'rely on, and would make little sense without, his commitment to dualism and belief in free will'. Free will makes man a moral being, freedom is an inalienable gift of nature, and renouncing it removes all morality from one's actions. Force yields only necessity, not obligation.

**Grotius and Hobbes conflated, by Rousseau's strategy.** Douglass argues (his own reading) that there is little evidence Grotius was a main target of the Discours sur l'inégalité, and that Rousseau's attack on Grotius in Du contrat social was late and tactical. He offers three possible reasons (Grotius as an authority for the Genevan patriciate; Rousseau's earlier praise of Grotius alongside Tacitus and Plutarch; the doctrine of men born for slavery). He finds it implausible that Rousseau really thought Hobbes a proponent of natural inequality, and concludes that Rousseau meant to implicate Grotius with the reputation usually reserved for Hobbes. The state of nature is not a state of war, so the supposed right to enslave in war cannot be derived from nature.

**Compulsion versus obligation: a prior natural-law critique.** Pufendorf set free will against Hobbes and distinguished compulsion (an external force on the will) from obligation (a moral force); 'purely Physical operations' give rise 'not to Obligation, but to Necessity'. Obligation resting on force alone would be fleeting, since once the fear ceases there is no reason left to obey. Douglass is explicit that his point about Hübner is prevalence, not direct influence, and that there is no evidence Rousseau knew Hübner. He also flags that this 'de facto' reading of Hobbes was entrenched by Pufendorf's followers; the reading of Hobbes as offering coercion rather than obligation is what the critics report, not necessarily Hobbes's own view.

**The two-pact model and the second pact as the point of attack.** Pufendorf's sovereignty is generated by a pact of association, a decree by majority vote, and a second pact of submission. For Rousseau, per Douglass, the second pact is decisive, since there the people ceases to be sovereign. Douglass also observes that Pufendorf's theory is not far from Hobbes's De cive account (Sorbière's French translation says the sovereign's will represents that of all others), so Rousseau could have known Hobbes on representation without reading Leviathan. The Genevan patriciate exploited the second pact to collapse two contracts into one of submission, invoking Burlamaqui.

**Hobbes and his critics as equally culpable.** In Rousseau's eyes, according to Douglass, they failed both to propose an association preserving freedom and to admit its possibility, hence the claim that the 'science of political right is yet to be born'.

There is only a faint rhyme with World Machines here. The contract and sovereignty-transfer models are early-modern designs for making a polity legible and unified, and Rousseau's rejection of alienated sovereignty concerns [[jean-jacques-rousseau]]'s own solution, which Douglass treats in later chapters. Douglass is a historian of political thought, not a machine theorist, and the note does not claim more than this.

## Notable passages

**c1: Hobbes has no free-will problem.** Hobbes could count fear-extracted covenants as valid and rest obligation on protection and implied consent, which is the problem Rousseau cannot share.

**c2: Force is physical, not moral.** 'Yielding to force is an act of necessity, not of will.' The passage introduces free will as the basis for the physical/moral distinction and quotes Rousseau on why renouncing freedom is incompatible with the nature of man.

**c3: Douglass's thesis on dualism.** Freedom is inalienable because free will makes man a moral being; Rousseau followed a prominent natural-law criticism of Hobbes in drawing the distinction.

**c4: Pufendorf's compulsion versus obligation.** The most direct precedent for Rousseau's language, casting Hobbes as offering coercion rather than obligation.

**c5: The second pact.** Why Pufendorf's and Burlamaqui's sovereignty looked like Hobbes's to Rousseau, tied to the Genevan controversies of the 1750s.

**c6: The problem restated.** Hobbes's critics alienate freedom too, so Rousseau's task becomes the formula of an association in which each obeys only himself.
