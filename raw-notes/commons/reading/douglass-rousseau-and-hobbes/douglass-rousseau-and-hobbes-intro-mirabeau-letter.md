# Rousseau's Dichotomy: Law Above Man or Man Above Law

Douglass begins from Rousseau's 1767 reply to the Marquis de Mirabeau, who had sent him the Physiocrat Le Mercier de la Rivière's *L'ordre naturel et essentiel des sociétés politiques*. The Physiocrats proposed 'legal despotism': an enlightened absolute monarch enforcing positive laws that conform to the natural order. Rousseau rejected it and framed the problem of politics as finding a form of government that places law above man. He judged that no tolerable middle exists between the 'most austere democracy' and the 'most perfect Hobbism'. Douglass then shows the same problem running through Rousseau's later writings (the *Discours sur l'économie politique*, *Du contrat social*, the Poland *Considérations*), contrasts Hobbes's ridicule of the rule of laws over men, and sets out the book's purpose: to examine Rousseau's engagement with Hobbes and with Hobbism as received in eighteenth-century France and Geneva, two targets Douglass insists were not the same. The chapter previews the structure, three interpretive themes, and a methodological question about what of Hobbes Rousseau actually read.

## Themes

**Legal despotism and the passions.** Douglass reports Rousseau's objection (Rousseau's own claim, which Douglass presents without dispute here): even if it is in a despot's interest to govern legally, his passions will too often lead him against his enlightened interest. The Physiocrats had calculated the despot's true interest but neglected the 'play of the passions', so their system suits 'the people of Utopia' and not 'the children of Adam'. This ties the passions, one of the book's three themes, directly to the design of political order.

**The two extremes.** Rousseau's stark dichotomy: either put man above law by making the sovereign a mortal God (perfect Hobbism), or put law above man under the voice of the general will. The conflict between man and law is what throws a state into continual civil war. Douglass notes this is among Rousseau's most pessimistic reflections, since he regretted that the second form could probably never be found; elsewhere he holds out more hope.

**Law above man as a persistent problem.** Douglass traces the formulation from the 1755 *Discours sur l'économie politique* ('the work of the law') through *Du contrat social* to the Poland project, where Rousseau, 'omitting any mention of Hobbism or civil war', still treats it as the fundamental problem of politics.

**Hobbes against the rule of laws.** Douglass reports that Hobbes in *Leviathan* ridiculed Aristotle's claim that laws, not men, should govern, since behind any law stands the will of one man or an assembly. Douglass adds that Hobbes advised the sovereign to govern by fixed, promulgated laws and held him bound by the law of nature (owed to God alone), but that his account of sovereignty still challenged the possibility of law above man and its link to republican or democratic government. Douglass hedges that Rousseau 'probably never read Leviathan'.

**Hobbes versus Hobbism.** The book's guiding distinction, Douglass's own thesis: Rousseau's target was often Hobbism, a caricature that frequently misrepresented Hobbes. Rousseau sometimes engaged Hobbes directly, sometimes attacked Hobbesian legacies in contemporaries, and sometimes subverted the prevailing understanding of Hobbism to criticise them. Douglass claims some of Rousseau's most important ideas were set out in opposition to Hobbes or in an anti-Hobbesian context.

## Notable passages

The opening account of the Physiocrat proposal supplies the context for the letter. The central passage, on the absence of a tolerable medium, states the dichotomy and the mortal-God alternative. The *économie politique* quotation shows Rousseau's optimistic version of the same problem, that law reconciles subjugation and freedom. The paragraph on republicanism and Hobbes's rejection of the rule of laws sets the two figures against each other and names the book's point of departure. The statement of purpose fixes the Hobbes/Hobbism distinction that governs the whole study.

## Resonances (tentative)

Rousseau's picture of law above man under the general will is one design for making a people politically one, comparable at a distance to the legibility programs of the [[modernity-machine]] and to [[the-legible-commonwealth]]; Douglass is an intellectual historian and does not frame it this way, and Rousseau's stress on the passions cuts against any purely rationalising order. Rousseau himself is discussed at [[jean-jacques-rousseau]]. The Physiocrats' 'natural order' enforced by an enlightened monarch is a thoroughly Enlightenment rationalism of the sort [[counter-enlightenment]] critics targeted, though Rousseau's own place in that lineage is contested.
