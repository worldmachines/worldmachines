# Nicole, Bayle, and the Moral-Political Emphasis of Hobbes's French Reception

Douglass explains how Bayle's article 'Hobbes' in the Dictionnaire historique et critique (1697, revised 1702) became a principal source for eighteenth-century French discussion of Hobbes, and why that discussion turned to politics and morals rather than mechanism and materialism. He traces the shift back through Pierre Nicole, whose Essais de morale adopted a Hobbesian state of war and Hobbesian remedies inside an Augustinian frame, and through Bayle's earlier works (Pensees diverses sur la comete, Commentaire philosophique), where civil laws, not religion, hold society together. The unit ends by noting that this political emphasis was not universal and that Malebranche offers a counterpoint. Rousseau owned the Dictionnaire, and Douglass flags where Rousseau follows or opposes Bayle's reading.

## Themes

**Separation of Hobbes's politics from his metaphysics.** Mid-century French friends of Hobbes cared about his mechanistic science; after Bayle the moral and political parts dominate. Douglass notes this separation was not original to Bayle (French Cartesians such as du Roure and Regis drew on De cive while dropping the materialism), and that the ready availability of the French De cive also mattered. Bayle dismisses Hobbes's metaphysics with a quotation from Descartes.

**Nicole's Augustinian Hobbism.** For Nicole, amour-propre marks man's post-lapsarian state; he accepts Hobbes's state of war as an accurate description of the disposition of men's hearts while denying it is just or reasonable. Regulated, 'enlightened' amour-propre can imitate charity perfectly, fear of death is the first bond of society, and the people, once they have set the constitution, may not lawfully rebel. Douglass's own point is that attributing the founding of society to amour-propre was 'little more than a literal reading' of De cive, since Sorbiere's French rendered Hobbes's self-love as amour-propre.

**Bayle: civil laws, not religion, keep order.** Bayle holds that a society of atheists could subsist much like one of pagans, provided severe, well-executed laws; religion is 'not a brake capable of restraining our passions' and often fuels conflict through intolerance. Douglass presents this as Bayle's Hobbism, possibly mediated by Nicole, and conscious or not. He notes Bayle diverges from Hobbes on the sovereign's control of religion's externals and takes liberty of conscience as fundamental, yet agrees that a separate ecclesiastical power threatens the sovereign and peace.

**Bayle's presentation of Hobbes.** Bayle praises Hobbes as one of the greatest minds of his century, defends him from the charge of atheism (reading him nearly as a fideist, a sceptical interpretation in Douglass's account), places his work in the context of English controversies, and summarizes Leviathan as a work about peace. Douglass hedges that Bayle's 'one person' may have helped seed the later French misreading that Hobbes's sovereign must be a single individual monarch, though whether Bayle understood the sovereign as a moral person cannot be proved. Hobbes's 'man is a wolf to man' actually concerned relations between commonwealths, but the French took it as describing individuals.

**Republics and monarchy.** Bayle reads Hobbes as writing against republican and anti-monarchical theory (from his Thucydides onward), and Douglass says Rousseau followed Bayle in reading Hobbes as writing against the claims of all republics, while most eighteenth-century French commentators neglected this. On civil religion, Rousseau praises Hobbes as the one who saw 'the evil and the remedy' while opposing Bayle, which Douglass calls ironic.

**Reception before the critics.** Around 1700 Hobbes's state of nature was largely accepted as description by French readers; challenges came later with Barbeyrac and Montesquieu.

## Notable passages

The Nicole passage on fear of death, regulated amour-propre and the ban on rebellion (c1) and the summary of his Hobbesian pessimism (c2) show the neo-Augustinian route by which Hobbes's ideas entered French moral thought. Bayle's society-of-atheists passage (c3) is the core of his civil-law argument. The Leviathan summary and Douglass's comment on 'one person' (c4) and the note on Rousseau's civil religion (c5) tie Bayle to Rousseau. The closing passage (c6) states the unit's thesis about the political emphasis of the reception.
