# From the State of Nature to Political Society: Rousseau's Hobbesian Problematic

Douglass uses the Manuscrit de Genève (Book I, Chapter II, later dropped from Du contrat social) as a partial bridge between Rousseau's state-of-nature theory in the Discours sur l'inégalité and the institutional theory of Du contrat social, which barely links the two. The chapter answers Diderot's 1755 Encyclopédie article 'Droit naturel', in which a 'general will of the human race' is offered against a violent, Hobbes-style interlocutor. Douglass argues (siding with Robert Wokler against Masters and Kelly) that the interlocutor is Hobbes rather than Rousseau, that Rousseau found Diderot's refutation inadequate, and that Diderot and Pufendorf, not Hobbes, are the chapter's main targets. Yet in rejecting Diderot, Rousseau restates Hobbes's problem: in a state of independence where passions divide men, private interest and the general good come apart, and the task is to show the independent man his interest in being just. Douglass concludes that this Hobbesian starting point is in tension with the Discours, where a Hobbesian state of war figured as a rich man's trick, and that nowhere in Du contrat social is the Hobbesian problematic repudiated.

## Themes

**Which state of nature? The Manuscrit's starting point is late, not primitive.** Douglass's own thesis, building on his Chapter 2 reading of the Discours: the Manuscrit begins not with man's primitive nature (which Hobbes failed to capture) nor with the golden age, but at the point where man's desires 'finally encompass the whole of nature' and political institutions become necessary. This state of independence is already miserable and closely resembles the condition from which illegitimate social pacts arose in the Discours.

**Rousseau against Diderot: two objections.** Rousseau took Diderot to be asking whether natural right has force before positive law (a question central to Hobbes's natural-law critics in France and Geneva). Douglass notes this was not really Diderot's intention. Rousseau objected that Diderot gave no incentive to obey natural law against one's interest, and wrongly supposed that knowing the general will needs only the simple use of reason. Generalizing ideas is 'one of the most difficult and belated exercises of human understanding'.

**Rousseau restates Hobbes.** The 'independent man' will keep the laws of nature only if assured others will too, and no such assurance exists. Diderot's failure was not in teaching what justice is but in giving the interlocutor a reason to risk his self-preservation. Douglass presents this as a greater awareness of the problems of the state of nature than most contemporaries showed, comparable only to Pierre Bayle.

**Hobbes versus Hobbism.** Douglass corrects the natural-law reception: for Hobbes the laws of nature do operate in the state of nature, as dictates of right reason, but bind only in foro interno where security is lacking. Critics read Hobbes as giving an account of interest without right; Diderot gave right without interest. Rousseau, Douglass says, may not have grasped all these nuances.

**Justice and utility.** The opening problem of Du contrat social, reconciling 'what right permits with what interest prescribes', is thereby framed as a response to the Hobbesian interlocutor. The Manuscrit passage on 'perfected art' repairing the ills art caused to nature is addressed to that violent speaker.

**Coherence problem, hedged.** Douglass grants that accepting the Hobbesian problematic sits awkwardly with the Discours, where the state of war was a trick of the rich on the poor and natural right was to be derived from the golden age. He also reports an argument that Du contrat social's viability requires a non-Hobbesian starting point, and notes an apparent incongruity between the conditions that make institutions necessary and those of a people fit for good laws. He still concludes the project was 'fundamentally Hobbesian'. The Du contrat social evidence is thinner: Book I, Chapter VI's toned-down passage and the loss of 'an unlimited right to everything that tempts him'.

## Notable passages

The c1 passage fixes the Manuscrit's starting point and its distance from the golden age. The c2 and c3 passages carry the core argument: Rousseau's Hobbesian rejoinder to Diderot, and the twin objections that reason alone neither motivates nor is widely accessible. The c4 passage is Douglass's corrective on Hobbes's actual view of natural law. The c5 passage shows Rousseau taking on Hobbes's problem while blaming Hobbes for calling the state natural, and quotes the 'perfected art' programme. The c6 passage states the resulting tension in Rousseau's thought and Douglass's verdict.
