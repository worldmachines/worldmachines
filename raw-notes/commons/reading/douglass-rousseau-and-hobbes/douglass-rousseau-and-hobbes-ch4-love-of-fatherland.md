# Cultivating Love of Fatherland: Ordering the Passions in the Republic

Douglass's claim in this section is that if modern depravity comes from the contradiction between amour de soi-même and amour-propre, then Rousseau's ideal body politic has to bring the two passions into harmony, and that this is done through public education and civic institutions. Emile, which Douglass calls Rousseau's fullest account of the passions, cannot show this, since Emile is educated in a modern state where public education 'can no longer exist'. Emile's domestic education can only delay amour-propre. The positive programme is therefore reconstructed from the Discours sur l'économie politique, the Considérations sur le gouvernement de Pologne and the Projet de constitution pour la Corse. Douglass then defends his reading against Laurence D. Cooper's, and places Rousseau against Montesquieu and other French writers on virtue and love, and against the Augustinian-Hobbesian picture of the passions.

## Themes

**Domestic versus public education (Douglass's reading of Emile).** Rousseau's three educations (nature, men, things) can be aligned only where society does not contradict nature. In modern states one must choose 'between making a man or a citizen'. Emile therefore delays social exposure to restrain amour-propre and cannot overcome the contradiction. Douglass nonetheless treats the theory of the passions there as instructive for republican institutions.

**Passions derive from amour de soi.** Douglass notes Rousseau's inconsistency between 'sentiments' and 'passions' but says it matters little, because amour-propre is an extension of amour de soi-même and all passions derive from the latter. Passions can therefore be corrupted, or can stay in conformity with the order of nature.

**Generalizing amour de soi into love of fatherland.** Public education forms 'patriots by inclination, by passion, by necessity'. Through memory and imagination a child's sense of identity can be attached to the fatherland, so that the citizen loves the republic with the love an isolated man has only for himself. Douglass presents this as denaturing in the same sense as the legislator's task in Du contrat social: a shift from the particular to the general perspective, affecting the object of the passions and not only the will.

**Cultivating (not just delaying) amour-propre.** In republics education activates amour-propre, in contrast with Emile: children learn to live 'under the eyes of their fellow citizens', and public games, festivals, honours and esteem sustain 'patriotic intoxication'. Using the Corsican distinction between orgueil (pride) and vanité (vanity) as the two branches of amour-propre, Douglass shows that pride, the positive branch, can take a general object, the glory of the fatherland. The Spartans under Lycurgus are the paradigm.

**Resolving the contradictions, including private and general will.** With both passions aimed at the same object, the contradiction between amour de soi-même and amour-propre is overcome. Douglass says the contradiction between private and general will is overcome too, since 'virtue is only the conformity of the private will to the general'. A citizen who loved the fatherland as himself would never willingly contravene the general will.

**Nature as normative standard, against Cooper.** Douglass holds that the citizen's life conforms to nature as a normative standard, because amour de soi is preserved by being generalized. He accepts Cooper's view that the main polarity is between living according to nature and living against it. He disputes Cooper's conclusion that 'Nature and citizenship are roads that do not meet', which he says presupposes the permanence of the very contradiction Rousseau sought to obviate.

**Context: Augustine, Hobbes, Montesquieu.** Love-as-virtue was not unique to Rousseau: Montesquieu defined political virtue as love of fatherland and the laws, and Saint-Evremond and d'Aguesseau suggested love of fatherland could be a species of amour-propre. Douglass says none of them gave a comprehensive account of the passions. On his account, Hobbes and the neo-Augustinians presented only the negative branch of amour-propre, while Rousseau showed its positive branch could serve the republic.

## Notable passages

- **Man or citizen (c1).** Rousseau's claim, quoted by Douglass, that in modern states 'one cannot make both at the same time' frames why Emile is a domestic and not a civic education, and why the republican material must come from other texts.
- **Patriots by passion (c2).** The Économie politique passage on the child who 'ought to see the fatherland' and on identifying oneself with the state is the core statement of the generalization of self-love. The 'dangerous disposition from which all our vices arise' is turned into 'a sublime virtue'.
- **Activating amour-propre (c3).** Public esteem, festivals, Lycurgus and the pride/vanity distinction show that Rousseau's republic uses amour-propre and does not suppress it.
- **Overcoming the contradictions (c4).** Douglass's central synthesis: both passions have one object, and the private will conforms to the general will.
- **Nature and citizenship (c5).** Douglass's argument against Cooper about whether civic life is natural.
- **Historical context (c6).** Montesquieu and the Augustinian legacy; the point that only Rousseau reversed the association of amour-propre with vice.

## Possible resonances (hedged)

Douglass is not a machine theorist, so none of this is his. Rousseau's education of citizens to feel 'no longer one but a part of the unity' is a design for making a people one and governable through shared sentiment. It is loosely comparable to the legibility programmes discussed in [[the-legible-commonwealth]], though Rousseau's mechanism is affective and civic, not administrative or measurement-based. The section supplies detail on [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] and on the republican-virtue tradition running back through [[niccolo-machiavelli]]; Douglass himself names Montesquieu and Lycurgus, not Machiavelli, in this unit.
