# Unity and Civil Religion: Rousseau's Hobbesian Problem and His Praise of Hobbes

This closing section of Chapter 3 does two things. First, Douglass summarizes how Rousseau's political thought starts from Hobbesian concerns (factitious passions, mutual assurance, the need to unite right and interest) yet reaches largely opposed conclusions, chiefly by inverting sovereignty so that it stays in the people and preserves freedom as well as life. Second, he turns to the chapter on civil religion in Du contrat social, where Rousseau calls Hobbes the only Christian author who saw both the evil and the remedy. Douglass reads this as the most remarkable reference to Hobbes in Rousseau's works, as evidence that Rousseau had recently revised his opinion of Hobbes (between the Manuscrit de Genève and the Contrat), and as a sign that both thinkers were preoccupied with unity as a political concept. That concern sets up Chapter 4.

## Themes

**Shared problematic, opposed conclusions.** Douglass's own thesis: the ills Rousseau diagnoses from the Discours sur l'inégalité onward resemble those of Hobbes's state of nature (factitious passions set men in contradiction, misery follows, political institutions become necessary). Political philosophy must therefore show men their interest in being just. Rousseau's acceptance of this Hobbesian problematic is clearest in the Manuscrit de Genève, and he remains committed in Du contrat social to uniting right and interest so that justice and utility do not diverge. Douglass hedges: this is 'less apparent' in the Contrat.

**Freedom, not just life; law above man.** Where Hobbes was primarily concerned with self-preservation, Rousseau insists that freedom must also be preserved. This requires inverting Hobbesian sovereignty: it always resides in the people and cannot be alienated to, or represented by, a superior will. Douglass adds that this undercuts the main distinctions between Hobbes and his natural law critics, all of whom, by Rousseau's standards, required a renunciation of freedom. Freedom survives only if law is placed above man, and citizens are dependent only on the law as the articulation of their general will. Any opposition between private and general will offends nature as a normative standard and also threatens the stability of the body politic.

**Absolute in power, limited in form.** Both thinkers hold that it would be self-defeating for individuals to hold rights against the sovereign. Rousseau faces a sharper tension, since legitimate conventions must accord with nature as a normative standard while citizens cannot appeal to natural law against the sovereign. His resolution (Douglass's reading): the sovereign is properly so called only when legitimately constituted, i.e. when sovereignty remains in the people and pertains only to general laws. It is 'absolute in power but limited in the form of its actions'.

**Civil religion and the audacity of citing Hobbes.** Douglass sets aside the scholarly debate over whether the civil-religion chapter shows totalitarianism (noting, with the critics of that reading, that it is no less tolerant than Locke's Epistola de tolerantia and was criticized in Geneva as too tolerant) and focuses on the neglected invocation of Hobbes. Hobbes was widely decried as an atheist and endorsement was rare; the label Hobbism could condemn an author, as in Archbishop Beaumont's 1758 condemnation of Helvétius's De l'esprit. Rousseau, who followed that controversy and withheld his own critique, nonetheless praises Hobbes, and insists Hobbes should have concluded that Christianity, being a religion of man and not of the citizen, is incompatible with love of fatherland. This keeps apart Hobbes's actual views from the Hobbism caricature.

**Rousseau's changing view of Hobbes.** Earlier, in the letter to Voltaire, Rousseau called him 'the Sophist Hobbes', and an early draft of the civil-religion chapter criticized Hobbes's intolerance (deleted before the final version). Douglass, following some scholars, infers Hobbes grew on Rousseau in the 1750s, perhaps through re-reading, and dates the change between the Manuscrit de Genève and the Contrat, while noting the affinities predate it.

**Bayle and the 'two Sovereigns'.** In the Manuscrit de Genève Rousseau echoes Hobbes and Bayle: religion breeds fanaticism and conflict, and a Church independent of the civil person yields 'two powers, two Sovereigns', which threatens ruin. Yet Rousseau rejects Bayle's claim that a society of atheists could live peacefully, deeming religion essential for morals and virtue. Douglass notes the irony that the chapter gives the most sympathetic reading of Hobbes's religion since Bayle's Dictionnaire article and then criticizes Bayle.

**Negative versus positive unity.** For Hobbes, reuniting the two heads of the eagle is primarily negative: remove ecclesiastical challenges that excite sedition. For Rousseau the civil profession of faith (sentiments of sociability) serves to cultivate citizenship, so that citizens love laws, duties and fatherland. Rousseau ties this to the legislator and to ordering the passions toward love of fatherland through public mores, topics developed in his other political works.

## Notable passages

The passage on the Hobbesian problematic (c1) states the structural affinity in Douglass's own voice. The account of law above man and the general will (c2) and of sovereignty absolute in power but limited in form (c3) give the conclusions on which Rousseau departs from Hobbes and from natural law theorists. The Rousseau quotation praising Hobbes (c4) is the pivot, and the discussion of its context, the reception of Hobbism and Rousseau's shifting opinion (c5), is what makes it remarkable. The last passage (c6) marks the contrast between Hobbes's negative aim and Rousseau's positive, passion-shaping aim.
