# Rousseau’s Critique of Hobbes, Reappraised

This closing section of Douglass’s chapter on the Discours sur l’inégalité returns to two worries raised at the chapter’s start: that Rousseau’s critique missed Hobbes because the state of nature served different purposes in their theories, and that Rousseau’s own use of a Hobbesian moment undermines the critique. Douglass answers the second quickly (the Hobbesian moment shows why societies justified against a state of conflict are illegitimate) and spends most of the section on the first. His thesis is that the critique does not simply miss the mark, for two interrelated reasons: the state of nature is the standard against which sovereignty is justified, so a false picture of natural man matters; and Hobbes’s natural man is historically contingent, which threatens Hobbes’s own claim to a science of civil philosophy. He then adds free will as the second axis of the critique and situates Rousseau relative to the French and Genevan reception of Hobbes.

## Themes

**The Hobbesian moment and the shared diagnosis (Douglass’s own claim).** Both Hobbes and Rousseau see conflict as the product of passions tied to inflamed amour-propre. The difference is causal: for Hobbes the passions lead men living in equality without civil law into conflict; for Rousseau they result from the transformed social relations produced by the development of inequality.

**Pettit’s defence of Hobbes, reported and partly resisted.** Douglass reports a consensus that Rousseau ‘completely missed the point’ (one commentator: ‘apples and oranges’). Philip Pettit’s reading, which Douglass calls the most important defence and one that looks very Rousseauian, has Hobbes see humans as originally moved by amour de soi until the invention of language replaces it with amour propre, so that Hobbes and Rousseau describe different states of nature. Douglass grants that this is more nuanced than the eighteenth-century commentaries Rousseau knew (and that a reader of De cive alone would not have seen the role of speech), but argues that it does not deflect the core criticism: Hobbes says little about how speech arose, and for Rousseau speech need not lead to amour-propre displacing amour de soi.

**Amour-propre and pity as the crux.** Rousseau accepts that Hobbes’s account of the passions fits men in civil society but denies they are natural or an inevitable result of sociability. Hobbes has no historical story of how the passions interact as society develops; in his state of nature glory is so rife it stifles every counter-passion. For Rousseau, early settled communities had pity stronger than amour-propre, so that pity assuages it and the result would have been a golden age rather than war.

**Negative versus positive standard for legitimacy.** Hobbes’s stark war/peace dichotomy makes the state of nature a negative standard justifying undivided sovereignty. Rousseau’s reading (which Douglass reports as Rousseau’s view) is that this is a trick of the rich and powerful against the weak and poor, and that depicting man at his worst can make even despotism look legitimate. The golden age supplies a positive standard: ‘Where Hobbes had lowered the bar, Rousseau sought to raise it again.’

**Historical contingency and Hobbes’s claim to science.** Hobbes studied men but not man. Since Hobbes wanted civil philosophy to be a science modelled on geometry and grounded in general principles of human nature, showing that his natural man is a product of present society undermines that claim. Douglass notes the qualification that the critique does not touch Hobbes if the state of nature is read solely as depicting the misery without a sovereign, but he calls that reading incomplete.

**Free will and the moral/physical distinction.** Rousseau’s free will underpins his distinction between the moral and the physical, so that force cannot legitimately impinge on a moral capacity. Douglass says this does not refute Hobbes but shows how much both thought was at stake; Hobbes, against Bishop Bramhall, held that moral determination is ‘a mere word’. For Rousseau, obligation and sovereignty are legitimate only if they leave free will uncompromised.

**Place in the French reception.** Rousseau’s remarks on free will resemble Pufendorf’s (echoed by Burlamaqui and Diderot), and his refutation of Hobbes’s natural passions had been rehearsed by Barbeyrac and Montesquieu, but only Rousseau comprehensively historicized them to refute Hobbes. Douglass rejects Richard Tuck’s reading of Rousseau as siding with Hobbes against the natural law theorists. Rousseau collapses the accepted Hobbes/Pufendorf bifurcation, tying both the sociability theorists and the doux commerce theorists to a Hobbesian vision of natural man, which both undermines and exposes the hypocrisy of their theories.

## Notable passages

The amour-propre and pity passage (c1) states the substantive disagreement: Rousseau grants Hobbes’s psychology for civil man but denies it is natural, and dates its decline from a golden age. The negative/positive standard passage (c2) is Douglass’s main reason the critique bites, tying natural goodness to legitimacy. The historical-contingency passage (c3, with Rousseau’s quoted remark about philosophizing ‘according to what happens around them’) and its follow-up (c4) turn the critique against Hobbes’s scientific ambitions. The free-will passage (c5) introduces the moral/physical divide that later chapters on sovereignty rely on. The last passage (c6) places Rousseau between Hobbes and Pufendorf.
