# Chapter 3 opening: Sovereignty and Law, the problem of placing law above man

This is the introduction to Douglass's chapter on [[jean-jacques-rousseau]]'s Du contrat social. It opens with Rousseau's own epigraph, comparing putting law over man to squaring the circle. Douglass then states his thesis for the chapter. Rousseau tried to combine modern theories of sovereignty (Bodin, Hobbes, Pufendorf) with the classical republican demand that law rule over men, while avoiding the objections he had raised in the Discours sur l’inégalité. Two problems carry over from that earlier work: freedom, as a gift of nature, must not be alienated, and nature must serve as a normative standard that restores man's unity. Douglass sets out three strands of the Hobbes relationship that the chapter will interweave, and he admits the analysis is deliberately selective.

## Themes

**Two inherited problems.** Free will makes man a moral being, so a legitimate order cannot make him dependent on another's will. Nature, as a normative standard, requires that man's duties and inclinations no longer be in contradiction. Both are Douglass's reading of what the Discours left Rousseau to solve.

**Continuity against a contested scholarly background.** Douglass says Rousseau exaggerated in claiming that everything bold in Du contrat social was already in the Discours, but that there is some truth in it. He reports Arthur Melzer as claiming that the theme of unity is absent from Du contrat social, which Melzer reads as argued from Hobbesian self-preservation, and he says free will is understated even in the best accounts of Rousseau's freedom. Douglass implicitly disagrees with both.

**Strand one: Hobbesian problems.** Douglass says Rousseau accepted that a legitimate order must answer the problems of Hobbes's state of nature theory. Like Hobbes, he held that rights against the sovereign are self-defeating and that the sovereign will must be absolute and incontestable. Both cared about unity in the body politic, and Rousseau's civil religion chapter names Hobbes as the only thinker to have grasped both problem and remedy. The Manuscrit de Genève chapter on the general society of the human race is the bridge text.

**Strand two: natural law and free will.** Du contrat social was read as a natural law work, and contemporaries compared it to Grotius and Pufendorf. Pufendorf had criticized Hobbes by stressing free will as what distinguishes obligation from coercion, and Rousseau adopted this criticism. He also thought Hobbes's natural law critics were just as inimical to freedom, and he conflated Grotius with Hobbes. Douglass's claim is that Rousseau inverted his predecessors' theories by holding that sovereignty can never legitimately be alienated from the people, and that this rests on his account of the will as moral rather than physical force.

**Strand three: nature and the charge of Hobbism.** Eighteenth-century French thought took Hobbes to have reduced justice to a superior's will and to have abandoned natural law. Douglass reports that some Straussian commentators repeat this reading against Rousseau. He rejects it and adds that it also misconstrues Hobbes. For Douglass, Rousseau's conventions are legitimate only if they stay in accordance with nature as a regulative standard. This needs the reconciliation of private will and general will, which Douglass says lay beyond the scope of Du contrat social, though the legislator's role in denaturing citizens partly indicates the route.

**Scope and method.** Du contrat social was extracted from the unfinished Institutions politiques. Douglass therefore does not offer a full reading. He treats the problems that best show the Hobbes relationship and defers the cultivation of the passions to the next chapter.

## Notable passages

The framing paragraph (c1) gives the chapter's problem: law above man, alienated freedom, and nature as a norm. The first strand (c2) carries the argument that Rousseau and Hobbes share the problems of absolute sovereignty and unity, ending with Rousseau's praise of Hobbes on civil religion. The second strand (c3) gives the thesis of an inversion of sovereignty resting on free will. The third strand (c4) separates Rousseau from the Hobbism caricature, and Douglass says explicitly that the caricature misreads Hobbes as well. The closing paragraph (c5) states the scope and how the chapter connects to the next.

A loose resonance with World Machines: the concern with making a people one, through sovereignty, general will and civil religion, is a design for political unity of the kind the project discusses under legibility. Douglass does not frame it that way, and this unit does not develop the link.
