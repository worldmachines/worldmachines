# Free Will and Virtue: Will-Formation Without Loss of Freedom

Douglass opens by noting a worry: if Rousseau's ideal republic orders amour de soi-même and amour-propre toward love of fatherland and aligns private with general wills, its public education might still seem to violate the inalienable gifts of nature (life and freedom) that Rousseau also insisted upon. He proposes to answer the charge first for the domestic education of Emile, where it is most often pressed, and then to show it applies no more to public education. His thesis is that Rousseau's conception of free will is thin enough (conscious choice among inclinations, without dependence on another's will) to be compatible with heavy socially determined will-formation, and that this also dissolves the alleged duty-versus-inclination conflict in the virtue of a well-ordered republic.

## Themes

**The charge that Emile's freedom is illusory.** Douglass reports critics who say Emile 'feels free but really is not', and that Rousseau settled for the appearance of freedom. The most forceful text is the governor's instruction that there is 'no subjection so perfect as that which keeps the appearance of freedom'. Douglass concedes that, read in isolation, this supports the critics, but argues that in context it describes the 'opposite route' to conventional education, and that the critics misunderstand Rousseau's conception of free will.

**Education as will-formation; freedom as equating power and will.** Douglass's own reading: nobody is born with a developed adult will, so education is inevitably will-formation, and the question is how to do it compatibly with freedom. Rousseau's answer is that unhappiness lies in the disproportion between desires and faculties, and the 'truly free man wants only what he can do and does what he pleases'. Of the two routes (the governor opposing and correcting Emile's will, or arranging conditions so it does not err), only the second is compatible with freedom.

**Freedom as non-opposition to other men's wills.** The crux: for Rousseau dependence on men is the only dependence incompatible with freedom, and it is the opposition between human wills that restricts freedom. Emile stays free, on Douglass's argument, because his will never opposes any other man's and is bound only by necessity. Even if he realized the governor planned his choices, he would remain free to choose either way. The sentiment of freedom consists in being able to choose without depending on another's will, not in determining the circumstances of choice. Douglass concedes the critics' observations (arranged freedom, the parallel with Jean-Jacques in the Dialogues) are 'accurate up to a point'.

**Extension to the citizen and the legislator.** Public education likewise requires forming the will, since making laws beloved requires an authority that reaches the inner man. The legislator plays the governor's role. Douglass argues the citizen is free in the same way as Emile: his actions are consciously willed and, to the extent that he wills generally, his will is never in contradiction with anyone's and he depends on no will but his own.

**Against proto-Kantian readings of autonomy.** Although Rousseau defined moral freedom as 'obedience to the law one has prescribed for oneself', Douglass insists this is not strong self-legislation. Free will means consciously resisting, acquiescing in, or choosing among inclinations, which are themselves shaped by passions. He disputes Patrick Riley's claim that Rousseau faces a problem in trying to keep will as a source of right while socialization lessens autonomy; that problem arises only for a Kantian notion of autonomy, which Douglass says was Kant's, not Rousseau's.

**Two conceptions of virtue.** Douglass takes issue with Timothy O'Hagan's Kantian tension between a naturalistic and a deontological pole (virtue as 'a state of war') and with the older idea, also found in Montesquieu, of civic virtue as self-renunciation. He grants textual support (virtue as strength, Kantian duty language in late autobiographical works) but argues those passages describe life under the contradictions of corrupt society. His own reading is that Rousseau uses two conceptions, duty over inclination and virtue as love of fatherland, and that only the latter governs the well-ordered republic, where duty is also inclination and the realm of virtue ceases to be a state of war. He hedges: it is 'quite plausible' to read Rousseau this way.

**Resulting picture.** Virtue requires the right use of freedom (God gave man free will and so 'the right to virtue'), but in a well-ordered republic willing the general will is not hard, and mastery is in following one's will, not forming it. Institutions, more than individual citizens, master the will, which Douglass calls a plain fact of social relations. The citizens' life is then in accordance with nature, with life and freedom preserved.

## Notable passages

**The governor's maxim (c1).** The 'appearance of freedom' passage from Emile, which critics cite as proof of a trap. Douglass treats it as the decisive text to be re-read in context rather than dismissed.

**Emile's freedom despite orchestration (c2).** The core argument that freedom is defined by the absence of opposition between wills, not by authorship of one's circumstances.

**Moral freedom and socially determined will (c3).** The Du contrat social formula is read as compatible with a high degree of will-formation through the passions. Douglass positions this against self-mastery readings.

**Two conceptions of virtue (c4).** Duty winning over inclination belongs to corrupt societies; in a well-ordered republic virtue is love of fatherland, the citizen's dominant passion, and the private will coincides with the general will.

**Mastery of will lies in the institutions (c5).** The conclusion that citizens freely acquiesce in virtue, moved by both duty and inclination, and that nature as a normative standard is honored.

## Connections

This unit is about [[jean-jacques-rousseau]]'s account of education and citizenship, and it does not mention Hobbes directly. There is a loose rhyme with the WMP interest in [[Legibility]] and the making of a unified people: the legislator shaping citizens' wills so that private and general will coincide is a comparable design for making a people one, and hostile readers such as [[isaiah-berlin]] saw exactly such projects as threats to liberty, though Douglass argues the freedom at stake is not compromised in Rousseau's own terms. The fit is partial: Douglass is doing intellectual history and defending a reading of Rousseau, not offering machine theory, and the text does not itself make the Berlin connection. The reference to Montesquieu's virtue as self-renunciation is plain-text here.
