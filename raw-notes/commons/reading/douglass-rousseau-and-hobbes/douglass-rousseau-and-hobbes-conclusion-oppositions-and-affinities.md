# Rousseau and Hobbes: Shared Problems, Opposed Solutions

This is the concluding review of Douglass's book, and it states his thesis in compressed form. He starts from the charge that Rousseau is a "closet Hobbesian" and answers it by separating *problems* from *solutions*. Rousseau and Hobbes largely agreed on what an adequate political theory must address: the disorder of the passions, the need for a unified political will, and the subordination of religion to the state. Rousseau's answers were mostly set against Hobbes. He treated sovereignty as inalienable and legislative, and he rested legitimacy on freedom as a moral power. He also relied on a normative conception of nature and on the principle of natural goodness. Douglass says these differences are best explained by their competing metaphysics of free will.

## Themes

**Shared rejection of sociability does not make Rousseau a Hobbesian.** Douglass (his own thesis) says that denying natural sociability was a way of distancing oneself from the natural law theorists, but Rousseau grounded natural right in pity prior to reason and amour-propre, not in Hobbes's principles. Disagreeing with Hobbes's critics is not the same as agreeing with Hobbes. Douglass also concedes that Rousseau said "Hobbes saw very clearly the defect of all modern definitions of Natural right".

**Affinity lies in the diagnosis of politics.** Both thinkers held that politics stems from the disorder of the passions. Neither thought abstract principles of right known by reason alone could fix this. Both wanted institutions that educate and re-orientate the passions to secure the unity of the body politic. Both wanted a unified political will, and both wanted religion to serve civil ends (the "two heads of the eagle" reunified). Douglass says Rousseau "followed and even extended" Hobbes on the unified will.

**Opposition lies in the resolution, above all sovereignty.** Rousseau's sovereignty sounds Hobbesian but redefines what counts as a sovereign act. This limits the sovereign to legislation and follows a more Lockean division of legislative and executive powers. Hobbesian justifications rest on force, a merely physical power, and require alienating freedom, a moral power. Rousseau held that the people can only be represented by all the citizens freely assembled, and that any Hobbes-style representative sovereignty involves an illegitimate alienation.

**Free will explains the divergence.** Hobbes made self-preservation the principle of natural right, and Rousseau made freedom equally fundamental. The moral/physical distinction depends on free will and is unavailable to a materialist metaphysics. Douglass presents his reading as parallel to recent Hobbes scholarship that ties Hobbes's politics to his materialism. He reads Rousseau's freedom as the consciousness of acquiescing in, resisting, or choosing between one's inclinations, not as Kantian self-legislation or reason mastering the passions. He concedes this is less robust than a Kantian conception and compatible with heavily socially determined will-formation, but says it is the only kind Rousseau thought was available. The legislator, republican institutions, and education can therefore cultivate the passions without destroying freedom. Douglass also rejects reading Rousseau as a Stoic, on the Augustinian ground that destroying the passions would "reform the work of God".

**Nature has normative force, not only descriptive.** Douglass argues against the charge that Rousseau adopted a purely scientific Hobbesian notion of nature. Rousseau took nature to be a regulative standard, in a Ciceronian mode like Burlamaqui's, and set it against the conventionalism and "non-purposeful" nature attributed to Hobbes and Pufendorf. Douglass says Rousseau assembled a notion of order from several sources: the classical republican tradition, neo-Augustinian post-lapsarian disorder of the passions (secularized in the second Discours), and the order and theodicy of Malebranche and Leibniz. Here Douglass treats Hobbes as read through the eighteenth-century French "Hobbism" view, not only Hobbes himself.

**Natural goodness is the deepest difference.** In the Discours sur l'inégalité the principle of natural goodness opposes Hobbes directly. It claims that modern misery is contingent on particular social relations and is not the natural state, and that it does not follow from all social institutions. Rousseau saw Hobbes, Pufendorf, and the doux commerce theorists as sharing a post-lapsarian picture of human nature. He proposed instead a republican politics for the naturally good, which cultivates pre-lapsarian passions before corruption. Douglass hedges: Rousseau sometimes indicated that institutions are needed only once such passions have developed, and natural goodness also limits how far the republican vision applies.

**Possible World Machines resonances (loose).** Douglass is not a machine theorist. The shared Hobbes-Rousseau project of making a people one, through educated passions, a unified will, and a civil religion, loosely rhymes with the legibility-building designs discussed under [[modernity-machine]] and [[Legibility]], though both are far from Scott-style administrative legibility. Rousseau's natural-goodness thesis and its critique of corrupting social forms belong with [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] and the [[kulturkritik]] tradition, though Douglass does not make that link. His Rousseau also differs from the [[noble-savage]] trope, since Douglass ties natural goodness to a normative account of nature and a specific republican politics.

## Notable passages

**The problem-level agreement (c2).** This is the core of the affinity claim: Rousseau's Manuscrit de Genève line that needs bring us together as passions divide us, the shared view that politics must appeal to the passions, the unified will, and the civil subordination of religion. Douglass sums it up: Hobbes and Rousseau "largely agreed on the problems confronting political societies".

**Sovereignty and moral versus physical power (c3).** This passage explains why Rousseau's sovereignty differs from Hobbes's: force versus freedom, alienation versus preservation, representation versus assembly.

**Free will as the root of the opposition (c4, c5).** The first passage links the metaphysical difference to the political one. The second sets out Douglass's non-Kantian account of freedom and its fit with education and the cultivation of the passions, and openly grants that this is a weaker notion of autonomy.

**Natural goodness (c6).** Douglass identifies natural goodness as the source of the most important difference from Hobbes and draws its consequences and limits for Rousseau's republican politics.

**The opening reply to the closet-Hobbesian charge (c1).** Douglass's argument from the shared denial of sociability, which sets up the distinction between agreeing with Hobbes and disagreeing with his critics.
