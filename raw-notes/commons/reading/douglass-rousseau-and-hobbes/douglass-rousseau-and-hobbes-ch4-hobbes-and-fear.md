# Hobbes on Fear and the Ordering of the Passions

This closing section of a chapter on Rousseau's theory of the passions turns to Hobbes to show why the two can profitably be read side by side. Douglass concedes that there is little evidence Rousseau built his account of amour de soi-même and amour-propre with Hobbes in view beyond the initial opposition, but he gives two reasons for the comparison: both thought the passions had to be ordered in political society to sustain political unity, and both held a similar view of how reason relates to the passions. He argues (against Bernard Gert) that Hobbes's reason does not supply its own ends, so that Hobbes's position is closer to Hume's than Gert allows, and then shows that fear is the passion Hobbes most needs to orient towards its proper object: the state of nature and violent death, not the 'Power of Spirits Invisible'. He also reads the law of nature against pride as a case where the commonwealth is meant to transform the passions, and concludes that Hobbes ordered fear where Rousseau ordered love.

## Themes

**Reason and the passions are not in combat.** Douglass's own thesis, argued against Gert, is that Hobbes's right of self-preservation is deduced by reason from an account of the passions, not given by reason alone; reason reckons consequences, while desire, fear and hope, 'the same in all men', cannot be eradicated, only directed. Douglass then reads Hume's 'slave of the passions' line as directed against those who 'talk of the combat of passion and of reason', so that Hume, Hobbes and Rousseau all deny a strict conflict between reason and passion. Douglass's hedge: this holds 'at least to the extent' that they deny conflict, and he notes that Hobbes sometimes used language of a state of nature as 'the empire of the passions'. (Hume appears here only as a comparison; a link to [[david-hume]] fits, though Douglass is not doing Hume scholarship.)

**Fear as the passion to be reckoned upon.** The laws of nature are dictates of reason guiding men's passions towards peace, but fear of death is the most prominent of the passions that incline men to it. Whether sovereignty arises by institution or acquisition, men covenant out of fear. Douglass presents this as Hobbes's project: to show that covenants made from fear are legitimate and that perpetual fear of the state of nature should lead all to consent to the sovereign, which requires shaping men's imaginations.

**Redirecting fear away from invisible spirits.** Douglass reads the last two books of Leviathan as an effort to dispel religious imagery, since 'the fear of Darknesse, and Ghosts, is greater than other fears' and produces sedition. Hobbes's reinterpretation of Hell reduces eternal torment to a second death, so that the fear of suffering in this life outweighs that in the next; Bishop Bramhall is quoted as remarking that Hobbes left 'no devils to be feared, but devils incarnate'. Douglass reports Richard Tuck's claim that Hobbes's eschatology was meant to relieve men of fear, calls it informative, but doubts Hobbes thought fear could ever be completely overcome: the aim was to re-orientate it towards the state of nature.

**Pride, equality and the transformation of the psyche.** Pride is glory disliked, one of the three causes of war, and the law of nature demanding acknowledgement of equality is set against it; the moral virtues (justice, gratitude, modesty, equity, mercy) are commanded where the vices lead to war. Douglass reports commentators who see Hobbes as proposing a transformation of the human psyche, accepts that education (Leviathan taught in the universities) is the means, yet insists that this remains an appeal to the passions through imagination, above all fear, rather than a purely epistemic correction.

**Hobbes and Rousseau compared.** For both, political institutions are needed because of the disorder of the passions, and unity depends on ordering them. Hobbes does not ask men to stop thinking of themselves as individuals (as Rousseau does) but to see their preservation as bound up with the commonwealth's; the difference Douglass draws is fear for Hobbes, love for Rousseau. The passage also distinguishes Hobbes's own view from a caricature only implicitly, by insisting Hobbes's politics rested on educating passions rather than on reason alone.

## Notable passages

**Reason as reckoning; passions as the source of ends (c1).** Douglass's central argument against Gert: reason deduces the right of self-preservation from an account of man, so the end comes from the passions, and the passions cannot be overcome but only ordered towards peace.

**Fear and the generation of the commonwealth (c2).** Shows that both institution and acquisition run on fear of death, and that Hobbes's task is 'ordering fear towards its rightful object' by affecting the imagination.

**Killing the devils, keeping the fear (c3).** The account of Hobbes's attack on superstitious fear and Bramhall's verdict, which grounds the claim that fear is redirected rather than abolished.

**Pride and equality (c4).** Pride as glory disliked, a cause of war, answered by the law of natural equality; the sovereign as 'King of all the children of pride'.

**Education and fear (c5).** Douglass's answer to the 'transformation of the human psyche' reading: education works by shaping the imagination, and fear remains the passion that 'enclineth men least to break the Lawes'.

**Fear for Hobbes, love for Rousseau (c6).** The concluding comparison and the key contrast that sets up Rousseau's ordering of love in the chapters that follow.
