# Law, Nature, and Denaturing: Nature as Normative Standard in the Social Contract

Douglass closes Chapter 3 by defending a thesis of his own: that nature stays a normative standard in Du contrat social even though Rousseau rarely names it there, and that this places Rousseau against the Hobbes of the eighteenth-century French imagination. He first sets the debate. David Lay Williams reads the general will as bound by a transcendent, indeterminate neo-Platonic justice, while Straussians (and others not necessarily Straussian) read Rousseau as following Hobbes in reducing justice to artifice and convention. Douglass then reconstructs the French and Genevan background of the charge of Hobbism (Malebranche, Leibniz against Pufendorf, Barbeyrac, Vattel, Burlamaqui, Cicero as the ancient authority) and argues that Rousseau's remark that 'law comes before justice' must be read on the Ciceronian pattern. He next examines the 'On Law' passage, where justice comes from God but needs reciprocity and conventions, and notes that Hobbes could have endorsed the part about the lack of a natural sanction. Finally he takes up the hardest challenge, the legislator and the language of denaturing, and argues that these are consistent with nature and with freedom.

## Themes

**Nature as a regulative standard, contested by commentators.** Douglass reports Williams's thesis (Rousseau answers Hobbesian positivism with a morally grounded contract) and Strauss's opposing claim (Rousseau abandons any attempt to find the basis of right in human nature). He sides with the view that nature is a normative constraint, resting it on Rousseau's own claim that the equality of right and the conception of justice are derived 'from the nature of man', and on the commitment to inalienable gifts of nature (life and freedom).

**The Hobbism charge and the nature-of-things dichotomy.** The accusation of 'Hobbesian positivism' was tied to divine voluntarism: justice as emanating from the will of a superior, God or sovereign, rather than from eternal rules of equality and proportion. Malebranche used the same arguments against both; Leibniz turned the charge on Pufendorf; Barbeyrac conceded a near-dichotomy; Vattel and Burlamaqui sided with the rationalist position. Douglass calls the resulting opposition 'somewhat crude' and later calls the Hobbes it targets 'largely a straw Hobbes, although one widely accepted at the time', keeping the distinction between Hobbes and Hobbism.

**Cicero as the key to 'law before justice'.** Douglass (his own interpretation) argues that Rousseau, like Cicero in De legibus, makes justice secondary to law while requiring that the law accord with the nature of things and of man. Legitimacy is not just positive law: the Discours sur l'inégalité shows that not all laws or contracts are legitimate, only those preserving inalienable gifts, and only good laws directed by the general will are obeyed.

**Convention is required but constrained.** Society cannot rest on nature descriptively (the state of nature was asocial), so it must rest on conventions, yet on conventions in accordance with nature. Rousseau's 'want of a natural sanction' line parallels Hobbes's De cive claim that the natural laws are not enough to preserve peace; both thinkers see that obligation must be supplemented by interest, artifice and sanction, without abandoning natural law as a moral standard. This is Douglass's most explicit rapprochement of the two.

**Denaturing as consistent with nature.** The legislator passage and the Emile line on institutions that 'denature man' are taken at face value. Douglass's resolution: man is by nature perfectible, so being transformed accords with nature; what is lost, independent solitary existence, was descriptive, not constitutive of his God-given nature; life and freedom survive. The legislator has no legislative right, only drafting and persuasion 'without convincing'; the people keep freedom by assenting or withholding assent, which parallels free will as consciousness of acquiescing in or resisting the voice of nature. Douglass defers fuller treatment to Chapter 4.

**Resonance with World Machines (hedged).** The legislator who makes a scattered people into a part of a larger moral whole is comparable to designs for making a people one, and to the legibility ambitions of the [[modernity-machine]] and [[the-legible-commonwealth]] (see also [[jean-jacques-rousseau]]); Douglass, however, frames it strictly as a question of natural law and freedom, not machinery, and the fit is loose. The reception of Rousseau by critics of the Enlightenment is a separate matter ([[counter-enlightenment]]) not argued here.

## Notable passages

The Williams/Strauss dispute sets the scholarly frame (c1). The Leibniz-to-Genevan genealogy of the Hobbism charge explains why Rousseau's contemporaries would have read 'law before justice' as suspect (c2). Douglass's Cicero-inspired reading of that phrase, backed by 'never do good laws change the nature of things', is the core interpretive move (c3). The reading of the 'On Law' passage and the Hobbes parallel show that the two thinkers share the problem of insufficient natural obligation (c4). The treatment of denaturing and the legislator turns a notorious contradiction into a claim about perfectibility and inalienable gifts (c5), and the closing passage ties law as 'celestial voice' to the unity of man and citizen, ending on Rousseau's own claim that he only reasserted the rights of nature (c6).
