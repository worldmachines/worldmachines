# Introduction: Rousseau against Hobbes and Hobbism, and the scholarship on it

This is the second section of Douglass's introduction. It does two jobs. First it surveys mid-twentieth-century and later scholarship on the Hobbes-Rousseau relationship and finds it predominantly Hobbesian in tenor and often ahistorical. Second it states Douglass's own thesis and chapter plan. The thesis is that Rousseau's critique of Hobbes, above all in the Discours sur l'inégalité, was polemical and was aimed as much at Hobbes's critics in the modern natural law tradition and at doux commerce theorists as at Hobbes himself. Douglass says he wants to avoid the opposite error of making Rousseau unequivocally anti-Hobbesian.

## Themes

**The Hobbesian reading of Rousseau (scholars Douglass reports and disputes).** Strauss holds that Rousseau was greatly indebted to Hobbes and deviated only by grasping the implications of Hobbes's premises. Derathé argues for Hobbes's influence, notably on Rousseau's psychology. Davy holds that Hobbes laid the foundations of Du contrat social, and Jouvenel calls Rousseau's theory 'Hobbism turned inside out'. Douglass's gloss is that this literature tried to show Rousseau was more Hobbesian than he would have admitted. Douglass notes that the Straussian reading of Hobbes, whatever its fairness to Hobbes, resembles how Hobbes was read in eighteenth-century France.

**The Plato-or-Hobbes dichotomy.** Melzer and Williams remain structured by the Straussian dichotomy. Douglass grants that Williams convincingly challenges one line of interpretation. He denies that Rousseau's engagement with Hobbes reduces to Plato or Hobbes.

**Ahistorical comparison versus Cambridge-style history.** Most existing work compares the state of nature, social contract, and sovereignty philosophically. Skinner noted that Rousseau's reading of De cive is under-researched. Tuck's Benedict Lectures (2000) are the fullest recent study, but Tuck seeks the 'Hobbesianism of Rousseau' and uses Rousseau to defend Hobbes. Douglass explicitly reverses this and uses Hobbes to improve our understanding of Rousseau.

**The unanswered question and Douglass's answer.** If Rousseau was so Hobbesian, why did he repeatedly set his ideas out against Hobbes? Douglass's thesis is that the Discours was set out against both Hobbes and Hobbes's natural-law critics. It also targeted doux commerce theorists, all of whom Rousseau took to rest their justifications of the social order on Hobbesian premises. He calls this the principal historical contribution of the study. He insists that to disagree with Hobbes's critics is not necessarily to agree with Hobbes.

**Chapter plan.** Chapter 1 covers Hobbes's French reception and 'Hobbism', which mattered as much as Hobbes's own texts because influence was often indirect. Chapter 2 covers the Discours: collapsing the Pufendorfian sociability versus Hobbesian Epicureanism split, exposing the Hobbesian premises of doux commerce, and stating free will and natural goodness against Hobbes. Chapter 3 covers Rousseau's affinities with Hobbes (an absolute, incontestable sovereign; religion as a threat to unity) and his inversion of Hobbesian sovereignty toward republican conclusions, making law sovereign while keeping nature as a regulative normative standard against conventionalism. Chapter 4 covers the passions: the neo-Augustinian background, Rousseau's repudiation of Original Sin, and fear for Hobbes versus love for Rousseau. The conclusion argues that Rousseau's republic suits only naturally good men not yet corrupted by inequality and luxury, and asks whether it is viable today.

## Notable passages

The survey paragraph (c1) lays out the Hobbesian readings of Strauss, Derathé, Davy, and Jouvenel. The Tuck paragraph (c2) locates Douglass's method and its reversal of Tuck's. The methodological paragraph (c3) states the polemical thesis. The caution paragraph (c4) blocks both simple readings, Hobbesian and anti-Hobbesian. The Chapter 3 summary (c5) gives the inversion of sovereignty and the regulative role of nature. The Chapter 4 summary (c6) gives the fear-versus-love contrast. Note a small internal inconsistency in the source's chapter numbering: the chapter 3 paragraph treats sovereignty and law, while the next paragraph refers back to 'the third chapter' as examining free will, so the exact chapter mapping is best taken from the chapters themselves.

## World Machines resonance (hedged)

Douglass is an intellectual historian, not a machine theorist, and nothing here is about world machines. The nearest rhymes are loose. Rousseau's project of 'placing law above man' and making a people one through the general will and civil religion is a comparable design for political order to the legibility programs discussed under [[modernity-machine]] and [[the-legible-commonwealth]], though Rousseau is explicitly hostile to the commercial and luxury-driven modernity that machine describes. The book's treatment of the Hobbism caricature as a received narrative that shaped later readers also echoes WMP's interest in how [[history-machine]]-style paradigms assemble diffusely rather than through single authors, although Douglass frames it as reception history.
