# Rousseau's State of Nature Against Hobbes and Pufendorf

Douglass reads the opening of the Discours sur l’inégalité and the unpublished l’Etat de guerre as Rousseau's first sustained response to Hobbes. Rousseau's two principles anterior to reason, self-preservation and pity, are set beside Hobbes's two postulates of human nature; the first is shared, the second is directed against Hobbes. Douglass then argues, against a simple reading on which Rousseau reverts to a Hobbesian starting point, that Rousseau's account of natural man's physical condition targets Pufendorf's claim that man is naturally weak and miserable, because Pufendorf's picture turns out to be very Hobbesian. L’Etat de guerre denies outright that the state of nature is a state of war and adds why this matters: the rights of nature must not entail rights of war, or slavery and sovereignty could be justified from them.

## Themes

**Two principles, one Hobbesian and one anti-Hobbesian.** Douglass reports Rousseau's claim that natural law must be known by nature's voice, and that two principles anterior to reason yield it: an ardent interest in self-preservation and a natural repugnance to see a sensitive being suffer. Douglass's own thesis is that the first is strictly Hobbesian, and that Rousseau credited Hobbes with seeing the defect of modern definitions of natural right. Hobbes's error, on Rousseau's view, lay in the conclusions he drew, notably in ascribing to natural man a multitude of passions that are products of society and in neglecting pity. Douglass says Rousseau 'might even have had Hobbes specifically in mind', a hedged inference.

**Against Tuck: rejecting sociability is not siding with Hobbes.** Douglass reports Richard Tuck's view that by jettisoning sociability Rousseau reverted to a more Hobbesian starting point, and answers that this is accurate only up to a point. Pity was directed against both pure self-preservation and Pufendorfian sociability, and dropping sociability cleared the ground for a critique implicating Hobbes and the sociability theorists alike. Douglass also distinguishes two ideas of sociability, a natural inclination and a rationally discerned principle of natural right, and says Rousseau rejected both.

**Pufendorf as the main target of the physical account.** Douglass argues that the section on natural man's physical attributes is aimed less at Hobbes (mentioned only in passing) than at Pufendorf, Cumberland and an 'illustrious Philosopher' (Douglass assumes Montesquieu) who held that man is naturally timid and weak. Rousseau answers four Pufendorfian points in turn: lack of education, hunger, wild beasts, and cold and shelter. Douglass's suggestion is that Pufendorf's weak, wretched natural man ended in a condition virtually identical to Hobbes's, one remediable only by a civil sovereign, so that Rousseau exposed affinities that Barbeyrac had tried to occlude.

**Epicureanism and the charge of Hobbism.** Rousseau's picture resembles Lucretius's first men, and early critics (Castillon, Castel) used the Epicurean-Hobbes link to tar him. Douglass cautions against taking a position for Rousseau from the opinions of those who sought to discredit him: Rousseau treated Pufendorf's natural weakness as just as erroneous as Hobbes's war of all against all, and both as carrying pernicious implications.

**L’Etat de guerre: no natural war, and why it matters.** Douglass dates the manuscript to roughly the mid-1750s, contemporaneous with the Discours. Rousseau defines war as a permanent state assuming constant relations, a constant and manifest will to destroy an enemy, and holds that self-preservation entails only a right of defence. He concedes that Hobbes's argument rests on the competition of the right of each to all things, but denies such competition is natural. Douglass notes two differences of emphasis from the Discours: war exists only between public persons, so Hobbes's natural man was really modelled on a state; and the rights of society, founded on those of nature, cannot annihilate them. Hence it was imperative to show that the rights of nature do not entail the rights of war, since Grotius and Hobbes used those to legitimize slavery.

**Consequence for sovereignty.** Both Hobbes and Pufendorf derive the necessity of civil sovereignty and positive law from a wretched natural condition; if that depiction fails, Douglass says, the rest of their theories is left open to question. He adds that Rousseau's state of nature also presents a model of the good life, to be taken up later.

**Resonance with World Machines (hedged).** Douglass is an intellectual historian and does not use a machine vocabulary. The general point that a political order's legitimacy is argued from a picture of what precedes it loosely rhymes with the way a world machine's assumptions supply its founding story; see [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] and [[noble-savage]], though Douglass's Rousseau is careful to separate natural man from the trope of a happy savage by way of historicized, Epicurean sources. This is a loose association only.

## Notable passages

**Two principles versus Hobbes's postulates (c1).** The core claim that Rousseau's first principle is Hobbesian and his second, pity, is set against Hobbes.

**Pity against both Hobbes and Pufendorf (c2).** Douglass's answer to Tuck and the reason Rousseau discards sociability.

**Epicureanism, Hobbism and realigning Pufendorf with Hobbes (c3).** The reception charge and Douglass's caution about it, with the thesis that Pufendorf's weakness is as erroneous as the war of all against all.

**Definition of war in l’Etat de guerre (c4).** Rousseau's reply to Hobbes's definition of war and his limit on the right of self-preservation.

**Why the state of nature must not be a state of war (c5).** The link from nature's rights to slavery and to the planned Institutions politiques.

**Summary of the critique's purpose (c6).** What is at stake for Hobbesian and Pufendorfian justifications of sovereignty.
