# Natural Goodness and the Recovery of the Golden Age

Douglass argues that the Discours sur l’inégalité answers two contexts at once: modern natural law (Pufendorf and the Hobbesian picture of man) and the eighteenth-century debate over luxury, in which Melon and the doux commerce theorists defended commerce and the arts. Rousseau's principle of natural goodness was worked out in his replies to critics of the first Discours, and Douglass claims it sits exactly where those two contexts meet. Rousseau's move is to argue that both camps assume man is naturally evil, and that this is their shared mistake. By separating amour de soi-même from amour-propre, and by placing pity before amour-propre, he historicizes the passions Hobbes called natural and reopens a place for a golden age in man's genealogy.

## Themes

**The luxury debate as the frame.** Melon (taken by Douglass as representative of doux commerce theorists, with Voltaire's praise as evidence, see [[voltaire]]) held that societies leave savagery only by procuring commodities, and that inequality, though a consequence of luxury, benefits states. Rousseau saw the critics of his first Discours as resting on the same premise, namely that modern societies can be justified by turning inflamed individualistic passions to good use. Rousseau's own genealogy runs inequality, wealth, luxury and idleness, then arts and sciences. Whether man is naturally good or evil therefore decides whether these things are remedy or cause.

**Shared error of natural law and doux commerce theorists.** Douglass's thesis is that Pufendorf's political theory and Rousseau's critics both relied on a Hobbesian depiction of natural man, and both misjudged the condition of man before arts and commercial society. The two contexts converge, he says, in Lecat's note on natural law, which treats the golden rule as a product of the science of morality, one of the very arts Rousseau attacked.

**Pity and Mandeville.** Rousseau answers Lecat with a maxim of natural goodness grounded in pity, a sentiment anterior to reason. Douglass stresses Rousseau's debt to Mandeville: their genealogies of modern society largely agree, and they differ chiefly on the moral status of pity and on whether the effects of luxury are beneficial. Rousseau's rejection of Original Sin removes the antithesis between natural inclination and virtue. Rousseau mentions Mandeville only briefly and does not acknowledge the deeper similarities.

**Was Rousseau's Hobbes a caricature?** Douglass grants that Hobbes explicitly denied that man is naturally evil, and that Rousseau apparently read the very page of De cive where he did so. His verdict is that Rousseau was neither deferring to the prevalent view nor misreading Hobbes: on Rousseau's definition, the passions Hobbes called natural (they are not natural for Rousseau) do make man evil, and Hobbes's account resembles neo-Augustinian post-lapsarian pictures. Barbeyrac and Montesquieu had made similar charges, but Rousseau alone charts the passions' rise genealogically. The polemical use of Hobbes also implicated hypocritical critics, though Douglass insists the critique has philosophical depth.

**Amour-propre versus amour de soi-même.** The famous note to the Discours sur l’inégalité is the first statement of the distinction, made in direct response to Sorbière's French Hobbes, who put both utility and glory under amour-propre. For Rousseau, advantage is amour de soi-même (pre-social, shared with animals), while comparison-dependent sentiments (vanity, pride, envy, glory, honour) are amour-propre. Douglass notes that Hobbes's glory, which 'consists in comparison and pre-eminence', is close to Rousseau's amour-propre. He also reports, with approval, Nicholas Dent's view that amour-propre can be directed to virtue or vice.

**Pity precedes amour-propre, and the golden age.** Douglass flags a tension between the Discours (pity precedes reflection) and the Essai sur l’origine des langues (pity needs imagination and acquired knowledge), but holds that the point for the Hobbes argument is only that pity comes before amour-propre. When amour-propre stirs, pity is at full force and tempers it, so no Hobbesian war follows. The stage of families and small societies is the golden age, 'the happiest and most durable epoch', before agriculture and metallurgy. Douglass's claim is that Rousseau, unlike Fénelon (whose Bétique remained fiction), built the golden age into a genealogy usable against those who call pre-commercial society barbaric.

## Notable passages

The Melon passage sets out the luxury-debate opposition, including Melon's claim that equality is a chimera. The chain from inequality to the arts, with Douglass's diagnosis that the critics and Pufendorf share a Hobbesian anthropology, is the argumentative centre. Rousseau's maxim of natural goodness ('Do what is good for you with the least possible harm to others') and Douglass's account of Mandeville show pity replacing reasoned justice. The amour-propre note and the golden-age passage carry the core anti-Hobbesian argument, and the Fénelon comparison closes the unit.
