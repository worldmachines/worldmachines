# Ordering the Passions: Rousseau's Republic and the Problem of Will-Formation

This opening section of Chapter 4 sets the agenda. Douglass argues that Rousseau's political project had two halves: taking men as they are, and then making them what the polity needs them to be by ordering their passions and forming their will. The vehicle is a well-ordered republic whose institutions cultivate love of fatherland as man's 'dominant passion'. Douglass promises to explain this through Rousseau's debt to neo-Augustinian French thought on the varieties of love, a context he says also illuminates Rousseau's opposition to Hobbes, since Hobbes's account of man resembled and inspired the French Augustinians. Hobbes stays mostly in the background until the chapter's end. The section also announces a polemical aim: to dispel proto-Kantian readings of Rousseau on free will, autonomy and virtue, and to recover the centrality of harmony and unity.

## Themes

**Two halves of Rousseau's project (Douglass's thesis).** Rousseau's opening move in Du contrat social, taking 'men as they are and laws as they might be', is only half the story. Citing the Discours sur l'économie politique, Douglass stresses that it is 'better still' to make men what one needs them to be. Overcoming the opposition of private and general wills requires ordering the passions and forming the will, which only a well-ordered republic can do.

**Hobbes and Rousseau share the problem of ordering the passions.** Douglass claims that both thinkers, perhaps more than any other modern political philosopher, treated ordering the passions to secure political unity as a central problem. Both wanted a unified political will, and both saw that will as driven by passion, so that reason-based interest in obeying the laws cannot alone bind citizens to the body politic. Hobbes's war of all against all is, in Hobbes's own words as Douglass quotes them, an 'Inference, made from the Passions'; Rousseau's needs bring men together as their passions divide them. The similarity is framed as shared problem, while the account of man's nature differs and is said to explain many of the most important distinctions between them.

**Nature versus the neo-Augustinian and Hobbesian picture.** Rousseau's principle of natural goodness is presented as set against both Augustinian and Hobbesian accounts of man. His republican vision presupposes that rival account of nature. Douglass indicates that the neo-Augustinian survey to follow will show why Rousseau found Hobbes's account of man's natural state objectionable.

**Against proto-Kantian readings.** Douglass worries that cultivating the passions might seem to undermine both nature as a normative standard and free will as the source of legitimacy. He resolves to clarify nature and free will, chiefly by rejecting readings that equate Rousseau's free will with Kantian autonomy, or that attribute a Kantian account of virtue in which inclinations and duties are perpetually at war and virtue is the triumph of duty. He contrasts these with Rousseau's emphasis on harmony and unity as the condition of a happy life in accordance with nature.

**Contradiction and amour-propre.** Drawing on an unpublished fragment on public happiness, Douglass frames human misery as contradiction (duties versus inclinations, nature versus social institutions, man versus citizen), to which he adds private versus general will and amour de soi-même versus amour-propre. The golden age had amour-propre active but tempered by pity. Recovering harmony means realizing amour-propre's positive potential, as Rousseau put it in 1755, 'combining the force of amour-propre with all the beauty of virtue'. Douglass reads political virtue as attained by cultivating passions over reason.

**Possible World Machines resonances (tentative).** Rousseau's project of making a people one through engineered passion and institutions rhymes loosely with the [[modernity-machine]]'s drive to make populations legible and governable, and with the designs discussed under [[the-legible-commonwealth]]. It does not fit neatly: Douglass is concerned with will-formation and love of fatherland, not with legibility or measurement, and he makes no machine-theoretic claim. ## Notable passages

The opening paragraph gives the thesis of the whole chapter: the second half of Rousseau's project is making men what one needs, by ordering passions so that love of fatherland becomes the dominant passion. The paragraph on Hobbes and Rousseau as theorists of ordering the passions is Douglass's main claim of kinship, hedged by 'perhaps' and qualified by the recognition that their accounts of nature differ. The methodological paragraph about the previous chapter's unresolved question of will-formation, and the proto-Kantian readings, sets out the interpretive stakes. The quoted fragment on public happiness ('if you divide his heart, you tear him to pieces') is the fullest statement of the contradiction that the ordering of the passions is meant to heal. The closing paragraph links amour-propre's heroic potential to the neo-Augustinian context and to Rousseau's rejection of Hobbes.
