# Barbeyrac, Burlamaqui, and the French Natural Law Tradition against Hobbes

Douglass introduces the context in which Rousseau most often met Hobbes: modern natural law as transmitted into French in the first half of the eighteenth century. There were no great original French treatises, but Barbeyrac's annotated translations of Grotius, Pufendorf and Cumberland, and Burlamaqui's *Principes du droit naturel et politique* (largely built on Barbeyrac's editions), created by the 1750s a self-identifying French natural law tradition. That tradition did not include Hobbes; he was the bête noire to be refuted. Douglass reconstructs Barbeyrac's four charges against Hobbes, the contrast of Epicureanism with sociability, the two men's revisions of Pufendorf on the state of nature, justice and conscience, and their reassertion of God. His own thesis is that this anti-Hobbism made Pufendorf look less Hobbesian than he was and the two Genevan/French authors look less original than they were.

## Themes

**A French tradition defined by its enemy.** Barbeyrac and Burlamaqui located themselves as followers of Grotius and above all Pufendorf, and adapted their arguments "to combat what they took to be the dangers of Hobbism." Douglass (in his own voice) says the tradition "did not include Hobbes as such." Barbeyrac's judgement was mostly negative, although he deferred to Bayle's remark that no one had penetrated so far into the foundations of politics.

**Barbeyrac's four charges.** Hobbes (1) revived Epicurus by making self-preservation and interest the origin of societies; (2) ascribed to men the will and power to hurt each other and so made the state of nature a state of war; (3) gave kings unlimited authority over state and religion; (4) made the sovereign the judge of justice and injustice, so that only his power, not divine will, binds the conscience. The most important was Epicureanism, which was set against sociability.

**An artificial gulf.** Douglass argues that the Epicureanism/sociability opposition "belied important similarities." Pufendorf's fundamental law (maintain a peaceful society with everyone) is not far from Hobbes's "seek peace when peace can be had," and Pufendorf insisted amour-propre and sociability need not be opposed. Barbeyrac's annotations nonetheless steered readers away from the similarities, casting Hobbes as Epicurus's more fervent heir who made men "wolves" to one another. Barbeyrac also understood sociability as a Grotian natural inclination, whereas for Pufendorf it was a principle of reason compatible with selfish motives; Barbeyrac elided that difference.

**The state of nature revised.** Pufendorf denied the state of nature was a war of all against all, yet his account of why civil society arose stayed "very Hobbesian" because he needed life without civil law to be miserable so as to justify civil authority. Barbeyrac, wary of the sovereign's power, rejected this and pointed to the simplicity of early societies, before luxury multiplied desires, a sentiment Douglass says foreshadows the *Discours sur l'inégalité*. Burlamaqui did not follow him, keeping a negative picture of pre-civil life (weakness, indigence, incessant wants) to justify the civil state. He was also the more thoroughgoing in leaving self-preservation behind as a foundation, grounding law in happiness and perfection and so reviving a teleological conception of human nature.

**Justice, conscience, and a shared misreading.** Douglass says Barbeyrac and Burlamaqui, as was common, conflated Hobbes's definition of justice with his account of the laws of nature. Hobbes held that justice has no place in the state of nature but that the laws of nature operate and can be sinned against. They therefore attributed to him the view that pre-civil life is a moral vacuum. Against this, Barbeyrac argued that even the most legitimate sovereign's laws must be disobeyed when they conflict with the immutable laws written in the heart, with conscience as the final tribunal. Burlamaqui defined obligation as a restriction of liberty produced by reason, said conscience is reason itself, and posited a natural moral instinct of compassion. Douglass notes Rousseau would use a virtually identical definition of compassion against Hobbes.

**God restored.** Grotius's etiamsi daremus claim made natural law readable without God. Pufendorf broke with this, and Barbeyrac (in defending Pufendorf against Leibniz's charge of Hobbism) and Burlamaqui pushed God's role further, Barbeyrac distorting Pufendorf somewhat. Hobbes's reputation for atheism, which Barbeyrac accepted despite Bayle, made it disputable whether he belonged to the tradition. Douglass adds the observation that modern natural law was largely Protestant and that both authors were Huguenot or Calvinist.

**Indirect attack on sovereignty.** Because the two attacked Hobbes on sovereignty and civil society only through his state-of-nature theory, they assumed refuting his premises would discredit the rest. Douglass closes by saying Rousseau, aware of the prevailing Hobbes versus natural-law opposition, would subvert it by attacking Hobbes and Pufendorf together.

For World Machines this is background rather than a direct resonance. It does bear on the way [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] inherited a stock image of Hobbes, and on the later, secular-to-theological shift in how political order was justified. Douglass makes no machine-theoretic claim and none is implied. His use of the Hobbes/Hobbism distinction is the takeaway to carry forward.
