# Chapter 2 opening: Rousseau's state of nature against Hobbes and the natural law tradition

Douglass introduces the chapter's thesis: Rousseau's critique of Hobbes's state of nature is best read as a move within modern natural law theory as received in France and Geneva, not as a stand-alone attack. Hobbes is named only rarely in the Discours sur l'inégalité, so his importance cannot be shown by frequency of reference; it has to be shown through his role in how the natural law tradition defined itself. Douglass then sets out three arguments, defends a reading of the Discours as a hypothetical but not unscientific history, distinguishes the nature of man from man in the state of nature, and disputes the view that Rousseau simply repudiated natural law. The epigraph (Rousseau, l'Etat de guerre) frames the theme: the commentators know the Bourgeois of London or Paris but not what a man is.

## Themes

**Rousseau challenged Hobbes without the principle of sociability.** Douglass (his own thesis) says Rousseau thought the prevailing refutations of Hobbes inadequate and contested him on new grounds that implicated the whole natural law tradition, not just Hobbes. The Discours can be read in several contexts (the luxury debate with the doux commerce theorists, Buffon's natural science, Lucretius/Cicero/Seneca), and Douglass says it would be misguided to reduce it to one, but he emphasizes the natural law context most.

**Three interrelated arguments.** (1) By denying that sociability supplies the fundamental law of nature, Rousseau elided the distinctions that followers of Barbeyrac used to separate Pufendorf from Hobbes; on Rousseau's account Pufendorf was no better than Hobbes, so Hobbes serves as a negative marker against which natural law accounts are judged. (2) On man's moral side Rousseau agreed with Pufendorf and Burlamaqui in a criticism of Hobbes rooted in free will, later turned into the inalienability of freedom against both Hobbes and his natural law critics (Douglass calls this argument indirect). (3) Rousseau claimed that, according to Hobbes, man is evil; the principle of natural goodness was set against Hobbes and, implicitly, against the critics of the Discours sur les sciences et les arts, whose defences of luxury rested on a supposedly Hobbesian account of man's miserable natural condition. Note that this describes Rousseau's claim about Hobbes, not necessarily Hobbes's actual view.

**Nature as normative standard.** For Rousseau the state of nature models a good life of harmony, free from contradiction, which man cannot return to but to which any good life must conform. This resonates faintly with the [[noble-savage]] trope, though Douglass's Rousseau treats nature as a regulative standard and not a lost golden age to be restored; see also [[jean-jacques-rousseau]].

**Hypothetical history, real conclusions.** The Discours is conjectural (Rousseau's 'ventured some conjectures'; a state 'which perhaps never existed'), but Douglass argues the purpose is to separate the original from the artificial in man, drawing on Buffon without resting on him. Rousseau treated the conjectures as akin to those of physicists, and held that the conclusions are therefore not conjectural. Douglass rejects the consensus that Rousseau abandoned finding the basis of right in human nature.

**Nature of man versus man in the state of nature.** Using Pierre Nicole's distinction between general and particular knowledge of man, Douglass reads Part I of the Discours as knowledge of man in general and Part II as a genealogy of modern man's particular, corrupted condition. He stresses that nature of man is not the same as man in the state of nature, otherwise Rousseau would be founding philosophy on a lost ideal.

**Rousseau and the natural law tradition.** Rousseau follows Burlamaqui in deducing natural right from the nature of man (neither marred by Original Sin), while reaching contrary conclusions. Douglass sets Vaughan (Rousseau sweeps natural law away) against Derathé and Rosenblatt (who locate him in the tradition) and sides with the latter, arguing that Rousseau's complaint is that predecessors failed to reach real natural man, grounding law on reason when reason is dormant in natural man.

## Notable passages

The thesis statement of the three arguments (c1) is the chapter's map and shows Douglass's claim that Hobbes is a negative marker even for Pufendorf. The passage on natural goodness (c2) ties the state of nature to the luxury debate and to nature as normative. The methodological passage on hypothetical history (c3) explains why the Discours is not merely speculative. The nature of man versus the state of nature passage with the Burlamaqui quotation (c4) is Douglass's key interpretive clarification. The closing paragraph (c5) states Rousseau's real objection to his natural law predecessors. For World Machines, the connection is thin: this is a treatise on political anthropology, not machine theory, although its picture of society's artificial layers accreting on a natural base loosely rhymes with the [[kulturkritik]] tradition; the wider [[counter-enlightenment]] framing is not made by Douglass here.
