# Sovereignty Inverted: Rousseau's Republican Use of Hobbesian Sovereignty

Douglass's thesis in this section of Chapter 3 is that Rousseau's political problems were Hobbesian, but his answer was a radical inversion of Hobbes's. Rousseau keeps what is most Hobbesian (total alienation of rights to the whole, no rights against the sovereign, the body-politic vocabulary and the citizen/subject double relation) yet relocates sovereignty permanently in the people, as an exercise of the general will that can be neither alienated, represented nor divided. In this way the modern discourse of sovereignty and contract, ‘so often used to justify the submission of the people’, is turned toward classically republican ends. The section closes by pointing ahead to ‘Freedom Preserved’, where Douglass will show how submission to the general will preserves rather than alienates free will.

## Themes

**Rousseau extends Hobbes's own argument about rights.** The single clause of the contract is ‘the total alienation of each associate, with all his rights, to the whole community’. Douglass says Rousseau surpasses Hobbes by denying even a right of self-preservation against the sovereign. Douglass reads Rousseau as one of the few who grasped Hobbes's real worry: that individual judgments about good and evil (including appeals to conscience, which Hobbes treats as mere judgment) cause war and dissolve the commonwealth. Critics such as Barbeyrac, in Douglass's account, either missed or evaded this point. Rousseau's sovereign is thus absolute, since ‘the Sovereign alone is the judge of what matters’. Douglass reports that liberal critics have therefore called Rousseau as dangerous as Hobbes, and says this elides the more significant differences.

**Shared vocabulary of the body politic.** Rousseau's account of the moral and collective body (City, Republic, body politic; State, Sovereign, Power; people, citizens, subjects) closely maps onto Hobbes, above all the double relation of citizen and subject unique to De cive. Douglass's genealogy: Grotius spoke of body, head and person, but the body-politic metaphor was fully developed only by Hobbes, then adopted by Pufendorf and Rousseau.

**Inalienability rests on a theory of will.** Sovereignty is ‘only the exercise of the general will’, and power can be transferred but will cannot. Hobbes and the whole natural-law tradition let the people bind its future will by instituting a superior who then speaks for it (Pufendorf's second pact; Sorbière's De cive). Rousseau denies that will can legitimately be transferred, alienated or represented, since that would make one dependent on another's will, the antithesis of freedom. Douglass stresses two qualifications: this does not make all contracts impossible (it applies only to contracts transferring will, not other goods), and the argument concerns the freedom of the people as a collective body, not of individual citizens. In practice the people's constant willing is presumed from its not revoking, provided it retains the power to revoke.

**Indivisibility, and the division of powers.** Everyone since Bodin held sovereignty indivisible, but Rousseau charges that thinkers ‘divide it in its object’ (Douglass suggests Grotius's ‘subjective’ or ‘potential’ parts and Barbeyrac are targets, with wider application to mixed legislative and executive powers). Sovereignty, the moral power, is only the ratifying of general laws; government, the physical power, executes them. Rousseau thus supports a division of powers while agreeing with Hobbes that holding rights against the sovereign is self-defeating. Douglass calls Rousseau's response to Hobbesian sovereignty largely definitional: absolute in power, limited in the form of its actions.

**Republican conclusions by natural-law means.** Placing law above men, defining liberty as freedom from dependence on others' wills, and keeping legislative right with citizens are republican conclusions, but reached by grappling with modern natural-law problems (sovereignty, will, contract) often set against Hobbes. Douglass suggests this suited Geneva, where natural-law arguments were current, and that republican and natural-law traditions are best seen as used by Rousseau in a complementary way, not as opposed sources. (Compare [[niccolo-machiavelli]] on republican liberty and [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] generally.)

**Possible World Machines rhyme, held loosely.** The concept of a single unified public person with one will, produced by the act of association, is a design for making a people one and answerable to a shared judge; this rhymes only loosely with the legibility ambitions of the [[modernity-machine]], and Douglass makes no such claim. His concern is intellectual history, not machine theory.

## Notable passages

The opening (c1) sets the inversion thesis and the claim that Rousseau surpasses Hobbes on rights. The second (c2) explains why Rousseau, unlike Hobbes's critics, understood the point about judgment and admits absolute sovereignty while insisting on differences from Hobbes. The third (c3) states the inalienability claim and its limit to the collective. The fourth (c4) sets Rousseau's theory of will against Hobbes's contract-as-sign-of-will and the natural-law tradition. The fifth (c5) gives the definitional resolution of absolute-but-limited sovereignty. The sixth (c6) offers the methodological conclusion on republicanism and natural law.
