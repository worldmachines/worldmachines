# Malebranche's Critique of Hobbes as a Source for Rousseau

Douglass argues that although Malebranche's references to Hobbes are fleeting, they matter. Following Patrick Riley, Douglass holds that Rousseau was greatly indebted to Malebranche, and that Malebranche's objections foreshadow those Rousseau later aimed at his materialist contemporaries. Unlike Nicole and Bayle, who focused on Hobbes's moral and political thought, Malebranche attacked on epistemological, metaphysical and theological grounds. That bridges the early reception of Hobbes's natural philosophy and the later, more political French reading. Douglass then places Malebranche beside Clarke and Leibniz, who also charged Hobbes with confusing mind and matter and so destroying freedom. He ends with Malebranche's charge that Hobbes made amour-propre the source of justice, a charge Douglass says recurs throughout eighteenth-century French thought.

## Themes

**Materialism, empiricism and the collapse of ideas into perceptions.** Malebranche (Entretien, 1707; letter to Fénelon, 1713) claimed that Hobbes and Locke dissolved the distinction between eternal, immutable ideas and subjective perceptions. In his view this would establish Pyrrhonism and leave no truth or falsity, justice or injustice, science or morality. Douglass reports this as Malebranche's argument, not his own verdict on Hobbes.

**Dualism as the guarantee of freedom.** Malebranche's radical dualism was meant to preserve free will and, through it, morality. If action followed from empirical perceptions there would be no real distinction between perceiving and willing. Douglass presents this as a theme Rousseau would adopt against the philosophes, citing his invocation of the distinction between the two substances. Douglass also notes that Locke was widely read in France as a materialist and determinist, a reading he calls 'very Hobbesian'. He suggests, with a hedge ('it is quite possible'), that Rousseau followed Malebranche in treating Hobbes and Locke as equally mistaken on mind and matter.

**Allies in the attack: Clarke and Leibniz.** Clarke (translated into French in 1717) charged Hobbes and Spinoza with confusing mind and matter and so rejecting free will and moral responsibility. Leibniz ([[gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz]]), writing in the Théodicée, blamed Hobbes and Spinoza ([[baruch-spinoza]]) for destroying freedom and contingency. Douglass stresses that Leibniz's opposition was more qualified than Malebranche's. Leibniz praised Hobbes's precision and clarity, though he did not always keep his public promise to do so. [[voltaire]], by contrast, praised Hobbes for anticipating Locke on thinking matter.

**From metaphysics to political morality.** In the Réflexions sur la prémotion physique (1715) Malebranche accused Hobbes and Locke of making the right to do whatever one wills the foundation of morality. Extended to God, this leads to voluntarism, so Malebranche insisted on eternal uniform laws of justice grounded in reason. Douglass says this anticipates the quarrel in which Leibniz accused Pufendorf of Hobbism and which Barbeyrac then brought into the French reception of natural law.

**Amour-propre as the alleged foundation of justice.** Malebranche's eighth Entretien sur la métaphysique (1688) does not name Hobbes, but Douglass judges it 'most probable' that he is meant. It describes a view on which laws are founded on amour-propre, everyone has a right to everything, and law is merely an external power. Douglass notes that here amour-propre 'means nothing more specific than self-love', unlike its later, more technical Rousseauian sense. He claims that Malebranche's charge that Hobbes made amour-propre the source of all morality recurs throughout eighteenth-century French thought, and that Rousseau revived Malebranche's criticisms against Hobbes and against modern natural law theory more generally. The caricature here is of 'Hobbism' as received in France, and the passage is Malebranche's characterization, not a demonstrated reading of Hobbes.

## Notable passages

The passage on Malebranche's opposition to Hobbes's mechanism and to shared Hobbes-Locke empiricism (c1) sets out the epistemological stakes. The dualism passage (c2) links this to freedom and to Rousseau's later anti-materialism. The Leibniz passage (c3) shows that the anti-Hobbes front was not uniform. The passage on the Reflexions (c4) moves from metaphysics to natural law. The long block quotation (c5) is the fullest statement of the amour-propre critique. Douglass's own gloss (c6) states what it means for Rousseau.

Resonance with World Machines is slight. The Malebranche-Hobbes dispute over whether justice is eternal and universal or made by contract can be read as an argument about whether the rules of a political order are discovered or constructed, loosely adjacent to the legibility questions in [[modernity-machine]] and to the later Rousseau ([[jean-jacques-rousseau]]). Douglass does not frame it this way, and the fit is loose.
