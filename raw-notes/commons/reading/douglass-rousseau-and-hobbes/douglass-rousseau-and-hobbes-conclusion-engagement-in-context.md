# Conclusion: Rousseau's Engagement with Hobbes in Context

The opening of Douglass's Review and Conclusion restates his central thesis and locates Rousseau's engagement with Hobbes in its eighteenth-century context. Douglass argues that readings of Rousseau as Hobbesian (Castel, Leo Strauss, and later scholars who speak of the 'Hobbesianism of Rousseau' or 'Hobbism concealed') are overstated, but he equally refuses to make Rousseau simply anti-Hobbesian. He proposes instead that Rousseau, who called himself a paradoxical thinker, is best approached as someone whose ideas do not map onto the categories of either his contemporaries or modern scholars. The unit then reviews the historical context: the evidence of direct reading is thin, the mediating role of Pufendorf, Barbeyrac and Burlamaqui is large, and Rousseau's invocations of Hobbes were polemical.

## Themes

**Neither Hobbesian nor anti-Hobbesian.** Douglass's stated aim is 'to show where and why Rousseau both followed and departed from Hobbes'. Douglass takes 'paradox' in Rousseau's own sense: an idea that counters prevailing opinion, and so is opposed to prejudice rather than being a logical anomaly. This is his own framing, offered as the right way to conclude.

**Thin evidence of direct influence (Douglass's own finding).** Most evidence that Rousseau read Hobbes is confined to the Discours sur l'inégalité; apart from that, very little beyond De cive, presumably in Sorbière's French translation. Douglass says the burden of proof lies on anyone claiming Rousseau read Leviathan, which matters given the temptation to pair Du contrat social with Leviathan. He hedges: Rousseau was certainly familiar with Hobbesian ideas, but it is often hard to tell whether that came from Hobbes's texts or from intermediaries. He also flags a gap: l'Etat de guerre in relation to international relations theory is left largely to other scholars.

**Modern natural law as the mediating context.** Pufendorf, rivalled only by Montesquieu as the most invoked political authority, reached Geneva's 1750s controversies and the Encyclopédie largely through Barbeyrac's editions. Pufendorf and followers such as Burlamaqui adopted many Hobbesian ideas while opposing others, so much of Rousseau's engagement with Hobbes should be situated here rather than in a direct reading of Hobbes.

**Polemic against Hobbes's critics.** Rousseau's target was often Hobbes's natural-law critics, whom he took to be 'little better than Hobbes': they too presented man without civil laws as miserable and then had him alienate his God-given freedom to a quasi-Hobbesian sovereign. The state of nature, social pact and sovereignty are the three areas in which this is argued.

**Doux commerce as covert Hobbism.** Douglass reports Rousseau's claim that justifying commerce and luxury as remedies for man's barbarism implicitly accepts the Hobbesian state of nature, and that commerce is the cause of inflamed passions rather than their cure. This sets the Discours sur les sciences et les arts to Discours sur l'inégalité sequence within the Hobbes story, and connects to the eighteenth-century luxury debate (see [[voltaire]] and [[adam-smith]] for later participants, which this unit itself does not discuss).

**Was Rousseau's critique unfair to Hobbes?** Douglass concedes the criticism that Rousseau missed the point of Hobbes is partly valid, but says it in turn misses the point of Rousseau's critique, which was to show Hobbes's pervasive influence on contemporaries. This echoes Douglass's wider care to separate Hobbes's actual views from the 'Hobbism' caricature current in France, though the unit itself puts it in terms of Rousseau's strategy.

**Tuck's question answered.** To 'what exactly did Rousseau think he was doing?', Douglass answers that Rousseau was exposing contemporaries as more Hobbesian than they would admit while setting out his own thought against both. Deep disagreements remain: human nature and free will.

## Notable passages

The thesis restatement (c1) sets the balanced position against Strauss-style readings. The evidence-of-reading passage (c2) is the most important methodological caution for anyone building a Rousseau-Hobbes influence claim. The natural-law passage (c3) and doux commerce passage (c4) give the two contextual frames in which Rousseau's Hobbes-invocations should be read. The polemic passage (c5) states Douglass's claimed novelty and his defence of Rousseau against the charge of misreading Hobbes. The Tuck passage (c6) gives his short answer and names the two disagreements he treats as fundamental.
