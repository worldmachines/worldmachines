# The Enchanters Unmasked, Teresa Panza's Letters, and Sancho's Rule of Mercy

This unit spans the close of Chapter L, all of Chapter LI, and the opening of Chapter LII. Cide Hamete first explains away the supposed 'enchanters' who flogged Doña Rodriguez and pinched Don Quixote: they were only the duchess and Altisidora, provoked to fury by the duenna's gossip. The duke's page then rides to Sancho's village, where Teresa and Sanchica are transported by a letter and a string of coral beads from the duchess, and begin dreaming of coaches and countesships. Meanwhile Sancho, starved by his absurd physician, presides over his first real case as governor — the paradox of a man who swears he crosses a bridge only to be hanged — and resolves it by equity rather than law, echoing advice Don Quixote gave him before he left. Don Quixote's own letter of counsel arrives, a serious set-piece of governance advice, and Sancho's reply reveals both his growing shrewdness and his mounting hunger and dread. The unit closes with Sancho issuing his own ordinances and, back at the castle, a second grieving duenna — Doña Rodriguez — arriving to draw Don Quixote into one more chivalric commitment.

## Themes

**Enchantment is always unmasked as human pettiness.** The chapter opens by dissolving the novel's central supernatural claim in one stroke: the 'enchanters' who scourged Doña Rodriguez and pinched Don Quixote were the duchess and Altisidora, driven by ordinary vanity and 'thirsting for vengeance' after being overheard. Cervantes repeats his structural joke — every marvel in the novel, examined closely, turns out to be theater staged by bored, cruel, or amused human beings. This is the deflationary machinery that makes the duke and duchess's whole game with Don Quixote and Sancho legible as cruelty rather than magic.

**Letters and trinkets manufacture rank out of thin air.** Teresa and Sanchica's transformation — from bare-legged laundress and skeptical wife to women planning coaches, hooped petticoats, and a 'high match' — is triggered entirely by a string of coral beads and two letters. Teresa's own line, 'as the time so the behaviour... when it was Sancho it was "Sancha;" when it is governor it's "señora,"' names the mechanism outright: status is performed through address and ornament, not earned through substance. The village's disbelief (the curate, the barber, Samson Carrasco) versus the physical evidence of the beads dramatizes how quickly material tokens can override social skepticism.

**Sancho's justice inverts the fool-sage hierarchy the novel has built around him.** The bridge-and-gallows riddle is a version of the liar's paradox dressed as law: a rigid statute cannot consistently classify the man who swears he is going to die on the gallows. Sancho's solution — let doubt resolve toward mercy rather than toward the letter of the law — is presented by the majordomo as superior even to Lycurgus, and Sancho himself credits it explicitly to Don Quixote's counsel. The scene is the clearest statement in the novel that Sancho's native shrewdness, once given real stakes, outperforms formal legal machinery. This also reads as a small parable about the limits of purely codified, legible rule: a law optimized for administrability collapses on a self-referential edge case, and only discretionary equity — a value outside the code — resolves it. **Don Quixote's letter is a serious mirror-of-princes in miniature.** Stripped of irony, his advice to Sancho — dress becoming your office, keep food abundant, make few proclamations but enforce every one you make, visit the gaols and markets in person, refuse to let any weakness be known — is coherent, humane statecraft advice, and it explicitly diagnoses the decay of unenforced authority: 'laws that threaten and are not enforced come to be like the log, the king of the frogs... that in time they despised and mounted upon.' An institution's legitimacy erodes the moment its instruments (proclamations, laws) stop being observed — a miniature decay sequence for legal authority embedded inside a comic prank narrative.

**The joke's machinery keeps grinding beneath the comedy.** Doctor Pedro Recio's starvation regimen, the majordomo's nightly reports to the duke and duchess, and the narrator's aside that 'those who were carrying on the joke against Sancho' were already arranging his dismissal all remind the reader that Sancho's government is an experiment being run on him, not an achievement he has earned space to keep. His own letter's hunger and dread ('I expect to take leave of it and my life together') is comic but also the first clear note of the government's coming collapse.

**Don Quixote's chivalric earnestness survives contact with the joke.** Even after being pinched and scratched by his hosts' cruelty, and even as the reader knows the whole household is amusing itself at his expense, Don Quixote responds to Doña Rodriguez's second sorrowful appeal with full solemnity, waiving his 'privilege of gentle blood' to fight a commoner in defense of a wronged daughter. The gap between what the duke and duchess intend (more sport) and what Don Quixote believes he is doing (justice) is the novel's most durable comic engine, and it is restated here without softening.

## Notable passages

The opening paragraph reframes the entire flogging episode as domestic jealousy rather than magic, the load-bearing move for the whole unit's demystification of enchantment. The bridge-and-gallows riddle and Sancho's resolution together form the unit's centerpiece: a formally impossible legal case solved by explicit appeal to mercy over rule. Don Quixote's line about unenforced proclamations becoming 'the log, the king of the frogs' is the sharpest political-theory statement in the passage. Teresa's 'as the time so the behaviour' captures the social-climbing comedy in a single proverb. And Don Quixote's vow to Doña Rodriguez shows his chivalric register undiminished even inside a household openly toying with him.
