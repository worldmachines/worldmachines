# The Trifaldi's Tale and the Flight of Clavileño

Spanning Chapters XXXVII through XLI, this unit completes the set-piece practical joke the Duke and Duchess stage for Don Quixote: the arrival of the Countess Trifaldi (the "Distressed Duenna"), her baroque, digression-riddled tale of the princess Antonomasia, the poet-seducer Don Clavijo, and the vengeful enchanter Malambruno who punishes the complicit duennas by bearding them; and then the farcical "flight" of Don Quixote and Sancho on the wooden horse Clavileño, blindfolded and buffeted by bellows and tow-fire while the entire court watches the two heroes narrate an imaginary sky-voyage that never leaves the garden. The episode is comedy built entirely from theatrical machinery — costumes, hidden stagehands, squibs, a scripted parchment — deployed by bored aristocrats to keep their knight-errant performing inside a plot they fully author.

## Themes

**Chivalry surviving only as costume.** The whole adventure is knowingly hollow: the Duke and Duchess know Malambruno, Clavileño, and the Trifaldi's whole kingdom of Kandy are fictions of their own devising, and the narrative repeatedly winks at this ("the duke and duchess...were ready to burst with laughter," the closing note that they were "beyond measure amused"). Chivalric romance here isn't believed in by anyone except Don Quixote — it survives purely as a genre of court entertainment, performed with full apparatus (heralds, fifes, veiled processions, a wooden Pegasus rigged with fireworks) by people who no longer need it to be true, only diverting. This is the decay sequence in miniature: an institution's forms (the errant knight, the distressed lady, the magical quest) persisting as elaborate ornament and spectacle long after their functional content has evaporated — [[decay-sequence]].

**Digression as characterization and satire.** The Trifaldi's tale is constructed almost entirely of self-interruption — apologies for wandering, mock-Latin superlatives ("Don Quixotissimus," "Panzissimo"), and a long excursus on why "amatory" poets deserve banishment that doubles as Cervantes's own satire of courtly love poetry's stock conceits ("in death I live," "in ice I burn"). The digression is the joke: a duenna trained in genteel excess cannot tell a straight story, and her rhetorical inflation is itself the comedy the Duke's household has commissioned.

**Sancho as the story's ballast and its skeptic-turned-liar.** Sancho supplies the practical counterweight throughout — questioning alguacils in Kandy, refusing to mount Clavileño for the sake of "stripping beards off duennas," and delivering an extended complaint that squires never get credited for the adventures they make possible ("Are they to have the fame of such as they go through, and we the labour?"). Yet once aloft (in the garden, blindfolded, thirty feet from where he started), Sancho invents an elaborate eyewitness account of tri-colored heavenly goats — matching, almost daring the reader to notice, Don Quixote's own unverifiable Cave of Montesinos vision from earlier in the book. The final line, where Don Quixote demands Sancho believe the Montesinos story in exchange for his own credulity about the goats, closes the episode on a knowing exchange of mutual fabrication.

## Notable passages

The unit opens with Sancho's blanket denunciation of duennas, seeded by a hated Toledo apothecary, which sets up the running duenna-antagonism that structures the whole episode. The Trifaldi's mid-tale meditation on treacherous love poetry is the closest thing to authorial commentary in her speech, folding Cervantes's critique of debased Petrarchan convention into the duenna's own confession of seduction. The unveiling of the bearded duennas is the tale's grotesque punchline, delivered with mock-tragic rhetoric ("it was an unlucky moment when we were born"). Sancho's protest about uncredited squires briefly breaks the comic register into something closer to class grievance. And the goat-counting fabrication, followed by Don Quixote's closing rejoinder about Montesinos, ties the whole flight sequence back to the novel's recurring question of who gets to certify what was seen and believed.
