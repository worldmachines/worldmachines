# The Author's Preface, Commendatory Verses, and Dedication to the Duke of Béjar

This unit is the front matter of Part I: a prose Preface in which Cervantes, addressing an "Idle reader" directly, stages his own anxiety about publishing a book with none of the scholarly ornaments expected of serious literature, then lets a visiting friend talk him out of the anxiety by supplying a satirical how-to for faking those ornaments; a sequence of comic commendatory sonnets and verses "spoken" by figures from the very romances the book will demolish (Amadis, Orlando, Don Belianis, Babieca and Rocinante, Sancho's counterpart Gandalin); and finally a brief, conventionally deferential Dedication of Part I to the Duke of Béjar.

## Themes

**The Preface performs the freedom it grants the reader.** Cervantes opens by dissolving the usual pieties of authorship — he refuses to beg pardon for the book's defects, insisting the "idle reader" owes the text nothing and is "master of it as much as the king of his taxes." This inverts the normal author-reader contract of the period and sets the book's ironic, self-aware register before the story itself begins.

**The friend's advice is a satire of humanist scholarly apparatus.** The invented dialogue lets Cervantes mock, point by point, the era's conventions for manufacturing literary authority: prefatory sonnets from titled patrons, marginal citations of Latin tags, annotations padding out a book with borrowed erudition (Goliath, the river Tagus, Cacus, Medea, Circe), and a catalogue of authors copied wholesale from another book "from A to Z." The friend is explicit that none of this needs to be true or earned — it only needs to "give a surprising look of authority to your book," since "no one will trouble himself to verify" it. This is a portrait of legibility-as-performance: institutional markers of erudition detached from any underlying substance, kept alive purely as ornament. It resonates with the WMP [[decay-sequence]] — the drift of infrastructure from Functional to Performative to purely Ornamental — applied here to the apparatus of humanist scholarship itself, staged self-consciously inside the very book that will use none of it.

**The stated purpose of the novel is named directly.** Amid the satire, the friend states the book's actual aim without irony: it is, "from beginning to end, an attack upon the books of chivalry," needing none of the borrowed authority of philosophers, scripture, or orators, and succeeding only if it destroys "that ill-founded edifice of the books of chivalry." This is the clearest programmatic statement in the front matter of what the whole novel is for.

**The commendatory verses continue the joke into verse.** Rather than solicit real sonnets from "dukes, marquises, counts, bishops," as convention demanded, Cervantes supplies mock-commendations voiced by characters out of the chivalric romances themselves — Amadis of Gaul, Don Belianis, Orlando Furioso, the horses Babieca and Rocinante in dialogue — turning the genre being satirized into its own ironic chorus of praise. Urganda the Unknown's poem even repeats the Preface's warning against borrowed Latin and false erudition, folding the same joke back into verse form.

**The Dedication closes the unit on a note of straight patronage convention.** Addressed to the Duke of Béjar with an elaborate title-string and the era's expected self-abasement ("the littleness of so humble a service"), it stands in unresolved tension with everything that precedes it — a real, unironized appeal for aristocratic protection sitting immediately after 250 lines of mockery aimed at exactly that kind of performed deference and borrowed authority.

## Notable passages

The opening address to the "Idle reader" sets the book's characteristic irony: the narrator disclaims fatherly pride in his "child," calls himself merely Don Quixote's stepfather, and grants the reader total interpretive freedom, framing the whole Preface as a refusal of customary authorial pleading. The friend's central passage on borrowed authority — instructing the narrator to copy out someone else's index of authors wholesale because "there will probably be some simple enough to believe" the borrowing is real — is the unit's sharpest statement of literary legibility as pure performance. Immediately after, the friend states the book's actual purpose without irony, naming it an attack on the books of chivalry that needs no philosophers, no scripture, no orators to succeed. Urganda the Unknown's commendatory poem mirrors this by warning the book itself against "ape not philosophy or wit" and borrowed Latin, extending the Preface's joke into a genuinely comic paratextual voice. The Dedication to the Duke of Béjar closes the unit in a register of unmediated patronage-seeking, its florid title-string and self-abasing tone standing as a real specimen of the very convention the Preface has just spent itself mocking.
