# Don Quixote Reads Himself into Knighthood

This unit covers the opening five chapters of *Don Quixote*: the introduction of the nameless La Mancha hidalgo undone by books of chivalry, his self-naming as Don Quixote and christening of Rocinante and Dulcinea, his furtive first sally, the tavern-keeper's mock dubbing ceremony, the Andres episode in which his idea of knightly justice backfires, his beating at the hands of Toledo traders and their muleteer, and his delirious carriage home, ending with the curate and barber resolving to put his library on trial.

## Themes

**Reading as a pathology that remakes reality.** Cervantes is explicit that the hidalgo's madness is a physiological consequence of reading: "what with little sleep and much reading his brains got so dry that he lost his wits." Once broken, his perception itself changes — he does not merely believe chivalric stories are inspiring, he believes them literally true ("it so possessed his mind that the whole fabric of invention and fancy he read of was true, that to him no history in the world had more reality in it"). Everything downstream — the inn read as a castle, the swineherd's horn read as a dwarf's trumpet, the traders read as knights-errant — follows mechanically from this initial category error. The unit dramatizes reading not as escape but as a totalizing overwrite of the world.

**Self-fashioning through naming.** Before he can act, the hidalgo must construct an identity: he spends four days naming his horse and eight more naming himself, then invents a lady (Aldonza Lorenzo, reforged as Dulcinea del Toboso) purely because the form of knighthood requires one — "a knight-errant without love was like a tree without leaves or fruit, or a body without a soul." This is world-building as pure formal completion: he is not falling in love or bonding with an animal, he is fabricating the minimum viable furniture of a genre so he can occupy its lead role. The care lavished on names ("lofty, sonorous, and significant") shows identity here as authored rather than discovered.

**Ritual persisting as pure form after its function is gone.** The dubbing scene is the clearest set piece: a fat, worldly innkeeper who has "already some suspicion of his guest's want of wits" performs the accolade with an account-book standing in for a missal, muttering "as if he was saying his prayers," while serving girls turned into ladies-in-waiting gird the sword with barely suppressed laughter. Knighthood here survives only as costume and gesture — the institution's Functional stage is centuries gone, and what's left is enacted as knowing parody by everyone except the one man taking it as real. It is a compact staging of an institution moving through something like a decay sequence: a form once functional, now performed cynically by its host and sincerely (deludedly) by its subject — worth flagging for [[decay-sequence]] as a literary precedent for the pattern, though Cervantes runs it as comedy rather than institutional history.

**Chivalric justice colliding with actual power.** The Andres episode is the unit's moral hinge. Don Quixote intervenes to stop a farmer flogging his shepherd boy, extracts a sworn promise of back wages, and rides off certain he has "righted the greatest wrong and grievance that ever injustice conceived." The text immediately punctures this: the farmer, freed of his oversight, ties Andres up again and flogs him worse, mocking the "undoer of wrongs" by name. Don Quixote's intervention has no purchase on the world because it addresses only the surface script (a sworn oath, invoked by the vocabulary of knighthood) without any mechanism of enforcement or attention to the local power relation it leaves intact — an idealized, imported order laid over real conditions it cannot actually reach or bind.

**A joke about historiography.** Even at this early stage Cervantes frames the tale as contested chronicle rather than settled fact — "authors who write on the subject" disagree about the hidalgo's true surname, and "writers there are who say the first adventure he met with was that of Puerto Lapice; others say it was that of the windmills." The narrator adjudicates between competing sources like a historian rather than an omniscient author, a running gag that will expand later in the novel into the fiction of the Moorish chronicler Cide Hamete Benengeli.

## Notable passages

The long paragraph on Feliciano de Silva's tangled prose ("the reason of the unreason with which my reason is afflicted...") establishes the specific *kind* of reading that breaks him — not heroic content but syntactic knots he compulsively tries to "worm the meaning out of." The passage pivoting to his resolve — "his wits being quite gone, he hit upon the strangest notion that ever madman in this world hit upon" — states the novel's premise as bluntly as it will ever be stated. The inn-as-castle passage shows the perceptual machinery of his madness operating in real time on a mundane setting. The mock-dubbing passage is the comic set piece of institutional form without institutional substance. The Andres reversal is the unit's sharpest irony, undercutting the chivalric register with a single brutal aftermath. And the curate's vow to burn the library by "public judgment" tomorrow closes the unit by naming the cause the household believes is at the root of everything: not the man but his books.
