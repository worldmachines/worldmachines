# Leaving the Castle: Freedom, Folly, and the False Don Quixote

Spanning chapters LVII through LIX, this unit carries Don Quixote and Sancho out of the duke and duchess's castle and back onto the open road, where the narrative alternates between set-piece rhetoric and slapstick comeuppance: Altisidora's mock-elegiac reproach and the garter joke; Don Quixote's grand declaration on freedom; a chance meeting with laborers carrying carved images of warrior-saints that prompts him to reflect on his own vocation; a disastrous public vow of gallantry toward a troupe of aristocrats playing at pastoral shepherds, punished by a herd of bulls; and, at an inn, the celebrated encounter with two gentlemen reading Avellaneda's counterfeit continuation of his own history, which sends Don Quixote into genuine indignation and reroutes his journey from Saragossa to Barcelona.

## Themes

**Freedom as the highest good, captivity as the deepest evil.** Once clear of the castle's "idleness" and obligatory hospitality, Don Quixote delivers an extended discourse to Sancho on liberty: even the duke's banquets felt like hunger to him because gratitude is itself a debt that "checks the independence of the spirit." The speech reads as a Cervantine gloss on something close to Spinoza's [[Conatus]] — the striving to persist and act from one's own nature rather than another's — and frames captivity (even gilded, hospitable captivity) as capture in the WMP sense: a force that suppresses self-directed persistence by making the captive perpetually answerable to a patron. [[capture-resistance]] is the throughline of the whole unit, resurfacing later in a very different register.

**Gratitude as an economy that must be settled.** Sancho's parting soliloquy on his ex-governorship ("naked I was born, naked I find myself, I neither lose nor gain") and Don Quixote's later formal oration to the mock-shepherdesses on ingratitude as the worst of sins both treat social bonds as ledgers requiring active discharge — a minor but persistent theme of the unit, comically literalized when Don Quixote's discharge of the debt (his vow to fight all comers on the shepherdesses' behalf) is answered not by a rival knight but by a herd of bulls.

**Comic deflation of chivalric grandiosity.** The unit repeats Cervantes's signature rhythm: exalted rhetoric immediately punished by physical indignity. Don Quixote's eloquent tribute to the warrior-saints (Saint George, Saint Martin, Santiago Matamoros, Saint Paul) casts them as fellow knights-errant who "won heaven by force of arms" while he, a sinner, fights with merely human ones — a moment of real self-reflection, situating him as heir to an older order of sacralized violence that has itself passed into ornament, carried on laborers' shoulders to be installed in a village retablo. Days later, his solemn challenge in the middle of the Saragossa road ("here I await him") is answered not by combat but by a stampeding drove of bulls that flattens knight, squire, and both mounts in the dirt — humiliation without even the dignity of an adversary.

**The self against its counterfeit.** The unit's most consequential episode is metafictional: overhearing Don Jeronimo and Don Juan discuss the newly published (fictitious, but historically real — Avellaneda's 1614) "Second Part" of his own history, Don Quixote is enraged less by the book's existence than by its claim that he has been cured of his love for Dulcinea and by its errors of fact (Sancho's wife renamed "Mari Gutierrez"). He does not merely dispute the book — he acts on the dispute, changing his real itinerary from Saragossa to Barcelona expressly to "expose to the world the lie of this new history writer." This is capture-resistance rendered as plot mechanics: a character asserting his own persistence and authorship against an unauthorized double that would rewrite his defining commitments. Cervantes has Don Juan invoke Apelles' exclusive right to paint Alexander's portrait — a direct claim that only Cide Hamete Benengeli's original may authentically "paint" Don Quixote, and that constancy to Dulcinea is the load-bearing trait a counterfeit gets wrong precisely because it cannot understand what makes the original cohere.

## Notable passages

Altisidora's sung reproach (the "Bireno, Æneas" refrain) sets the register for the whole leave-taking: mock-tragic, theatrical, and immediately subjected to a farcical inquiry into stolen garters that both Don Quixote and Sancho handle with straight-faced gravity. Sancho's "naked I was born" soliloquy closes his brief governorship with resigned equanimity, a counterpoint to his master's later, angrier assertion of self against Avellaneda's fabrication. The freedom speech and the warrior-saints reflection both show Don Quixote at his most lucid, reasoning outward from his own condition to general truths about liberty and vocation — moments the surrounding slapstick (garters, bulls) exists partly to puncture. The inn-scene exchange with Don Jeronimo and Don Juan is the unit's centerpiece: a fictional character defending his own reality against a rival fiction, and doing so by altering the story's actual future course, one of literature's earliest and most self-aware performances of a protagonist refusing to be captured by an imposed narrative.
