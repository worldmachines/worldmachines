# Enchantment, Consensus, and the Law of Chivalry at the Inn

This unit closes Chapter XLIII, runs through Chapter XLIV, and carries Chapter XLV up to Don Quixote's unfinished tirade against the Holy Brotherhood. Doña Clara, overhearing the muleteer's song, confesses to Dorothea that the singer is Don Luis, a wealthy neighbor's son who has followed her from Madrid in disguise; meanwhile Maritornes and the innkeeper's daughter play a cruel prank on Don Quixote, tying his wrist to a loft door so he hangs half-suspended through the night, certain he is enchanted. Daylight brings Don Luis's father's servants to retrieve him, a second confrontation over the ownership of a basin/helmet and a pack-saddle/caparison (stolen earlier from a barber), and finally a general brawl that culminates in an officer of the Holy Brotherhood recognizing Don Quixote as the man wanted for freeing the galley slaves — cutting off, mid-declamation, with Don Quixote's defiant claim that knight-errantry is a law unto itself.

## Themes

**A second courtship shadows the main one.** Clara and Don Luis's story — secret signs through curtained windows, a disguise assumed for love, a father whose wealth makes the match seem impossible — deliberately echoes and miniaturizes the Cardenio–Luscinda–Dorothea–Fernando tangle that dominates the surrounding chapters. Clara's breathless, half-formed vocabulary ("I know not how," "I don't know how the devil this has come about") marks her as still nearly a child, which makes Dorothea's amused, protective response ring true; the episode is Cervantes multiplying his marriage-plot machinery rather than resolving it yet.

**Farce repeats and escalates the inn's earlier humiliations.** Maritornes' prank against Don Quixote is a direct sequel to the blanket-tossing and earlier misadventures at this same inn: physical comedy staged specifically because the women know "the weak point of Don Quixote's humour." The image of him standing rigid on Rocinante's saddle, arm through a hole in the wall, straining toward the ground "like those undergoing the torture of the strappado," is grotesque and pitiable at once — chivalric dignity reduced to a body in mechanical distress.

**Don Quixote's explanatory frame absorbs every disconfirmation.** Rather than question his premises, Don Quixote interprets his humiliating night as proof of enchantment, invoking "a settled point with knights-errant" that a failed adventure simply belongs to someone else and need not be retried. This is not merely comic delusion; it is a closed rule-system that converts any evidence — however damning — into confirmation of itself, a miniature of how a totalizing paradigm assimilates anomalies rather than breaking under them.

**Truth by acclamation, not by evidence.** The dispute over whether a barber's basin is Mambrino's helmet, and a pack-saddle a horse's caparison, is resolved not by inspecting the objects but by Don Fernando privately polling the company and then simply declaring the vote against the barber. The scene is a sharp, comic literalization of consensus manufactured through social pressure and in-jokes rather than through what is actually seen — a small satire of how a shared fiction, once enough people perform belief in it, hardens into operative fact ("the pack-saddle remained a caparison till the day of judgment"). It resonates with how `[[world-machines]]` frame legitimacy: not a property inherent in things but a settlement enforced by an interlocking group of believers, comic here because the stakes are trivial and the mechanism is transparent.

**Old order versus new jurisdiction.** The chapter's closing brawl pits Don Quixote's self-authorizing chivalric code against the Holy Brotherhood's written warrant — bureaucratic, textual, backed by the Crown's police apparatus. Don Quixote's cut-off speech insists knights-errant are "independent of all jurisdictions, that their law is their sword, their charter their prowess, and their edicts their will," a declaration of total exemption from tax, toll, and legal process. Read against `[[capture-resistance]]`, it is a fading world-machine's last unconditional assertion of autonomy against an encroaching regime of legibility and enforcement — the officer with his warrant, comparing description to face, is proto-modern administrative apparatus meeting a knight who denies its authority can even reach him.

## Notable passages

Clara's recognition of her disguised suitor at the inn door crystallizes the courtship subplot: she knows him "in the dress of a muleteer," trusting only the image "graven on my heart," which frames the whole episode's stakes before her father's servants arrive to end it.

The Maritornes prank passage — Don Quixote's grandiloquent offer of his hand ("this scourge of the evil-doers of the earth") followed immediately by her binding it to the door bolt — is the unit's clearest set-piece of dramatic irony: his heroic diction collides with a servant's practical cruelty in the same breath.

The enchantment-logic passage shows Don Quixote reasoning his way out of any need to revise his beliefs, a compact statement of the self-sealing rule system chivalric romance gives him.

Don Fernando's vote-taking scene is the unit's most pointed piece of social comedy: a decision about physical fact settled by canvassing opinion in secret and then announcing the desired result as if it were simply true.

The closing tirade, broken off by the narrative, is Don Quixote at his most totalizing — asserting that no toll, tax, court, or warrant has power over a knight-errant, immediately before the Holy Brotherhood's arrest warrant proves otherwise.
