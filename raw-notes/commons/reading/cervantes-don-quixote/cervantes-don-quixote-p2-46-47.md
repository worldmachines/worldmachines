# The Cat Fright and the First Trials of Sancho's Government

This unit spans the end of Chapter XLVI and the start of Chapter XLVII. Don Quixote, still tormented by Altisidora's feigned love, serenades her with a ballad of constancy to Dulcinea; the duke and duchess reward his devotion with a staged terror — a cord of bells and a sackful of bell-tailed cats unleashed into his chamber. One cat mauls his face before the duke pulls it free, and Don Quixote spends the ballad's aftermath bedridden and bandaged, his wounds tended by a mock-tender Altisidora who threatens that his suffering will continue unless Sancho breaks his penitential vow to free Dulcinea. The narrative then turns, as promised, to Sancho, newly installed as governor of the invented "island" of Barataria. His inaugural banquet is ruined by Doctor Pedro Recio de Aguero, whose invocation of Hippocratic aphorisms empties every dish before Sancho can taste it; Sancho's fury at the doctor gives way to a forged alarm-letter from the duke and then to an absurd farmer's petition — for a marriage recommendation and six hundred ducats — that ends in Sancho threatening to brain the petitioner with his chair.

## Themes

**Cruelty dressed as comedy.** The cat-and-bells prank is engineered entertainment for an aristocratic household, and Cervantes is careful to note its cost: it is not merely funny but genuinely harmful, leaving Don Quixote "with a face as full of holes as a sieve" and confined to bed for five days, an outcome that leaves even its authors "greatly grieved." The duke and duchess's amusement depends on real physical damage to their guest, a running motif of this middle section of the novel in which Don Quixote's dignity is sacrificed for aristocratic sport.

**Don Quixote's unbreakable interpretive frame.** Attacked by ordinary house cats, Don Quixote experiences them only as "malignant enchanters" and "witchcraft-working rabble." His chivalric ontology absorbs the incident without strain — pain and humiliation are simply reprocessed as proof of the sorcerers' malice, never as evidence against his worldview. He even declines to credit the actual danger afterward, thanking the duke not out of fear of "that bell-ringing rabble of enchanters in cat shape" but for the couple's good intentions. The delusion is total and self-sealing.

**Sancho's government as instant collision with administrative capture.** No sooner is Sancho seated at the head of his banquet table than an unelected "physician," Doctor Pedro Recio, begins vetoing every dish with a wand-tap, citing Hippocrates against Sancho's own hunger and judgment. The scene stages a small, comic version of legibility asserting itself over lived need: a body's plain appetite is overruled by inherited textual authority ("our master Hippocrates... says in one of his aphorisms") applied as bureaucratic protocol, regardless of the governed subject's actual condition. Sancho's blunt insistence — "I can't go on without eating" — and his threat to "take a cudgel" to the doctor read as a small assertion of bodily persistence against an apparatus that would rather manage him abstractly than feed him. This is Sancho's conatus meeting his first capture regime, and it will recur across his governorship. [[Conatus]] [[capture-resistance]] [[legibility-as-machine-core]]

**Satire of petition and patronage.** The farmer's petition — an elaborately, grotesquely detailed marriage suit that turns out to be a request for six hundred ducats — parodies the theater of governance itself: florid rhetoric, courtly deference ("I humble myself before him"), and a punchline reveal of naked self-interest. Sancho's explosive response, threatening to "lay your head open with this chair," shows a governor whose plain-spoken volatility is itself a kind of honesty amid a court performance staged, unbeknownst to him, entirely for the amusement of his masters.

## Notable passages

The cat attack itself is rendered with genuine violence beneath the comedy — candles extinguished, a legion of devils evoked, and Don Quixote's declaration of chivalric invulnerability collapsing into a cat fastened to his nose. Altisidora's bedside taunt afterward reveals the manipulative core of the duke and duchess's ongoing plot: her tenderness is a lever aimed at coercing Sancho's self-flagellation, tying Don Quixote's suffering directly to the mock-enchantment of Dulcinea. In the Barataria scenes, Doctor Pedro Recio's speech is the unit's sharpest satire — an entire philosophy of denial delivered in the register of expert care — and Sancho's retort, ending "a trade that does not feed its master is not worth two beans," is his first governing principle stated in his own voice. The farmer's petition, and Sancho's eruption at its end, closes the unit on the note that will define his governorship: comic, quick-tempered, and startlingly clear-eyed about the absurdities put in front of him.
