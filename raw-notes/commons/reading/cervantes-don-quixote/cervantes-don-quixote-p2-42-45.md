# Don Quixote's Counsels and Sancho's First Judgments as Governor

This unit spans chapters XLII through XLV: Don Quixote's two sets of counsels to Sancho on the eve of his government (one on governing the mind, one on governing the body and manners), Sancho's ceremonial departure for the island of Barataria, a nighttime interlude in which the duchess's maid Altisidora serenades a mortified Don Quixote in mock courtly love, and finally Sancho's arrival at Barataria and his first three acts of judgment as governor—each resolved not through law or literacy (Sancho cannot read) but through proverb-laced common sense that repeatedly outperforms expectation.

## Themes

**Virtue as achievement against blood as inheritance.** Don Quixote's core counsel to Sancho is not tactical but ethical: know thyself, and do not be ashamed of low birth, because virtue is something *acquired* while blood is merely *received*. This is the unit's most explicit ideological content—a leveling claim that dignity of office does not require dignity of origin, delivered by a knight whose entire self-conception rests on inherited chivalric idiom. The irony is pointed: Don Quixote, devoted to an archaic aristocratic fantasy, is the one instructing the peasant Sancho that "blood is an inheritance, but virtue an acquisition."

**Mercy over rigor as the substance of good government.** The second block of moral counsels (fear God, know thyself) gives way to a dense catalogue of judicial advice: weep with the poor man but do not let his tears outweigh justice; do not press the utmost rigour of the law; let the staff of justice swerve toward mercy rather than gifts; do not let passion or a woman's tears sway judgment; punish in deed but not in word. Don Quixote explicitly warns against governing "by arbitrary law, which is so much favoured by ignorant men who plume themselves on cleverness"—a warning that reads as a critique of legalistic formalism divorced from equity. This tension between codified, "legible" law and situational, mercy-tempered judgment runs straight through into Sancho's actual rulings, which resonate with the World Machines idea of [[legibility-as-machine-core]]: Sancho, illiterate and outside the apparatus of written law (he cannot even read the inscription of his own installation on the wall), governs by reading intention and character rather than by rule—and is repeatedly vindicated.

**The comic undercutting of counsel by character.** The narrator explicitly flags Don Quixote's split nature here: lucid and wise on every subject except chivalry, where he lapses into nonsense. The second, "bodily" set of counsels (trim your nails, don't eruct, wear hose of a certain length, stop stringing proverbs) plays this split for comedy—grave moral wisdom collapsing into fussy etiquette, capped by Don Quixote losing his temper at Sancho's proverb-flood ("sixty thousand devils fly away with thee and thy proverbs!") only to be undone by his own admission that constructing one apt proverb costs him as much effort as digging. Sancho's proverbs are less noise than an alternate epistemology—a densely compressed folk archive that, however garbled in application, keeps producing right answers.

**Sancho's stated ethics of office.** Before ever ruling on a case, Sancho articulates his own governing principle unprompted: he would rather remain "simple Sancho" than be governor if governing risks damning him, declaring he'd "rather go Sancho to heaven / than governor to hell." Don Quixote's response—that for this single answer alone Sancho deserves to govern a thousand islands—reframes the entire episode: right governance is grounded not in learning or lineage but in a disposition that values the soul's integrity over office. This is the payoff of the counsel scene: the pupil already possesses, natively, the disposition the teacher spent two chapters trying to instill.

**Judgment without legibility.** Sancho's three rulings at Barataria (the cane concealing the ten gold-crowns, the false accusation resolved by testing the plaintiff's grip on a purse, the five caps dispute resolved by splitting the loss) are folk-wisdom set-pieces: no documents, no precedent, no literacy—only close observation of behavior (the old man's telling gesture with the stick; the woman's ferocious defense of a purse undermining her claim of having been overpowered) and a willingness to render blunt, sometimes harsh equity. The narrator repeatedly notes the bystanders' astonishment that a man taken for a fool renders judgments worthy of Solomon—restating, in miniature, the novel's abiding question of where wisdom actually resides.

## Notable passages

The counsel on virtue versus birth ("Glory in thy humble birth, Sancho...") is the unit's ethical keystone, explicitly ranking acquired virtue above inherited status. The long judicial counsel ("Let the tears of the poor man find with thee more compassion, but not more justice...") is Don Quixote at his most systematically wise, articulating a theory of merciful governance that Sancho will shortly enact. Sancho's declaration that he would rather "go Sancho to heaven than governor to hell" is the moral hinge of the whole episode, earning Don Quixote's most unqualified praise. Don Quixote's proverb-tirade ("Oh, God's curse upon thee, Sancho!") supplies the comic counterweight, undercutting his own gravity. Finally, the cane-and-gold-crowns reveal and the purse verdict ("if you had shown as much... spirit and vigour in defending your body as you have shown in defending that purse") are the concrete demonstrations of folk judgment succeeding where formal legal apparatus is absent—Sancho's illiteracy notwithstanding.
