# The Cure That Wasn't: Testing Don Quixote's Sanity and Discovering His Fame

This unit opens the Second Part of Cervantes's history with four linked chapters. The curate and barber, after a month's absence, visit Don Quixote to gauge whether his madness has lifted; a discussion of the Turkish threat draws out his old chivalric enthusiasm, and the barber's cautionary tale of a "cured" madman in the Seville asylum only provokes a full-throated defense of knight-errantry as a lost golden age. Sancho then storms the house past the niece and housekeeper, and in private conversation reports to Don Quixote the village's divided opinion of him — mad, but variously droll, unlucky, or meddling. The chapters pivot on a great metafictional surprise: Sancho reveals that their first sally has already been printed as a book, and the bachelor Samson Carrasco arrives to discuss its reception, its errors, and its omissions with author and squire alike. The unit closes with Don Quixote resolving on a second sally toward Zaragoza, and Sancho negotiating, with surprising shrewdness, the terms of his continued service.

## Themes

**Diagnosis by parable, not proof.** The curate and barber do not confront Don Quixote's madness directly; they probe it obliquely, first through state-craft conversation, then through the barber's story of the Seville licentiate who is pronounced sane by a chaplain only to be denounced as still mad by another inmate claiming to be Jupiter. Don Quixote recognizes the trap instantly ("Master shaver, master shaver! how blind is he who cannot see through a sieve") and turns the parable back on its teller — a demonstration that his reasoning faculties are intact even as his central delusion is untouched. The episode dramatizes how difficult it is to legibly diagnose a mind that reasons perfectly well from a false premise; sanity here is not a single measurable line but a contested judgment, foreshadowed by the frame-within-frame of madmen judging madmen.

**The golden age as a refusal of the present.** Don Quixote's defense of chivalry — "our depraved age does not deserve to enjoy such a blessing as those ages enjoyed" — is explicitly a temporal argument: the present is degenerate ("sloth triumphs over energy... theory over practice in arms") against an idealized past he insists on re-enacting. This is less nostalgia than a stubborn refusal to be governed by his era's clock; his conatus (the drive to persist as himself) expresses itself as a bid to keep an anachronistic order of being alive against a world that has moved on — an image close to the WMP idea of the [[untimely]], the surplus that disciplinary time cannot fully absorb or extinguish.

**Master and man as one mechanism.** The curate's aside that knight and squire "had both been cast in the same mould" and Don Quixote's own head-and-members analogy (borrowed from scholastic maxim, *quando caput dolet*) frame Sancho and Don Quixote's madness and simplicity as functionally interdependent — neither is intelligible without the other. Sancho's rebuttal, that his head "was on the other side of the wall" while he was blanketed, punctures the analogy with concrete bodily logic, establishing the pattern that will recur throughout Part Two: Sancho's earthy empiricism corrects his master's abstractions without ever dissolving the partnership.

**Reputation as an uncontrollable, distributed judgment.** Asked to report village opinion honestly, Sancho delivers a taxonomy of complaint — the common people call him mad, the hidalgos resent his assumed "Don," the caballeros resent squire hidalgos generally — and mixed epithets ("mad but droll," "valiant but unlucky," "courteous but meddling"). Don Quixote's response, cataloguing the calumnies suffered by Caesar, Alexander, and Hercules, reframes slander as the necessary tax on eminence. The passage stages fame/reputation as something that assembles itself out of many independent, uncoordinated local judgments rather than descending from any single verdict — a shape distantly reminiscent of the WMP's [[history-machine]] idea that paradigms and reputations accrete distributedly before they become visible as a single fact.

**The book discovers its own characters.** The unit's central event is metafictional: Sancho learns from the newly graduated Bartholomew Carrasco's son that Part One is already in print, authored (he garbles the name) by "Cide Hamete Berengena." Don Quixote and Samson Carrasco's ensuing conversation about the book's reception — readers who complain about the interpolated novel "The Ill-advised Curiosity," the continuity error over Dapple's theft, the unresolved hundred crowns — turns Cervantes's own text into an object of in-world literary criticism. Samson's distinction, "it is one thing to write as a poet, another to write as a historian... the historian has to write them down, not as they ought to have been, but as they were," states the novel's central epistemological joke: a self-consciously fictional "true history" arguing for historiographical fidelity.

## Notable passages

The barber's parable reaches its comic-ominous climax when the madman claiming to be Jupiter threatens three years of drought on Seville for releasing a man he insists is still mad — a passage whose absurdist authority ("I mad, I disordered, I bound!") mirrors the very question the curate and barber are using it to probe.

Don Quixote's lament for the vanished chivalric age gives the fullest statement of his creed in this unit: a vision of virtue as a lost, superior order of being that his own conduct is meant to restore against a debased present.

The curate's private verdict that knight and squire were "cast in the same mould" crystallizes the pair's structural interdependence just before their own conversation demonstrates it — Don Quixote's scholastic head-and-members logic met by Sancho's blunt bodily counter-example.

Sancho's unflinching relay of village gossip — "the common people consider your worship a mighty great madman, and me no less a fool" — is the unit's clearest instance of Don Quixote's demanded, undecorated truth-telling actually being delivered, and sets up his stoic response about calumny and eminence.

Samson Carrasco's formula distinguishing poetic invention from historical duty is the unit's thematic keystone, articulating the mock-historiographical stance the whole Cide Hamete Benengeli framing device depends on.
