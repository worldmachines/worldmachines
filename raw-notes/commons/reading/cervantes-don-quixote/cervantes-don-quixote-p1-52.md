# The Fall Before the Penitents, and the Mock Epitaphs of Argamasilla

This is the closing chapter of Part One (1605): the goatherd Eugenio's tale ends the pastoral interlude, and after Don Quixote's chivalrous but overwrought offer of aid provokes a brawl (a thrown loaf, a chokehold, a pile-on that leaves Don Quixote's face "streamed with blood"), a trumpet interrupts the fight — a village rain-procession carrying a black-draped image of the Virgin. Don Quixote reads it as an abduction, charges the penitents despite Sancho's frantic warnings, is knocked flat by a stick-wielding bearer, and is carried home on an ox-cart to conclude his first sally. The chapter — and Part One — closes with a metafictional flourish: a found leaden box of worm-eaten parchment containing sonnets and epitaphs "by the Academicians of Argamasilla" on Don Quixote, Dulcinea, and Rocinante, ending with the Italian tag promising another poet may yet "sing with a better plectrum."

## Themes

**Comic violence as demotion, not tragedy.** The goatherd fight and the penitent-charge are structurally identical: Don Quixote's chivalric idiom (redressing wrongs, defending ladies, demanding "estimation" for knight-errantry) collides with an uncomprehending literal world that answers not with irony but with fists and a forked stick. Cervantes refuses to let either scene turn tragic — the canon and curate are "bursting with laughter," the officers "capering with delight," hissing the brawlers on "as they do dogs." The violence deflates rather than ennobles; it is the novel's mechanism for repeatedly resetting Don Quixote's fantasy against unyielding matter.

**Two incompatible orders sharing one field.** The rain-procession scene stages, almost schematically, the friction of two total systems occupying the same ground at once: the villagers' penitential Catholic order (organized around drought, supplication, and a real communal crisis) and Don Quixote's chivalric order (organized around captive ladies and enchanter-villains). Neither side can parse the other's frame — the ecclesiastic dismisses him as too strange to bother answering, Don Quixote cannot hear Sancho's plain correction ("that is a procession of penitents... the blessed image of the immaculate Virgin") because it does not compute inside his operating system. This is a compact instance of what [[world-machines]] calls friction between coexisting frameworks — not a war of ideas but a mutual illegibility that resolves only through blunt force (the stick) rather than persuasion.

**Sancho's persistence and its limits.** Sancho's lament over the "dead" knight ("Oh flower of chivalry... Oh pride of thy race") is comic in form — mock-heroic diction draped over a man merely stunned — but it is also the chapter's most sincere passage: Sancho has absorbed enough of Don Quixote's idiom to eulogize him in it. Later, at home, Sancho's own reckoning with his wife Teresa is unsentimental and mixed: he admits "out of some I came blanketed, and out of others belaboured," that "ninety-nine" adventures in a hundred turn out badly, yet insists there is "nothing in the world more delightful" than the seeking itself. This is conatus-adjacent — a striving to persist in and expand a chosen way of being that survives repeated material defeat, closer to [[Conatus]] than to naive optimism; Sancho keeps the register of hope ("you will soon see me a count") even while cataloguing the costs.

**Ironic monumentalization.** The closing apparatus — a discovered box, worm-eaten parchment, an editorial committee of fictitious "Academicians" producing epitaphs — parodies the machinery of textual authentication and posthumous canonization at the exact moment the "first sally" narrative ends. Don Quixote, Dulcinea, and Rocinante are each given a sonnet that inflates them into classical-epic company (Amadis, Jason, Mars, Bellona) while simultaneously undercutting the inflation (Dulcinea, in her epitaph, is merely "plump" and now "ashes and dust"). It is the decay-sequence in miniature: a living, disputed figure instantly rendered ornamental and monumental in mock-Latin apparatus, even as the narrator promises a further sally to come.

## Notable passages

The goatherd brawl (Don Quixote's insult, the thrown loaf, the pile-on ending in blood on both faces) shows Cervantes' preferred register for puncturing his hero: not villainy but slapstick indignity, adjudicated by an audience that finds it purely entertaining. Don Quixote's cavalry speech to the "noble company" before charging the penitents ("ye shall see how important it is that there should be knights in the world") is the clearest statement in the unit of his self-appointed stakes — the honor of knight-errantry itself, not just one damsel — immediately undercut by the narrator's aside that Rocinante is, for once, actually galloping, the sheer novelty flagging how rarely Don Quixote's resolve is matched by his horse's capacity. Sancho's lament over his master's fall repurposes chivalric high style sincerely rather than satirically, marking a shift in how thoroughly Sancho has been absorbed into Don Quixote's language. Sancho's later domestic speech to Teresa is the chapter's clearest first-person cost-benefit accounting of squirehood. The Academicians' epitaph on Don Quixote closes Part One by turning the still-living, still-mad knight into an object of premature commemoration, a joke whose target is as much the conventions of chivalric and elegiac verse as it is Don Quixote himself.
