# Two Tales in the Sierra Morena: Cardenio Concludes, Dorothea Begins

Chapter XXVII opens with the curate and barber rigging themselves out — she a damsel-in-distress, he her squire — to trick Don Quixote out of his mock-penance, but their scheme is repeatedly deferred: first by the curate's crisis of clerical propriety, then by a mysterious song, then by Cardenio himself, who at last finishes the story of Luscinda's forced betrothal to Don Fernando that had been interrupted earlier in the novel. Chapter XXVIII abandons the rescue plot almost entirely for a second embedded tale: the three men discover a beautiful young woman disguised as a peasant boy bathing her feet in a mountain brook, and she turns out to be Dorothea, a rich farmer's daughter seduced and secretly pledged by the very same Don Fernando who abandoned her to marry Luscinda.

## Themes

**Disguise as both concealment and involuntary betrayal.** The unit is bookended by two costume set-pieces — the curate's petticoat-and-mask getup and Dorothea's peasant-boy dress — and in both cases the disguise partially fails against the body's own legibility. The curate balks at wearing women's clothes as "indecorous" for a priest and swaps roles with the barber; Dorothea's masculine dress cannot hide feet "like two pieces of shining crystal" or hair that unspools "like the beams of the sun," and it is precisely these signs that give her away to her observers. Cervantes repeatedly stages identity as something that leaks out through the body even when clothing tries to suppress it — a minor but persistent motif of concealment defeated by involuntary disclosure.

**The interrupted tale as narrative architecture.** Cardenio's story, left hanging chapters earlier when "Master Elisabad" derailed Don Quixote's attention, is finally allowed to run to its end here — the intercepted letter, the sham errand to fetch horse-money, Luscinda's fainting "I will," and the discovered dagger-note. But the completed tale is itself interrupted almost immediately by a second voice in the wilderness (Dorothea's), so that one embedded narrative hands off to another without the frame story (Don Quixote's rescue) advancing at all. This nested, deferred structure — story interrupting story interrupting quest — is the chapter's real formal subject as much as anything that happens in it.

**Seduction under promise of marriage as institutional violence.** Both Luscinda's forced "yes" at the altar and Dorothea's coerced night-time betrothal turn on the same device: Don Fernando extracts consent (a spoken vow, a ring, invoked saints and images as witnesses) that mimics the legal and religious form of marriage while gutting its substance. Dorothea's own speech to him — refusing to yield her honor "though I hold my body clasped in your arms" — makes the point explicit: bodily capture is not the same as capitulation, and she extracts a formal oath from him precisely because she understands that form, not sentiment, is what obligates a man of his rank. The two women's stories are structurally identical (same seducer, same broken promise, same fainting/found-document climax), which underlines that this is a pattern of predation enabled by class privilege and social protocol, not a series of accidents.

**Retreat into the Sierra as a space outside ordinary jurisdiction.** Cardenio's cork-tree hollow and Dorothea's flight into the same mountains both function as a deliberate withdrawal from a social order that has failed them — she flees a father's house, a public crier's reward notice, and a treacherous servant's assault (whom she herself pushes over a precipice) before finding refuge in the wild. The Sierra Morena here reads less as backdrop than as a terroir-defended retreat where wronged parties can exist unlegible to the honor economy that injured them, faintly resonant with the World Machines idea of a [[The Thicket]] — an opaque, self-defended interior space outside a capturing order — though Cervantes's version is involuntary and precarious rather than constructed.

## Notable passages

The curate's onset of scruple ("it was an indecorous thing for a priest to dress himself that way") is a small but telling comic beat — even inside a rescue plot built entirely on deception, decorum reasserts itself and forces a mid-journey renegotiation of roles. The interpolated song ("Disdain... Jealousies... Absence") and sonnet on false Friendship, sung by an unseen voice before Cardenio is found, do double duty: they are Cardenio's own lyric self-diagnosis and a formal set-piece of the pastoral-romance mode Cervantes is simultaneously indulging and needling. Cardenio's opening address to his rescuers — "I see plainly, sirs, whoever you may be, that Heaven... sends me... those who seek to draw me away" — frames his own madness and self-narration with unusual lucidity, insisting that his hearers let him tell the story rather than simply prescribe a cure. The wedding-hall climax (Luscinda's faint "I will," the ring, her collapse) is the hinge on which both later tragedies turn. And Dorothea's own words to Don Fernando in the dark of her chamber — refusing to equate bodily helplessness with consent, and later physically shoving her would-be assailant servant off a precipice — give her, unlike Luscinda (who acts only through a hidden dagger and a fainting fit), a directly assertive agency that the chapter foregrounds as her defining trait.
