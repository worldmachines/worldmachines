# The Caging of Don Quixote and the Canon's Case Against the Romances

This unit closes out the inn's tangle of subplots (the Holy Brotherhood's warrant, Mambrino's helmet, the landlord's reckoning, the reunion of Dorothea and Don Fernando, Luscinda and Cardenio, Doña Clara and Don Luis) and then pivots the novel's center of gravity: the curate and barber trick Don Quixote into a wooden cage under the fiction that he is "enchanted," and on the road home they fall in with a Canon of Toledo who, hearing the whole story, delivers a lengthy critique of the improbability of books of chivalry and, by extension, of the plays currently packing Spanish theatres.

## Themes

**Enchantment as a consensual fiction engineered by the sane.** The curate's stratagem is a piece of social engineering: friends and strangers alike don disguises, a barber intones a mock-heroic prophecy, and the entire cast colludes in producing "evidence" tailored to confirm what Don Quixote already believes. It works because it is addressed to his interpretive frame rather than his senses — he is not fooled so much as furnished with material his madness can process into confirmation. The chapter frames this as therapeutic ("the curate be able to take his madness in hand at home"), but it is also a study in how a fabricated narrative, skillfully staged, can substitute for coercion in managing a person who cannot otherwise be controlled.

**A self-sealing epistemology.** Sancho mounts an empirical case against enchantment — enchanted people don't eat, sleep, or need the privy, and this devil "smells of amber" rather than brimstone — but Don Quixote's system has an answer for every disconfirmation: enchanters are crafty, they can disguise their scent, and they can even wear the faces of his own neighbors precisely in order to sow the doubt Sancho is now voicing. The passage where he explains that the curate and barber walking beside the cart only *appear* to be the curate and barber is a small, exact portrait of an unfalsifiable belief structure — any counter-evidence is reclassified as further proof of the conspiracy.

**The Canon's defense of verisimilitude — a literary manifesto planted mid-novel.** The Canon of Toledo, revealed only by his badge as "a canon of Toledo and master of the others," delivers Cervantes' most sustained piece of literary theory in the book so far: romances fail not because they are fiction but because they are *bad* fiction, violating proportion, unity, and probability. His argument — that invented material should still be bound to plausibility, that a well-made romance could rival the epic, that a text needs its "middle" to agree with its "beginning" and its end with both — is essentially an Aristotelian brief delivered by a genial, self-effacing author-manqué who admits he once tried and abandoned writing exactly such a corrected romance.

**A parallel indictment of the contemporary stage.** The curate extends the same complaint to Spanish drama — infants aging to bearded men within a single act, plots spanning Europe, Asia, and Africa without regard for the unities, saints' miracles misattributed, all driven by playwrights and actors who insist "the public wants this and will have nothing else." Both men propose the same fix: a central, credentialed examiner who vets every book or play before it circulates, standardizing a chaotic, market-driven proliferation of texts into something legible and answerable to a single authority — a small but vivid instance of a **legibility**-seeking apparatus proposed against an unruly, self-multiplying cultural production; see [[legibility-as-machine-core]].

## Notable passages

Sancho's confrontation with the fiction of "Queen Micomicona" — noticing Dorothea and Don Fernando's stolen intimacies and blurting that she is no more a queen than his own mother — is the clearest moment of Sanchoesque clear-sightedness undercutting the aristocratic romance being performed around him, and it triggers Don Quixote's most furious tirade of the unit.

The caging scene itself, executed in "profound silence" by disguised friends who bind Don Quixote in his sleep, is the unit's structural hinge: the moment the "enchantment" plot is materially enacted, converting a social fiction into an actual physical captivity.

The disguised barber's mock prophecy — promising the marriage of the "raging Manchegan lion" and the "white Tobosan dove" — is Cervantes' parody of chivalric prophetic diction, deployed cynically as a management tool and yet genuinely comforting to its target.

Don Quixote's explanation to Sancho that the curate and barber accompanying them only wear the *likeness* of themselves, conjured by enchanters to sow doubt, is the unit's tightest demonstration of a belief system immunized against refutation.

The Canon's statement that "fiction is all the better the more it looks like truth" is the unit's — and arguably the novel's — most direct statement of Cervantes' realist poetics, later book-ended by the curate's parallel complaint about plays that ignore time and place entirely, jumping from "the time of King Pepin or Charlemagne" to the historically unrelated Emperor Heraclius "there being years innumerable between the one and the other."
