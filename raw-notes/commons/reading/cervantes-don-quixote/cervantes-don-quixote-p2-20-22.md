# Camacho's Wedding, Basilio's Stratagem, and the Descent into the Cave of Montesinos

This unit spans the close of the Camacho's-wedding episode (Chapters XX-XXI) and the opening of the Cave of Montesinos adventure (Chapter XXII). At the wedding feast — rendered in an inventory of abundance (roasted oxen, wine-jar cauldrons, allegorical dances of Cupid versus Interest) — the rejected suitor Basilio interrupts the ceremony, stages his own death by impaling himself on a concealed blood-filled tube, and extracts a dying 'marriage' from Quiteria before leaping up unhurt to reveal the trick, which he and Don Quixote defend as licit strategy in love's warfare rather than deception proper. Don Quixote's armed intervention prevents Camacho's partisans from taking revenge, and the newlyweds depart with Basilio's party while Camacho, mollified, lets the feast continue without them. Three days later Don Quixote and Sancho, guided by a bookish 'cousin,' set out for the Cave of Montesinos; Don Quixote has himself roped and lowered into it, is pulled back up asleep after the rope goes slack, and wakes lamenting that he has been torn from 'the sweetest and most delightful existence' — the vision itself withheld from the reader (and from Sancho and the cousin) until the following unit.

## Themes

**Wealth versus worth as the feast's organizing argument.** The episode stages its own thesis twice — once discursively, in Sancho's blunt materialist creed ('there are only two families in the world, the Haves and the Haven'ts'), and once allegorically, in the 'speaking dance' where Cupid and Interest contend for a damsel penned inside the 'Castle of Caution,' with Interest's purse of coin literally breaking down the castle's defenses. Sancho's hunger-driven opportunism (he defects to Camacho's kitchen mid-argument) and his crude economics form a comic bass line under the more romantic drama above, and Don Quixote's rebuke of him as a fair-weather flatterer ('one of that sort that cry "Long life to the conqueror"') marks the moral distance the narrative wants between the two positions without erasing the force of Sancho's logic.

**Basilio's ruse as an assertion of will against a capture economy.** Basilio, dispossessed of Quiteria by Camacho's wealth, does not petition or despair — he engineers a scene in which death itself is turned into leverage, forcing consent while ostensibly beyond worldly stakes, then reclaims his life once the marriage is sealed. Don Quixote's ruling — 'That is not and ought not to be called deception which aims at virtuous ends' — legitimizes the stratagem as a form of self-assertion permitted to the otherwise powerless, a small instance of striving (a Basilio-shaped [[Conatus]]) that route around a rigged social contract rather than confronting it directly; the maneuver only works because Quiteria's own will (freely reaffirmed even after the trick is exposed) ratifies it, so the trick reads less as fraud than as a jointly authored escape from Camacho's purchasing power.

**Don Quixote as self-appointed jurist of romantic sovereignty.** His 'Hold, sirs, hold!' speech — arguing that 'love and war are the same thing' and that wiles justified in war are justified in courtship provided they don't dishonor the beloved — extends chivalric code into a general theory of legitimate deception, and it is delivered with real physical force (lance leveled, partisans overawed) rather than mere rhetoric. The subsequent lecture to Basilio on poverty as love's true enemy, and on seeking a wife by 'good name rather than wealth,' shows him pivoting instantly from armed intervention to moralizing counselor — Sancho's private mockery ('two [pulpits] on each finger') captures the comic excess of this role without undermining its sincerity.

**The cave as a threshold into withheld interiority.** The elaborate preparations — buying a hundred fathoms of rope, Sancho's fretting that his master will end up 'like a bottle put to cool in a well,' the prayer to Dulcinea, the ill-omened flight of crows — build toward an experience the text pointedly does not narrate here: Don Quixote emerges asleep, and his outburst on waking ('all the pleasures of this life pass away like a shadow and a dream') gestures at content deferred to the next unit. The chapter break itself enacts a kind of narrative capture-and-release: what happened underground is bracketed from both the other characters and the reader, its report withheld pending Don Quixote's own telling.

## Notable passages

The unit opens with Don Quixote's meditation on the master's sleepless anxiety against the servant's untroubled rest — an aside on hierarchy and burden that frames Sancho's subsequent hunger-driven materialism as, in its own register, a rational response to exactly the precarity Don Quixote describes. Sancho's 'Haves and Haven'ts' speech crystallizes that materialism into doctrine. Basilio's death-speech and self-impalement, and Quiteria's subsequent free (if theatrically staged) consent, form the episode's dramatic core, resolved by Don Quixote's martial defense of the marriage's legitimacy. The unit closes on the emotionally charged but informationally empty report from the cave — Don Quixote naming Montesinos, Durandarte, Belerma, and the weeping daughters of Ruidera without yet explaining who they are or what he saw, a cliffhanger structure that hands the actual revelation to the following unit.
