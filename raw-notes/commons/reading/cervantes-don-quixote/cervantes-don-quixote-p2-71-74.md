# Homecoming, the Rival Knight, and the Death of Alonso Quixano

This unit spans Chapters LXXI through LXXIV, the novel's final movement: the homeward road from the duke's estate, the village entry, and Don Quixote's illness, deathbed recantation, and death. Three set pieces carry the weight — Sancho's mercenary negotiation over his self-scourging for Dulcinea's disenchantment, the encounter with Don Álvaro Tarfe (a character borrowed from Avellaneda's counterfeit 1614 sequel) which lets Cervantes stage a legal repudiation of the rival book within his own fiction, and the death scene in which Don Quixote sheds his chivalric identity, becomes plain Alonso Quixano the Good, makes a will, and dies. The unit ends with Cide Hamete Benengeli's direct address to his pen, sealing the fiction against any further continuation.

## Themes

**Penance as commerce.** Sancho's whipping, ostensibly a mystical remedy for Dulcinea's enchantment, is negotiated like a wage dispute — quarter reals per lash, doubled rates, running totals — and executed largely against tree bark rather than his own back. The scene deflates the novel's central quest-object (Dulcinea's disenchantment) by routing it through Sancho's practical, mercantile cunning, continuing the book's long habit of undercutting idealism with appetite and self-interest.

**The rival text made flesh.** Don Álvaro Tarfe, a character from Avellaneda's unauthorized continuation, is folded into Cervantes's own narrative and made to swear before a village magistrate that this Don Quixote and Sancho are not the ones he knew. It is an audacious move: fiction litigating against a competing fiction, using the apparatus of legal testimony to assert authenticity. The device culminates the novel's long argument about the instability of authorship and the vulnerability of a popular character to imitation and theft.

**Omens and residual superstition.** Even as Don Quixote nears his declared cure, he reads a boy's idle remark and a hunted hare as ill omens for Dulcinea, and Sancho — the former believer in giants and enchantments — is now the one arguing against superstition, having internalized the curate's rationalism. The role reversal previews the larger inversion to come: Sancho pleads with the dying Don Quixote to keep believing in the chivalric fictions that once afflicted him.

**Recovery of reason as death sentence.** The narrative frames Don Quixote's sanity not as restoration but as termination — his friends recognize his lucidity as the surest sign he is dying, and his return to himself as Alonso Quixano coincides exactly with his renunciation of the books of chivalry and his life's meaning within the story. The will he dictates disinherits any niece who marries a reader of chivalric romance, and forgives Sancho and even begs forgiveness of Avellaneda's imagined author, closing every thread the novel has opened.

**Metafictional sealing.** Cide Hamete's final address to his pen — refusing any third sally, warning off the "Tordesillesque writer," and declaring pen and knight as one — is Cervantes's own gesture of authorial closure, guarding the text against further unauthorized sequels by fictionalizing the guard itself.

## Notable passages

Sancho's lash-pricing negotiation turns the mystic remedy into an itemized bill, epitomizing the novel's comic collision of the sublime and the venal. The Don Álvaro Tarfe episode is the novel's boldest metafictional stroke, using in-fiction legal testimony to repudiate a real rival book. The hare-and-omens exchange shows Sancho, now the rationalist, correcting his master's superstition. Don Quixote's announcement that he is "no longer Don Quixote of La Mancha, but Alonso Quixano" marks the character's dissolution back into his original, unenchanted self. The description of his death — dying "so calmly and so like a Christian" that even a fictional notary within the text vouches for it — completes the novel's turn from parody to elegy. Cide Hamete's closing charge to his pen is Cervantes's signature act of authorial self-protection, written into the fiction as its own epitaph.
