# Ana Félix's Exile and the Fall of Don Quixote

This unit (Chapters LXIII–LXVI) closes out the Barcelona sequence. Sancho is comically hazed aboard the viceroy's galleys, which then capture a Turkish brigantine whose young "rais" turns out to be Ana Félix, a Morisco woman raised as a devout Christian and swept into the 1609–1614 expulsion of Spain's Moriscos; her tearful reunion with her disguised-pilgrim father Ricote leads to a scheme to rescue her Christian lover Don Gaspar Gregorio from the Algerian court. In parallel, Don Quixote — strolling the beach in full armor — is challenged and unhorsed by the Knight of the White Moon, who exacts a vow that he retire from arms for a year; the knight is revealed to Don Antonio Moreno (and to the reader, not yet to Don Quixote) as Samson Carrasco, staging the whole duel as a therapeutic ruse to cure his neighbor's madness. The unit ends with Don Quixote and Sancho's melancholy, philosophical homeward journey, punctuated by a village wager Sancho judges and a comic encounter with Tosilos.

## Themes

**Illegible identity under an expulsion machine.** Ana Félix's entire narrative is built on the impossibility of her being read correctly by the apparatus that expels her: she is "a true Catholic Christian" by conviction and upbringing but "Morisco" by decree, and no amount of protestation — hers or her father's — registers with "those charged with our sad expatriation." The episode stages, almost too literally for the [[world-machines]] frame, a legibility project (the crown's drive to sort and purify the population into clean categories) grinding against a person whose lived identity refuses to fit the category assigned to her. Don Gaspar Gregorio's cross-dressing rescue compounds this: survival under the Barbary court requires a second, deliberate illegibility (a man passed off as a woman) layered on top of the first.

**Ricote's apologia is the episode's most uncomfortable passage.** Rather than condemning the policy that exiled his family, Ricote praises Don Bernardino de Velasco's execution of the expulsion in the most extravagant terms — "he applies to it the cautery that burns rather than the salve that soothes" — even while begging exception for himself and his daughter as good Christians. Cervantes lets a victim endorse, almost verbatim, the state's own justification for his people's collective punishment, without editorial resolution; the passage reads as an unresolved knot rather than either satire or sincerity, closer to Akomolafe's [[untimely]] than to simple irony — a charge the text holds without discharging it.

**The chivalric code as a still-binding, dying machine.** Don Quixote's defeat matters less as combat than as contract: the Knight of the White Moon defeats him fairly by the rules Don Quixote himself insists on honoring, and it is precisely Don Quixote's fidelity to that code — "though I lost my honour I did not lose nor can I lose the virtue of keeping my word" — that lets Carrasco's therapeutic ruse work at all. Knight-errantry here functions as a world already in its terminal phase: its institutions (the duel, the vow, the point of honor) are hollowed of any social function yet still command total obedience from their last true believer, a small-scale instance of a machine persisting past its dominance into an era that no longer needs it.

**Sancho's folk-wisdom stoicism.** Across the unit Sancho answers catastrophe with proverb after proverb ("where they give they take," "a good hope is better than a bad holding"), and Don Quixote's own closing meditation on Fortune — insisting there is no such thing as chance, only "the special preordination of heaven" and self-authorship — gives the pair's defeat a philosophical frame that outlasts the immediate humiliation.

## Notable passages

Ana Félix's opening self-description stakes her claim to a Christian selfhood the expulsion apparatus refuses to see, while the flight into Algiers ("much the same as if we had taken it up in hell itself") and the cross-dressing rescue of Don Gregorio show survival purchased through calculated illegibility. Her father's recognition scene — "I am thy father Ricote, come back to look for thee, unable to live without thee, my soul that thou art!" — is the unit's emotional hinge, followed later by his startling, unresolved praise of the official charged with expelling his own people. On the Don Quixote plot, his defeat speech ("Dulcinea del Toboso is the fairest woman in the world, and I the most unfortunate knight on earth") and Samson Carrasco's confession of the whole ruse ("I am called the bachelor Samson Carrasco... I hit upon a device for keeping him there") together frame the knight's fall as an act staged from outside his world but honored entirely within its rules.
