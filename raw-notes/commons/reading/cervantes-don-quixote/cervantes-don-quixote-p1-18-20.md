# Sheep Armies, a Funeral Procession, and the Fulling Mills

This unit spans the back half of Chapter XVIII, all of Chapter XIX, and the first half of Chapter XX. Fresh from the blanket-tossing at the inn, Don Quixote and Sancho argue over whether Sancho's ordeal was enchantment or plain thuggery; Don Quixote then conjures two dust clouds into rival armies (they are droves of sheep) and charges them, taking a beating from shepherds' slingstones and losing several teeth. That night he attacks a funeral cortege of priests escorting a corpse, mistaking their torches and mourning robes for phantoms, and unhorses a bachelor of the Church, earning himself the name "Knight of the Rueful Countenance." Making camp, hungry and thirsty, they hear a terrifying rhythmic pounding in the dark; Sancho secretly hobbles Rocinante's legs to stall his master until daylight and spins out a maddening, self-interrupting shepherd's tale to pass the night. At dawn the fearsome noise is revealed to be nothing but fulling-mill hammers, and Don Quixote, mortified at being laughed at, briefly turns violent before conceding the incident is "worth laughing at" while defending the reality of the courage he showed regardless of what the danger turned out to be.

## Themes

**Delusion is not passive misperception but active narrative imposition.** Don Quixote does not simply misread ambiguous stimuli; he narrates the dust clouds into named emperors, nations, and heraldic devices with encyclopedic fluency ("brimful and saturated with what he had read in his lying books"), building a fully elaborated world before any contradicting evidence can register. The sheep's bleating and the shepherds' stones do not correct the vision in real time — they are absorbed into it (a sword thrust becomes an enemy's blow) until material consequence (lost teeth, a body on the ground) finally breaks through.

**The unfalsifiable frame: enchantment as an epistemic immune system.** When reality fails to confirm his narrative, Don Quixote does not revise it — he adds an enchanter. The "malignant sage" who "can alter and falsify things" is invoked twice in this unit (once for the inn beating, once for the sheep), each time absorbing disconfirmation as further proof of persecution rather than error. This is a closed epistemic loop: any outcome, including total humiliation, is legible as evidence *for* the chivalric world-picture. Sancho, tellingly, proposes the empirical test his master never performs — follow the sheep and see if they turn back into men — which of course he will not have to honor, since Don Quixote has already declared the terms of confirmation on his own authority.

**Chivalry as an institution already run past its own decay sequence.** Don Quixote invokes the forms of knight-errantry — challenge speeches, heraldic blazons, a squire's oath, excommunication law, chivalric wage customs — as though they were still live and Functional, when the world around him (innkeepers, shepherds, a startled bachelor with a broken leg) treats them as at most Ornamental curiosities or outright madness. His grandiose self-introduction (born "in this our iron age to revive in it the age of gold") is the purest expression of this: a man performing an institution's founding myth centuries after its Dawn, oblivious that its Day has passed. This maps cleanly onto [[decay-sequence]] — the gap between an infrastructure's self-understanding and its actual social function is exactly the comedy (and pathos) of the whole book, and this unit stages it twice, back to back, at escalating cost.

**Sancho as counter-narrator and covert agent.** Sancho's skepticism is not simply comic relief; he does real epistemic and physical labor — naming the actual men who tossed him in the blanket, hearing sheep as sheep, and finally tying Rocinante's legs so his master's next act of self-endangerment is quietly deferred until daylight can do the disenchanting for him. His maddening, digression-looping shepherd's tale (which he refuses to finish once the goat-count is lost) is its own small essay on narrative control — he can suspend a story exactly as arbitrarily as his master suspends disbelief, and does so on purpose, to buy time.

**Naming and self-fashioning.** The "Knight of the Rueful Countenance" is adopted not because Don Quixote looks noble but because Sancho jokes that hunger and missing teeth have left him ill-favored; Don Quixote instantly re-narrates the joke as prophetic destiny ("the sage aforesaid must have put it into your mouth"), and resolves to have the image painted on his shield. Identity, in this book, is a title one claims and then retroactively justifies — precisely the operation he performs on every failed adventure.

## Notable passages

Sancho's rebuttal after the blanketing — naming Pedro Martinez, Tenorio Hernandez, and Juan Palomeque the Left-handed as flesh-and-blood culprits, and proposing they simply go home for the harvest — is the clearest statement of his empiricism against Don Quixote's enchantment theory, and of the practical motive (harvest-time, wages, safety) that will recur every time he tries to turn his master back.

The charge into the flock is framed explicitly as an epistemology of fear: "one of the effects of fear is to derange the senses and make things appear different from what they are." Don Quixote diagnoses Sancho's correct perception as the disorder and his own hallucination as clarity — a total inversion that the narrative then punishes with slingstones and lost teeth, without ever letting him hear it that way.

After the beating, his recovery move is the "malignant being who persecutes me" passage — the enchanter hypothesis stated in its purest, most self-sealing form, complete with an offered (but never honored) test of falsification.

The fulling-mills sequence turns the same machinery toward outright bathos: Don Quixote's grandiloquent self-introduction as reviver of the Round Table and the Nine Worthies, delivered at night to an unseen threat, is immediately followed at dawn by the narrator's own deflating aside — "if, reader, thou art not disgusted and disappointed" — before the "awe-inspiring noise" is revealed as six fulling hammers. Don Quixote's furious retort to Sancho's mockery ("turn me these six hammers into six giants... and if I do not knock them head over heels, then make what mockery you like of me") is the unit's clearest defense of the position that courage is real even when its object is imaginary — the psychological core that keeps the comedy from curdling into pure mockery.
