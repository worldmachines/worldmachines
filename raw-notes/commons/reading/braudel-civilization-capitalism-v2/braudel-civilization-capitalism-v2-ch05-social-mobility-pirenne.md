# Social mobility, European synchrony, and Pirenne's cycle of capitalist families

The unit moves through three linked arguments: elite reconstitution is normally too slow to observe except when crisis (war, dynastic upheaval, economic downturn) accelerates it into visibility, as in the post-Wars-of-the-Roses English aristocracy; this reconstitution ran on a loosely synchronized European timetable despite national time-lags, with a boom in social promotion from roughly 1470-1580 followed by a continent-wide closing of ranks; and Henri Pirenne's classic thesis that merchant families never survive as capitalists beyond two or three generations is well evidenced at the level of individual family names but, Braudel argues, mistakes the merchant for the capitalist — capital itself advances by stages (merchant to banker to financier to rentier) and survives far longer once tracked as a group phenomenon rather than a family biography.

## Themes

**Elite reproduction is slow and nearly unmeasurable, except when crisis compresses it into a visible event.** Ordinary turnover happens through shifts "so slow and so slight that they cannot be measured or even properly observed." Stone's link between prosperity and advancement, Kellenbenz's coast/interior dichotomy (Lübeck, Bremen, Hamburg more fluid than Nuremberg), and Laslett's finding that downward mobility outpaced upward mobility in preindustrial England all point to a system that mostly conserves itself, occasionally reaching "critical thresholds" (Oldenbourg's 200 noble families reduced to 30 by 1600) that force the door open for "fresh blood." The Wars of the Roses example — 29 of 50 lords surviving 1485, the old baronial families wiped out, minor gentry and bourgeois purchasers filling the vacuum, a new aristocracy respectable by 1540 — is offered as the clearest case because crisis compresses decades of invisible drift into a single legible "political geology."

**Reproduction, not novelty, is the deeper pattern.** Braudel explicitly adopts a Bourdieusian frame: society's "essential task" is to reproduce its topmost hierarchy, so that when an old order wears out, the newcomers who replace it "nine times out of ten reproduced or closely imitated the former state of affairs" rather than transforming it. The Savoy case (a new business-and-legal aristocracy supplanting the old feudal nobility amid plague and war) illustrates the rule that crisis enables succession, not novelty — and that someone at the bottom (the peasantry) always pays the price of the transition.

**European social mobility was loosely synchronized despite national time-lags.** This, not the economy-mobility link itself, is what Braudel flags as the surprising claim: the 1470-1580 boom ("almost like a spontaneous burst of biological growth") and the late-sixteenth-century closing of ranks recur across France, Spain, Italy, Burgundy, Rome, Naples, and — on a different clock — England, suggesting a shared continental rhythm riding on a shared long economic cycle. This is also where Braudel stakes out a position against purely political explanations: he is skeptical of Goubert's account of French noble decline via the Ligue's conflicts alone, insisting the downturn in economic conditions is doing real work even when it manifests through locally specific political forms.

**Henri Pirenne's "no capitalist dynasty" thesis is well evidenced at the level of the family.** Pirenne, studying pre-industrial and even pre-Renaissance capitalism, found merchant families surviving as such for at most two or three generations before converting trade profits into land, office, or nobility. Kellenbenz's northern German towns, Barcelona's merchant dynasties moving into the *estament of honrats*, the swift disappearance of Augsburg's great sixteenth-century names (Fuggers, Welsers, Hochstetters), Hexter's demonstration that the "rise of the bourgeoisie" is a phenomenon of every period rather than one, and cases from Rouen and Amsterdam all confirm the pattern independently.

**But Braudel argues Pirenne conflates the merchant with the capitalist.** "If every great merchant was a capitalist, the opposite was not necessarily true": capital can advance by stage, from merchant to banker to financier to rentier, surviving as capital for far longer than any one family's trading identity — the Genoese bankers, or Bardi descendants resurfacing a century later among the Medici bank's partners. Braudel further shifts the unit of analysis from the family to the *group*: Chaussinand-Nogaret's study of Languedoc's financiers shows three successive cohorts (roughly 1520-1600, 1600-1670/80, 1670-1789) each lasting far longer than Pirenne's two-or-three-generation window, which Braudel reads as broadly confirming Pirenne's intuition while relocating its real timescale — "these were not individual but collective movements... spread over the long term." This move — capital surviving by changing register within a stratified economic order rather than persisting as one family's possession — resonates with [[braudel-three-storey-house]]'s vertical distinction between ordinary market exchange and the upper-storey "anti-market" zone of finance and privilege where capitalists actually operate across generations. The insistence on collective, structural succession over individual biography also rhymes, loosely, with WMP's [[history-machine]] idea of a pattern assembling from independent components until it becomes visible — though here the pattern is a recurring reproduction rhythm within one social order, not a new paradigm displacing an old one.

**Where society blocks the exits, the "rule" is exposed as a social choice, not a law of capital.** The Jewish banking house of Norsa allegedly lasting six hundred years, Indian merchant-caste bankers confined exclusively to money, Osaka's merchants denied access to nobility, and Cairo's capitalists "devoured young" by comparison to Pirenne's European cases all show that the two-or-three-generation cycle depends on whether land, office, and nobility are actually open as exits — the same "door open / door slammed shut" logic that governs ordinary social mobility earlier in the unit.

## Notable passages

The Wars of the Roses passage (c1) is the unit's clearest worked example of crisis compressing decades of invisible elite drift into a single visible "political geology." The Bourdieu passage (c2) states Braudel's governing assumption most directly: society reproduces its topmost level even through apparent upheaval. The synchronization passage (c3) is the unit's actual surprising claim — not that mobility tracks the economy, but that it does so on a shared European clock despite national time-lags. Pirenne's core statement (c4) and Braudel's rebuttal (c5) form the unit's central debate: family-level truth versus capital-level truth. The Languedoc passage (c6) is Braudel's own resolution, relocating Pirenne's rhythm from the family to the group.
