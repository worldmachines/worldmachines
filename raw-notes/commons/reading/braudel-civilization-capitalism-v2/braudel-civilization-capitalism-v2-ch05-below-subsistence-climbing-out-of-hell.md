# Below Subsistence: The Sub-Proletariat and the Narrow Roads Out of Hell

Braudel turns from wage-earners to the population beneath them: a permanent European (and global) sub-proletariat of the unemployable, the pauper, the beggar and the vagrant. He argues this mass was produced structurally by an economy that could never generate full employment, traces the moral and administrative machinery used to manage it — poverty thresholds, the line between 'worthy' and 'unworthy' poor, branding, galleys, workhouses — and then, in "Climbing out of hell," shows that the only real escapes from this condition (organized crime, the army, domestic service) were themselves new forms of bondage rather than an exit from bondage as such.

## Themes

**A universal sub-proletariat, Europe included.** Braudel opens comparatively: India, China, the Islamic world, Russia and southern Italy all had populations bordering on "endemic slavery," and the practice spread across the Atlantic to the New World. Europe's vaunted freedom is qualified immediately — serfdom persisted in Poland and Russia, and functionally in Scottish coal-mining life-contracts until nearly 1800 — and "all men were harshly bound to their toiling condition" except the rich and powerful.

**An economy powerless to create full employment.** The permanent mass of "unfortunates" is dated to the eleventh–twelfth-century division of labour between town and country. Braudel is explicit this is an economic failure as much as a moral one: society is blamed "no doubt," but the deeper fault lies in an economy structurally unable to absorb everyone it displaced. It is a labour-market ceiling that rhymes loosely with Braudel's own [[limits-of-the-possible]] — though there the constraint is usually biological or demographic (yields, transport speed, disease), while here it is a shortfall of employment opportunity the market economy simply never solved. This population also sits beneath the market/capitalism distinction elaborated elsewhere in the volume and beneath the ground floor of material life surveyed in Vol. I ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1]]); Braudel doesn't literally extend the [[braudel-three-storey-house]] metaphor with a basement here, but the paupers and vagrants he describes occupy exactly that unnamed space below it.

**A moral sorting of poverty.** Contemporary vocabulary split "pauperdom" from "beggary and vagrancy": the pauper was "wretched but not despised," entitled to registration and public charity; the beggar or vagrant was a ne'er-do-well who forfeited sympathy. Oudard Coquault's 1652 Reims journal draws this line with startling contempt for those who ate "bran, herbs, ... slugs, cats and dogs."

**Quantifying the poverty threshold.** Using Lyons wage records set against bread prices, Braudel derives a measurable "poverty line," crossed with increasing frequency by unskilled workers and casual labourers across the sixteenth century — data marshalled to argue that "the cost of progress was, as usual, paid principally by the workers."

**Punishment as policy, confinement as slavery.** Vagrancy was criminalized: branding, ear-cropping, the galleys, threats of hanging "without trial in any shape or form." Braudel's own gloss is blunt: the workhouses, Zuchthäuser, and maisons de force of the seventeenth century existed because "what are imprisonment and forced labour but forms of slavery?" Mortality inside them (Genoa, 1710) did the state's work for it.

**Scale and persistence into the eighteenth century.** Vagrancy was not marginal: 6,000 in Venice in 1545, 17,000 outside Paris in 1587, an estimated 91,000 without fixed abode in Paris by 1776. Braudel attributes the further eighteenth-century swelling of these ranks to a rural crisis (property concentration, enclosures) compounding demographic growth even amid economic expansion.

**The floating population as an anti-society.** Citing Nina Assodorobraj on late-eighteenth-century Poland, Braudel highlights a population that not only failed to find work but actively resisted redomestication — one that "deliberately avoids anything that might re-create fresh bonds of ... dependence." This is the passage that resonates most directly with [[capture-resistance]] and [[Conatus]]: a group whose only visible agency is refusal of re-capture into dependency. Braudel immediately qualifies the resonance, though — it was "not a class but a rabble," lacking the cohesion to convert refusal into a durable counter-structure; useful friction on labour markets, never an organized alternative.

**Climbing out of hell: escape as a change of master, not an end to mastery.** The only routes out — organized crime (smuggling, banditry, piracy, each needing its own "disciplined organization" and often a city's protection), the army (a "haven of regular life" until desertion sent one back to hell), and domestic service (a labour market with "ever-open doors" but total subordination to an unchosen master) — all substituted one dependency for another rather than removing dependency itself.

**Braudel's closing argument: hierarchy is inescapable.** Weighing Sartre's call to dismantle hierarchy, Braudel is skeptical: "to say society seems invariably to mean saying hierarchy." Abolish one chain — slavery, serfdom, colonial subjection — and another appears; he pairs this with the era's own self-serving justifications (Abbé Fleury asking where "the workers, soldiers and servants for the rich" would come from if the poor had no children; Melon declaring colonial slavery contrary to neither "Religion" nor "Morality").

## Notable passages

**The sub-proletariat, worldwide and in "free" Europe** opens the unit's comparative frame, immediately undercutting any complacent story of European exceptionalism — Scotland's life-bound coal-miners are the local proof that "free" labour and serfdom sat closer together than the label suggests.

**An economy that could not employ everyone** states the causal claim underpinning the whole unit: displacement by a medieval division of labour, not simply moral failure, produced a permanent surplus population, "labelled in contemporary vocabulary as pauperdom, beggary and vagrancy."

**Worthy poor vs. unworthy poor** shows the distinction was lived and enforced at street level, not merely a category in administrative registers — Coquault's journal is Braudel's evidence that ordinary observers policed this line with real contempt.

**Imprisonment as slavery** is the unit's sharpest formulation of continuity between chattel slavery and the era's "modern" carceral and forced-labour institutions, naming the workhouses and maisons de force across five countries in one breath.

**The floating population as anti-society** is the clearest resonance with the World Machines vocabulary of refusal and persistence, precisely because Braudel pairs the resonance with its limit: a population that resists capture but never organizes into something that could outlast the pressure against it.

**Climbing out of hell** functions as Braudel's own inventory of escape routes and doubles as an argument: even the "way out" ran through new hierarchies — gang, regiment, household — reinforcing the closing claim that hierarchy, not freedom, was the real alternative on offer to destitution.
