# By Way of Conclusion

Closing *The Wheels of Commerce*, Fernand Braudel ([[fernand-braudel]]) distills the whole volume into three conditions that, together, allowed "the capitalist process considered as a whole" to develop — a worldwide market economy, a particular kind of hierarchical society, and the liberating leverage of long-distance world trade — then pivots forward to Volume III's promised treatment of the world-economies.

## Themes

**A worldwide market economy is necessary but not sufficient.** Braudel's first condition is a "vigorous and expanding market economy," built from geographic, demographic, agricultural, industrial and commercial factors that operated everywhere — inside and outside Europe, in Islam, India, China, Japan, parts of Africa, and eventually the Americas — producing the same recurring sequence of towns (fortress, monastery, administrative, crossroads). He reads this parallel, world-wide development as proof that the market economy is the spontaneous, normal base of any society past a certain size, arising "of its own accord" once a critical threshold is crossed. But he immediately blocks the inference that a thriving market economy yields capitalism automatically: China is his "perfect illustration" of a market economy that never produced a capitalist superstructure. This is Braudel's clearest late statement of the market-economy/capitalism distinction that structures the whole book — transparent, competitive exchange at the base versus the "anti-market" storey above it — see [[braudel-three-storey-house]]. The claim that proliferation follows "once a critical threshold had been reached" faintly echoes the demographic and ecological ceilings of Volume I's [[limits-of-the-possible]], though the direction is inverted: there the limits cap growth from above, here a threshold unlocks it from below.

**A hierarchical, dynastic society is the second condition.** Capitalism could only take root where a society had, unknowingly, built a favourable environment over centuries: durable hierarchies, surviving dynasties, and uninterrupted wealth accumulation across generations via unhindered inheritance and profitable marriages, within groups ranked but not entirely closed to mobility. Braudel is explicit that this is a "multisecular and general movement of society" — political and 'historical' rather than narrowly economic — and that Japan and Europe each satisfied it "in their own separate ways," reinforcing his comparative, non-Eurocentric method rather than treating Europe's path as the template.

**Long-distance world trade is the liberating third condition, and the hinge into Volume III.** Where the market economy is the necessary base and hierarchical society the enabling context, "the special and as it were liberating action of world trade" is what actually opens a "superior profit level" — the doorway into capitalism proper. Braudel uses this to pivot forward: the next volume will examine the world-economies themselves, "autonomous patches of the planet" with their own histories and shifting boundaries, expanding as Europe's conquests proceeded, governed by a "different level of competition" whose recurring rules can for once be followed "without risk of error" — culminating in "the international division of labour." These world-economies — large, quasi-autonomous, historically bounded systems that expand, compete, and reorganize the world around a dominant centre — loosely rhyme with WMP's [[world-machines]] framework, though the fit is partial: Braudel's units are defined by trade dominance and competitive rules rather than an interlocking bundle of institutions, technologies and assumptions, and he assigns them no fixed thousand-year Dawn/Day/Dusk cycle.

**World-scale comparison as method.** Across all three conditions, Braudel insists the market-economy stage is not a European peculiarity — Islam, India, China, Japan, Africa and the Americas all pass through it — while capitalism's emergence is conditional and rare, dependent on the second and third conditions layering on top of the first. This comparative logic, refusing to treat Europe's outcome as inevitable or universal, is the throughline connecting this conclusion back to the Volume I groundwork in [[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1]].

## Notable passages

The first curated passage carries the necessary-but-not-sufficient argument in full, from the naming of the first condition through the China counter-example that blocks any simple market-to-capitalism inference. The second lays out the demanding social prerequisites — dynasty, inheritance, rank, mobility — that Braudel treats as a slow, unconscious "multisecular" achievement rather than a policy choice, with Japan cited alongside Europe. The third is the pivot passage: it names world trade as the "liberating" third condition and previews the world-economies that will organize the whole of Volume III, ending on the summary tag "the international division of labour."
