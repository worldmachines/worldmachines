# Capitalism's Peripheral Machinery: Serfdom and the Plantations

Across two paired case studies -- the Baltic-to-Balkans "second serfdom" and the Atlantic sugar plantations of Brazil, St Domingue, and Jamaica -- this unit argues that neither the coerced grain estates of Eastern Europe nor the slave-worked plantations of the Americas were capitalist enterprises in the textbook sense (rationalization, calculation, investment, profit maximization), yet both were indispensable machinery of a merchant capitalism whose real seat of profit lay in Amsterdam, Gdansk, Bordeaux, Lisbon, and London.

## Themes

**The second serfdom was a colonial relationship disguised as feudal reversion.** Braudel rejects "refeudalization" as sufficient description: the peasant freedoms lost between the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries (mobility, marriage, commutation of dues to cash) were driven by West European demand for grain, timber, and livestock, pulling Eastern Europe "back... towards a colonial destiny as a producer of raw materials." Compulsory labour escalated by legislative ratchet -- Poland moves from one day a week in 1519 to six by 1600; Hungary and Livonia show comparable trajectories -- into what Braudel calls "an enormous machine harnessed to serve... Gutsherrschaft."

**Braudel accepts Witold Kula's diagnosis of the landlord but inverts its conclusion.** Kula shows the Polish or Hungarian magnate fails every test of the capitalist "identikit portrait" -- no calculation, no investment, no interest in productivity, because "the machine worked on its own." Braudel agrees the landowner was "not a capitalist," but insists he was nevertheless "a tool and a collaborator in the service of capitalism in Amsterdam and elsewhere... part of the system," drawing advance payments from Gdansk merchants exactly as the Segovia sheep-breeder sold fleeces forward to Genoese buyers. The qualification is the argument's hinge: unlike the sheep-breeder or cereal-grower, who were "under a usurer's thumb," the Polish lord "ruled the roost at home," commanding peasants and towns by raw power rather than market dependency. The result is neither feudalism (not self-sufficient) nor modern capitalist agriculture, but a **monopoly economy** -- "monopoly of production, monopoly of distribution" -- in the service of a genuinely capitalist international system.

**The American plantations, by contrast, are "capitalist creations par excellence" from the very start**, with no gradual drift required. Settlers, enslaved labour, and the cane itself (with techniques relayed via Madeira and Sao Tome) were all imported, and production was "remote-controlled" from Seville, Bordeaux, Amsterdam, Bristol. Braudel first surveys the divergent institutional experiments Europe ran on American soil -- a seigniorial regime that failed outright in Canada, a free-labour north against a slave-owning south in English America, Spanish encomiendas that stayed "benefices rather than fiefs" because the Crown refused to let them harden into feudalism -- before narrowing to the plantation as the unit's real subject.

**Case by case, the plantation reproduces the second serfdom's structure: an undercapitalized, often indebted local operator serving a remote merchant-financier who takes the surplus.** The Brazilian engenho de assucar returned only 4-5%, and "only the senhor de engenho had anything to do with the market economy" -- he was himself dependent on Salvador and Recife merchants tied in turn to Lisbon. Debien's account of the Galbaud du Fort plantation in St Domingue shows a chronically cash-poor operation ("I am spending nothing"; "my supper consists of a little bread with preserves") squeezed by import costs and seasonal price swings, and above all by Bordeaux's "wholesalers, commissioners and shippers," whom Braudel calls "the masters of the machine that turned out the riches of the colonies." Jamaica repeats the pattern in English dress -- Braudel calls it outright "a wealth-creating machine, a capitalist machine serving the rich" -- quoting Burke's description of West Indian trade as effectively domestic English trade, with roughly £1.5 million in net annual benefit reaching England in 1773, largely to families who combined planting with banking and trading from London.

**The apparent trade-balance paradox -- metropolitan imports from the islands exceeding exports to them -- is resolved by where the accounting happens, not by colonial prosperity.** Prices were recorded at metropolitan rather than colonial value, the colonial livre was depreciated roughly a third against the mainland livre, and invisible transfers (freight, insurance, commissions, interest, absentee-landlord remittances) flowed back to Europe -- so the wealth the raw trade figures imply for planters was in fact captured upstream, in Bordeaux and London.

**A closing gesture toward divergence:** beyond the plantation proper (tobacco in Virginia, livestock in Cuba, cocoa in Venezuela's Caracas Company), Braudel points to frontier zones outside merchant reach -- the bandeiras of Sao Paulo, cattle in the Bahian sertao, the Argentine pampas, the Venezuelan llanos -- as an "ancient, not to say primitive" capitalism in which livestock itself was money, noting Max Weber's own passing interest in the type.

## Notable passages

- The description of compulsory labour as an ever-tightening machine ("An extra turn of the screw was always possible...") states the second serfdom's coercive mechanics in their own terms, before Braudel supplies the capitalist reading of them.
- "He was not a capitalist, but he was a tool and a collaborator... part of the system" is the unit's thesis sentence for Eastern Europe: capitalism operating through, not as, the feudal-seeming landlord.
- The "monopoly economy" passage is Braudel's explicit verdict on the question posed by the section's title -- neither feudalism nor capitalist agriculture, but a third form serving a genuinely capitalist system above it.
- The Brazilian engenho passage states the market-economy/capitalism distinction almost programmatically: the local producer touches only the market economy, while the merchant chain above him is where capital and control actually sit -- a clean instance of [[braudel-three-storey-house]].
- "The masters of the machine that turned out the riches of the colonies" and "a wealth-creating machine, a capitalist machine serving the rich" are Braudel's own machine-language for merchant capital's grip on the plantation complex -- a strong, if narrower and more literal, echo of [[world-machines]]' image of an era's infrastructure running to its own logic; WMP's machines are civilizational and centuries-long, where Braudel's is a specific extractive apparatus operating within one.
- The catalogue of divergent colonial institutions -- failed Canadian seigniorialism, free-labour north versus slave south, non-feudal encomiendas, frontier "primitive capitalism" in the llanos and pampas -- shows one European pressure (capital seeking commodities) producing radically non-convergent local forms, a loose echo of [[divergence-machine]]'s variety-under-proliferation, notwithstanding the centuries' distance in setting.

This continues the analytic frame introduced in [[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1]] and developed under [[fernand-braudel]]'s three-storey model, applying it here to two structurally parallel cases at the geographic edges of the early modern world-economy.
