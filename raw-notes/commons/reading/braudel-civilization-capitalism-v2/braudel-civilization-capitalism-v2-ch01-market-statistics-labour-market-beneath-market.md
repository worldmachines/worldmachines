# The Market's Statistics, the Labour Market, and What Lies Beneath It

This unit moves through five linked arguments about the market economy in early modern Europe: what quantitative studies (Everitt on England and Wales, a map of the generalite of Caen, Schremmer on Bavaria) reveal about market density and specialization; how private trading and smuggling grew up as structural workarounds to the closely supervised public market, in England and then across the continent; how land, money, and finally labour were successively drawn into market exchange, with the labour market treated as the culminating and most consequential case; how the market was experienced as a "watershed" depending entirely on one's social position; and why Braudel insists that a genuine economic history must also recover the large zone of rural life that never fully passed through the market at all.

## Themes

**Markets can be counted, and counting reveals a hierarchy of specialization rather than a universal law.** Braudel leans on Everitt's figures (roughly 800 market towns in England and Wales, each animating trade across a population six or seven times its own size) and a rough parallel with Bavaria (one market per 7,300 inhabitants) only to immediately warn against generalizing: "one must always check the method of calculation used." The specialization data (over 300 of the 800 English towns confined to a single trade, down to Wymondham's wooden spoons) is offered as the kind of serial evidence that could, if extended across Europe, replace "purely descriptive information" with something closer to a map of economic growth.

**The regulated market generated its own evasion.** Private trading — itinerant merchants buying at farmhouse doors, deals struck in inns, smuggling that "laughed at rules and at tolls and excise duties" — is presented not as marginal criminality but as a structural response to a public market that was "inadequate and too closely controlled." Braudel follows Everitt in treating this as "a revolution which went beyond the economy, into social behaviour," linking it to the political emergence of the Independents in Civil War England.

**Land, money, and finally labour all had to pass through "the narrow opening of the market."** The chapter narrates an escalating sequence: property and land markets (Paris rents, Place des Vosges speculation, Breton seigneuries), then money markets (bills of exchange, public debt, moving from Italy toward Holland and London), and finally the labour market, which Braudel calls "the most important, from the point of view of this book." He marks the conceptual novelty of treating labour as a commodity (Hobbes, the astonished French consul in Genoa) against the concrete historical record of wage-earning spreading from medieval hiring fairs and strikes to Tudor England and pre-Revolutionary Paris, where more than half of households depended on wages.

**The market was a watershed, not a uniform condition.** Whether the market meant dependency, opportunity, or near-irrelevance depended on where one stood: silk-workers in Messina were wholly reliant on the urban food supply, while richer English and Dutch farmers became "regular customers as well as suppliers." For the 80–90% of the population living as peasants, the market was at most a partial, occasional presence — a source of ploughshares and tax money rather than a way of life.

**Beneath the visible market lies a whole self-sufficient economy that resists direct documentation.** Braudel treats the reconstruction of this invisible layer — via Morineau's and Cattini's inferential methods (comparing calculated local consumption against what actually reached the market) and Berce's study of rural rebellion — as unfinished, frontier work, and closes the unit by making it a precondition for any adequate history of rural society.

This unit sits squarely on the middle floor of Braudel's own architecture — the transparent, competitive **market economy** rather than the "anti-market" of long-distance capitalism proper (see [[braudel-three-storey-house]]) — while its final movement, the insistence on a self-sufficient rural stratum "beneath the market," gestures back down toward the ground floor of material life and its structural ceilings, as in [[limits-of-the-possible]]. The statistical, cartographic effort to measure market density (Everitt's county-by-county density maps, tallies of trades and radii of influence) is also a small, genuine instance of the counting-and-mapping impulse that WMP's [[legibility-as-machine-core]] associates with the [[modernity-machine]]'s drive to render economic life measurable and comparable — though this is scholarly/administrative measurement of a market centuries after the fact, not the state's own cadastral or bureaucratic legibility project Scott describes, so the rhyme is with the impulse rather than the mechanism.

## Notable passages

The **market-statistics** material sets up the unit's method: Braudel wants serial, comparable figures rather than description, but is careful the reader not mistake a coincidence (England and Bavaria converging near one market per several thousand people) for a discovered regularity.

The **private-trading** material culminates in Hugh Peter's 1647 exclamation on the reopening of England's roads after the Civil War, which Braudel and Everitt read as evidence that market circumvention had become entangled with a broader social and political "revolution," not merely an economic adjustment.

The **labour-market** material is the unit's spine: Braudel stages the conceptual shock of treating "a mans Labour" as a commodity (Hobbes, and the disbelieving Genoa consul) alongside the blunt concrete case of the Joachimstal miners, forced from independent artisanship into wage-earning under merchant-supplied capital — their aldermen's own formula, "One gives money, the other does the work," Braudel reads as an early, plain-spoken statement of the Capital/Labour confrontation.

The **watersheds** material is carried by the vignette of the Spanish soldier amazed at the abundance of a Zaragoza fish market in 1645 who nonetheless buys only what fits an ordinary purse — Braudel's way of showing that access to the market's plenty was gated by resources and habit, not merely by geography.

The unit's closing passage is its methodological thesis: that no complete history of rural society is possible "until we have systematically explored human life below the level of the market," naming this as still-open work rather than a settled finding.
