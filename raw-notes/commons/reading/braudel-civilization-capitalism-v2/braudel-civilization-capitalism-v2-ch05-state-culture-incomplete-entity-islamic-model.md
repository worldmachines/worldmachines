# State, Society, and the Islamic Roots of European Capitalism

Braudel closes his argument on the early modern state by calling it an "incomplete entity": too thinly staffed to govern its own territory directly, so it ruled instead through society (compromises with clergy, nobility, and town patriciates, cemented by the sale of office) and through culture (ceremony, royal propaganda, the "language" of monarchy). He then turns to the state's relationship with capitalism, concluding that the state both encouraged and discouraged capitalism by turns because capitalism operated on a wider, extranational plane than either the market economy or the state itself — so the real "power apparatus" that governs a society is always larger than the state alone. The unit closes with a substantial comparative excursus, "Cultural transmission: the Islamic model," arguing that the instruments of European capitalism — double-entry bookkeeping, the bill of exchange, the commenda partnership — were largely borrowed, directly or convergently, from a more mature medieval Islamic trading world, and that Rome itself was only ever one node within a much older Eurasian world-economy running from Gibraltar to China.

## Themes

**The state as an unfinished entity, propped up by society and culture.** Braudel frames a choice between Sombart's view (the state as engine of capitalism, via luxury and war) and his own preferred alternative: the state was "as yet an unfinished entity," unable to reach the whole of its own population. He backs this with concrete numbers — some 12,000 royal officials for a France of 15–20 million people, some 70,000 posts for Spain's much larger empire — a quantifiable ceiling on state capacity that echoes, at one remove, the kind of hard structural constraint Vol. I calls the [[limits-of-the-possible]], though that concept targets demographic and physical ceilings on material life rather than bureaucratic reach specifically. Braudel's conclusion is that the state could only "save face" by outsourcing authority to those who commanded society's prestige (church, nobility, towns) and to culture, "the millions of voices and millions of ears."

**Venality of office as the mechanism of that compromise.** Selling offices let the crown tax the bourgeoisie's wealth while building a durable, self-perpetuating officeholding class (the *noblesse de robe*); Braudel treats this not as automatic corruption but as a functional, if ambiguous, redistribution of "a slice of public authority," comparable to how land had once been ceded as fiefs.

**Culture as governance by other means.** Ceremony — coronations, the royal touch for scrofula, royal progresses like Catherine de Medici's two-year tour showing the young Charles IX to his subjects — did the persuasive work administration could not reach. Braudel treats propaganda and the cult of monarchy as continuous with, not opposed to, bureaucratic rule, while noting the bourgeois's private "muttering" as a discordant undertone.

**The state neither made nor fully controlled capitalism.** Braudel refuses a single-cause answer: the state "undoubtedly encouraged capitalism," but the "formula can be reversed" — it also discouraged and was harmed by capitalism, "one after another or simultaneously." The deciding structural fact is scale: capitalism worked on "an infinitely wider plane" than the national market economy or the state's own concerns, illustrated by Lisbon, where the city's real capitalist transactions (Macao, Goa, Brazil, Russia) were invisible to its own townspeople. This restates, as a claim about capitalism's geography rather than its sector, the [[braudel-three-storey-house]] framing from Vol. I — capitalism as a level above the transparent market economy. He concludes that the true "power apparatus" is "something more than the state": the sum of political, social, economic and cultural hierarchies together, of which the state is often the "keystone" but "seldom if ever solely in control," an aggregate that "always reconstitutes itself... as if it were somehow a biological necessity of society." That image of power as a layered, self-perpetuating composite rather than a single institution rhymes with [[world-machines]]' definition of a world machine as interlocking institutions, technologies and assumptions rather than any one regime; the fit is inexact since Braudel's aggregate is a synchronic sociological claim, not a centuries-long Dawn–Day–Dusk lifecycle.

**Civilization resists the market before yielding to it.** In the short bridge section "Civilizations do not always put up a fight," culture is described as an inherited reservoir that initially said "no to the market, no to profit-making, no to capital," conceding only "with a bad grace" under the pressure of "everyday life." This friction — an old normative order refusing, then slowly ceding ground to, an emergent economic logic — loosely echoes a [[gramsci-gap]] dynamic, though Braudel's is a civilizational-cultural friction rather than a dated transition between two named world-machines.

**Capitalism as a diffused cultural good, largely borrowed from Islam.** The Islamic-model section argues that capitalism's own instruments — bookkeeping (Pacioli's manual, learned in Venice and carried home by Fugger), the bill of exchange (probably adapted from the Islamic *sutfaya*), the commenda (a long-standing Islamic partnership form) — were "cultural goods, which travelled and were exchanged like any other," reinforced by loanwords (*douane*, *fonduk*, *magasin*, *mohatra*) and imported goods (silk, paper, Arabic numerals, gunpowder, the compass). Braudel draws the historiographical conclusion directly: admitting these borrowings means "turning one's back on traditional accounts of the history of the West as pioneering genius, spontaneous inventor," and denying Rome's status as civilizational origin-point — Rome was merely one node in a Eurasian trading zone running from Gibraltar to China. This is the project's world-scale-comparison move (Europe/Islam/Byzantium) in a clean form, and reads as a strong case for a [[history-machine]]-style account of capitalism's emergence: instruments assembling convergently across a distributed trading world long before they are claimed as European inventions, against the "great man"/pioneering-genius alternative Braudel names and rejects outright.

**Islam's own commercial civilization as a full model, not a passive donor.** Braudel stresses Islam's positive valuation of trade — attributed sayings of the Prophet ("the merchant enjoys the felicity both of this world and the next," "he who makes money pleases God"), the Mamluke invitation to foreign merchants, Ottoman advice to protect them — against the "horror of usury" it shared with Christendom, and dates the end of "Europe's apprenticeship" to the cities of Islam and Byzantium to 1252, when the West resumed minting gold.

## Notable passages

- The administrative headcounts (c1) make the "incomplete state" claim concrete and falsifiable rather than rhetorical.
- The definition of society and culture as the state's substitute apparatus (c2) is the chapter's thesis sentence.
- The encouraged/discouraged, wider-plane passage (c3) is Braudel's refusal of a monocausal state–capitalism relationship.
- The "power apparatus... more than the state" passage (c4) is the unit's most resonant sentence for the project: power as a self-reconstituting composite of hierarchies rather than a single institution.
- The bookkeeping-diffusion passage (c5) grounds the "capitalism as cultural good" claim in a specific, traceable transmission chain (Pacioli → Fugger → Augsburg).
- The rejection of Western "pioneering genius" (c6) is the payoff of the whole comparative excursus and the strongest single sentence for the project's historiographical interests.
