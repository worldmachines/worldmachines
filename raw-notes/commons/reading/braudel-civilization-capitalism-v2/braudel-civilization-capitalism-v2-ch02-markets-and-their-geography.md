# Markets and Their Geography: Catchment Areas, Sugar, and the World Traffic in Precious Metals

Braudel studies the market economy not through the individual merchant but through geography: the shifting territorial reach of firms, cities, and specific commodities (sugar, precious metals), read as a retrospective map of where exchange actually organized space and where, within each chain, profit concentrated.

## Themes

**Catchment areas make the market economy visible.** Braudel's organizing device is the firm's or city's "catchment area": the geographic footprint traced by a firm's buyers, suppliers, lenders and creditors, or by a city's overlapping rings of supply, currency-and-weights, credit, sales, purchases, and news. Reconstructed from surviving account books, letters, and trading manuals (Datini, Saminiati, Meder's Nuremberg Handelsbuch), these footprints are offered as hard evidence of "the impact the market economy made over a given area."

**Scale of network tracks prosperity.** Across the Gianfigliazzi, Capponi, Salviati, Saminiati, Fugger, Welser, and Buonvisi examples, the same pattern recurs: a merchant's fortune scales with the size and richness of the city he is plugged into ("the fattest fish are caught in the biggest lakes"). Nuremberg's mid-sixteenth-century centrality — pivoting between the Venice–Antwerp "isthmus" — is offered as a near-geometric explanation of German commercial predominance at the time.

**The chain has a "high point," and it is rarely at production.** For pepper and sugar alike, Braudel locates a single point in the sequence of production, transport, refining, wholesale, and retail where real profit concentrates (the Fondaco dei Tedeschi for pepper; the wholesale stage after refining for sugar), stating outright that "any capitalist market has a series of links in a chain" with one link more remunerative than the rest. Production itself — plantation sugar, silver and diamond mining — is consistently the most exploited, least remunerative link, worked by slaves or the "wretched and deprived," while merchants and rulers capture the surplus further along the chain.

**Europe as distributor, Asia as reservoir: a world-scale asymmetry.** The precious-metals section stages a comparison across Europe, Islam (the Ottoman Empire), India and China: producing countries (mostly "primitive" mining zones worked as slavery), transit zones (Europe, the Ottoman Empire), and "receptacle-countries" that hoard rather than recirculate metal, becoming "sponges" or "graveyards" for gold and silver. Braudel inverts the mercantilist reading of the resulting one-way outflow of silver — not a European "haemorrhage" but Europe "bombarding" otherwise-closed economies with currency to force trade open — reinforced by intra-European bimetallic arbitrage (over-valuing gold to keep it, letting silver flow outward) and by later state fights over free currency export (French mercantilist restriction versus Dutch and English open gates).

There is a loose resonance with [[world-machines]] framing: the catchment-area exercise is Braudel rendering dispersed, informal merchant relationships **legible** as bounded, mappable, comparable territories, in the spirit of [[legibility-as-machine-core]] — though the tool doing the rendering is retrospective historiography, not an era's own administrative apparatus, so the fit is loose rather than exact. More load-bearing is the resonance with the market-economy-versus-capitalism distinction central to this project's reading of Braudel ([[braudel-three-storey-house]], [[fernand-braudel]]): the Fugger–Welser–Buonvisi megafirms and the sugar/pepper "high point" argument are the anti-market "zone... where the great predators roam" made concrete — privileged position and monopoly, not competitive exchange, generating the outsized returns. The Europe/Islam/India/China comparison is also a clean instance of the world-scale comparison that runs through the source (see [[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1]] for its Vol. I counterpart).

## Notable passages

- The opening definition of the firm's catchment area, tested against four Florentine and Tuscan houses (Gianfigliazzi, Capponi, Salviati, Saminiati) spanning the thirteenth to seventeenth centuries, shows the method in action: reconstructing a merchant's world from the addresses in his correspondence.
- Cotrugli's aphorism and the Fugger founding story compress the scale argument into a memorable image and a dated origin story (1367 weaver to 1442 long-distance merchant).
- The Von Thünen aside on Caen — concentric rings of market gardens, cereals, and grazing radiating from the town — tests a general spatial model against a specific French case, and finds it holds for the inner rings but breaks down for the outer, international ones.
- The pepper/sugar "high point" passage states outright the anti-market logic of a capitalist chain: one link is structurally the most profitable, and it is not the one closest to the soil.
- The "receptacle-countries" passage names China and India as the two great reservoirs where metal enters and does not return, using Braudel's own sponge/graveyard metaphor.
- The closing reflection on Europe "bombarding" closed economies with silver reframes three centuries of bullion outflow as an instrument of commercial power rather than a loss — the unit's clearest statement of world-scale comparison as argument, not just backdrop.
