# Textiles, Guilds, and the Verlagssystem: Merchant Capital from Cottage Industry to the Mines

The unit asks whether the pre-industrial economy had anything like a modern 'leading sector,' provisionally nominates textiles (with heavy qualification), and then follows the guild-organized craft economy as merchant capital colonizes it through the Verlagssystem, or putting-out system - a slow, distributed capture that culminates, at the end of the fifteenth century, in mining's forced conversion of free craftsmen into a genuine wage-earning proletariat.

## Themes

**Textiles as a provisional 'key industry,' heavily qualified.** Braudel is wary of importing a modern industrial-economics concept (a sector whose take-off drags the rest of the economy along) into an ancien-regime world with 'little coherence' and no reliable knock-on effects. Textiles are the closest fit by sheer volume and social importance (fashion, household linen, ceremonial dress), but the comparison to a modern leading industry is undercut by textiles' persistently luxury character until the English cotton revolution gave it 'a popular character' late in the eighteenth century. The internal history of textile primacy is itself a relay race across regions: wool (Netherlands and Italy, thirteenth century) to silk (Italy, then Lyons and the north) to English-style woollens (seventeenth century) to cotton and printed calico, imitated from India after Indian imports threatened to 'flood the European market' - a small but real moment of the world-scale comparison Braudel favors, here Europe borrowing a technique from India rather than the reverse. Cotton's late-arriving dominance feeds directly into the opening of the English industrial revolution.

**Guilds as the market-economy floor that merchant capital captures from above.** The guild system (corps de metiers, Zunfte, Arti, gremios) organized craft production town by town from the twelfth through fifteenth centuries, and merchants were embedded in it from the start - producing, in Braudel's words, ambiguity and conflict rather than a clean division of labour. Guilds proliferated and specialized as the market grew (101 guilds in Paris by 1260; Nuremberg's Metallgewerbe splitting into dozens of trades), but the same boom that multiplied guilds also armed the merchants who would eventually dominate them. The Zunftrevolution - guilds rebelling against patricians - reads in Braudel's account as a genuine, if short, class war between merchant and artisan that the merchant ultimately won; Florence's split between Arti maggiori and Arti minori (il popolo grasso versus il popolo magro) is the visible fossil of that outcome. This is a clean instance of the market economy / capitalism distinction central to this volume: the guild-regulated town market is the transparent, rule-bound layer, while merchant capital operates as an extractive layer above it, seizing control of the labour market without necessarily entering production itself. Resonates with [[braudel-three-storey-house]].

**The Verlagssystem as a paradigm that assembled itself before anyone named it.** The putting-out system - a Verleger supplies raw materials and a part-wage, paying the balance on delivery - is documented far earlier than usually reckoned (a Paris ordinance of 1275 already assumes it), and the unit piles up independent, geographically scattered instances: Lucca silk merchants (1400), a Genoese silk-spinning dispute (1582), Le Puy-en-Velay lace (1740), Uzes weavers, Segovia's 'cloth manufacturers' under Philip II, Solingen's Fertigmacher cutlers, London hatters, Venetian shipyard labour, Brescia armourers, and the Zunftkauf contracts with whole guilds in Bohemia and Silesia. No single country invented it - Braudel hesitates between the Netherlands and industrial Italy - yet German historians were the ones who eventually 'detected, christened, analysed and explained' it as a system, centuries after it had been quietly operating. That shape - independent, unplanned instances accumulating until a pattern lurches into retrospective visibility and gets named - resonates with [[history-machine]], though here the 'assembly' is economic practice rather than an explicit paradigm shift.

**Institutional form outlives institutional function.** As the Verleger took over sourcing and sale, 'the guild system was gradually being destroyed, although outward appearances were maintained' - guild masters became wage-earners inside a shell that still looked like guild autonomy. This half-fits [[decay-sequence]]: a functional institution surviving as a performative or ornamental husk after its real power has migrated elsewhere - though the decay-sequence properly describes civilizational-scale infrastructure decaying over centuries, not a two-century hollowing of one economic institution, so the fit is a rhyme in shape rather than in scale.

**Capture was uneven.** Braudel quotes an unnamed historian's image of cottage industry caught in 'an invisible financial spider's web, its threads controlled by a few merchants' - and immediately qualifies it: the web did not catch everything. Parts of English wool manufacture, the nail-makers around Bedarieux, and Troyes linen-making stayed outside merchant control. The clearest case is the Gevaudan, where a specifically local, non-portable custom - immediate cash payment at the local market, gripped by the poverty of the peasantry - resisted a deliberate, well-documented attempt (by the entrepreneur Colson) to install the Verlagssystem there. This rhymes, loosely, with [[capture-resistance]]: a local condition functioning like terroir, repelling a standardizing network purely by being too entrenched to dislodge - though WMP's capture-resistance names a property of the coming Liveness era resisting algorithmic capture, not a peasant cash economy resisting merchant capital, so the connection is structural rather than substantive.

**Mining as the hinge into industrial capitalism proper.** Braudel treats mining, not textiles, as the sector where merchant capitalism achieves 'a new and decisive stage' - because here merchants did not just market a craftsman's output, they took over and reorganized production itself. The trigger was technical and financial: once shafts went deep, drainage, propped galleries, and winding-gear demanded capital far beyond an individual miner's or small team's means, opening the door to financiers like the Fuggers and the Augsburg houses across the Harz, Bohemia, the Tyrol, Hungary and Poland. Free Gewerkschaften miners became dependent wage-labourers - Braudel notes this is exactly when the word Arbeiter, worker, first appears - organized under a Werkmeister/Gegenmeister command hierarchy and exploited further through the Trucksystem of company-store goods. He treats this hesitantly but seriously as the emergence of 'a genuine working-class proletariat.' The boom was brief: diminishing returns and American silver competition pushed merchant capital to withdraw from the risky extraction end and retreat into safer downstream activities (smelting, distribution) - illustrating, in Braudel's own gloss, that 'the removal of investment is as characteristic of capital as investment,' capitalism's readiness to move sector to sector rather than binding itself to any one branch of production.

## Notable passages

The Nicholas Barbon passage (c1) is the unit's wittiest evidence for textiles' centrality to the pre-industrial economy - fashion as, in Barbon's phrase, 'the life and soul of commerce.' The merchant-versus-guild passage (c2) states the class-conflict reading of the Zunftrevolution directly, ending on the flat claim that what was at stake was 'economic domination by the merchant, not to say by capitalism' - the clearest single sentence tying guild history to the market-economy/capitalism distinction. The Verlagssystem definition (c3) gives both the mechanism and the earliest documentary trace, a 1275 Paris ordinance about silk spinsters. The spider's-web passage (c4) supplies the unit's central image of merchant capital as a network with uneven reach. The Gevaudan passage (c5) is the concrete counter-case: a region merchant capital could not net. The mining-proletariat passage (c6) is where Braudel most directly (if tentatively) claims that a Marxian-style proletariat was actually forming, and forming specifically through mining rather than textiles or guild crafts.
