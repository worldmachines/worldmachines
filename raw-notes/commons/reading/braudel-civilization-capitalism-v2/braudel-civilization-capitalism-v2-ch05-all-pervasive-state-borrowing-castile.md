# The All-Pervasive State: Its Tasks, Its Violence, and Castile's Debts

Braudel traces the rise of the modern European state from the fifteenth century — Bacon's 'Three Wise Men' (Henry VII, Louis XI, Ferdinand of Aragon) — as a parallel 'enormous movement' alongside capitalism, the Renaissance, and the scientific revolution, contrasted with the political continuity of the Ottoman, Mughal, and Manchu empires. He defines the state's three permanent tasks (monopolizing legitimate violence, controlling economic circulation and revenue, and managing spiritual and cultural authority), shows law and order as a class settlement enforced through spectacular public violence documented across Europe and beyond, and turns to state finance: the structural mismatch between expenditure and revenue that drove European states to invent public credit, worked out in detail through Castile's juros (government bonds) and asientos (short-term credit contracts) and the succession of bankers — German, then Genoese, then Portuguese New Christian — who financed, and were periodically ruined by, the Spanish crown's serial bankruptcies.

## Themes

**The modern state is a parallel construction project, not a single invention.** Braudel dates its 'determined expansion' to the fifteenth century and insists 'Only Europe was innovating in politics (and not in politics alone)' — the Osmanli, Mughal, and Manchu states meanwhile reproduced older imperial forms. This rhymes loosely with [[modernity-machine]]'s construction phase (roughly 1200–1600 built per the project's own dating), though Braudel's frame here is narrowly political/institutional rather than the full legibility apparatus — the passage on currency, taxation, and justice being imposed uniformly across a kingdom (Philip Augustus's denier tournois, Saint Louis's royal currency, Philip the Fair's royal taxation) is the clearer fit for [[legibility-as-machine-core]]: centuries-long, halting standardization of measure and command across contested local structures.

**Three tasks define stateness across eras and civilizations.** Braudel enumerates them explicitly: first, the monopoly of force, replacing private violence with what 'Max Weber called "legitimate violence"'; second, control of economic circulation and extraction of a share of national income; third, participation in and policing of spiritual and cultural life, from religious legitimation to watching for subversive ideas (humanists in Florence, Enlightenment philosophers). The examples deliberately range outside Europe (the Great Mogul's treasure, the Chinese emperors' storehouses, the Ottoman sultan's gold) to make the tasks civilization-general, not a European peculiarity.

**Law and order is a negotiated class settlement, not a neutral good.** 'When the state intervened for someone, it usually meant going to the rescue of the social hierarchy'; when it intervened against someone, 'it was inevitably the masses who had to be contained.' No state, Braudel argues, could function without 'the collaboration of the dominant class.' This settlement is enforced through spectacular, legitimated violence — public executions catalogued from Palermo, Toledo, Lyon, Cahors, and Paris to Tyburn, Teheran, and Salonika — presented as guaranteeing 'domestic peace,' 'the safety of the roads,' and 'the reliable provisioning of markets.'

**Fiscal structure forces the invention of public credit.** The state could no longer live off the prince's personal estate; taxation took centuries to become fully effective (Philip the Fair to Charles VII, roughly a hundred years for the taille alone); and 'state expenditure always ran ahead' of revenue. Braudel draws an explicit parallel to capitalism: both erect a 'hierarchy' (discreet in commerce, 'spectacular and conspicuous' in the state), and both 'resorted to monopoly to raise money' (pepper in Portugal, silver in Spain, salt in France, copper in Sweden, alum in the Papal States). The observation that 'it was simply not possible to go beyond certain limits' in taxing a kingdom's currency reserves echoes Braudel's own [[limits-of-the-possible]] (from [[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1]]): a structural ceiling on state action, not a matter of will.

**Castile's juros and asientos illustrate capitalism's 'anti-market' upper storey.** Juros (government bonds, from the fifteenth century, widespread under Charles V and Philip II) came as perpetual annuities, life annuities, or redeemable bonds, traded on an informal secondary market below face value. Asientos were short-term advances by 'businessmen' — overwhelmingly Genoese after 1552–57 — that let Spain convert the irregular flow of American silver into a steady stream of cash and, crucially, gold for troops in the Netherlands. The financiers who served the crown form a clear sequence: German bankers (Fuggers, Welsers) through 1557, then Genoese houses (Grimaldi, Pinelli, Lomellini, Spinola, Doria) via the Piacenza exchange fairs through 1627, then Portuguese New Christians fronting Dutch capital in the 1620s — each cornering the juros market ('they could hardly lose') until a royal bankruptcy (1557, 1560, 1575, 1596, 1627) burned them. This is Braudel's capitalism-as-anti-market thesis in miniature — monopoly, privilege, and long-distance credit dominating a nominally competitive bond market — resonant with the top-floor 'great predators' of [[braudel-three-storey-house]].

## Notable passages

The three-tasks passage (c1) is the unit's most systematic definition of stateness, anchoring the section that follows. The law-and-order passage (c2) states baldly that state violence is class-partisan, not neutral arbitration. The execution passage (c3) shows how that violence was legitimated and made spectacle. The state/capitalism parallel (c4) is the pivot into the finance discussion, naming the shared reliance on hierarchy and monopoly. The juros passage (c5) gives the concrete mechanics of Castile's bond forms and rates. The closing line (c6) — 'International capitalism could now behave as if it ruled the world' — is Braudel's verdict on the Genoese withdrawal from the asientos in 1627: state power outlasted by the mobile capital it depended on.
