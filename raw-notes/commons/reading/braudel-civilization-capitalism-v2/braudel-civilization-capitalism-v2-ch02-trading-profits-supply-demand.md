# Trading Profit, and the Riddle of Which Comes First

The unit opens Braudel's chapter on the mechanics that make long-distance commerce move at all. First he establishes 'trading profit' — the near-universal law that goods cost more the farther they travel, illustrated with a cascade of price ratios (a piece of eight worth 320 reis in Portugal and 480 in India; muslin at 3 reals in Le Mans and 12 in America; Chianti tripling in price between Greve and Milan) — and asks what this markup is actually paying for: transport cost, risk, and the merchant's motive to bother at all. From there he widens into the classical economists' oldest argument, supply versus demand as the *primum mobile* of exchange, using the Carrera de Indias silver fleets, French fashion for Indian cottons, and the physiocrats-versus-Say's-Law debate as test cases, and concludes the question is largely unanswerable in the abstract — exchange creates both terms simultaneously, and the balance of power between the parties often matters more than either.

## Themes

**Trading profit is the fuel, and 'royal merchandise' is where it is guaranteed.** Braudel treats the price gradient of long-distance trade as close to a natural law, then singles out a special category — Simon Ruiz's phrase 'royal merchandise' for pepper, spices, cochineal, pieces of eight — where profit was assured regardless of short-term price swings, because demand for it was 'virtually always there.' This is a market segment insulated from the ordinary friction of buying and selling: risk is real, but ruin is not, because these goods are structurally guaranteed a buyer. This maps closely onto Braudel's own market-economy/capitalism distinction elsewhere in the book: ordinary bulk goods (grain, salt, wood, wine) travel only when necessity forces them and profits are thin and competitive, while 'royal merchandise' occupies the privileged, monopoly-adjacent zone he elsewhere calls capitalism proper, the 'anti-market' of assured returns — resonant with [[braudel-three-storey-house]], though this unit states the idea in commercial rather than institutional terms.

**High prices are a magnet, not just a burden.** Braudel inverts the intuitive reading of expensive countries (Venice, Holland, Spain, England) as merely unfortunate: he stacks up Quesnay ('Abundance plus high prices equals wealth'), Arthur Young, Abbé Galiani, and Fynes Moryson's 'no more certaine signe of a flourishing and rich commonwealth, than the deere price of these necessaries' to argue that dearness is itself a symptom, maybe even a cause, of commercial supremacy — cost of living rising ahead of England's rise to trade dominance is the clearest case. This is presented as counterintuitive but well-attested across three centuries of commentators, not as Braudel's own novel claim.

**Supply and demand cannot be split apart, let alone ranked.** The chapter's structural argument, via Turgot's 'quadruple schema' (two things possessed, two things desired), is that supply and demand are mutually constitutive rather than sequential — there is 'no supply without demand and vice versa: both arise from the exchange that they create, and which creates them.' Braudel tests this against the Carrera de Indias: shipping from Seville was a closed conveyor belt that could seemingly be 'set in motion at any point,' and in fact was, twice, from the American side (silver-mine slowdown c.1610–20, revival c.1660) — yet after 1713 the same circuit could equally be driven by British supply flooding the market on credit. His conclusion is deliberately unresolved: the question of which came first is 'a question to which there is really no answer,' and that irresolution is itself the analytic payoff.

**Power decides what supply and demand even mean.** Beyond pure economics, Braudel insists the Spanish Atlantic trade must also be read as a command structure — the Crown's quint, the early voyages returning 'virtually with their ballast' of silver, French reports of 40 million livres out and 150 million back every five years — an 'unequal exchange' underwritten by force, not price signals. He extends this to the Manila galleon, where Mexico City merchants 'held the whip hand' over Manila (who in turn squeezed Chinese merchants), exactly as Amsterdam held it over its Leghorn agents. Under conditions of unequal power, he suggests, 'supply' and 'demand' stop being neutral categories — a claim that rhymes with [[braudel-three-storey-house]]'s picture of capitalism as privilege and domination layered above a competitive market, though Braudel frames it here as a caveat on economic vocabulary rather than a structural claim about eras.

**Demand splits into durable subsistence and volatile 'super-demand.'** Braudel separates massive, patient, structural demand (salt, wood, grain, wine — moved at extraordinary logistical effort across China's Imperial Canal, the Rhône, the Atlantic-to-Baltic salt routes) from fashion-driven 'super-demand' for luxuries (silk, then English cloth, then Indian calicoes) that is fickle but, once triggered, 'always had its way' — no amount of French sumptuary law, confiscation, fines, or threatened galley sentences could suppress the craze for printed cottons. The durable/volatile split matters because it is durable demand, in Quesnay's 'subsistence consumption,' that Braudel treats as the reliable long-run driver of supply, not luxury spending.

**Pre-industrial supply was structurally inelastic, and Say's Law doesn't retroactively apply.** Braudel walks through Say's Law (supply creates its own demand, via Smith, Steward, Turgot, Mill, Gide, Guitton) and Keynes's rebuttal, then asks whether Say was ever right for the ancien régime. His answer is no: agricultural supply chronically lagged demand and population growth, industrial 'supply' was hobbled by subsidized, non-entrepreneurial manufacturers, and workers' wages went straight to subsistence food rather than circling back as demand for the very manufactured goods they produced. F. J. Fisher's formula — agriculture limited by supply, industry limited by demand — is offered as the best summary, and Michelet's account of the 1842 French cotton-market collapse and sudden mass-demand recovery ('the sound of six sous seemed to act as a trigger') is used to dramatize how the industrial revolution was, in the end, a revolution in demand rather than supply.

## Notable passages

The 'royal merchandise' passage names the mechanism by which certain goods are lifted out of ordinary market risk — a structural precondition for what Braudel elsewhere calls capitalism's upper storey. The Quesnay/Young/Galiani/Moryson stack builds the counterintuitive case that high prices track wealth and supremacy rather than poverty. Turgot's 'two things possessed, two things desired' is the pivot on which the whole supply/demand argument turns — the formal statement of why the two cannot be prised apart. The Carrera de Indias and Manila galleon material push past pure economics into power: exchange conducted under a 'balance of power' isn't well described by supply and demand at all. Fisher's supply/demand asymmetry between agriculture and industry is Braudel's own preferred summary formula for the ancien régime economy, and Michelet's 1842 vignette is the closing illustration of demand, not supply, as history's actual engine at the threshold of industrialization.
