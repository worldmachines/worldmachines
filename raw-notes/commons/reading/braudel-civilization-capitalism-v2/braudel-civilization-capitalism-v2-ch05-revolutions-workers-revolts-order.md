# Peasant Revolts, Workers' Strikes, and the Politics of Order

This unit surveys five centuries of popular unrest beneath Europe's "established order" — hundreds of peasant insurrections, a scatter of urban workers' strikes (Lyons printers, Leyden weavers, Salisbury clothworkers) — and then asks why this constant friction so rarely produced durable change, and why the language that finally did challenge inherited privilege (Enlightenment revolutionary ideology, the *cahiers de doléance* of 1789) attacked the nobility's privileges but stayed almost silent about the privileges of capital.

## Themes

**Revolt was endemic but structurally contained.** Braudel piles up quantitative evidence — Yves-Marie Berce's five hundred Aquitaine peasant insurrections (1590–1715), two hundred clashes in a hundred German towns (1301–1550), a Lyons marked by disturbance in more than one year in three — to insist that "the innumerable revolts against the established order" were not exceptional but a continuous baseline condition, one Ingomar Bog reads as an illustration of *the long term* itself: repetitive, revivalist, monotonous. Yet the same evidence shows revolt losing almost every round: the state, nobles, bourgeois property-owners, the Church and the towns were "almost constantly in league against the peasant." Braudel's verdict is double-edged — failures were real, but "not however as complete as they appeared": the 1358 Jacques secured Paris-region peasant liberty, the 1525 Bauernkrieg rebels west of the Elbe kept their ancient rights where their eastern neighbours became serfs, and the crushed 1548 Guyenne rising still got the hated *gabelle* salt tax abolished. Partial, deferred, uneven gains rather than clean victories or defeats — a pattern that fits Braudel's *longue durée* sensibility of change working through structures too slow and diffuse to register as single events.

**Workers' movements were even more fragmented than peasants', for structural reasons.** Braudel attributes this not to weaker grievance but to weaker cohesion: an unstable, itinerant workforce (the *compagnon* system, workers split between town and country) denied protest "the solidarity born of stability which alone promised success," while the guild system stratified urban labour into wage-paying artisan-subcontractors, family-employing masters, ordinary wage-earners, and day-labourers "paid by the day if they were lucky and by the job if not." The result reads "like a succession of short and usually unconnected episodes" — but Braudel explicitly resists the inference that class consciousness was therefore absent: "the entire world of labour was caught in a vice between low wages and the threat of incurable unemployment," a bind he likens directly to modern unemployment, and violence "erupted" precisely because organized recourse was foreclosed.

**Where labour did concentrate, the market/capitalism distinction becomes visible in miniature.** The Lyons printing trade is Braudel's set-piece: a genuinely capital-intensive, wage-labour industry from its 1473 founding, yet the master-printers who owned the presses "did not really represent capital" — they were themselves dependent on merchant "publishers" who financed them, sat on the town's governing Consulat, and were backed by the municipal authorities against their own workforce. This is Braudel's [[braudel-three-storey-house]] logic playing out at trade-guild scale: production (the print-shop floor) sits on the transparent, competitive "market" level, while the real leverage — capital, credit, political backing — sits one storey up, in commerce that never touches a press. The workers' 1539 and 1572 strikes ("calling *tric*," forming the breakaway Griffarins society, inventing the *seigneur de la Coquille* as propaganda) were sophisticated for their date but lost both times, because the fight was never actually with the master-printers at all.

**Concentrated industry produced strong employer coordination well before strong worker coordination.** Leyden — Europe's largest cloth town, entirely vertically integrated from wool-washing to finishing, its manufacturers "united in a kind of cartel covering the whole of Holland" and meeting biennially in a "synod" to fix prices and wages and plan responses to unrest — shows the asymmetry starkly: Posthumus concluded "the class struggle was waged more consciously and aggressively by the employers than among the workers." Braudel pushes back on reading this as worker passivity: any workers' organization was prohibited outright, so "workers could not speak out openly in their regular assemblies. But the employers' reaction alone proves that silence did not mean indifference, ignorance or acceptance." The capacity to persist and resist was there; only the channel for expressing it was closed off — a capture apparatus (guild controls, wage-fixing cartels, criminalized assembly) holding down a pressure that the employers' own defensive posture betrays. This is a loose echo of what this project calls [[capture-resistance]] and [[Conatus]] — the underlying drive to organize and persist doesn't disappear under suppression, it just goes dark in the documentary record, though Braudel's frame is straightforwardly historical-materialist rather than about liveness or self-organization as such.

**The territorial state changed the scale, but not the outcome, of revolt.** Small city-republics (Ghent 1280, Florence's 1378 Ciompi) resolved unrest locally, "a little world in itself"; but the Lyons printworkers' 1539 grievances had to be referred all the way to the Paris Parlement, dispersing conflict in time and space and, Braudel suggests, complicating any general explanation of when revolt succeeds. He is explicitly skeptical of monocausal theories on offer (Laslett: rapid development calls for firmer order; Vierkand: diversified society permits more protest) — "a strictly-controlled society cannot develop very freely; a diversified society may bring pressure to bear on the individual from ten directions at once." What does hold, in his reading, is that state weakness both permits and signals unrest (France 1687–89, 1696–99, the "sliding" authority of Louis XV and XVI), but that turning discontent into effective action needs "ideological leadership, a language, slogans" — infrastructure that was "rarely forthcoming."

**When that ideological infrastructure finally arrived, with the Enlightenment, it targeted the wrong privilege.** Revolutionary ideology attacked "the privileges of a leisured aristocratic class" in the name of progress and the "active population" of merchants and manufacturers — but, Braudel notes, "the privileges conferred by capital were somehow brushed aside." The 1789 *cahiers de doléance* assault the nobility mercilessly and fall almost silent on the Crown and on capital. Braudel's explanation is not merely that the revolutionaries were themselves bourgeois, but that capitalist privilege got a free ride on the revolutionary demolition of aristocratic mythology (Boulainvilliers' "pure new blood" of Frankish conquerors, the myth of a society of orders): once *that* hierarchy was delegitimized, "the hierarchy of money as opposed to the hierarchy of birth" simply wasn't named as its own unjust order. Out of this came the model-employer myth and the laissez-faire mythology of automatic social harmony that fed nineteenth-century capitalism's self-satisfaction — Braudel closes pointedly by noting Marx himself "identify[ied] capitalism with economic progress until such time as its internal contradictions should manifest themselves." This is Braudel's own three-storey argument again: the anti-market storey survives scrutiny precisely by keeping itself out of the frame that reformers use to see privilege at all, continuous with the account of the merchant "publishers" standing behind the printers earlier in the unit, and with the broader argument of [[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1]] that material daily life is the visible floor beneath an often-invisible commercial and financial superstructure.

## Notable passages

The **peasant-revolt statistics** (Berce, German towns, Lyons) establish the sheer continuity of unrest as a baseline social fact rather than an aberration, setting up the essay's central puzzle: why so much revolt changes so little, visibly.

The **Lyons printers' strike** narrative (the "call tric," the Griffarins, the Printer's Devil) is Braudel's most concrete case of organized worker resistance with real tactics and institutions, immediately undercut by defeat in both 1539 and 1572 — the passage that earns his aside that the conflict was "frankly modern" in character despite its early date.

The **master-printers-are-not-capital** passage is the unit's clearest statement of Braudel's market/capitalism distinction in miniature: capital-owning masters are themselves subordinate to merchant financiers, which is why wage suppression, not product competition, was their only lever.

The **Leyden cartel/synod** passage shows organized capture (price- and wage-fixing, banned worker assembly) operating well ahead of organized resistance, with Braudel's rebuttal that enforced silence should not be read as acceptance.

The **Enlightenment-ideology** passage is the unit's payoff: revolutionary language that dismantled one hierarchy (birth) while leaving another (money) unnamed and unexamined, explaining why capitalist privilege stayed invisible until the Industrial Revolution made it impossible to ignore.
