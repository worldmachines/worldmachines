# Mining, Manufactories, and the Finance of Early Capitalist Enterprise

Braudel traces how, after the mid-sixteenth century, European merchant capital largely withdrew from mining and metallurgy at home — pushed out by falling yields, deforestation, and rising wood, coal, and wage costs — delegating extraction to Sweden, Norway, Russia, Siam, China, Japan, and above all Spanish America, while retaining tight control only of the mercury mines (Almadén, Idria) that the silver amalgamation process depended on; in the colonies as in Europe, profit accrued not to the miners or even the local supply merchants but to the long trading chains linking Potosí and Mexico to Seville. He then follows the same logic upward into pre-industrial manufacturing: salt, iron, and coal stayed mostly artisanal and landowner-run until the eighteenth century, while manufactories and factories — concentrated workshops still hybridized with dispersed outwork (the Verlagssystem) — grew chiefly under mercantilist state sponsorship and privilege (Colbert, Henri IV), illustrated at length by the Van Robais woollen works at Abbeville (labor discipline, a 1716 strike broken by troops) and by the finances of enterprises like Saint-Gobain, whose capital passed from risk-bearing financiers into the hands of noble rentiers content with modest fixed dividends.

## Themes

**Capitalism withdraws from extraction while the market economy absorbs it.** Once European ore yields fell and fuel costs rose, capital did not modernize domestic mining — it relocated the activity to the periphery and itself retreated into commerce. This restates [[braudel-three-storey-house]]'s market-economy/capitalism split in miniature: extraction itself, wherever it happened, stayed close to the market-economy floor — competitive, thin-margin, "the rule... was that the profits went to the merchants" rather than the miners — while capitalism proper occupied the long, discretionary, credit-and-fraud-riddled trading chains from Potosí and Mexico to Seville. Braudel's own closing line on the American mines — real profits made "along these trading-chains... not so much at the stage of extraction itself" — states the distinction almost programmatically.

**Ecological and energetic limits, not lack of technique, drove the relocation.** The immediate cause of Europe's withdrawal from its own mines was material — deforestation pricing wood and coal out of the blast furnaces, immobilizing fixed capital "to no purpose" — a ceiling on the pre-industrial energy regime rather than a failure of capitalist organization, in the spirit of Vol. I's [[limits-of-the-possible]] register carried forward into [[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1]]. Only in mercury, where no substitute source existed, did capital hold on stubbornly to European extraction (Almadén under the Fuggers until 1645, Idria under Austrian state monopoly) — which sharpens the point: capital's mobility tracked scarcity of alternatives, not any general aversion to extractive risk.

**Manufactories were market-economy institutions wearing capitalist state privilege.** Merchant capital rarely entered iron or coal directly — landowners, guilds, and small operators kept possession into the eighteenth century — and even the larger manufactories were, "virtually without exception," half-embedded in the Verlagssystem, keeping out-workers alongside a concentrated core so output could flex with demand without payroll risk. What let manufactories scale past artisanal size was less technique than the mercantilist "bullionist" state (Colbert, Henri IV) dispensing monopoly privilege, subsidy, and exemption from guild inspection to a favored few — an apparatus for building a nationally-scaled market by administrative fiat rather than organic competition.

**Labor discipline and rentierization mark capital's maturing grip on enterprise.** The Van Robais works at Abbeville show capitalist enterprise policing its workforce directly — armed troops broke the 1716 strike, and the subdélégué's own words ("they are there for the manufactory") make the inversion of purpose explicit. Saint-Gobain's finances show a complementary drift at the top: after its 1702 refinancing by Parisian tax-farmers, ownership passed by marriage into noble families who, as rentiers, preferred small steady dividends to the full extraction of profit — active entrepreneurial risk-bearing settling into passive, status-bearing yield. That drift only loosely echoes [[decay-sequence]] (a financing instrument turning ornamental rather than vanishing), and Braudel does not frame it that way himself, but the structural rhyme — function persisting while its social meaning shifts from enterprise to rentier status marker — is worth flagging.

## Notable passages

- **Mining's retreat to the periphery** opens the unit: falling yields, fuel costs, and rising wages drove Europe to "delegate the trouble" of mining and metallurgy to Sweden, Norway, Russia, Siam, China, Japan, and America, rather than reinvest at home.
- **Profit lives in the trading chain, not the mine**: Braudel's verdict on the American silver trade — merchants controlled supply but not the full chain to Seville, and "it was along these trading-chains with their ample opportunities for fraud that the real profits were made."
- **Iron stayed in landowners' hands**: the 1785 Upper Silesian survey (191 of 243 blast furnaces owned by landowners, only 2 by merchants) is Braudel's concrete evidence that merchant capital rarely entered iron production directly.
- **The mercantilist state manufactures manufactories**: Colbert and Henri IV's privilege-granting is presented as the real engine of manufactory creation — by 1610 Henri IV alone had set up 40 of France's 47 manufactories.
- **Van Robais: "they are there for the manufactory"**: the subdélégué's response to the 1716 strike, put down by troops, states the capitalist enterprise's inverted logic of purpose in its own words.
- **Saint-Gobain's capital passes from financiers to rentiers**: between 1702 and 1720 the mirror manufactory's shareholders shifted from risk-taking Parisian financiers to noble families married into the fortune, content with modest fixed dividends.
