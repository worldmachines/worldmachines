# Investigating the Hypotheses of Chinese Industrial Stagnation

This unit weighs various historical theories regarding the "Great Divergence" and the absence of industrialization in China. The author evaluates the tension between restrictive imperial policies and the reality of economic activity, explores the ossification of meritocratic institutions, and considers whether a cultural preference for stability ultimately stifled the conditions necessary for a technological takeoff.

## Themes

**Economic Agency vs. State Control**
While libertarian perspectives argue that Ming-era policies of centralization and state monopolies effectively killed innovation, the author suggests that the actual economy was more resilient. The drive for [[legibility-as-machine-core]]—through the control of movement and trade—was frequently bypassed by smugglers and decentralized market forces, suggesting that political intent did not always match economic reality.

**Bureaucratic Calcification and the Decay Sequence**
The imperial examination system represents a critical mechanism of social organization that may have undergone a [[decay-sequence]]. What began as a meritocratic tool for talent selection eventually became a mechanism of orthodoxy, favoring classical learning over the creative heterodoxy required to fuel a [[divergence-machine]] and driving the state toward stagnation.

**The Stability Imperative**
A recurring argument is that Chinese civilization prioritized social and political stability above all else. This cultural orientation acted as a brake on the disruptive, high-variance forces of industrial change, favoring a managed, predictable state over the unpredictable outcomes of a rapid technological transition.

## Notable passages

The author notes that the Ming dynasty was not as economically stagnant as often theorized, observing that the restrictive policies of the Hongwu Emperor were often bypassed, and concluding that "most of the Ming era was one of economic laissez faire."

The examination system is analyzed as a potential driver of stagnation, where the pursuit of meritocracy resulted in a system that selects for exam proficiency rather than creativity, eventually becoming an "engine of stagnation during the Ming and Qing dynasties."

Finally, the text touches on the cultural preference for stability, noting that the civilization's desire to avoid social consequences led to a state where "Nothing ever really changed, because the Chinese didn’t want anything to change."
