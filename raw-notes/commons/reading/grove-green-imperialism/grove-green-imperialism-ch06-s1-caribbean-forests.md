# Ch 6 opening: Tobago, St Vincent, and the legal machinery of British Caribbean forest reservation, 1760–1774

Grove opens Chapter 6 by pivoting from French Mauritius (Chapter 5) to the British 'Ceded Islands' of the Eastern Caribbean — Tobago, St Vincent, Dominica and Grenada, annexed from France in 1763 as the 'Grenada Governorate.' He argues that a comparable desiccationist conservation policy emerged here almost as early as on Mauritius, driven by two unlikely figures (Soame Jenyns and Alexander Anderson) and institutionalised through the London Society of Arts, but that it unfolded in a harsher political register: land-hungry colonial administrators used the ideology of 'cultivation' to strip the indigenous Carib population of any claim to land, provoking resistance that had no equivalent on the largely uninhabited Mauritius.

## Themes

**The Caribbean as a harder-edged version of the island story.** Grove is explicit that Mauritius was 'not... the only colony' developing conservation policy by the 1790s, but he immediately marks the disanalogy: where resistance to Mauritian forest protection came only from European landowners, Caribbean forest reservation collided with an indigenous population still contesting the ground itself. He reaches forward to compare this pattern to the much later, and equally contentious, forest conflicts of Western India and Southern Africa after 1830 — signalling that the Caribbean, not Mauritius, is the truer ancestor of colonial forestry's coercive strand.

**Cultivation as the legal technology of dispossession.** The chapter's sharpest move is naming Emmerich de Vattel's *Law of Nations* (translated into English in 1760) as the ideological instrument that fused environmental claims to territorial ones: land not visibly 'cultivated' — that is, not cleared of forest — could be treated as unowned and annexable. This makes forest classification a direct technology of control over people as much as over trees; Grove flags that 'whoever controlled the terms of this powerful discourse, in science and in legislation, might also affect or even police the control of people and land.' This resonates with [[Legibility]] and [[legibility-as-machine-core]]: land is rendered forest-reserve or cultivated-and-therefore-sovereign-territory as a classificatory act that makes populations governable, though here the legibility scheme is turned punitively against an indigenous population rather than merely organizing a settled peasantry.

**An institutional relay, not a direct doctrinal transfer.** Unlike Poivre's more philosophically Rousseauist French desiccationism, Grove says the British version arrived 'haphazard' and mercantile, relayed through personal ties between the Académie des Sciences, the Society of Arts (founded 1754, with an explicitly Baconian lineage — Solomon's House, the Hartlib circle, the Dublin Society precedent) and the Lords Commissioners for Trade. This traces a concrete information-network path: prize incentives for tree-planting and plant transfer (logwood, cinnamon) that by 1765 produced the first botanical garden in the Americas, at Kingstown, St Vincent — read against the orientation note on the colonial botanical garden as an infrastructure of empire, this fits the [[modernity-machine]] frame of an information network organizing colonial knowledge and incentive, and the Baconian self-description is explicit enough to link directly to [[francis-bacon]].

**Theory legislated ahead of local belief.** The formal instruments — the March 1764 Tobago proclamation and the January 1765 Barbados ordinance — state the desiccationist rationale outright: forest reserves exist 'to preserve the seasons' and 'to prevent that drought which... is the usual consequence of a total removal of the woods.' Yet the official actually charged with siting the reserves, Dr George Young, privately held the opposite (miasmatic) view — that forest removal made islands healthier by purifying 'sea air... by reason of its not yet being infected in its passage over hot and reeking woods.' Grove uses this to show colonial science arriving in practice, well ahead of any local consensus, and immediately compromised by the priority given to clearing lowland for sugar.

## Notable passages

The unit introduces Soame Jenyns and Alexander Anderson as the Caribbean analogues to Poivre, both critics of imperialism for very different reasons, with Anderson already marked as 'first curator of the St Vincent Botanic Garden' — foreshadowing his larger role later in the chapter. The Carib-resistance paragraph is the chapter's pivot, moving from 'was largely confined to the protests of European landowners' on Mauritius to 'episodes of determined resistance by the Carib population,' before landing on Vattel's doctrine that those who did not cultivate land had no right to retain it. The 1764 proclamation and 1765 Barbados ordinance are quoted directly as Grove's primary legal evidence for desiccationist state policy, and Young's own words are quoted at length to dramatize the gap between metropolitan theory and colonial administrator's belief. The Society of Arts paragraph supplies the institutional backstory Grove needs before turning, in the section that follows, to the intellectual origins of English desiccationism itself.
