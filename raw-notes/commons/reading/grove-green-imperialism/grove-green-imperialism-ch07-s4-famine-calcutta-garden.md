# Fear of famine and the founding of the Calcutta and St Helena botanical gardens

Captain Robert Kyd's 1776 proposal for a Calcutta botanical garden, framed explicitly as a hedge against Bengal famine, becomes the seed of an imperial network: Calcutta, St Vincent and St Helena are drawn into a single botanical-garden axis over the 1780s-90s that carries French physiocratic conservation thinking (via Poivre's Mauritius and Alexander Anderson's St Vincent) into East India Company policy, culminating in an explicit 1794 Company directive tying tree cover to rainfall on St Helena.

## Themes

**Famine, not commerce, was the garden's founding rationale.** Kyd's original case to the Board rested on moral responsibility: British rule had brought riches to Britain while Bengal continued to suffer famine "in the course of twenty-two years," and "every possible means of averting these dreadful visitations" remained "yet unthought of, untried." His remedy was not Warren Hastings's grain-depot model (the Bihar public granary) but a stock of drought-resistant plants, above all the sago tree, propagated at Calcutta and disseminated village by village as a famine reserve independent of the grain harvest.

**The humanitarian pitch was politically unstable and gave way to a commercial one.** Grove notes that in Kyd's "later and more successful approaches to the Board, he was compelled to stress the commercial advantages that might accrue to the EIC while significantly playing down the merits of the project for famine prevention" — the garden survived institutionally only once recast as a commercial and scientific asset rather than a famine-relief scheme, with the St Vincent precedent (Anderson, backed by Banks) supplying the decisive argument.

**Physiocratic ideology moved from Mauritius to Bengal via Banks's network.** Poivre's Pamplemousses garden was the acknowledged model; Banks, Arthur Young and John Sinclair are named as "English physiocrats" who absorbed French agronomic thinking and used their growing influence over EIC garden appointments (Wallich, Roxburgh) to keep Calcutta and Kew in direct contact, embedding a state-interventionist conservation ethic that later fed 1840s Indian forest conservation.

**The garden network functioned as an information infrastructure, not just a plant-transfer system.** By 1790 roughly fifteen men ran the empire's tropical botanical gardens (Kyd at Calcutta, Roxburgh at Samulcottah, Porteous on St Helena, the Andersons in the West Indies, and others), part of a wider cadre of some 130 collectors and superintendents between 1770 and 1820. The Calcutta-St Vincent axis, transiting through St Helena, moved plants west-to-east and new environmental ideas east-to-west; Grove's own gloss is that "environmental centres of calculation" were "succeeded by an information network" — a striking image of colonial-scientific legibility built out of gardens and correspondence rather than cadastres or censuses.

**St Helena carried a double load: practical way-station and symbolic paradise-lost.** Kyd pushed for a formal St Helena garden as a horticultural relay point (fruit trees were dying in transit to Bengal), but Banks had separately invested the island, since 1771, with a "lost paradise" symbolism set against Tahiti's unspoiled image — a preoccupation that Grove traces forward into Burchell, Roxburgh, Beatson and eventually Darwin's island thinking on extinction and species change.

**By 1794 desiccation theory had become explicit Company doctrine.** The 1791-92 drought was global in the tropics — St Helena, the Northern Circars (mass famine deaths and a fatal exodus from Rajahmundry), and Montserrat all suffered — and the Company's own directors wrote to St Helena that "trees have an attractive power on the clouds" and ordered replanting at Longwood accordingly. Grove traces this doctrine to a convergence of two channels reaching the Court of Directors by 1794: the St Vincent/Anderson/Dundas line and Alexander Beatson's 1793-94 survey of Mauritius, which introduced him to Poivre's example directly. Roxburgh's parallel meteorological record-keeping at Samulcottah and his teak-planting programme in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa round out a converging, if still practically motivated, climatic consciousness inside the colonial state.

## Notable passages

Kyd's letter to the Board (c1) lays out the moral-accounting argument for famine relief as the price of legitimate rule. His drought-resistant-crop plan (c2) shows the specific, distribution-network logic — plants reaching "every village" as insurance against grain failure — that made the garden a famine-mitigation technology rather than a pleasure ground. The network paragraph (c3) contains Grove's own "centres of calculation... information network" formulation, the clearest single sentence connecting this unit to a legibility-infrastructure reading. The 1794 Court of Directors letter (c4) is the primary-source high point: an administrative body stating desiccation theory as settled fact and issuing a planting order on that basis. The drought-famine passage (c5), anchored in Beatson's testimony, supplies the human and geographic scale — India, St Helena, Montserrat — that made the doctrine feel empirically forced rather than merely fashionable.

Grove's framing of an "information network" succeeding "centres of calculation" resonates with [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and [[modernity-machine]]: a colonial-scientific correspondence network standing in for the more familiar cadastral/statistical apparatus of legibility, built for plant transfer but repurposed to move climatic and political knowledge (famine data, rainfall theory) back into metropolitan and colonial decision-making. It also brushes against [[the-legible-commonwealth]], since Kyd's sago-tree scheme imagines famine relief delivered through a legible, garden-fed distribution network reaching "every village" — though Grove's interest is institutional and scientific-historical, not a theory of state legibility as such, and the hedge matters: this is a fragile, improvised, often underfunded network (Kyd's own commercial reframing, the Company's "grudging" response to drought), not a mature machine core.
