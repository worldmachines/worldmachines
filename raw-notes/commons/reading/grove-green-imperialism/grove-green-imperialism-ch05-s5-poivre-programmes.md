# Pierre Poivre's Physiocratic Conservation Programme on Mauritius, 1767–72

After the bankrupt Compagnie des Indes collapses and the French Crown takes over the Mascarenes by royal edict, Pierre Poivre is urged into the new post of commissaire-général (intendant) of Mauritius; he lands on 14 July 1767 and over the next five years launches a sweeping, physiocratically-inspired programme of forest conservation, land-use regulation, agricultural reform, and anti-slavery advocacy, cast in openly Edenic and providential language, and fought out against the resistance of Governor Dumas, speculating colonists, and a hesitant Minister Praslin.

## Themes

**Physiocracy fused with Edenic utopianism.** In his inaugural speech to the assembled colonists (3 August 1767), Poivre tells them they inhabit a "physical and social paradise" of "perpetual spring" — a phrase Grove traces to Dante's *Purgatorio* and to the language of early European botanical-garden builders — contrasted with a France crushed by taxation and "a horde of men more terrible in the countryside than all the insects." Grove calls this a "Utopian vision of a tropical island" projected onto the colony's planters; Poivre later locates the literal Garden of Eden "in a corner of Asia" in his treatise *De Amazonas*, and treats the restored botanical garden at Le Réduit as a physical, cultivable Eden-substitute. This reads as a colonial, state-enacted descendant of the [[thomas-more]] / [[more-utopia]] tradition of the imagined ideal commonwealth — though Poivre's Utopia is not a literary fiction but an ordinance-backed programme he expects planters to build on an already-existing island.

**Deforestation as sacrilege, aridity as revenge.** Poivre indicts "reckless and ignorant men" who "ravaged the island, destroying the trees by fire," leaving "arid lands abandoned by rain." He binds a causal, proto-desiccationist claim — forests "determined... where a fertilising rain would fall" — to a theological one: past exploitation is "sacrilegious," the landscape is sacralised, and future good conduct will see "Providence... respond with blessings." Grove is careful to note Poivre exaggerated the pace of change after a decade's absence, even though Bellin's 1763 survey confirms real clearance around Port Louis. This collapsing of natural and moral order into a single register is a vivid instance of the [[nature-culture-dialectic]] running through the book.

**Conservation enacted as state legibility.** The programme is concrete administrative machinery, not just rhetoric: a new Land Court (Tribunal Terrier) to review concessions handed out "without principle and without any fixed plan"; an attempted survey and reversion of unworked grants to the Crown; mandatory windbreaks and small cleared plots; a 20-arpent felling cap per concession; forest belts reserved along the coast (the future *pas réservés*) and rivers; a fixed common timber price to kill speculation; and bans on wood in construction and palisades. Poivre requests, and is refused, the formal title Inspecteur des Eaux et Forêts. Grove frames this explicitly as "formal land-use planning" aimed at "state control of land use on a rational basis" — a small colonial instance of the mapping-and-standardizing project [[Legibility]] and [[legibility-as-machine-core]] describe, though the resemblance is functional (surveying, licensing, quota-setting) rather than doctrinal: Poivre's legibility drive is explicitly moralized and providential, not a disenchanted bureaucratic rationality, and Grove keeps it embedded in physiocratic doctrine and personal mission rather than any general theory of the state.

**Land speculation, absentee landlordism, and slavery as one moral economy.** Poivre's forest policy cannot be separated from his fight against land speculators — the same colonists who burn woodland for quick concessions — including a bitter dispute with Governor Dumas over a 10,000-arpent grab at Trois Islots, and objections to absentee grandees like the Comte de Polignac. Grove draws out a further pairing: Poivre's "new moral economy" contains "two essential underlying elements: the restoration and protection of nature and the restoration and protection of the slave population," and Poivre speaks of deforested ground as "land in servitude." His anti-slavery sympathy (Jesuit-formed, sharpened by arguments against Buffon's American-degeneracy thesis) and his environmentalism are treated as two faces of one moral vision, though Grove notes Poivre had far less power to act on the former.

## Notable passages

The opening speech's paradise language (c1) shows Poivre's rhetoric drawing on the same Edenic-island discourse Grove traces across the book. The "reckless and ignorant men" indictment (c2) is Poivre's own fusion of causal and moral argument against deforestation. The windbreak instructions (c3) are a verbatim policy prescription translating desiccation theory into a clearing ordinance. The land-use-planning passage (c4) is Grove's clearest statement that conservation here meant comprehensive state rationalisation of land. The "land in servitude" passage (c5) states outright Grove's thesis that Poivre's environmental and anti-slavery commitments were one moral economy.
