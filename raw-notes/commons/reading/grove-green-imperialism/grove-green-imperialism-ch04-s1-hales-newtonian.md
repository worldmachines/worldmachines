# Stephen Hales and the Newtonian roots of desiccation theory, 1700–1763

Grove traces the deep prehistory of colonial “desiccationism” — the theory that deforestation dries the climate and reduces rainfall — from scattered, discontinuous early-colonial observations, through John Woodward’s empirical but untheorized forest–rainfall claims of 1699–1708, to Stephen Hales’s Newtonian synthesis of plant physiology and atmospheric physics in *Vegetable Staticks* (1727), and finally to the rapid uptake of Hales’s ideas by the French Jardin du Roi under Buffon and Duhamel du Monceau — the institutional lineage that later chapters show delivering Poivre-era conservation on Mauritius.

## Themes

**Desiccation theory has ancient antecedents but no continuous tradition before the eighteenth century.** Grove traces the theme to Theophrastus and its Renaissance revival with Columbus, but stresses discontinuity: Diego de Esquivel’s 1579 account already links reduced Indigenous cultivation, deforestation and worsened health/climate in Mexico, yet forests are still read as "disease-ridden and dangerous" well into the seventeenth century — a prejudice Grove says only a scientifically grounded belief in forests’ climatic benefits, not sentiment or naval-timber shortage alone, could dislodge.

**John Woodward supplies the empirical correlation but no mechanism.** Woodward’s 1699 transpiration experiments and his 1708 claim that tree cover correlates with rainfall and humidity are, in Grove’s account, asserted "with conviction" but without "firm mechanical or physiological or even statistical data" — a gap Newtonian physics, applied by Hales two decades later, would close.

**Hales supplies the mechanism by fusing Newtonian physics with a physiological “circulation” world-picture.** Hales’s intellectual path runs from Newtonian planetary models (1707–12) to blood-circulation experiments (1712–14) to sap-flow and finally atmospheric experiments culminating in *Vegetable Staticks* (1727) — the same circulation logic applied at bodily, planetary and atmospheric scale. Applying "Newtonian ideas about motion and the conservation of energy" to plant–atmosphere relations let Hales reverse the old assumption that forests were unhealthy, giving later desiccationists an experimentally credentialed authority to invoke. [[isaac-newton]] is the figure whose physics this entire chapter's intellectual genealogy runs through, though Grove's interest is strictly in Newtonianism's uptake by naturalists and physicians, not in Newton's own natural philosophy.

**A global data-gathering apparatus underwrites the new theory.** Woodward’s correspondence network of colonial naturalists and his 1696 *Brief Instructions for Making Observations in All Parts of the World* — which became the template for official Royal Society travel instructions in 1704 — is presented as integral to how desiccation theory formed: standardized instructions turned scattered colonial anecdote into comparable, theory-building data. This loosely resonates with [[legibility-as-machine-core]] — a protocol for rendering the world's climates and vegetation legible and comparable across independent observers — though the resonance is partial: this is a scientific-method infrastructure feeding theory, prior to and separate from any colonial-state administrative use of the resulting climatic claims.

**Hales’s authority long outlives its own moment.** Grove shows the theory’s staying power through Edward Long’s 1770s *History of Jamaica*, which invokes Hales’s account of vegetation "reviving" vitiated air without apology nearly fifty years after *Vegetable Staticks* — evidence of how long an early-Enlightenment scientific claim could go on authorizing colonial forest-protection arguments.

**The theory crosses the Channel faster than it spreads at home.** Hales’s ideas, Grove argues, found "a far more rapid and favourable impact in France than they did in England," carried by the Jardin du Roi’s post-Colbert institutional appetite for English science: Du Fay’s tours, the de Jussieu brothers’ 1727 visit, and especially Duhamel du Monceau’s 1729 stay (ostensibly to study shipbuilding timber) feed directly into Buffon’s decision to translate *Vegetable Staticks* and into Duhamel’s own work correlating rainfall with vegetation growth. Grove notes the Royal Society, the Académie des Sciences, and Colbert’s 1660s forest reforms were all instituted "on Baconian lines" — the institutional precondition, per [[francis-bacon]]'s programme, for both national scientific establishments this unit follows.

## Notable passages

- Diego de Esquivel’s 1579 account of Mexican deforestation (c1) is the chapter’s earliest primary voice: he links reduced Indigenous cultivation to "great jungles and forests which make all the region wild, swampy and unhealthy," but — Grove is careful to note — Esquivel still wants the forest cleared, not preserved; the desiccation logic here runs in the opposite direction from the island conservationism the book builds toward later.
- Woodward’s 1708 claim (c2) that American settlers burning and destroying woods left "the Air mended and clear’d up space, changing into a temper more dry and serene than before" is curated as the first clear published forest–rainfall claim — notable for arguing deforestation *improves* climate, the reverse of the desiccationist conclusion Hales’s generation would draw.
- Woodward’s *Brief Instructions* and its adoption as the Royal Society’s travel-instruction template (c3) is curated because Grove credits it as the infrastructural precondition for turning global anecdote into theory-building data.
- The passage on Hales’s Newtonian synthesis followed by Edward Long’s 1770s Jamaican citation of it (c4) is the hinge of the unit: it states Grove’s causal claim in his own voice, then shows via Long’s own words how durably that claim retained authority in a live colonial-policy debate decades later.
- The passage on Buffon, Duhamel du Monceau and the Jardin du Roi (c5) is curated as the concrete transmission chain — Cambridge Newtonians to Paris state science — that the book’s later French chapters (Poivre, Mauritius) depend on.
