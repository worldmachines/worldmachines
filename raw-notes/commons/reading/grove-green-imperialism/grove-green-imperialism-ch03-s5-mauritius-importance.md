# Mauritius before 1716: Dutch Governors, Dodo Extinction, and Huguenot Edenic Anxiety

Grove traces roughly a century of Dutch VOC occupation of Mauritius during which unregulated ebony extraction, introduced predators, and hunting drove the dodo and the island's land tortoises to extinction, prompting the first tentative Dutch conservation measures (Hugo's game laws, Lamotius's proposed timber fee, Deodati's hydrological observations) even as later French Huguenot refugees, above all François Leguat, reinterpreted the same destruction through a Calvinist lens of Edenic loss — setting up Grove's pivot to the more systematic, Newtonian-inflected French climatic environmentalism that follows the 1716 annexation.

## Themes

**Proto-conservation grew out of administrative self-interest, not ecological principle.** Governor Hugo's 1670s legislation against feral dogs and pigs targeted damage to deer and cattle stocks, not habitat protection; Lamotius's proposed per-plank fee on ebony (c2) was framed as a fiscal safeguard for "the state" rather than for the forest itself. Grove reads this as an early instance of a pattern that recurs across the book: colonial administrators, lacking a personal stake in extraction profits, tend toward a more critical view of rapid resource depletion than the commercial interests they govern.

**The dodo became Europe's first modern extinction-as-spectacle.** Long before Buffon gave species loss scientific weight, the dodo's rarity was already a literary and collector's trope — Clusius, Sir Thomas Herbert (1627), Tradescant's 1656 catalogue, Bontius's 1658 natural history — circulating in European print well ahead of any conservationist response (c1). Grove is explicit that this circulating "idea of rarity" did not translate into protection; even Lamotius, who likely knew of the bird's decline, should not be credited with a conservationist reaction to it.

**Hydrological observation anticipates, but is not yet, desiccation theory.** Deodati's 1696 description of Mauritius's mossy, water-retaining "wet forest" (c3) is a pre-theoretical noticing of the link between forest cover and water regime — exactly the kind of observation that Poivre and the Newtonian-influenced French would later formalize into desiccation theory. Grove is careful to deflate Deodati's originality, tracing the insight back to van Reede and van der Stel at the Cape, and cites Deodati's own claim that Mauritius's timber was functionally inexhaustible as evidence of how shallow his grasp of the problem actually was.

**Huguenot Calvinism recast island extinction as sin.** For François Leguat and his refugee colleagues (c4), each vanished species was not a resource loss but "a step further away from a God-given Eden and... towards imperfection and corruption" (c5) — the unspoiled island read as both a literal Eden and an allegory of religious and social restoration after persecution. Grove treats this as a hinge: Dutch empiricism (governors noticing declining stocks) gives way to a more ideologically loaded, moralized register of environmental description that the French would inherit and systematize.

**Mauritius, not the Cape, becomes the intellectual center of French climatic environmentalism.** The unit closes by repositioning the island: where Dutch responses were ad hoc and empirical, the French would fuse the same raw material — deforestation, extinction, changed local climate — with Newtonian physics into "a fully developed and reformist social ideology" (c6). The essay's real argument is not that the Dutch were proto-environmentalists, but that their century of undocumented damage created the observational deposit that French science would later theorize.

**World Machines resonances.** Lamotius's touring, mapping, and reporting of Mauritius to the Cape administration — he "produced the first detailed map of Mauritius" and sent surveys to Governor van der Stel — is a small, concrete instance of a colonial state rendering territory legible before it can be administered or protected ([[Legibility]], [[legibility-as-machine-core]]); the abortive botanical garden he tried to establish at Flacq gestures toward the same information-infrastructure role Grove elsewhere assigns to Pamplemousses and the Cape garden within an emergent [[modernity-machine]], though on Mauritius the institution collapsed for want of a resident botanist rather than taking root. The unit's own arc — decades of scattered, often contradictory observations (Beverwyk, Hugo, Lamotius, Deodati, van de Velde, Leguat) that only cohere into a systematic French ideology after 1716 — rhymes with the [[history-machine]] pattern of silent, distributed assembly, though Grove's story is intellectual and religious rather than institutional. Leguat's restored, unspoiled island as both literal Eden and allegory of social reformation also echoes the island-utopia trope of [[more-utopia]], though More's island is a secular political device and Leguat's is explicitly Calvinist and providential.

## Notable passages

- c1 records the dodo's descent from scientific curiosity (Herbert 1627, Tradescant's catalogue, Bontius) to actual extinction by 1681, the earliest thread in what becomes a book-length argument about islands making ecological loss visible within a human lifetime.
- c2 is Grove's clearest early statement of the state-versus-capital theme that structures the book: an administrator without a financial stake in timber becomes its likely critic.
- c3 preserves Deodati's own 1696 words on the water-retaining "wet forest," the observational seed of the desiccation theory Grove develops in later chapters, framed here with explicit scepticism about Deodati's originality.
- c4 and c5 together carry Leguat's voice and Grove's gloss on it: the primary account of vanishing geese, tortoises, and sharks, and the Calvinist cosmology that turned that loss into a narrative of Edenic corruption.
- c6 is the pivot sentence: Dutch empiricism giving way to a French, Newtonian-inflected "reformist social ideology," which is where the rest of the chapter is headed.
