# Pierre Poivre: Cosmopolitan Formation and the Making of a Climatic Forest Policy on Mauritius, 1719–66

This unit traces the intellectual and biographical formation of Pierre Poivre (b. 1719, Lyons) from Jesuit novice and captive-traveller across China, Cochin China, Batavia, Pondicherry and the Cape Colony, through his absorption of French physiocratic economics in the 1750s circles of Quesnay, Mirabeau and Cantillon, to his 1766 appointment as intendant of the Isle de France (Mauritius) — an appointment whose founding instructions, drafted by the Duke of Praslin, made rainfall anxiety and forest conservation explicit objects of French colonial policy for the first time. Grove argues that Poivre's hard-won, comparative grasp of soil, drought and irrigation across several non-European agricultural traditions, fused with a physiocratic political economy that treated agriculture as the sole source of national wealth, gave him both the causal theory (desiccation from deforestation) and the institutional template (the state botanical garden, the licensed and reserved forest) needed to turn island-scale ecological anxiety into state policy.

## Themes

**Praslin's 1766 instructions inaugurate a state climatic forest policy.** The instructions, quoted at length, tie spice-production ambitions to an explicit fear that if the forests do not regenerate, rain will grow less frequent and the exposed soil will burn; they direct the new governor and intendant to survey existing regulation, study the actual condition of the forests, license only conservation-compatible cutting, and declare 'Royal Reserves'. Grove calls this a wholly new departure in French colonial policy, and is careful to note that the instructions were themselves reverse-engineered from Poivre's own 1763 Lyons lectures and lobbying — the state script follows the scientist-propagandist here, not the other way round.

**A cosmopolitan, largely accidental education.** Poivre's authority did not come from formal training but from an itinerant career forced on him by circumstance: Jesuit missions and house arrest in Canton gave him working Chinese and access to centuries-old Chinese writing on denudation, erosion and flooding; capture by the British in 1745 (in which he lost an arm, his botanical collections, and his vocation) landed him in Dutch Batavia; a 1749 stopover at the Cape exposed him to the VOC's state botanical garden and its wind-break tree-planting regime; later voyages carried him through Malabar, the Philippines, the Moluccas and Pondicherry. Grove treats this itinerary as the empirical substrate underlying everything Poivre later argued about Mauritius.

**Physiocracy supplies the political economy.** Pensioned and returned to France between 1757 and 1766, Poivre entered the circle around Quesnay (whose *Tableau économique* and Hippocratic, body-as-system reasoning he absorbed) and Richard Cantillon, whose image of the state as a tree — agriculture its roots, population its trunk, commerce its leaves, a storm-battered canopy renewable only if the roots keep their vigour — gave Poivre a naturalistic vocabulary for treating deforestation as a threat to the state itself, not merely to land. Physiocracy's hostility to plantation cash-cropping and mercantilist bullion-accumulation, and its championing of resident smallholders over absentee landlords, shaped Poivre's later attacks on the Compagnie des Indes's mismanagement of Mauritius.

**Rejection of degeneracy theory and elevation of non-European knowledge.** Against Buffon's fashionable theory that hot climates and corrupted customs degenerated tropical peoples from an undegenerate European original, Poivre insisted on a cosmopolitan levelling of all peoples as equally fallen — a move that let him treat Chinese, Cochin-Chinese and Malabari irrigation and tree-planting knowledge as technically authoritative rather than merely curious. Dupont de Nemours also attributed to Poivre a personal debt to Zoroastrian ethics of agriculture as a near-sacred duty.

**Desiccation theory carried different stakes in the tropics.** Grove distinguishes Poivre's tropical desiccationism from the temperate-zone version current in Europe: in seasonally arid Mauritius and South India, deforestation-driven rainfall decline threatened agriculture directly, whereas in contemporary Europe and North America forest clearance was widely seen as a positive good (a consensus that would not reverse in the United States until George Perkins Marsh's 1864 *Man and Nature*). Poivre's comparative method — the Cape's planted windbreak groves against Mauritius's fire-cleared, unsheltered fields — supplied him the closest thing to a controlled experiment available.

## Notable passages

- Praslin's 1766 instructions (c1) are the policy's charter document, bundling spice ambition, rainfall fear and forest licensing into a single directive — arguably the unit's clearest instance of a colonial state making its forests legible and governable ([[Legibility]]), an early move of the kind the [[modernity-machine]] makes wherever it reaches the periphery, though Grove's own framing is administrative-historical rather than machine-theoretic and the instructions are as much propaganda victory for Poivre as state initiative.
- Poivre's 1763 Lyons speech (c2), reconstructed by Grove from quoted fragments, states the causal mechanism baldly: fire-clearing without interval woods stops the rain from falling on cleared land and leaves fields with no shelter from the wind — with the Dutch Cape's planted windbreaks offered as the positive counter-case.
- Cantillon's organic image of the state as a tree (c3) — roots, trunk, canopy — is the physiocratic root-metaphor underlying Poivre's fusion of agronomy and statecraft; it resonates with a broader nature-culture dialectic ([[nature-culture-dialectic]]) in which bodily and vegetal metaphors do real argumentative work in political economy, though here the metaphor serves a static, equilibrium-seeking physiocratic vision rather than any evolutionary or systems-dynamic one.
- Poivre's 'man has forgotten his destiny' passage (c4) rejects Buffon's degeneracy theory and, implicitly, the contemporary vogue for an idealised primitive figure ([[jean-jacques-rousseau]]; compare [[noble-savages-and-english-gardeners]]) — Poivre's own position is neither primitivist nor supremacist but a discriminating respect for specific non-European technical traditions (Chinese, Malabari), which only half-rhymes with Rousseau's more abstract, politically loaded figure.
- The Cape VOC garden passage (c5) describes the garden as an experimental and redistributive institution — free seed and instruction to any settler, provisions for passing ships — the template Poivre carried to the Jardin du Roi on Mauritius; a small institutional case of the collection-and-circulation infrastructure that Bayly's account of an already interconnected, 'archaically global' eighteenth-century world ([[archaic-globalization-bayly]]) describes at much larger scale, though Grove's interest here is horticultural-administrative rather than world-historical.
- Poivre's own habit of picturing Mauritius as a garden — fertile, watered 'like a garden', yet chronically failing — plays on the same island-as-Eden imagery Grove traces elsewhere in the book, and echoes, loosely and without the political-satirical edge, the bounded, cultivated commonwealth-ideal of [[more-utopia]].
- Adam Smith appears only in passing here (his *Theory of Moral Sentiments*, translated into French in 1764, is cited as a link between mercantilist and physiocratic thought) ([[adam-smith]]), a reminder of how thoroughly cross-Channel the political economy Poivre absorbed already was.
