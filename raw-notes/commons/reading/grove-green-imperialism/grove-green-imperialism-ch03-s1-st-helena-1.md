# St Helena's fall from paradise: goats, plantation schemes, and the East India Company's first ecological crisis, 1502–1698

Grove opens Chapter 3 by contrasting metropolitan Europe's environmental anxieties — largely confined to fears of a timber shortage — with the much wider, more urgent range of land-management and conservationist responses provoked at the colonial periphery, especially on tropical islands that had also become a literary and philosophical vehicle for Utopian and scientific speculation about nature. The unit then turns to St Helena, held by the English East India Company from 1659, as the case study: first sighted by the Portuguese in 1502 as an entirely forested ‘earthly Paradise’ for passing ships, the island was transformed within less than two centuries into a byword for water shortage, deforestation, and failed plantation schemes, chiefly through the runaway spread of goats and the Company's own agricultural ambitions — all encountered and locally, idiosyncratically managed well before comparable problems became visible to the Company in India.

## Themes

**St Helena as the Company's first, paradigm-setting encounter with degradation.** Grove insists St Helena's importance is “quite disproportionate to the size of the territory”: it was where the EIC first met the consequences of land degradation, “long before such problems became apparent in India itself,” and its later Indian environmental policy was shaped by these early experiments. Crucially, the St Helena response was not scientific: it developed “without a structured scientific or professional critique’s being developed by the home authorities in London,” making it “idiosyncratic and dependent on the initiatives taken by individuals” — a control case Grove will later set against Mauritius, where Poivre's conservationism was explicitly theorised.

**From mariners' Eden to a logged-over rock.** Early travellers — Linschoten in 1589, Pyrard de Laval in 1601 — described St Helena in unambiguously paradisal, even providential terms (“an earthly Paradise for the Portuguese ships”). Grove tracks the same witnesses' return visits and successors (Pyrard again in 1610, Tavernier in 1649, Navarette in 1673) registering the same island as stripped of fruit trees and wood, attributing the first wave of destruction to competitive over-harvesting between rival ships' crews rather than settlement itself — a paradise narrative degrading within the lifetime of single observers, not across generations.

**The goat as the primary engine of anthropogenic change.** Introduced, with other livestock, by the first settler Fernando Lopez in 1533, goats “constituted the single most effective agent of deforestation and landscape change,” reaching “thousands” by 1582 and drawing, in Beatson's retrospective 1816 verdict, sole blame for “the total ruin of the forests.” Grove is explicit this was not unique to St Helena — the same animal would do the same work “on Montserrat and Mauritius and many other islands” — and closes the unit noting that by 1700 the goat's global ecological impact was provoking legislation “in many parts of the world, including South-Eastern Europe.” The paradise-to-ruin arc, in which an ostensibly untouched ‘natural’ Eden is unmade by animals introduced for human economic ends, is a vivid instance of the [[nature-culture-dialectic]]: the goat is simultaneously a tool of settler intent and an autonomous ecological force outrunning anyone's control.

**The island as a literary laboratory for the new cosmology.** Francis Godwin's fictional *The Man in the Moone* (1638) sets its hero down on St Helena as “the only paradice... that the earth yieldeth,” then uses the island's bounded smallness as a springboard — literally, via a flying machine of trained swans — to dramatise Copernican astronomy and “an emerging consciousness of the limits of the earth itself.” Grove reads this as inaugurating a genre, running through Cyrano de Bergerac to Swift and Defoe, in which the literal island microcosm becomes the imaginative device for thinking about ecological and civilisational limits.

**Known knowledge, ignored at home.** Grove flags a pointed irony: the EIC itself funded and hosted Edmund Halley's 1676 St Helena expedition, whose data on “the relative water-storage capacities of bare soil, rock and vegetation” made it one of “the first systematic studies of environmental processes” — and Royal Society publications had already documented West Indian deforestation's environmental costs as early as 1675. Despite direct institutional access to both, the Company “persisted with its blinkered refusal to appreciate the environmental effects of developing a colonial island economy.”

**Mapping, enclosure, and the limits of control at a distance.** The Company's attempt to fix and defend property boundaries — commissioning Antony Bealle's map of “every man's land on the Island with... due and just Buttals and Boundrys” — ran directly against colonists' de facto assertion of common grazing and forest-use rights, a conflict Grove says “faithfully reflected in microcosm many of the debates about enclosure which had long been familiar in England.” A 1683 directors' letter tried to abolish commons outright, calling them “nurseries for thieves and beggars,” but distance defeated intent: island governors, unlike an English improving landlord, routinely compromised with colonists' customary land use, and full enclosure was never achieved.

## Notable passages

The opening paragraph states Grove's chapter-level thesis in miniature — the periphery, not the metropole, produces the wider range of environmental response, with the tropical island doing double duty as ecological case study and literary/Utopian metaphor. The second paragraph makes the specific claim for St Helena's priority and idiosyncrasy that structures everything that follows. The goat passage supplies the unit's central causal mechanism, moving from Lopez's 1533 introductions through Cavendish's 1582 headcount to Beatson's 1816 verdict.

The Godwin passage is the unit's clearest Utopian-literary material and its most direct engagement with the island-as-bounded-world theme; it resonates with [[more-utopia]] and the wider Utopian tradition associated with [[thomas-more]], though Godwin's target is Copernican cosmology and the discovery of earthly finitude rather than More's social-institutional critique — a natural-philosophical Utopia rather than a political one.

The Halley passage is Grove's sharpest irony about institutional knowledge versus institutional action: a company that had itself just financed some of the first systematic environmental-process data in the world went on to ignore its implications for decades.

The mapping and enclosure passage is the unit's clearest instance of an early-modern actor attempting to render colonial land tenure legible — exact boundaries, a named surveyor, a map for transmission back to London — in the sense flagged by [[Legibility]]. Grove's own narrative then shows the attempt failing against distance and entrenched local custom, a concrete, small-scale illustration of why [[legibility-as-precondition-not-cause]] insists that legibility infrastructure does not by itself yield state control. The episode also prefigures, in miniature, the plantation-economy and enclosure ambitions that a World Machines reading might connect to the early [[modernity-machine]]'s drive to render land and people administrable — though Grove's own frame is agrarian-administrative history rather than machine theory, and his point here is precisely that Company control remained partial and contested, not totalising.
