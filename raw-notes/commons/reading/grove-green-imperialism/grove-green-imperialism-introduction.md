# Introduction: Environmentalism's Colonial and Insular Origins, 1660–1860

Grove's Introduction stakes out the book's central, revisionist claim: a scientifically informed critique of "development" and a programme of state conservation were fully established by the early nineteenth century, and their origins lie not in Romantic-era metropolitan Europe or the American tradition of George Perkins Marsh, Henry David Thoreau, and Theodore Roosevelt, but at the colonial periphery — above all on small, previously uninhabited tropical islands (the Canary Islands, Madeira, the West Indies, St Helena, Mauritius) where deforestation, soil erosion, and species loss became visible within decades. This island-scale crisis fused with an old European fantasy of the tropics as Eden and with emerging climatic "desiccation" theory to produce, on Mauritius under Pierre Poivre, Philibert Commerson, and Bernardin de Saint-Pierre (1768–1810), the first systematic colonial conservation programme, whose logic was then carried by company surgeons and botanical gardens to the Caribbean and, after 1820, to India.

## Themes

**Colonial periphery, not metropolitan Europe, as the source.** Grove opens by directly contesting the historiography that treats environmentalism as a home-grown Euro-American response to industrialization. He argues that elevating Marsh, Thoreau, and Roosevelt to a "pantheon of conservationist prophets" has obscured "the central significance of the colonial experience in the formation of western environmental attitudes and critiques," including the diffusion of Indian environmental philosophy into Western epistemology. This resonates with [[c-a-bayly]] and [[archaic-globalization-bayly]] as parallel arguments for writing connective, non-nation-bound intellectual history, though Bayly's frame is a broader global political economy while Grove's is a narrower, traceable chain of institutions and individuals running through company botany.

**Islands as compressed, legible ecological experiments.** Bounded, small, previously uninhabited islands made ecological cause and effect visible within a human lifetime in a way continental landscapes did not; Grove treats the island as a totemic, world-scale analogue, "an easily conceived allegory of a whole world" prefigured by the "detached self-consciousness" of Robinson Crusoe. This is a strong resonance for World Machines' interest in islands as bounded test beds where a system's feedback surfaces fast — what Grove attributes to physical geography and scale, WMP would also read as a legibility effect: a small, enclosed system is easier to render measurable and governable than a continent ([[legibility-as-machine-core]]).

**The Edenic imagination as a colonizing frame.** The search for Eden in the tropics — rooted in pre-Christian myth and Persian Zoroastrian *Pairidaeza*, reinforced by Calvinist ideas of nature-knowledge as a path to God — supplied the imaginative scaffolding letting islands and botanical gardens stand in for the whole world. Grove pointedly rejects a simple "Judaeo-Christian attitudes are inherently destructive" thesis, attributing rapid ecological change instead to the transforming force of colonizing capital rather than to culturally specific religious attitudes.

**The colonial state as heterogeneous and unexpectedly interventionist.** Grove's most argumentative theme is a direct rebuttal of "monolithic theories of ecological imperialism": colonial states, he argues, had a "heterogeneous and ambivalent" character and were "peculiarly open" to the radical agendas of the scientific lobby, at times overriding short-term capitalist interests to protect long-term state security — producing interventionist land-management regimes "very difficult to impose in Europe." The theme is double-edged: the same conservation apparatus, he notes, later became "a highly convenient form of social control" over "unruly marginal subjects" and a trigger for anti-imperialist resistance. This resonates strongly with [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and [[modernity-machine]] — a state building the capacity to see and manage a territory's ecology in fine detail — though Grove's point inverts the usual assumption that legibility radiates outward from an imperial core: here the colonial periphery is *more* capable of environmental intervention than the contemporary metropole.

**Company science as an administrative machine.** Conservationist ideology survived and spread because it was carried by a professional caste — physicians and surgeons doubling as botanists and garden-keepers, already "an essential part of the administrative and hierarchical machinery" of the trading companies (Grove cites over 800 East India Company surgeons by 1838) well before comparable state-science careers existed in Europe. Colonial botanical gardens (Pamplemousses on Mauritius, St Vincent, Calcutta, the Cape) and scientific societies, drawing on "the Baconian organising traditions of the metropolitan institutions of Colbert" ([[francis-bacon]]), formed a network through which climatic and desiccationist theory diffused between colonies and back to the metropole.

**Desiccation theory and its transmission chain.** Grove traces a specific intellectual lineage: Woodward and Hales's plant-atmosphere physiology reached Buffon and Duhamel du Monceau at the Jardin du Roi, who linked trees to climate; this fed French physiocrat reformers and [[jean-jacques-rousseau]]'s followers on Mauritius, and, separately, English Society of Arts figures who pushed Caribbean forest-protection schemes (St Vincent, 1765) and eventually persuaded the East India Company to apply the same logic to St Helena and, after 1820, to India via Alexander von Humboldt's ecological holism — itself indebted, Grove stresses, to Hindu philosophical monism transmitted through Herder rather than a purely European invention. [[alexander-von-humboldt]] and [[wulf-invention-of-nature]] are directly on point, though Wulf's account centers Humboldt as an individual synthesizer where Grove foregrounds the indigenous-knowledge debt and the institutional relay through Scottish-trained East India Company surgeons (Gibson, Balfour, Cleghorn), whose Edinburgh/Glasgow/Aberdeen medical training resonates with [[scottish-enlightenment]], and who used the theory to win a forest-conservancy system in India by 1852.

**Nature and culture as jointly constructed objects.** Grove frames the "garden" and the "island" as twin "symbolic (or even totemic) forms" — global analogues, manageable in scale, offering "the possibility of redemption, a realm in which Paradise might be recreated or realised on earth" — through which Europeans organized their perceptions of nature. Medical botanists doubled as arbiters classifying both plants and indigenous peoples along climatic lines, and non-European knowledge (the Malabar sources behind the Dutch *Hortus Malabaricus*) forced changes in European categories of natural knowledge. This resonates with [[nature-culture-dialectic]] and, more loosely, with [[the-english-garden-as-epistemic-object]] and [[noble-savages-and-english-gardeners]] — Grove's garden is colonial and multi-sited rather than specifically English, and his "noble savage" reference is a passing aside about stereotyping rather than a developed argument, so the fit is partial. The invocation of Tahiti and Mauritius as "Utopias" likewise brushes against [[more-utopia]] without developing the connection.

**Anxiety about nature as displaced anxiety about the settler self.** Grove closes by arguing that fear of climatic and ecological collapse was never purely about nature: it mirrored colonists' anxiety about their own physical and social survival in an alien climate, sharpened by the loss of biblical chronological certainty — "the whole potency of man and his capacity for primacy and control might mentally be lost." Environmental protection and social reform were, he argues, historically bound together, which is why Calvinist Holland, the ex-Jesuit Poivre, and a "procession of Scottish Protestant doctors, missionaries and travellers preaching an environmental gospel" each recur as carriers of the same impulse.

## Notable passages

The opening reframing (c1) insists that the current sense of environmental crisis as novel is a misreading of a much older policy history, reaching back through eighteenth-century state forest policy to Theophrastus. Grove then names his target directly (c2): the Anglo-American conservationist pantheon has crowded out "the central significance of the colonial experience" and the diffusion of Indian knowledge into Western environmental thought. The island passage (c3) is the empirical core of the book's title claim — the paradox that the "Edenic island discourse" reached full flower exactly as Canary Islands, Madeira, West Indies, St Helena, and Mauritius plantations were visibly degrading, producing "a coherent and wide-ranging critique of environmental degradation." The colonial-state passage (c4) carries Grove's sharpest historiographical argument, that "monolithic theories of ecological imperialism" misread a colonial state that was open to scientific lobbying and capable of overriding capital's short-term interests. The Mauritius passage (c5) names the book's first concrete case — Poivre, Commerson, and Bernardin de Saint-Pierre's 1768–1810 conservation programme, grounded in Rousseauian and physiocratic thought — and the closing passage (c6) traces how Humboldt's Hindu-inflected ecological holism and the Scottish-trained East India Company surgeons Gibson, Balfour, and Cleghorn turned that Mauritian precedent into an Indian forest-conservancy system by the 1850s.
