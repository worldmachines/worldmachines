# Alexander Anderson and the King's Hill Forest Act: official environmentalism on St Vincent, 1784–1811

After St Vincent's return to British control in 1783, Grove traces how the desiccationist forest-conservation project abandoned in the Tobago model was revived and locally re-rooted through the institutional presence of the St Vincent Botanic Garden and its long-serving superintendent, the Scottish physician-botanist Alexander Anderson (curator from 1785 until his death in 1811). Anderson's advocacy, together with Assembly member William Bannatyne's sponsorship, produced the King's Hill Forest Act of 1791 — a modest, contested, but institutionally consequential piece of legislation that reserved a hilltop forest 'for the purpose of attracting the clouds and rain,' and that Grove treats as a template later echoed on St Helena and in India.

## Themes

**The botanical garden as a local 'centre of calculation.'** Grove explicitly borrows Latour's term: before Anderson, the intellectual authority behind Caribbean desiccationism sat in Paris (the Académie des Sciences) and London (the Society of Arts), with colonial administrators like William Young merely executing (or resisting) ideas formed elsewhere. Anderson's arrival, and the garden's growing patronage under Sir Joseph Banks, gave St Vincent its own node of scientific authority — 'partially dependent on outside networks' but also doing genuinely 'autonomous intellectual work.' This is a clean instance of the botanical garden functioning as an information infrastructure, not merely a plant collection.

**Carib resistance reshaping, not just blocking, environmental policy.** Grove is careful that the shift from Tobago's top-down model to St Vincent's more autonomous one was not simply an intellectual maturation — it was forced. The Caribs' 'practical and symbolic rejection of the colonial map' had already broken the dependent, purely metropolitan structure of desiccationism, pushing policy toward 'a compromised construct based more on indigenous social reality.' Later in the unit this same fault line resurfaces in the Assembly debate: some planters wanted the King's Hill land itself cleared for cotton and sugar, but the sharper anxiety was that forest reservation would leave the Caribs 'potential cover for future insurrections' behind the 1773 treaty boundary line — meaning clearance, not conservation, was what colonial social control of the Caribs actually required. Environmental legibility and political control did not align here; they pulled in opposite directions.

**Contested legislative history as a record of political friction.** The bill, tabled in 1788 and drafted by Bannatyne 'with Anderson's advice,' took over two years and several deferrals to pass, delayed chiefly by planters fearing their own estates might be 'sterilised' by future reserves modeled on it. Even after Assembly passage, Governor James Seton forced an amendment reasserting Whitehall's authority — changing the reservation from being made 'on behalf of the colony' to 'on behalf of the king' — and the final wording added 'clouds' to 'rain,' a small textual change Grove reads as a trace of otherwise-unrecorded technical meteorological debate among Seton's advisers.

**Cross-fertilisation with the Indian Ocean environmentalism of Mauritius.** Grove places Anderson's unpublished manuscript 'A Geography and History of St Vincent' (along with draft materials for 'The Delugia,' c. 1799–1805) alongside Pierre Poivre's 1767 speeches to Mauritius colonists and Bernardin de Saint-Pierre's *Studies and Harmonies of Nature* — framing Caribbean and Indian Ocean desiccationism as two branches of a single emerging global environmental critique rather than parallel but unconnected developments.

## Notable passages

The unit opens by locating the pre-Anderson intellectual geography of Caribbean environmentalism in Europe's own institutions (c1), then pivots to how Carib resistance forced a more autonomous, locally-constrained 'centre of calculation' to emerge on St Vincent around Anderson and the garden (c2). The Assembly debate over the King's Hill bill exposes how forest reservation and social control of the Caribs were seen as working against, not with, each other (c4). The Act's final enacted wording — reserving the hill 'for the purpose of attracting the clouds and rain' — is quoted in full as the concrete legal instrument this advocacy produced (c3). Finally, Grove's aside comparing Anderson's manuscript to Poivre's Mauritius speeches and Bernardin de Saint-Pierre's natural philosophy situates Anderson within a wider Enlightenment environmentalist lineage (c5).

The unit resonates with [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and [[Legibility]]: the garden network functions as exactly the kind of legible, information-gathering infrastructure the [[modernity-machine]] runs on, letting a distant colonial hillside be surveyed, gazetted, and administratively 'reserved' for a stated climatic purpose. But it resonates just as strongly, and more precisely, with [[legibility-as-precondition-not-cause]]: the same garden-and-networks capacity produces a failed, purely top-down desiccationism on Tobago and a contested, hybridised, locally-authored one on St Vincent — the infrastructure is constant, but what it is coupled to (Carib resistance, planter self-interest, an individual advocate's persistence) determines the outcome. Grove is a historian of colonial science, not a machine theorist, and draws none of this framing himself; the claim is offered as a resonance, not his.
