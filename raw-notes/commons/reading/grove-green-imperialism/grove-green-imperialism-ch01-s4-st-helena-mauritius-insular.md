# St Helena and Mauritius: The Insular Discourse of Nature in Early Travel Literature

Grove reads seventeenth-century travel accounts of the uninhabited oceanic islands on the Cape–India trade route — above all J. A. von Mandelslo's 1647 descriptions of St Helena, Ascension, and Mauritius, and Le Guat's 1690 account of Rodriguez — as the seedbed of a distinctively insular discourse of nature: increasingly empirical rather than purely allegorical, obsessively concerned with water and climate, and, by the end of the century, capable of valuing wild nature for its own beauty rather than only as fallen or useful.

## Themes

**Empiricism displaces pure allegory, but the Edenic frame persists.** As long-distance travel became more frequent and regular, and as European interest shifted from the Americas toward the India-oriented East, travel writing grew "increasingly practical, empirical and accurate" without abandoning "the old Edenic images." Uninhabited islands like St Helena and Mauritius, valuable only as staging posts, were "peculiarly amenable" to this projection: to exhausted sailors they read as untouched paradises of unfamiliar plants and birds.

**Water, not fruit, is the real measure of paradise.** Mandelslo's St Helena account piles up conventional plenty — figs, pomegranates, citrus, fowl, fish — but Grove shows the underlying logic is practical: an island is desirable to the extent it can water and revictual a fleet without the disease risk of a continental landfall. The waterless Ascension is dismissed by the same measure. Strikingly, man's presence is here read as *improving* the island's fruitfulness ("without man, it is implied, the island would have been less fruitful") — the inverse of the deforestation-and-desiccation story Grove tells about these same islands once plantation colonization begins.

**An embryonic desiccation theory.** Mandelslo's aside on St Thomas Island in the Cape Verdes — a mountain "covered with trees, and overspread with a cloud, which supplies it with fresh water" — links tree cover to rainfall well before Grove's later protagonists (Poivre, the East India Company surgeons) turn that link into an explicit theory and a conservation programme. It appears here only as an observational curiosity, not yet a policy argument.

**The island as psychological and allegorical laboratory.** Mauritius's tame, easily clubbed birds foreshadow the dodo's extinction, but Grove's more striking point is literary: Mandelslo's account of a Frenchman shipwrecked and marooned on Mauritius in 1601 fed directly into Defoe's *Robinson Crusoe*. The isolated island becomes a testing ground for a European stripped of "previous custom or knowledge" — a proto-empirical thought-experiment in what nature does to a person with no inherited framework for it.

**National-literary divergence on the same islands.** Grove contrasts Dutch empirical secularism (Mandelslo) with English political-escapist imagery (Marvell's island as "mental refuge from political turmoil") and, after 1690, a French Huguenot register in which Rodriguez becomes a literal refuge-Eden for religious exiles under du Quesne. Three different national sensibilities produce three different discourses about materially similar places. The same islands are also being folded, via du Quesne's language of an "Isle of Eden" sufficient "to contain easily a long descent of generations," into a Utopian tradition of the bounded island-society — a real-world echo of [[thomas-more]]'s [[more-utopia]], though Grove's islands are being colonized in fact, not merely imagined.

**Disconnecting the Fall of Man from the Fall of Nature.** The unit's capstone move is Grove's argument that Le Guat's praise of Rodriguez's "wilderness... undisturbed by man" — and his illustrations' "bookish and intellectual sense of paradise," against the frankly utilitarian Dutch illustrations of Van Neck a century earlier — marks a Calvinist-inflected reassessment that separates the "earthly paradise" from the theological Fall, letting nature be admired for its intrinsic beauty. Grove explicitly calls the Huguenots "precocious forerunners of eighteenth-century Romantic thought," naming Bernardin de Saint-Pierre as the line's later heir. This is a real shift in the [[nature-culture-dialectic]], and it anticipates the nature-veneration later associated with [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] — though the Huguenots arrive at it through exile and Calvinist theology, not Rousseau's secular civilizational critique, so the resonance is partial.

For World Machines: this unit is pre-administrative — it documents a literary and perceptual construction of the island as a bounded, legible, describable Eden, prior to and separate from the scientific/state conservation apparatus Grove builds in later chapters. That makes it a plausible precondition rather than a cause of the later legibility project: the discourse makes islands *thinkable* as measurable, boundable microcosms before any state acts on them that way, which resonates with [[legibility-as-precondition-not-cause]]. Grove is a historian of ideas and institutions here, not a machine theorist, and this connection is my inference rather than his claim.

## Notable passages

Mandelslo's St Helena report (c1) is Grove's primary evidence for the paradise-as-provisioning-station logic: abundant, watered, safe for sick sailors to recover — with the King of Portugal deliberately leaving it undeveloped so "all ships passing might find refreshment there." The framing paragraph that follows (c2) draws out the water-as-paramount reading and the odd inversion whereby man's presence enhances rather than degrades the island. The St Thomas Island aside (c3) is the unit's smallest but most consequential passage for Grove's larger argument: the tree-cover/rainfall link that will become desiccation theory proper by the 1760s. The Mauritius passage (c4) pivots from paradise to vulnerability, tracing a line from tame, clubbable birds through the dodo to the shipwrecked-Frenchman narrative that shaped *Robinson Crusoe*. Le Guat's own words on Rodriguez (c5) — "this little new world," "this earthly paradise to which we had been conducted" — are the refugees' own paradisal register, worth curating in their own voice rather than only in Grove's paraphrase. The closing synthesis (c6) is where Grove states his strongest claim: that the Huguenots' wilderness-admiration, contrasted with earlier Dutch utilitarian illustration, marks a genuine theological and aesthetic turn toward valuing nature undisturbed by man.
