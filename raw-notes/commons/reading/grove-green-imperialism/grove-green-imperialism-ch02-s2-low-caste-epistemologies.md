# Low-caste epistemologies and the diffusion of Asian botanical knowledge

Grove traces, across two case studies a century apart — Garcia da Orta’s Cologuios (Goa, 1563) and Hendrik van Reede’s Hortus malabaricus (Malabar, 1678–93) — how European medico-botanical knowledge of Asia was substantially assembled from indigenous, and specifically low-caste, sources rather than transmitted classical or Arabic learning, and argues that low-caste contributors (above all the Ezhava toddy-tapper caste and the physician Itti Achuden) directly shaped the printed taxonomy that later fed Linnaeus and colonial Indian botany.

## Themes

**Orta’s Cologuios stages a dialectic that favours the empirical and local over inherited authority.** Structured as a dialogue with a sceptical interlocutor, the 1563 text is “remarkably subversive and even hostile to European and Arabic knowledge,” repeatedly privileging personal experience, friendship-mediated access to guarded local systems, and the testimony of eyewitnesses over Galen, Aristotle, or the Arabic canon Orta was formally taught in. Grove links this stance to Orta’s own precarious, boundary-crossing position — a hidden Jew dependent on Muslim patronage (the Nizam Shah), running an experimental garden on Goa, “suspended between botanical traditions.”

**Caste, not only colonial hierarchy, gated access to knowledge.** Orta’s text shows real intimacy with Arab and Khorasani medicine and admiration for Malayali doctors, but Grove stresses that caste compartmentalization meant Orta achieved “very little substantial access” to non-Brahmin/Malayali knowledge — a limit overcome more successfully by van Reede a century later. Grove is careful that this cuts against any easy equation of “empirical/technical knowledge” with “Hindu or Indian knowledge” generally: Brahminical teaching itself sat uncomfortably with the empirical stance, and it is specifically low-caste, practically-trained knowledge (Ezhava Vaidyar physicians, Curumbi cultivators, Mali gardeners) that carries the evidentiary weight.

**Van Reede’s Hortus malabaricus found Brahmin learning “merely academic” and pivoted decisively to low-caste field experts.** Starting from a European template (Father Mathew of St Joseph’s Viridarium orientale) and then Brahmin consultants (Ranga Bhatt, Vinayaka Bhatt, Apu Bhatt), van Reede found their botany “quite weak and entirely dependent on the restatement of dictums from old texts,” and within two years had shifted “entirely to a reliance on Malayali sources” — above all the Ezhava physician Itti Achuden and his fellow toddy-tapper-caste tree climbers, who selected, named and explained the roughly 780 species eventually illustrated. Grove treats this as the book’s real, under-credited authorship, evidenced by the Malayalam-script certificates (Carneiro’s and Achuden’s own, both attested 20 April 1675) appended to the printed Hortus.

**Aesthetic wonder, not only commerce or medicine, drove the project.** Van Reede’s own preface describes the Malabar forest in the language of Dutch urban architecture — a “magnificent, elegant and delightful palace” — and extends the same imagination to conceive all of Malabar as a cultivated “garden of the world,” comparable to the island of Ceylon/Taprobana; Grove notes this mental template of landscape-as-garden later shaped van Reede’s response to deforestation at the Cape (1685).

**The Ezhava classificatory system survived in print and travelled far beyond Malabar.** Ezhava plant names encoded genuine botanical affinity together with social/ritual meaning (e.g. Onapu, named for the Onam harvest festival), and Leiden’s editors (Syen and Commelin) preserved that sequence even against their own European taxonomic instincts — a sequence Linnaeus (1740), Adanson, Jussieu, and later Roxburgh, Buchanan-Hamilton and Hooker in India all built on. Since Achuden’s own papers are lost, Grove treats the printed Hortus as “the only faithful textual record of the accumulated Ezhava botanical knowledge of the seventeenth century” — a case where an indigenous institution (modelled on Cochin’s Royal Council) was co-opted successfully enough to fix orally/textually compartmented low-caste knowledge into a durable, internationally circulating form. This resonates, with real qualification, with [[legibility-as-machine-core]]: what gets fixed into print here is scientific/commercial rather than fiscal-administrative legibility, and Grove’s interest is in whose knowledge survives the fixing, not in state control as such. The Goa and Leiden gardens acting as nodes that channel, filter and re-export local knowledge also echoes a colonial-garden-as-information-infrastructure reading of the [[modernity-machine]], though Grove’s frame here is intellectual/scientific history rather than machine theory. Grove’s aside that the “Portuguese networks were marginal extensions of an existing Indian Ocean system of plant transfer” fits [[archaic-globalization-bayly]]’s point that pre-existing Afro-Asian circulatory networks, not European initiative, did the underlying work Europeans later claimed credit for. Van Reede’s architectural, garden-saturated vision of the Malabar forest is also a strong instance of [[nature-culture-dialectic]] — “wild” tropical nature rendered legible to a European eye only once seen as cultivated, built and ordered.

## Notable passages

- Orta’s own indictment of Portuguese incuriosity — merchants interested only in “how best to dispose of that merchandise,” never “from what tree it comes” — is Grove’s clearest evidence that indigenous knowledge-gathering was not a natural byproduct of Portuguese trade but something Orta had to argue for against the grain of colonial practice.
- Van Reede’s first-person account of dispatching Ezhava collectors in teams of three to the forests, and stationing painters to depict what they brought back “nearly always in my presence,” is the closest the unit comes to showing the Hortus’s actual production process, and makes plain that Ezhava labour and expertise, not European botanical training, generated the book’s raw material.
- Van Reede’s “palace” simile for the Malabar forest shows the aesthetic register operating prior to, and shaping, the scientific one.
- Itti Achuden’s own attested certificate — identifying him as “the hereditary Malabar physician” whose book of plant properties was examined and dictated for the Hortus — is the rare instance of a low-caste source naming and describing his own contribution in his own words (in translation).
- Grove’s synthesis of the Ezhava naming system’s afterlife in Linnaeus and Indian colonial botanists is the unit’s strongest claim for the “diffusion” in its title: a specific low-caste epistemology outliving its bearers and reorganizing formal European taxonomy.
