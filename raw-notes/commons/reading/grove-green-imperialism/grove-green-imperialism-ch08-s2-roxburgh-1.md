# William Roxburgh's Hippocratic and Priestleyite programme: climate, famine and tree-planting in Madras and Bengal, 1776–1825

Grove traces the thirty-seven-year career of William Roxburgh, an East India Company surgeon who arrived in Madras in 1776 and died superintending the Calcutta Botanic Garden in 1813: trained under John Hope in Edinburgh (and so an inheritor of Stephen Hales's and Duhamel du Monceau's plant physiology), Roxburgh fused Royal Society meteorology, Priestleyite pneumatic chemistry, and a company surgeon's medical brief into a single interventionist programme — decades of instrumented weather-recording on the Coromandel Coast, a diagnosis of the 1789–92 (and, by historical comparison, 1685–8) droughts and famines as compounded by East India Company land-tenure policy, and an expanding regime of food-tree and timber plantation across Madras, then Bengal, Bihar and Orissa, continued after his death by Nathaniel Wallich. Running alongside this is Grove's account of the botanical garden itself as an institution carrying Mughal and European Edenic connotations, and of the contested, epistemically limited status of the tree-atmosphere theory Roxburgh bequeathed.

## Themes

- **Priestleyite “atmospheric virtue” as moral economy.** Roxburgh's Edinburgh training under Hope made him heir to Hales and Duhamel du Monceau, but it was his identification with Joseph Priestley's dissenting Warrington Academy circle, and with Sir John Pringle's reading of Priestley's pneumatics as underwriting a “benevolent economy,” that let Roxburgh equate the “virtue” of the atmosphere with the “virtue” of a society — vegetation literally cleansing and moralising the polity it grew in, storms burying “putrid and pestilential effluvia” that plants alone could not consume.

- **Meteorological data collection as an imperial “centre of calculation.”** From 1776 Roxburgh kept an unbroken instrumented weather record for the Coromandel Coast, forwarding it to Pringle for publication in the Royal Society's *Philosophical Transactions* — plugging Madras into a data-collection network reaching back to Jurin's 1724 international call for observations (endorsed by Newton's circle) and revived after 1770 as European states, notably France's Société Royale de Médecine (1778), institutionalised medical and agricultural climatology as state policy.

- **Famine as an object of climatic and institutional diagnosis.** The 1789–92 droughts — now attributed to an unusually strong El Niño — devastated the Circars; Roxburgh's response combined historical research into a comparable 1685–8 drought (using indigenous oral and documentary testimony) with an indictment of company-reconstructed zamindari landlordism as the deeper cause of famine vulnerability, converging independently with Edmund Burke's contemporary critique and with praise for pre-colonial irrigation.

- **Tree-planting as policy developed in India, not imported from Europe.** Roxburgh's Bengal teak plantations (from 1793, extended to Bihar and Orissa) predate, and Grove insists owe nothing to, German forestry science (Hartig); they depended on indigenous skills and advice, drew in zamindars as planting agents, and by 1817 were being worked with convict labour — later systematised by Wallich's 1823 Plantation Committee.

- **The botanical garden as syncretic Paradise.** The EIC gardens at Saharanpur and Calcutta (Sibpur) were founded on or revived from Mughal imperial garden sites, carrying the Farsi *paradaeza* into an ostensibly commercial institution; Bishop Heber's description of Sibpur as answering “Milton's idea of Paradise” shows Mughal and European Edenic discourses fusing under Roxburgh's and Wallich's stewardship.

- **A contested and epistemically limited theory.** James Johnson's reversal from praising St Helena's afforestation (1807) to warning that “mountain countries with trees give rise to fevers” (1818) shows tree-cover medicine was disputed even among EIC surgeons; and despite decades of measurement, Roxburgh, in Grove's telling, could not conceive of climatic variation on a regional scale — a conceptual step Grove attributes to Humboldt's 1817 isotherm.

## Notable passages

- The unit's clearest thesis statement links Roxburgh's Priestleyite formation to a moralised reading of climate: he “linked the quality and ‘virtue’ of the atmosphere with the quality of a society,” inherited via Hope from Hales and Duhamel du Monceau and via Pringle from Priestley's pneumatics.

- Grove roots Roxburgh's data practice in a longer Royal Society tradition, noting that Jurin's 1724 international appeal for meteorological observations — the man himself “a close colleague of Isaac Newton” — drew records from St Petersburg, Massachusetts and Bengal, the template Roxburgh's own Coromandel records later filled.

- Roxburgh's own 1793 report on the Circars drought is quoted directly: “There are but few, if any... of the lower Maritime Provinces of India that are not subject to... visitations of drought,” and he describes himself as “a constant Eyewitness” to three consecutive years of “dreadful effects” — first-person testimony grounding his later policy claims.

- Roxburgh's historical drought research is unusually attentive to indigenous testimony: an informant, “the Rajah of Pittenpore's family Brahmen,” supplied a grandfather's record of the 1685–7 famine in which “only one shower fell” and “very few people survived these three years” — oral/documentary evidence Roxburgh used to argue drought was a recurring, not novel, hazard requiring structural (land-tenure) remedy.

- Heber's description of the Calcutta Botanic Garden as a beautiful, plant-rich “institution” that “more perfectly answers Milton's idea of Paradise... than anything which I ever saw” captures the Edenic register in which the garden network was understood even by its most practical administrators.

- Grove's closing qualification on Roxburgh's limits — that he “does not seem to have been able to conceive of climatic variation taking place on a regional basis,” a step requiring “the isotherm as a means to describe continental climates” that Humboldt advocated only in 1817 — marks the edge of what point-based colonial meteorology could achieve on its own.

For World Machines: the Royal Society/Jurin/company weather-record network is a small but clean case of [[legibility-as-machine-core]] — climate rendered into comparable, transmissible numbers is what lets Roxburgh's local famine diagnosis become an argument for state land and forest policy; the botanical-garden network functions as connective information infrastructure of the [[modernity-machine]], though here explicitly hybridised with Mughal antecedents rather than a purely European invention. Roxburgh's Edinburgh formation under Hope situates him squarely in the diffusion of the [[scottish-enlightenment]]'s experimental natural philosophy into colonial medicine. The explicit citation of Jurin as Newton's colleague is a direct textual link to [[isaac-newton]]'s afterlife in institutional science, and the 1817 isotherm passage is a direct, if brief, link to [[alexander-von-humboldt]]. The synchronised 1787–92 droughts across India, West Africa and the Caribbean that Grove notes in passing resonate with [[converging-world-crises-bayly]], though Grove's frame here is climatic (El Niño) rather than Bayly's political-economic synchronicity. Grove is an environmental historian narrating scientific and administrative response, not a machine theorist — these are resonances, not claims he himself makes.
