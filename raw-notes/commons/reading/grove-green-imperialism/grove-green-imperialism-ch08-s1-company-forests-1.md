# Chapter 8 opens: EIC surgeons as diagnosticians, indigenous forest management before Company rule, and timber-driven expansion, 1760–1810

Grove opens the India chapter by framing it as a scaled-up, information-intensive repeat of the island pattern (Mauritius, St Helena, St Vincent) rather than a fresh start: he states the chapter's actual aims — tracing how desiccationist arguments linking deforestation to rainfall were elaborated and propagandised by a new professional class of EIC surgeon-scientists — then works backward to show that Company rule inherited, rather than invented, both an indigenous conservationist epistemology and an already-intensive pre-colonial forest-revenue system, before turning to the concrete military-commercial timber shortages that drove Company territorial expansion from the 1760s to the 1810 Napoleonic-era timber 'crisis'.

## Themes

**A scientific civil service diagnoses ecological crisis.** Grove's stated object is narrower than a full ecological history: to trace the EIC Medical Service's 'increasing degree of direct involvement... in formulating a diagnosis of the ecological transition' and how German- and Scottish-trained surgeons, incorporated into 'an entirely new kind of scientific civil service,' used Hippocratic environmental analysis to argue for interventionist land-use control against both laissez-faire capital interests and Malthusian accounts of famine. Botanical gardens (Calcutta under Roxburgh and Wallich, Madras under Berry) and scientific societies did the same professionalising work here that Grove traced on the islands, with J. G. Koenig's employment as a naturalist under the Nawab of Arcot marking one of the earliest instances of a surgeon serving as non-medical state scientist.

**Indigenous knowledge, not imported theory, did the real work.** Grove is emphatic that Hindu, Muslim, Zoroastrian and Ezhava concepts of man–environment relations were 'more important to the evolution of company environmental policy than any set of ideas imported from outside India,' with the Humboldtian ideology that 'so much influenced EIC scientists' itself mediated through this indigenous stimulus rather than arriving unmixed from Europe — a claim that resonates with [[alexander-von-humboldt]] as a synthesising rather than originating figure here. Grove also notes the trajectory reversed after Company rule ended, when German formulations gained the upper hand and local resistance to state forestry grew.

**Against the 'Merrie India' myth.** In the unit's sharpest historiographical move, Grove attacks recent essays that cast pre-British India as 'an ecological and pre-capitalist golden age of common property rights,' even treating caste as an 'effective ecological adaptation' — naming this move 'inherently Orientalist in the Saidian sense' and comparing it to the discredited 'Merrie Africa.' Against this he marshals evidence of extensive pre-colonial state forest management: Mughal successor states raising timber demand as their power grew, Cochin's forests run by feudal chiefs, and above all the Amirs of Sind's reafforestation of a million acres of the Indus flood plain into up to eighty-seven shikargah (hunting-and-timber reserves), often by forcibly removing the villages that had occupied the land — reserves the EIC simply inherited and ran on, later folding the Maratha equivalent into the Bombay forest-conservancy system after 1805. Grove's conclusion is pointed: British rule is 'a less significant episode in Indian forest history' than the Mughal-successor-state phase that preceded it. This is a strong candidate for [[Legibility]] and [[legibility-as-machine-core]]: forest classification and revenue extraction here are shown as a *pre-colonial* state technology the Company merely absorbed, complicating any reading of colonial rule as the origin of environmental legibility in India, though Grove's own argument is historiographical rather than about legibility as such.

**Timber shortage as a motor of territorial expansion.** From 1761 Fort William anxieties over misused ship- and house-timber, through 1780s west-coast complaints about 'indiscriminate lease of cutting,' to the 1785 Godavery/Parkinson case (rejected because the Company lacked territorial control) and Warren Hastings's friction with Bombay over Maratha-war interventions justified only by access to teak, Grove builds a case that 'company expansionism was normally associated with timber shortage' — paralleling British strategic-timber motives in North America and the Cape. This culminates in the Napoleonic Wars' 'rapid growth in demand for shipbuilding timber,' which by 1810 had become a worldwide timber 'crisis' Grove reads (via Rodger) as an early instance of industrial-scale raw-material competition between Britain and France, and as the point of departure for the sharpening of Indian environmental concern between 1790 and 1850. The chapter also notes the double function of the later mountain-timber search after 1800: securing resources while enabling control of resisting tribal groups (the Dangs Bhils, Paharia, Rampa) through suppression or co-option — a pairing of resource-legibility with population control that again gestures at [[Legibility]] without Grove naming it as such. The global, war-driven convergence on a single strategic commodity also loosely rhymes with the war-cost/extraction loop [[c-a-bayly]] traces across his linked world crises, though Bayly's frame is about state fiscal-military reform broadly and Grove's is specifically about timber and forest policy.

## Notable passages

The chapter's thesis-statement paragraph lays out its actual scope (desiccationist argument-formation, professionalisation, botanical gardens) against the vaguer promise of a full ecological history. The indigenous-knowledge paragraph is the unit's most consequential claim, subordinating even Humboldtian ideology to local epistemology. The 'Merrie India' passage is Grove doing historiographical combat in his own voice, and the Sind shikargah passage (with the Eastwick quotation and hard revenue figures) is his primary evidence for pre-colonial forest intensity. The Parkinson/Hastings passage and the 1810 timber-crisis passage together carry the chapter's second thread: that Company expansion and the sophistication of Indian environmental concern were driven as much by strategic-commodity anxiety as by scientific diagnosis.
