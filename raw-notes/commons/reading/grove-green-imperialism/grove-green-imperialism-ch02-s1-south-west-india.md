# Indigenous Knowledge and the South-West Indian Origins of European Tropical Botany

This is the opening of Grove's second chapter, tracing how the European botanical garden — born from fifteenth-century oceanic trade, Hippocratic medicine, and the Renaissance revival of Pliny, Theophrastus and Dioscorides — became, by the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the vehicle through which South-West Indian (Malabar) natural knowledge was absorbed into, and came to structure, European science, rather than the reverse.

## Themes

**The botanical garden as index of imperial expansion.** Grove opens by tying the university apothecaries' gardens of Padua, Pisa, Leiden and Heidelberg directly to the "growing volume of new long-distance oceanic trade" after Henry the Navigator's voyages: gardens stocked with imported plants became sites for the first global classification of flora, and their spread "remained an accurate indicator in microcosm of the expansion in European knowledge of the global environment." The Portuguese, though usually overshadowed by later Dutch and Italian institutions, had already built acclimatisation gardens and plant-transfer networks between 1415 and 1487, linking the Indian Ocean world to West Africa, the Caribbean and Brazil.

**Gardens as Paradise, and as "ecological control."** Grove reads the new botanical gardens on two symbolic registers at once: as "re-creations of Paradise" (citing John Prest) expressing the "inherent religiosity of much natural history," and as "landscape 'texts'" signifying "a particular type of ecological control that had not previously been available" — gardens as both Edenic microcosm and instrument of a new, more totalising grip on global nature. This double register (sacred model, technology of control) recurs across the book's treatment of islands and gardens alike.

**The reversal of epistemic direction: Orta and van Reede.** The chapter's central and most striking claim concerns the two texts Grove treats as the hinge of European tropical botany: Garcia da Orta's *Coloquios dos simples e drogas* (Goa, 1563, quickly Latinised by Clusius) and Hendrik van Reede's *Hortus Malabaricus* (compiled after the Dutch displaced the Portuguese in Cochin). Grove argues these are "profoundly indigenous texts" — "compilations of Middle Eastern and South Asian ethnobotany, organised on essentially non-European precepts" — and that their Hippocratic emphasis on empirical accuracy and efficacy "strongly privilege[d] Ayurvedic and local Malayali medical and botanical... knowledge" over older Arabic, Brahminical, and European classical systems. Because these two texts sat at the core of the transmission network (Clusius–Leiden–Chelsea–Oxford–Vienna), "almost all subsequent substantial 'European' texts on South Asian botany retain [their] essential indigenous structure."

**Caste, Brahminical knowledge, and the limits of the standard colonial-discourse story.** Grove explicitly distinguishes his case from the usual account in which Brahminical texts and interpretations were "reinforced by the colonial situation" to shape European perception of India. Medico-botanical diffusion instead "privilege[d] non-Brahminical epistemologies and impose[d] an indigenous technical logic, thereby transforming European botanical science" — an "apparent deviation from the mainstream" that Grove attributes to shared Indo-Arabic-European scientific roots and to the genuine technical superiority of regional (Malayali) botanical systems. He gestures to Latour's "centres of calculation" and to Heniger's biographical work as the right analytic tools for tracing this diffusion.

**A methodological aside.** Grove notes that the historiography of botany is underdeveloped relative to other sciences, and proposes environmental history — rather than conventional history of science or colonial-discourse analysis — as the more appropriate frame for studying contact between Asian and European bodies of natural knowledge.

## Notable passages

The chapter opens by grounding the botanical garden in the economics and medicine of expansion — apothecaries' gardens becoming "sites of the first attempts to classify plants on a global basis," with garden-founding read as "an accurate indicator in microcosm" of Europe's expanding environmental knowledge; this frames gardens as both effect and instrument of the world system Grove is describing, a use of institutional infrastructure to render nature legible and controllable that resonates with [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and with the garden network as an information infrastructure of the [[modernity-machine]] — though Grove's own emphasis, developed a few paragraphs later, is that the resulting knowledge flowed from periphery to center rather than being imposed top-down, which complicates a purely top-down legibility reading.

The short paragraph on gardens as "re-creations of Paradise" links directly to the book's recurring Edenic/Utopian discourse about islands and gardens, and resonates loosely with [[the-english-garden-as-epistemic-object]] — loosely, because Grove's gardens here are Portuguese, Dutch and pan-European scientific institutions charged with religio-political meaning (he singles out Heidelberg), not the specifically English aesthetic-political garden tradition that concept describes.

The chapter's real argumentative payload is the paragraph on Orta and van Reede: their texts are "profoundly indigenous," and downstream "European" botanical writing on South Asia inherits that indigenous structure rather than superseding it. This is Grove's evidence for a genuinely non-European epistemology shaping the metropole's science, which is worth reading against [[nature-culture-dialectic]] — the dialectic runs from colonised periphery to colonising center, an inversion of the usual assumed direction, which Grove underlines again at the chapter's close: "The division between European and Asian botanical systems is anyway an arbitrary one... a centre of balance well to the east of Venice."

The caste/Brahminical paragraph does similar work at the level of internal Indian social structure: Grove distinguishes the (Brahminical, colonially reinforced) discourse story historians usually tell from the (non-Brahminical, Malayali/Ayurvedic) technical knowledge that actually shaped medico-botanical diffusion — a caveat future readers should carry forward when this book's "indigenous knowledge" claims get generalized.
