# The Forsters and the Resolution Voyage

Grove argues that Johann Reinhold Forster and his son Georg, the German naturalists carried on Cook's second voyage, grasped better than almost anyone the long-term implications of the Cook expeditions' large-scale empirical study of environments and societies, and traces two developments that flowed from this: a new climatic and anthropological way of thinking about non-European peoples, and — via J. R. Forster's earlier career surveying Catherine the Great's Volga German colonies — the template of the independently employed professional naturalist whose expertise a state wants but whose criticism it may not. On the voyage itself, Forster's own environmental thinking evolved from a doctrinaire Buffonian view of 'beneficent man' toward a more critical, empirically grounded account of European impact, worked out above all on islands.

## Themes

- **Two professional-naturalist types are founded on the Cook voyages.** The Forsters (German, father and son) and, on the third voyage, the Scottish surgeon William Anderson — praised by Cook as 'by far the most accurate and inquisitive person on board' — establish the two career paths that would dominate British colonial science for the next eighty years, the Forsters' type recognised especially by Banks and Anderson's by Cook.

- **Climate and society become a live comparative question.** The chance to set distant lands against the homeland directly stimulated new thinking about climate's effect on society, with the hygrometer and thermometer becoming 'a standard part of the scholar's equipment'; Grove cites Lord Kames's *Sketches of the History of Man*, which drew on Hawkesworth's compilation of the Cook voyages to argue that a balmy climate hindered rather than stimulated human progress. This is the same climatic-environmentalist current that later surfaces among Scottish surgeons in India, touching [[scottish-enlightenment]].

- **An anthropological turn shades into doubt about European 'modernity' itself.** The Forsters pioneered ethnology and anthropology from the voyages' findings, but alongside this grew 'more empirically based doubts... about the supposed intellectual superiority of the European over other races' and unease at what colonial 'modernity' might do to tropical peoples — doubts Grove traces also among the French on Mauritius (Commerson, Poivre). Georg Forster's Tahiti, idyllic against the 'noisome realities of the poor at home', and his warning that discovery might cost Pacific islanders their happiness, sit alongside Cook's and Banks's own admiration for indigenous self-sufficiency. This resonates with the noble-savage tradition gathered under [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] and [[noble-savages-and-english-gardeners]], though here the idealisation arrives through empirical travel observation and a sense of foreboding about contact, not through a philosophical or garden-design argument.

- **Forster's imperial-science apprenticeship pre-dates Cook.** Educated at Halle, Forster was engaged by the Russian resident Rehbinder and then Catherine the Great's favourite Orlov to survey the Volga German colonies — a commission he expanded, on his own initiative, into a full natural-history and ethnological survey, and for which he supplied reports that were 'praised for their accuracy' yet 'highly critical of the predicament of the German settlers'. Grove reads this as the precedent for a recurring colonial conundrum: an expansionist state wants a competent independent scientist's technical expertise but risks 'unpalatable technical and political criticism' in return, and as the template later followed by Flinders, Baudin, and Buchanan-Hamilton's Bengal revenue surveys. A state commissioning ostensibly independent scientific survey of territory and people for administrative purposes resonates, loosely, with [[legibility-as-machine-core]] — though the very conundrum Grove names, that the surveyed information came bundled with criticism the state hadn't asked for, shows this legibility was never fully captured by its commissioners.

- **A German scientific network channels into British and Indian colonial science.** Forster's Halle and Göttingen connections, his membership of the Society of Antiquaries, and his years of scientific translation work gave him an unmatched command of contemporary European and English science, which he brought to bear as naturalist on the Resolution — and which he passed on directly: Grove notes that Forster 'exercised a great influence' on [[alexander-von-humboldt]].

- **Forster's environmental thinking evolves from doctrine to empirical critique.** His publicly stated view in the *Observations*, following Buffon's *Vue de la nature*, still held that nature left to itself 'languishes' and is improved 'by the industry of man'; but his private Resolution journal, Grove argues, 'tells quite a different story', moving toward a more critical, empirically grounded view of European impact, especially on islands. Table Mountain at the Cape first prompted Forster to link mountains, clouds, trees and rainfall — the mountain read as 'an island in the sense of being a circumscribed physiographic form upon which processes could be observed operating' — and the same pattern recurred at Norfolk Island, before culminating, later in the voyage, at St Helena and Ascension. Islands function here as legible, bounded microcosms where causal chains are visible fast — the same observational logic behind the desiccation theory Grove traces through Poivre and Bernardin de Saint-Pierre — and the tension between the published Buffonian text and the private critical journal is itself a small case of [[nature-culture-dialectic]].

- **A Romantic voyage motif frames the personal transformation.** Quoting Allan Frost, Grove contrasts the eighteenth-century voyage-as-action (a Grand Tour) with the Romantic voyage as a 'progress from inherited and conventional views of reality' toward a new one — exactly the shift Grove tracks in Forster's own theorising on man, climate and environment as the voyage proceeds.

## Notable passages

The opening passage states the unit's premise directly: among contemporaries, the Forsters most clearly saw that the Cook voyages' empirical study would 'strike deep roots' and shape 'the activity of man', and situates the voyages as the first major opening for Central European and German naturalists into British naval, colonial and employment networks.

The Tahiti/Europe contrast and Diderot-echoing warning is the unit's clearest statement of the emerging critical, quasi-Rousseauian view of contact: Georg Forster set Tahiti's unambition against European 'absolute want' and 'unbounded voluptuousness', concluded that it 'were better for the discoverers and the discovered that the South Seas had remained unknown', and Grove pairs this with Cook's and Banks's own parallel admiration for indigenous peoples 'content with little nay almost nothing'.

The Volga German colonies passage is Grove's fullest account of Forster's pre-Cook apprenticeship in state-commissioned survey work, showing both his own conception of himself as a professional naturalist and the political risk — for the Russian government as later for the British — of employing an independent, critical scientific mind.

The passage on Forster's shifting thinking states the unit's central intellectual claim outright: a move 'from a doctrinaire environmental viewpoint closely allied to that of Buffon towards a more empirically based viewpoint which emphasised the indiscriminate influence of human agency', driven above all by island observation.

The Tahiti-as-garden passage shows Forster's initial paradisal, Cytherian framing of the island — schooled by his own translation of Bougainville — culminating in the image of Tahiti as a 'flourishing and well-kept garden' whose fruit trees shelter the turf beneath from a tropical sun that 'would otherwise scorch and destroy'.

The mountains-clouds-trees passage is Forster's own mechanistic account of an island's hydrology — high hills that 'attract... all the vapours and clouds', tree-covered summits that screen collected moisture from the sun — the direct empirical seed of the desiccation-theory reasoning Grove carries forward to St Helena and Ascension.
