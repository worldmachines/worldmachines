# St Helena under Pyke and Byfield: conservation, extinction, and the discovery of desiccation, 1715–1794

Governor Pyke's arrival on St Helena in 1715 opens a period, running to the East India Company's climatic deforestation order of 1794, in which the island's administrators are forced by worsening drought, soil erosion, and firewood scarcity to reinvent their government as an apparatus of environmental control -- reafforestation, species rescue, goat eradication, and eventually a forest law -- while the Court of Directors in London mostly ignores, undercuts, or overturns them, only reversing course under external pressure from Joseph Banks and the precedent of legislation already passed on Mauritius and St Vincent.

## Themes

**Paradise transposed: Pyke's Mauritius daydream.** Confronted with St Helena's exhausted, waterless valleys, Pyke's mind drifts to an alternative Eden: he proposes resettling the island's "younger and more destitute" inhabitants on Mauritius, which he had visited and remembered as an untouched paradise abandoned by the Dutch. Grove reads the proposal as a diagnostic instrument: the comparison between a ruined St Helena and a still-fertile Mauritius made the scale of degradation legible to contemporaries, and it rehearses, decades early, the same paradisal-island trope that later attaches to Mauritius itself once French and then British settlers begin to despoil it in turn.

**From profit to triage: the island as an old ship needing repair.** Grove marks 1715 as the hinge at which St Helena's governors stop proposing agricultural improvements as profit ventures and start proposing them as survival measures against "the island in a declining state." The 1716 letter's extended simile -- the island as "an old ship that must have a thorough repair to hold out long" -- is, in Grove's framing, the governors' own theory of ecological crisis, though it doubles, ironically, as a request for more enslaved labour, the very input that had driven deforestation and erosion in the first place.

**Endemism, extinction, and empirical natural history.** Governor Byfield's rescue of the redwood -- down to two seedlings "neither of them above an inch high" -- registers, for Grove, an early and explicit awareness that a species restricted to St Helena could be lost altogether, alongside deliberate imports of alien trees (margossa, banyan) for restoration experiments. The same empirical turn appears in the Council's careful description of an unfamiliar storm-blown bird species, evidence of a colonial administration acquiring the habits of natural-historical classification soon to be applied, more systematically, across the empire's botanical network.

**Legibility from below: surveys, fines, and the 1731 Forest Act.** The island government's response to decline is bureaucratic as much as horticultural: annual surveys of planting, fines for illegal felling ("23 persons were summoned for neglect"), fenced enclosures, and leases stipulating tree-planting quotas. This apparatus culminates in the 1731 St Helena Forest Act, prompted by a petition from forty islanders demanding goat eradication rather than by any directive from London -- an early instance of the colonial state building capacity to observe and discipline its resource base at exactly the moment the metropolitan company remains indifferent. This resonates with [[Legibility]] and [[legibility-as-machine-core]] as a case of environmental control built through documentation and enforcement, though Grove's own register is institutional history rather than machine theory, and the apparatus here is a handful of governors and clerks, not an epochal infrastructure.

**Disease as environmental symptom.** A 1717 report links "the decay of trees" to a "pestilent sulphorous air" that blinded two colonists, which Grove glosses as windborne dust from denuded, desertifying ground; Pyke's second term (1731–1738) extends this into an early stagnant-water/mosquito/fever epidemiology. The unit repeatedly folds bodily and ecological risk into a single register of colonial anxiety.

**The Company's belated conversion to desiccation theory, 1794.** For most of the century the Court of Directors is worse than passive: it overturns fines against islanders who refuse to pen their goats, remarking acidly that "the Goats are of more use than Ebony." Only in 1794, prompted by Joseph Banks and by legislation already passed on Mauritius and St Vincent, does the Company adopt the governors' argument in its own voice -- now cast as the claim that "trees have an attractive power on the clouds" and that drought follows deforestation. This anticipates the climate-vegetation theorizing later associated with [[alexander-von-humboldt]] (whose South American observations come after this letter), though the Directors arrive at it as administrators reacting to institutional pressure, not as natural philosophers; the resonance is with the idea's colonial-administrative origin, which is exactly Grove's larger thesis.

**Halley's ghost: science without institutions.** Grove closes the unit by crediting St Helena's decline with feeding into Halley's 1670s–1700 observations on vegetation, the hydrological cycle, and soil loss, while stressing that the absence of institutional scientific continuity on the island meant this knowledge was never systematically compared to erosion elsewhere -- St Helena's significance up to the 1760s is, in Grove's own words, "as an historical lesson in the failure of a conservation policy, even if a closely observed failure."

## Notable passages

**Pyke's Mauritius letter (c1).** The Directors are told of a Mauritius "yet uninhabited," "wild," and "overstocked with cattle" in the same breath as St Helena's drought and soil erosion -- the paradisal-island discourse doing diagnostic work for a colonial administrator, not a poet.

**The "old ship" letter of 1716 (c2).** The governors' own extended metaphor for ecological crisis, embedded in a request for more enslaved labour -- the passage Grove uses to mark the shift from profit-seeking to environmental triage.

**Byfield's redwood rescue (c3).** A single sentence records what Grove treats as a landmark: explicit recognition that an island-endemic species could go extinct, and a deliberate, successful act of ex-situ propagation to prevent it.

**The 1731 Forest Act (c4).** Grove's framing sentence for one of the earliest pieces of colonial conservation legislation, shown originating from islander petition rather than Company directive.

**The Greentree fines overturned (c5).** The Directors' own rebuke -- "the Goats are of more use than Ebony" -- is Grove's clearest evidence that London, for decades, simply outranked its governors' conservation judgment with short-term economic interest.

**The Company's 1794 climatic reversal (c6).** The Directors themselves now argue that trees have "an attractive power on the clouds," adopting, under external pressure, the desiccationist logic the book traces from the colonial periphery inward.
