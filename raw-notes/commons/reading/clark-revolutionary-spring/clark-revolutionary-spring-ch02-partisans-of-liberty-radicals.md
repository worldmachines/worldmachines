# Liberals and radicals: the two camps before 1848

Clark opens chapter 2 with a portrait of early-nineteenth-century liberalism and then turns to the radicals on its left. Liberalism, he says, resists definition: there were many 'liberalisms' linked in a transnational network of activists and journalists, and the term itself only acquired a specific political meaning during the Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars. What liberals shared was a middle path between old-regime privilege and Jacobin or Napoleonic authoritarianism: representation without democracy, political but not social equality, popular sovereignty carefully limited. Radicals, by contrast, wanted universal (male) suffrage, direct action and a politics that took the Social Question seriously, but were split between state-organisers (Blanc), anti-statist harmonisers (Fourier, Cabet, Weitling), Saint-Simonian technocrats, and Marx, who embraced conflict. The unit ends with Lorenz von Stein reading socialism as a symptom that 'society' had become the driver of politics.

## Themes

**Liberalism as metapolitics.** Liberals thought of their politics not as an ideology but as a procedural kit for mediating conflicting interests, with constitutions as 'peace treaties' between antagonistic groups. Clark notes this is both its openness and its vulnerability (charged with emptiness). Martinez de la Rosa's claim that absolutists and democrats are alike, both appealing to remote abstract authorities, is the clearest statement of the stance.

**Limits as the hidden fault line.** Every liberal commitment implied a contested threshold: where to set the franchise, how to balance sovereign against legislature, unicameral or bicameral chambers, how free trade should be. Moderates such as Martinez de la Rosa were denounced by radicals as traitors, 'a fate that would befall many moderate liberals in 1848'. Clark repeatedly flags forward to 1848, where these disputes became fissures. The franchise question is where political rights 'threatened to become entangled with the language of social needs'.

**Political over social solutions; regional and national variation.** Liberals were aware of the Social Question but tended to prefer political remedies (d'Azeglio's English 'spirit of legality'). Free trade versus protection split liberals by regional economic interest (Prussian grain exporters versus Baden industry), and from about 1840 'the nation' became the frame for economic argument (France, List, Kossuth's Védegylet), so that liberalism's universalist language fed into [[nationalism]] via political economy. The gendered and confessional dimensions (anticlericalism as a liberal cultural code, masculine householder ideal) are also stressed.

**Reluctant revolutionaries.** In 1830 the liberals resisted the ordinances but had to run to keep up with a popular uprising and then tried to 'put the lid back' on it; constitutions were a way to capture revolutionary impetus 'in ropes of law'. 'Ambivalence was their thing.'

**Radicals: distribution, harmony, conflict.** Radicals saw competition as socially toxic and wealth as zero-sum with poverty (Buchner, the 1847 Frankfurt flysheet, Becker). They differed sharply among themselves: Blanc wanted the state as 'supreme regulator of production', Lamartine (also a radical by his democratic suffrage) rejected this as the Jacobin Convention applied to labour. Fourier, Cabet and Weitling dreamed of harmony that would make conflict unnecessary (Weitling's deviants 'cured' rather than punished), Saint-Simonians imagined rule by engineers and industrialists, and Marx, in the 1840s 'just one voice among many', alone embraced class conflict as the engine of progress. There was no socialist consensus, including on women's emancipation. The Saint-Simonian idea of 'industrials' versus 'idlers' echoes [[henri-de-saint-simon]]; Herzen's view of socialism as inheriting Christian brotherhood appears via [[alexander-herzen]].

**Stein: socialism as symptom.** Lorenz von Stein read socialism diagnostically: the emergence of a class-conscious proletariat showed that 'the age of purely political movements was over' and the 'social' was an autonomous category. His remedy was a 'science of society' guiding state strategies of social pacification, an idea Clark says lay beyond the mid-century revolutions.

**Fit with the World Machines frame (hedged).** Clark is describing a political spectrum, not a machine. There is a loose rhyme with legibility: Stein's 'science of society' and Weitling's regulated harmony are proposals to make a new social subject legible and governable to the state (compare [[legibility-as-machine-core]]), and the liberal-radical divergence over limits is an early instance of the coalition fracture the book later develops. These are analogies only; Clark does not use that vocabulary.

## Notable passages

**The liberal middle path (c1).** The list of paired affirmations and rejections (revolution but not terror, wealth-privilege but not birth-privilege, representation but not democracy) is the compact definition on which the rest of the argument about liberal vulnerability rests.

**Liberalism as metapolitics (c2).** Clark's claim that liberals believed their politics was 'not an ideology' but a mediating kit, and why that left it open to the charge of emptiness.

**The generic predicament (c3).** Martinez de la Rosa's fate, denounced as a revolutionary by the right and a servile dealmaker by the left, is offered as the archetype of mid-century liberalism and a foreshadowing of 1848.

**Reluctant revolutionaries (c4).** The 1830 example and the Valencia newspaper's dilemma between revolution and despotism.

**Harmony versus conflict (c5).** Clark's sharpest contrast between radical harmonisers and liberal conflict-managers, ending with Mazzini's duties-over-rights as a radical marker.

**Stein's diagnosis (c6).** The passage that turns the survey into an argument about the arrival of 'society' as the subject of politics.
