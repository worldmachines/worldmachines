# The iron net: France and Prussia close down, and Britain's small counter-revolution on Cephalonia

The unit opens in the second half of 1848, after the June Days, when the centre of gravity moves rightward. In France the left is purged and its press priced out of existence, Proudhon's credit scheme is jeered down 600 to 2, an elected presidency is chosen, and on 10 December Louis-Napoleon Bonaparte wins about 74 per cent of the vote. In Prussia the king's ministers, and then the king himself, turn on the Berlin assembly: it is adjourned, occupied by General Wrangel's troops, placed under martial law and dissolved, while a constitution is octroyed from above. The Rhineland resists through a tax boycott but is disarmed. The second half, on the Ionian Islands, is a compact case study: a British protectorate with a sham constitution, a reforming Tory commissioner whose concessions fed a radical opposition, a currant-price crisis and a Galician-style peasant jacquerie in 1849, and the savage reprisals of the liberal Whig Sir Henry Ward. Clark uses it to argue that such violence was a European liberal elite's response to plebeian unrest, not merely the boomerang of colonial practice.

## Themes

**The right's institutional recovery.** Repression is done through ordinary-looking instruments: club regulations, caution money and stamp tax to 'silence the poor', arrests and exile, then a presidency elected separately by the people. Clark stresses that the December election ran smoothly, 'a hopeful sign that the mechanisms of democracy were settling in', and the result was a huge shock even to the winner. Democratic procedure itself delivers the reversal. This resonates with [[caesarism-gay]] and with counter-revolution as modernizing reconstruction rather than mere restoration, though Clark here is narrating the election, not yet theorizing it.

**Legibility and illegibility of the peasantry.** Marx (whose claims are his, not Clark's) reads the roughly four million peasant Bonaparte voters as 'homologous quantities', a sack of potatoes recognizable to everyone but themselves. Clark qualifies: the vote crossed class lines, thirty-four departments gave Bonaparte over 80 per cent, and the name had been prepared by a decade of the July Monarchy's neutered Napoleonic cult. This touches the gap between how a class is described by the observer and how it acts, and the uses of a widely legible name (compare [[legibility-as-machine-core]], though the fit is loose: Clark's point is about symbolic capital, not state measurement).

**Fear and its asymmetry.** In Berlin, as in Naples, the 'disequilibrium of two fears' operates: the assembly grows trapped while the king grows less afraid, and news from Vienna emboldens the court. The revolution's contagion runs in reverse, as counter-revolutionary synchrony across capitals (see [[history-machine]] only as a loose analogue: Clark describes a chain of events, not a silently assembling paradigm). The army's unbroken loyalty to the crown makes the liberal institutions 'living on sufferance'.

**Improvisation on both sides.** The Prussian court repackages the revolution's own form: it grants a constitution on 5 December, an 'astute political move' later copied by the Austrians. The Rhineland's tax strike, people's committees and Civil Guard oaths show the depth of the democratic network but also that losing the assembly removed its political focus. Fanny Lewald's 'iron net' gives the section its name.

**Liberal violence against Europeans made 'other'.** In the Ionian section, Clark argues that the reprisals (sixty-eight courts-martial, forty-four death sentences, twenty-one hangings, mass flogging with the naval cat, burned houses, deportation to Cerigo) match the counter-revolutionary violence of Austria and Naples per head of population. Ward was no colonial hand but an 'advanced liberal' political economist, so the episode does not show the 're-importation into Europe of an extra-European colonial practice'; rather, elites used a racialized vocabulary against fellow Europeans ('removed but one degree from donkeys'). Schwarzenberg's retort to Gladstone via Aberdeen shows how Britain's self-image of liberal exceptionalism was contested at the time. Relevant loosely to [[contested-uniformity-bayly]] and [[c-a-bayly]]'s global framing, and to the gap between official liberal ideology and practice, but Clark does not theorize it in those terms.

**Structural conditions of a peripheral revolt.** A one per cent franchise, 'double lists', dependency on landed elites, the colonia tenure and prostichio credit system, and monoculture in olives and currants make Ionian society brittle; a price fall triggers violence that Clark compares to Galicia 1846. The Ionian radicals (Rizospastai) invoke self-determination and popular sovereignty, so [[nationalism]] is present in the periphery too.

## Notable passages

**Proudhon's 600 to 2.** Clark uses the jeering chamber, and the socialists' abstention over their own 'systems', to show how narrowed the assembly's tolerance had become and how fragmented the left remained, even for a radical who had taken no part in the violence.

**Potatoes and the Napoleonic name.** The passage sets Marx's derisive account against Clark's corrective that the Bonaparte vote was cross-class and had been culturally prepared.

**Berlin's disequilibrium of two fears.** The two-sentence diagnosis explains why the king risked confrontation, and the Rhineland paragraph that follows records a boycott that was deep but leaderless, ending with Lewald's image of an 'iron net'.

**Ward is not a colonial governor.** The paragraphs on Ward's diplomatic career, his opposition to the Factories Act and Chartism, and his belief that 'nothing but the most rigorous measures would do', turn the Ionian case into an argument about liberal elites rather than empire alone.

**The Cephalonian reprisals and Cerigo.** The catalogue of courts-martial, floggings and deportations, capped by the blinded deputy Pylarinos, supplies the evidence for the comparison with Austria and Naples.

**Schwarzenberg's point.** Clark grants that what-aboutery is a standard evasion but concedes 'Schwarzenberg had a point': Britain did not reprimand Ward and later promoted him to Ceylon.
