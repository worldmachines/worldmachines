# Liberty and Risk: Jewish Emancipation and Anti-Jewish Violence in 1848

Clark opens with Moritz Steinschneider's ecstatic letter from barricaded Berlin on 20 March 1848 (Purim), in which the falling away of the line between 'Jew' and 'Christian' seems to promise imminent emancipation. He then sets out the pre-1848 legal landscape (a patchwork of special laws, jurisdictions and local vetoes, with France the exception), the slow and non-linear build-up of the emancipation campaign in the 1840s, and the darker side of the revolutionary spring: a Europe-wide wave of pogroms and looting, from Alsace and Baden to Pressburg, Pest, Posen, Thuringia and Rome. He argues that this violence was simultaneously economic, ideological and political, and that emancipation legislation passed in 1848–49 was largely reversed after the revolutions, arriving durably only between 1861 and 1871. The unit closes with the rise of the slogan 'emancipation from the Jews' as a bridge between left and right anti-capitalism.

## Themes

**Emancipation as meandering paths, not a road.** Clark rejects the notion of emancipation as a 'forward-driven, unitary transformation defined teleologically', for Jews as for women and enslaved Africans. Toleration edicts were one-off concessions to a group in a territory, revocable, and often blocked by guilds, towns and local statute even where states conceded citizenship (Baden Jews were citizens yet barred from over 80 per cent of cities). Officials demanded that Jews first 'earn' rights by abandoning the very trades that restrictions had forced on them. Clark leans on Reinhard Rürup's image of a 'tortuous and thorny' set of paths. This is a direct counter to a Whiggish reading of liberal progress, and it echoes WMP's suspicion of smooth-arc historiography (cf. [[history-machine]]), though Clark's target is a moral narrative of rights rather than a theory of infrastructure.

**Old order versus standardization.** Clark notes that in a world of 'standardization and the suppression of privilege and juridical exception', the Jews 'continued to embody an older order' of special jurisdictions. The state's push toward uniform citizenship is thus the frame within which emancipation is contested; this loosely rhymes with the drive to uniformity discussed in [[contested-uniformity-bayly]] and with [[legibility-as-machine-core]], although Clark himself does not use such language and the point is the historian's aside, not his thesis.

**Violence as economic, ideological and political at once.** The perpetrators were mostly landless rural labourers and poor townsfolk hit by the crisis of 1846–8, and Jews occupied exposed positions as creditors and small traders. But Clark insists the violence was also shaped by ambient frameworks (Toussenel, Leroux) that focused anti-capitalist anger on a minority, so that Jews became a 'metonym for the dysfunctions of a society passing through crisis'. And it was demonstrably political: riots followed rumours or votes on emancipation (Baden, Pressburg, Pest, Gleiwitz). Looters shouting Vive la République! show that 'liberty' could mean licence or deliverance from distress rather than universal rights. Clark hedges: what the rioters meant 'is difficult to ascertain'.

**Near-simultaneous yet local.** The attacks were 'wave-like in their near-simultaneity, but also intensely local', pivoting on mayors, commanders and community leaders. News of the Paris Republic triggered Altkirch within hours; this fits the book's contagion-by-news theme, but here the contagion spreads violence against a minority as well as constitutional hope. Forces of order (National and Civic Guards) frequently failed or took part, an example of improvised revolutionary institutions being captured by local majorities.

**Reversal and delayed settlement.** Emancipation laws in Italy, Hungary and the German states were repealed or made dead letters after the revolutions; Prussia's 1850 constitution kept civil equality but declared Christianity the basis of state institutions, which was used to exclude Jews from state employment. Final settlement came in 1861 (Italy), 1867 (Hungary), 1866–71 (Germany). Clark counters sanguine contemporaries (Auerbach, Zunz, the Viennese observer) who thought pogroms would speed reform: Kossuth argued the opposite. This suggests a revolutionary moment's gains as institutional improvisations that lapse when the executive's authority wanes, in a way that resembles the counter-revolution theme of the book.

**A modern anti-Semitism takes shape.** Jews were newly visible as club members, deputies and ministers, and the slogan 'emancipation from the Jews' (Wagner in 1850) put them on the wrong side of history, bridging left and right critiques of capital. Clark presents this as a modern articulation, not merely a medieval remnant.

## Notable passages

The framing paragraph on real relief and new dangers (c1) sets the double edge of 1848 for Jewish communities, including internal factional conflict and the murder of the reformist rabbi Abraham Kohn. The methodological paragraph on emancipation's 'paths' (c2) is the unit's historiographical core. The Alsace-era analysis (c3) shows the scale, the unsettling cry of Vive la République! and the embarrassment to the new government. Clark's account of the ideological frame (c4) and of the political trigger (c5) together explain why economics alone is insufficient. The final passage on 'emancipation from the Jews' (c6) traces the afterlife into modern anti-Semitism.
