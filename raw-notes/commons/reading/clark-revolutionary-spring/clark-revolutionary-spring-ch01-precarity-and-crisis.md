# Precarity and crisis: pauperism, contested commons and the subsistence crisis of 1845–7

Clark opens his account of the pre-1848 social question by distinguishing nineteenth-century ‘pauperism’ from older poverty, then documents it across Europe (Bologna, Prussia, the Veneto, Lombardy, Ireland, Minden-Ravensberg). He weighs rival contemporary explanations (decorporation, capitalism, female and child labour, competition) and finds each partly right and none sufficient; industrialization, population growth and Malthusian scarcity are all tested and found wanting as single causes. He then turns to conflicts over land, forests and water as feudal usage rights gave way to liberal private property (Andalusia, Sicily, the Ariège ‘War of the Girls’, the Rhineland, the Liri valley), before narrating the 1845–7 crisis: potato blight, grain failure, a hard winter, a credit and cotton shock, mass unemployment, food riots and excess mortality. The unit closes with comparative state responses: Jerez de la Frontera's improvised bread deal, Prussian repression, Rhenish bourgeois relief, Belgian public works and, at the worst extreme, British laissez-faire in Ireland.

## Themes

**Pauperism as structural, not accidental.** Clark stresses that the neologism ‘pauperism’ registered poverty seen as collective, permanent and now reaching secure groups such as artisans and smallholders. Statistical fragments (Bologna's 10,000 ‘permanent beggars’ out of 70,000; heights falling among the poor) give the claim empirical weight. This is the era's social question becoming legible as a category, echoing the way [[legibility-as-machine-core]] describes states learning to see new populations, though Clark does not frame it this way.

**No single cause.** Explanations from conservatives, Engels, Petitti and Louis Blanc are each granted partial truth; Clark cites Wilhelm Abel for the point that deepening poverty predated industrialization, and notes that surviving guilds sometimes protected nutrition. Population growth outpaced by roughly doubled food supply rules out a simple Malthusian trap. The real fragility was vulnerability to shocks and dependence on a single commodity (the potato).

**Power relations, not nature.** Sufferers saw scarcity as produced by shifting relations between people, not as divinely or naturally ordained in a Malthusian sense. Opaque wage and piece-rate systems, changing property law and abolished feudal tenure (which settled who owned land but not who could use it) were the friction points. Local revolts such as the Andalusian rent strikes were ‘not primitive or apolitical’ but organized and legally literate. Solidarity was not automatic: workers also attacked migrant ‘strangers’.

**Property law as social and ecological rupture.** The shift from communal ‘agrosilvopastoral’ use to market-oriented private property triggered protests, but also deforestation and flooding (Rhone 1843, the Liri valley). Clark warns against a simple heroic-peasant story, since in the Corbières smallholders themselves drove privatization.

**Compound crisis, 1845–7.** Blight, drought, rye rust, depleted reserves, a severe winter, price shocks, falling demand, credit contraction and a cotton shortfall interlocked to close the scissors on landless piece-workers. Excess deaths were 48 per cent above normal in Austria in 1847; around 100,000 people joined roughly 200 riots in spring 1847. This synchrony across food, industry and finance is the kind of coupled-system failure that may loosely rhyme with a Gramsci-gap style breakdown of legitimacy ([[gramsci-gap]]), though Clark presents it as an immediate crisis, not an interregnum.

**Hunger is political; responses diverged.** Citing Amartya Sen, Clark argues the crisis forced officials into decisions under pressure. Jerez's negotiated subsidy, Belgian public works and Rhenish bourgeois committees contrast with Prussian faith in repression (authorities ‘preferred the riot’ to an empowered middle class) and Russell and Wood's laissez-faire in Ireland, where an eighth of the population died. The differential outcomes are Clark's evidence that institutional choice, not blight alone, shaped who suffered and which order was left legitimate. Institutions improvised under pressure and a liberal order's reluctance to intervene recall themes in [[history-machine]] style accounts of hidden accumulation before a lurch, but this is our resonance, not Clark's.

## Notable passages

The opening on pauperism (c1) defines the conceptual novelty that the whole book's social-question thread relies on. The passage on perceived power relations (c2) supplies the mechanism by which scarcity became politically threatening. The land-usage passage (c3) states the paradox that abolishing feudalism settled ownership but not use. The ‘overlayering’ passage (c4) explains how food, industrial and credit shocks compounded. The Sen paragraph (c5) states Clark's interpretive claim that famine is political. The Prussia paragraph (c6) shows how a state's fear of the bourgeoisie shaped its handling of crisis, setting up the later liberal–radical and state–society tensions of 1848.
