# Improvising authority: provisional governments and March ministries in 1848

Clark opens by rejecting the metaphor of revolution as a "transition" between regimes: in February 1848 the new form of state did not exist even embryonically, and no rules of succession could exist in a real revolution. He then compares how authority was improvised across Europe. In Paris two newspaper offices, Le National and La Réforme, drew up rival lists of ministers that were ratified by acclamation in a chaotic sequence of crowds. In Milan, Casati grounded his legitimacy in a decree the Austrian vice-governor himself had issued. In Venice, Manin was carried to power by an arsenal mutiny he had not planned. In Palermo committees grew directly out of insurrection. Where old sovereigns survived (Naples, Prussia, Vienna) the result was a hybrid: liberal 'March ministers' sharing power with monarchs. Wallachia is the outlier, where a small, tightly knit elite planned the revolution in advance. Clark concludes that the routes differed as much as the political "biodiversity" of the states, but every new structure was fragile, factional, short of money and unsure of who controlled the armies.

## Themes

**Hiatus, not transition.** Clark's key conceptual move is that the February Revolution "was not a ‘transition’, but rather a hiatus, in which the future was suddenly blown open". The emergence of a provisional authority is therefore a more mysterious process than timelines suggest. The Provisional Government was "not a government at all, but a ‘revolutionary authority’" drawn from networks able to wield influence in that gap. This resonates with the [[gramsci-gap]] (the interregnum in which the old legitimacy is dead and the new cannot yet be born), though Clark's hiatus is a matter of days and weeks and he offers no theory of epochs; the rhyme concerns the improvisation of institutions under pressure, not the scale.

**Print as switchboard of legitimacy.** In 1830 and again in 1848 the liberal and radical papers shaped the emerging authority. Clark says they could do so because they were "already important switchboards for liberal and radical opinion", the mark of a "civilization of the journal". In Milan the Gazzetta di Milano performed a similar function, and in Wallachia Curierul Românesc carried the news of the European revolutions. This is contagion and legitimation through print, close to the themes of [[benedict-anderson]] on print publics, though Clark's case is about editorial offices acting as kingmakers, not imagined community.

**Legitimacy as fiction and self-appointment.** Belgioioso reports that the Milanese ministers "assigned to themselves portions of power" while the public read printed lists as if an election had taken place. Casati derived his authority from an Austrian transfer of responsibility, "the fiction … that the Austrians themselves had laid the foundation" of the authority that expelled them. Caussidière and Étienne Arago simply seized the police prefecture and the post office, and were retrospectively acknowledged. Authority was assembled from acclamation, seizure and paper.

**Social legibility of the worker.** "Albert the worker" and Manin's tailor Toffoli were placed in government as symbols. Marie d'Agoult read Albert's nomination as a sign that the revolution was passing from the political to the social. Clark notes that Toffoli was really a master craftsman with an atelier, so the symbol was a curated representation of the lower classes, not their presence. This is a small case of a new class becoming legible to a state through a chosen emblem (compare [[legibility-as-machine-core]], though here the legibility is symbolic and self-consciously performed by the revolutionaries, not imposed by an administrative apparatus).

**Hybrid governments where sovereigns survived.** In Naples the liberal ministers were stuck with holdovers from the absolute regime and a king who kept fighting in Sicily; in Prussia Camphausen and Hansemann, "beneficiaries of the revolution, but not revolutionaries", set out to stabilize the monarchy; in Vienna the revolution "rolled … past and around the structures of power". Clark stresses the triangle of monarch, liberal elites and radical activists, and the way the March ministers would be "ground between parliament, public opinion and royal power".

**Wallachia as counter-type.** Almost everywhere the new leaders "were not the authors but the inheritors of revolution" ("the circumstances dominated the men", Regnault). In Wallachia a compact elite tied by kinship, journalism, the Frăția and study in Paris planned the seizure of the executive, modelling it on European precedents and on the pronunciamento revolutions of 1820–21. Clark calls it "a bid by one sector of the elite to secure control of the executive structure".

**Fragility and the unsolved question of power.** The closing paragraphs list what all these regimes shared: no common view of how to govern, factions, dependence on the strata that raised them up, an expanded franchise, the mismatch between the slow politics of chambers and the fast politics of clubs and streets, capital in hiding, and armies still under old or foreign control. This foreshadows the fissuring of the liberal–radical coalition that Clark develops later.

## Notable passages

The opening (c1) states the hiatus thesis. The paragraph on the newspapers (c2) places the source of legitimacy in the press, and Belgioioso's account of Milan (c3) shows the same process from a sceptical insider who knew the men involved. The Wallachian comparison (c4) supplies Clark's contrast between inheritors and authors of revolution. The two closing syntheses (c5, c6) give the typology of routes to restabilization and the shared fragility of the results. Clark is a historian, not a machine theorist; the resonance with WMP is with the improvised, distributed assembly of institutions in a legitimacy gap, which fits the [[history-machine]] idea of order assembling from many independent components only loosely, since here the components are named men, newspapers and crowds acting over days.
