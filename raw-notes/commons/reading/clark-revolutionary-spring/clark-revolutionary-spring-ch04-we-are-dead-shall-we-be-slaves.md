# Vienna and Pest, March 1848: the fall of Metternich and the staged revolution

Clark opens on 10 March with the Wiener Zeitung's official insistence that events in Paris were a French domestic matter, set against the paper's own non-official columns reporting concessions extracted by crowds in Cologne, Munich, Nassau and Darmstadt. He then reconstructs Vienna's rapid escalation: financial panic and high food prices, petitions and clubs, Kossuth's 3 March Diet speech circulated in German translation, the 13 March demonstrations at the Landhaus, the shooting that killed twenty-nine, the suburban riots, and Metternich's forced resignation that evening. The second half (Shall We Be Slaves?) follows 15 March in Pest, where Petofi's circle turns Kossuth's speech into the Twelve Demands and a street march, and the Hungarian delegation's trip to Vienna wrings a constitution promise and a Hungarian ministry from a shaken court. It closes on the theatricality of the early revolution: costume, staged speech, stage fright.

## Themes

**Official ideology versus social reality, and contagion by print.** The Wiener Zeitung's 'breathtakingly placid announcement' sits in the same issue as reports of crowds forcing concessions elsewhere. News travelled by newspaper, translated speech and delegation, and was itself a causal force: Kossuth's speech fell, in an eyewitness's words, 'like a kindling spark' among the Viennese; news of Vienna then pushed Pest radicals from petition to direct action; Pest's deputy mayor invoked not being left behind 'other European movements'. This is continent-scale synchrony working through information channels. It resonates loosely with the [[history-machine]] idea of a paradigm lurching into visibility, though Clark gives contingent, personal narrative rather than any structural machinery.

**Loyal, system-saving protest.** Early bourgeois demands were framed as warnings to rescue the monarchy from its custodians. Even Lohner's radical manifesto avoided the word 'revolution' and aimed to 'rescue the state, the empire and the emperor'. Anger targeted ministers (Metternich, Sedlnitzky), not Emperor Ferdinand. Clark reads these as claims to power by the propertied classes while protecting property. Kossuth's demands aimed to renew, not destroy, the monarchy.

**Two revolutions in one city.** The central analytic claim: the political revolt of burghers, students and some artisans and the social revolt of suburban workers were simultaneous and separate. Workers destroyed machines and the premises of bad employers (sparing paternalist ones), and sacked police and consumption-tax offices, echoing the Silesian weavers of 1844. The National Guard and Academic Legion, newly armed, suppressed them as criminal 'riff-raff' with lethal violence and contempt, while working-class victims in the inner city were folded into the heroic narrative. Bourgeois observers, despite years of agonizing over the Social Question, had little sympathy for need-driven protest. The state answered the suburbs administratively by cutting staple taxes. This bears on how classes become legible or illegible as political actors: some deaths were 'revolutionary', others not. Compare [[legibility-hardens-hierarchy-bayly]], though Clark does not use a legibility vocabulary.

**A fragmented power centre and the gradual-then-sudden speed of revolution.** Clark denies a unitary 'Metternich system': an incapacitated emperor, the Staatskonferenz, and the Metternich-Kolowrat rivalry produced a hive of power centres. Metternich's line that reconstruction 'cannot be improvised' had an internal logic, but as events accelerated his politics slowed. Clark borrows Hemingway's 'gradually and then suddenly' for the dual tempo of revolution, and quotes Saphir on three days richer than three centuries. The improvised institutions (Academic Legion of 30,000, National Guard, Committee of Public Safety) illustrate institutions built under pressure. There is a loose echo of the [[gramsci-gap]] in the vacuum of authority, though Clark's story is of rapid reconfiguration rather than morbid interregnum.

**Kossuth, Petofi and the escalation game.** Kossuth pushed the initiative to the radical Society of Ten, then reclaimed control through the Committee of Public Safety; Petofi's National Song frames national memory as freedom, humiliation and 'slavery', then transformative present (compare [[nationalism]] and, at a distance, [[benedict-anderson]]). The Hungarian delegation turned petition into a rescript demanding a separate ministry; the court substituted a version preserving Habsburg form. Szechenyi contrasts his incremental 'compound interest' temporality with Kossuth's catastrophism and is haunted that night by a sinking-ship dream: a hedge on whether the gamble is sustainable. Note that even Petofi's twelve demands ignored urban workers and the landless peasantry.

**Revolution as theatre.** Costume, tricolours, illuminated cities and improvised oratory gave events an epic mood; participants had to find public voices and suffered stage fright. Tocqueville and Flaubert winced at the mummery, but Clark argues theatricality drove events and let people experience the present as history unfolding.

## Notable passages

**Loyal protest (c1).** Clark's interpretive point that early articulations of protest sought to save the system, and that Ferdinand's popularity and court divisions focused ire on ministers.

**Suburban violence (c2) and two worlds (c3).** The core of his argument about the gap between political and social revolution, with the workers' own words about machines that 'are guilty of our ruin' and the guardsman Kneisel's relish for killing.

**Metternich's fall (c4).** Plurality of power centres, 'we must make everything anew, but such reconstructions cannot be improvised', and the 'We are dead' exchange.

**Pest, 15 March (c5).** Petofi's march, the Twelve Demands, and the National Song's structure of national memory.

**Theatre (c6).** The performative dimension of revolution and Clark's defence of its causal role.
