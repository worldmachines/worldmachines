# Wave, News and the February Revolution in Paris

The unit opens by questioning the ubiquitous 'wave' metaphor (Mark Traugott's Palermo-or-Paris question) and proposes thinking along two axes at once: propagation across space from nodal points, and accumulation of instability over time in many places. Clark then shows, through La Réforme and a survey of Spanish, Dutch, Prussian, Finnish and Romanian papers, that Europeans in the weeks before 22 February read the continent's disorders as one connected phenomenon; Metternich and Guizot watched the same horizon through diplomatic channels and drew opposite lessons from it. The second half narrates the Paris February: the banquet prohibition, the disenfranchised National Guard, the crowds of 22-23 February, Guizot's calm debate of a bank law amid barricades, the boulevard des Capucines volley, the abdication, and the Provisional Government's proclamation. Clark closes with an analysis of why the authorities prepared for the wrong revolution, an intermediate 'time of politics' between remote and proximate causes, and the onset of a 'fission phase' from March.

## Themes

**Revolutions as cognate, not domino-like.** Clark rejects the diffusion-from-an-origin picture: revolutions 'did not, for the most part, cause each other' but were not independent either, being rooted in one economic space and kindred political cultures. Synchronous propagation effects interacted with local, evolving instability. The Palermo-versus-Paris question 'loses much of its importance'. This is a hedged, historian's claim about synchrony, not a model; it does rhyme with the [[history-machine]] idea of a paradigm assembling from distributed components until it lurches into visibility, though Clark speaks of instability and contagion, not of infrastructure.

**News created a shared present.** Newspapers across the continent, including on the 'periphery' (Bucharest, Helsinki), stitched together coverage from private letters and translated copy from other papers, so readers felt they 'belonged to a shared present'. Left, moderate and conservative papers all showed the pattern. La Réforme explicitly denied any line between 'advanced' and 'backward' countries. This connects to print-mediated imagined community ([[benedict-anderson]]), though Clark's point is simultaneity of alarm rather than nation-building, and to the ideological uses of that connective view (socialists, Tocqueville and Metternich each drew the connection for different ends).

**Two Europes: networked publics versus the 1815 security culture.** Metternich and Guizot saw a Europe of treaties and laws, informed by diplomacy rather than newspapers; Metternich compared revolution to a book in which Europe was at the preface and France at the final pages. Guizot held that concessions would only breed instability. Attribution matters: these are the actors' own readings, and Clark contrasts them with the radicals' networked view.

**Regime deafness and the autopilot of a dying order.** Guizot's chamber debated a Bank of Bordeaux law while barricades rose outside; Clark describes 'a kind of autopilot that often comes into play in regimes on the verge of extinction' and a man who believed reality would eventually catch up with his theory. This resonates with the WMP theme of a gap between official ideology and social reality and, loosely, with the interregnum of the [[gramsci-gap]], though Clark describes a single regime's collapse, not a civilizational transition.

**The wrong revolution.** Counter-insurgency planning was tuned to conspiratorial insurrections (1832, 1834, 1839); what came was inchoate, multifocal and socially deep, unified by the tripwire issue of the right to assemble. The franchise wall between the 'small political and the much larger social nation' left a large social nation illegible as a political constituency, in the terms of [[legibility-as-machine-core]]; the resonance is partial, since Clark's concern is representation and fear of the 'covetous classes', not statecraft's measurement.

**The intermediate plane of causation, the 'time of politics'.** Between remote causes (economic cycles, ideas) and proximate events (an accidental shot) lies a plane of accumulating tension, hardened language, collapsed consensus and tripwire issues, operating in months and weeks and shared across 1848's crises. From March the sequence enters a 'fission phase' of near-simultaneous detonations and feedback loops, where linear narrative fails.

**Constitutions as sacred objects.** Ledru-Rollin invoked the constitution of 1791 to argue a right of assembly the 1830 Charte omitted; Clark notes comparable veneration for the Cadiz constitution of 1812, the Sicilian constitution of 1812 and the Polish constitution of 1791, calling the 'numinous presence' of past constitutions a distinctive feature of these revolutions.

## Notable passages

**The wave critique (c1).** Clark unpacks the physics of a wave to argue for reading 1848 along both spatial propagation and temporal accumulation, and gives his formula for the relation among revolutions.

**News and a shared present (c2).** Provenance of stories mattered less than the sense of belonging to a shared present in which 'history was awakening to rough life'.

**Metternich and Guizot (c3).** The 1815 web of treaties as the connected Europe of the ministers, and Guizot's insistence on standing firm.

**Guizot's autopilot (c4).** The clause-by-clause banking debate during the first day of street fighting, and the diagnosis of a trance of normality.

**Preparing for the wrong revolution (c5).** The failure of conspiracy-focused policing and the hollowing of the juste milieu into a purely mathematical middle.

**The time of politics (c6).** The conceptual core of Clark's causal account: a plane between remote and proximate causes.
