# Parliaments of 1848: from insurgency to assembly

Clark opens with La Farina's account of the Sicilian parliament's opening in Palermo on 25 March 1848 as a template for turning insurgency into orderly government, then surveys the bewildering variety of representative bodies that sprang up across the continent: new mass-franchise parliaments in Piedmont, Prussia, Austria and France, the unprecedented Frankfurt assembly, converted corporate diets, and rival bodies that overlapped and were played against each other. Euphoria at their convocation gave way to disappointment at their ponderousness, inexperience and lack of self-confidence. Above all they proved predominantly moderate or conservative, which stunned the left (Sand, Proudhon) and posed the problem of what to do when a revolution generates the conditions of its own negation. Clark then turns to what the parliaments did accomplish: they were spaces of political experience, they hosted the emergence of caucuses and parties, they nationalized debate, they pioneered modern stenographic reporting, and they trained a cohort of politicians who resurfaced after the reaction. Most parliaments were short-lived, crushed or dispersed by counter-revolutionary executives in 1848-9.

## Themes

**Institutions improvised under pressure.** Parliaments appeared "under a bewildering variety of circumstances and in a wide range of forms". Jurisdictions overlapped (Frankfurt ministers were also state employees; Moravian and Vienna authorities disputed who could legislate on feudal dues; six parliament-like bodies operated in parallel in the Austrian Empire), and authorities could manoeuvre parliaments against each other (Stadion's Ukrainian assembly against the Poles; Jelacic's early Croatian Diet against Hungary). This is a close-up of a political order being reassembled while running, though Clark presents it as contingent improvisation and not as any deep machine.

**Elections consolidated old elites.** The franchise in France leapt from under 1 to 23.1 per cent, yet deputies were overwhelmingly drawn from existing officeholders and notables; Frankfurt deputies were 83 per cent former state officials. Clark attributes this to hybrid or indirect franchises that excluded itinerants and waged workers, to habits of deference, and to the fact that most electors were themselves moderate. Tocqueville's rural triumph and Schuselka's remark on smocks that do not obey a smock giving orders are the emblems. The exclusion of migrants who could not prove residency is also a case of who is legible to the state; it echoes [[legibility-as-machine-core]] only loosely, since Clark is describing franchise design rather than a legibility regime.

**The revolution disowns itself.** Liberals and moderate conservatives treated parliament as the return to order that made insurrection illegitimate; the Berlin assembly refused to honour the March fighters, and the word 'revolution' fell out of favour. Clark gives this a family metaphor via Lewald and Die Lokomotive. For the left, the result was a crisis: Sand threatened a second popular expression of will, Proudhon called universal suffrage a way to make the people lie, and radicals faced three options (minority politics, extra-parliamentary mobilization, renewed insurrection). This is the seed of the liberal-radical fissure that Clark develops as revolutionary 'entropy'. It resembles the interregnum logic of [[gramsci-gap]] only faintly: here the old order is not dying but reasserting itself through the new institution.

**Parliaments as apprenticeship.** Partisan life began as flux (clubs named after cafes and hotels such as the Casino, Milani, Donnersberg) and hardened into disciplined caucuses (Rue de Poitiers, later the Party of Order; 80 per cent of Frankfurt deputies in clubs by October 1848). Clark stresses that internal dispute over the parliament's own status (delegated organ of states versus organ of a sovereign people) was corrosive in a way that disputes over policy were not. Cohesion suffered from inexperience, procedural pedantry, clientelism (Malvica's hostile account of Sicily) and a self-doubting sense of powerlessness.

**Nation, print and infrastructure.** The first 'Italian' and 'German' elections were an apprenticeship in nation-building. A feedback loop of printed speeches, local newspapers and petitions between districts and Frankfurt normalized national discourse, comparable in spirit to the print-community mechanism of [[benedict-anderson]] and touching [[nationalism]], though Clark does not frame it in those terms. The Frankfurt Economic Committee's telegraph discussions show a unity of infrastructure displacing the cultural dream of the nation. Modern stenography in Paris (rolling stenographer, reviser, next-day publication, Prevost's 'readable form of orality') made debate almost simultaneously legible to outsiders; Groen van Prinsterer welcomed this from a conservative standpoint, to weaken the press as an intermediary power.

**Suppression and afterlife.** Parliaments were relocated, starved of ministers, dissolved or dispersed by troops (Kremsier, Brandenburg, the Stuttgart rump). Yet the organizational experience carried into the Second Empire's Bonapartism, the Italian kingdom after 1859, and the Old and Young Czechs: the 1848ers were no lost generation. This supports the book's wider rejection of 'failure' as a verdict.

## Notable passages

**La Farina's template.** Clark reads the Palermo opening as a rhetorical transformation: the squadre become pillars of order, the insurgent bell rings for fraternity, dictatorial power is handed meekly to legislators. He signals that this is La Farina's artful and wishful account, which the rest of the chapter complicates.

**The conservative parliaments.** The statistics on continuity of personnel, oaths sworn to Louis Philippe, and the small share of deputies ever imprisoned for political offences are the chapter's central empirical claim.

**The left's dilemma.** Clark states the problem of parliament as a 'blocked artery' and the three possible responses, foreshadowing the June Days and later fissures.

**Corrosive dissension.** The distinction between arguments within a parliament and arguments about the parliament's own powers is Clark's analytical point about why Frankfurt and Vienna sapped their own energy.

**Parliaments and the infrastructure of the nation.** The feedback loop with local papers and the telegraph debate show public resonance and material unity as lasting effects that outlived the assemblies.

**Stenography.** The Paris system solved the problem of conflicting versions of speeches in circulation and made debate a public event; it is a small, concrete case of institution-building around information flow.
