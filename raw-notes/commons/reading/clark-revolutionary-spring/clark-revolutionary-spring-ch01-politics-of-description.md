# The Social Question and the politics of description

The chapter opens with a framing claim: social discontent does not ‘cause’ revolutions, but material distress was the backdrop to the political polarization that made 1848 possible, and the era’s ways of seeing and tabulating dysfunction mattered as much as the suffering itself. The extract covers the first section, ‘The Politics of Description’. It begins with the doctors Ange Guépin and Eugène Bonamy and their 1836 statistical portrait of Nantes, with its eight classes, household budgets and mortality gradients. It then sweeps across a Europe-wide genre (Kay in Manchester, Villermé on textile workers, Petitti on child labour, Patissier on Lyons weavers, Grunholzer in Berlin, Engels, Sue’s fiction and his imitators). Clark then shows how the same evidence was turned to opposed politics: Engels’s class war, Guépin’s Saint-Simonian technocratic reform, Frégier’s moralizing policing of the poor, and Buret’s claim that poverty is a phenomenon of civilization. He closes by noting the blind spots of this way of seeing and its feedback into socialist politics.

## Themes

**Distress as backdrop, not cause.** Clark’s opening hedge is that if misery caused revolution, revolutions would be far more common. Distress fed the motivation of urban crowds and the polarization that followed, and that is all the chapter claims for it.

**The Social Question as a way of seeing.** The question was ‘a constellation of real-world problems’ but also a genre of knowledge. Official reports, prize essays, journalism and fiction merged in a ‘culture of enquiry’. Numbers, tabulation and a ‘period eye’ for precise description were its hallmarks, and it was mostly posed in the third person: what should be done about them? Guépin, who also examined the wealthy, was an exception. This resonates with [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and the [[modernity-machine]]: the poor and the city become readable, countable and governable through tables. Clark, however, treats this as a historical moment in the birth of social science and realism, not as a machine theory, and he stresses that the descriptions were also moral and literary performances.

**The statistical turn.** Quetelet’s ‘social physics’ made it possible to connect averages and individuals and to state causal claims such as income on mortality. Nantes shows the gradient: one death per seventy-eight residents a year on a rich quay against one per seventeen on the rue des Fumiers, and average age at death of 59.2 against 31.16. Numbers were also rhetorical, lifting description ‘away from mere political assertion’.

**Same facts, opposed politics.** How questions were posed depended on the politics behind the enquiry. Engels read exploitation and a coming revolution ‘compared with which the first French Revolution … will seem like a child’s game’. Guépin and Bonamy wanted to reach the future without a new Jacquerie or ’93, through association-based reform and a Saint-Simonian ‘physiology’ of society run by an industrial class of hygienists and planners (see [[henri-de-saint-simon]]). Frégier blamed the poor’s vice and prescribed renewed patriarchal patronage. Buret argued that moral condition follows physical condition and that poverty is inherent in industrial competition, drawing on Sismondi. Ramón de la Sagra blamed governments and moral neglect, while proposing a disciplined ‘social physics’.

**Moral panic and gender.** Commentators fixated on working women as emblems of purity and dissolution, and on the supposed link between industrial employment and prostitution. Some (Buret, Engels, Guépin) read prostitution as a product of wage and power asymmetries; Dronke’s account shows the scale of the sex trade among Berlin servants. The panic reveals anxieties about the gender order more than dispassionate observation.

**Literary form and its limits.** Social description had a voyeuristic, metonymic, sensory dimension and spilled into fiction (Sue’s Mystères de Paris and its German imitators). Clark warns that its ‘reality effect’ hid things: middle-class diagnosticians fixated on slums and contagion and often missed working-class vitality, such as thickening commercial networks and new forms of labour organization emerging ‘from below’. That is a gap between official or bourgeois description and social reality.

**Feedback into politics.** Engels shaped the Communist Manifesto; Louis Blanc used Guépin’s life-expectancy data to call competition ‘a system of extermination for the people’. Description was itself a political act.

## Notable passages

The opening hedge (c1) sets Clark’s method: distress is necessary background, not a trigger. The Nantes mortality passage (c2) is the clearest instance of how numbers made class inequality visible. The paragraph on the family resemblance of the genre and Quetelet (c3) explains the shift in how society was understood. The Engels-versus-Guépin passage (c4) shows one dataset feeding rival politics. Buret’s reversal of Frégier (c5) makes poverty a product of the system, not of the poor. The closing critique (c6) names what the new gaze systematically failed to see.
