# Galicia 1846 and the Indirect Path from Social Distress to Revolution

The unit has two parts. The first narrates the Galician events of February 1846. A Polish noble-led national insurrection, planned by exiles of the Polish Democratic Society, collided with a peasant counter-insurrection that killed roughly a thousand people and destroyed about 500 estate houses. Clark then works through the causes, the contested memory, and the lessons drawn by Schwarzenberg, Hungarian and Croatian elites, and Polish democrats. He ends with Sacher-Masoch's fictional and autobiographical treatments of the violence. The second part, the chapter's Conclusions, generalizes. Pre-1848 social protest was chronic and local, and there was no direct causal line from hunger to revolution. It still fed the revolutions through its political effects, through its unruly and sometimes anti-revolutionary forms, and through the fear it generated.

## Themes

**Two uprisings, misaligned grievances.** The Polish nobility promised to lift labour services (robot) and the salt and tobacco monopolies. The peasants had reason to distrust the promisers, who were the beneficiaries of feudalism. Some disputes were already before Austrian courts, which put the Austrian state in the role of mediator and guarantor. In the east the peasants were Ukrainian (Ruthene) and the Polish national appeal had even less purchase. Clark's summary is that "the estate-owner’s national dream might turn out to be the peasant’s feudal nightmare", so political and social grievances may fail to align, and upheavals may magnify or cancel each other.

**The politics of description and contested memory.** Surviving records come from victims' relatives or from Austrian authorities, and almost every element of the narrative is disputed: peasants or off-duty soldiers, spontaneous loyalty or a pre-planned assault, Szela as peasant revolutionary, vexatious litigant or 'peasant king'. Clark separates the legend of a 'Judas bounty' on heads, for which there is no documentary evidence, from the better-supported Austrian passivity and a few local rewards. Nations and classes here become legible only through partisan accounts. This is a small case of how the state and its rivals fight over the description of a social event (compare [[legibility-as-machine-core]], though Clark is not theorizing legibility).

**Peasants as a resource for order.** Schwarzenberg's 'imperial peasants' anecdote, which Clark treats as possibly apocryphal, and Metternich's line that 'a democracy without the people is a chimera' anticipate the conservative and counter-revolutionary use of the countryside in 1848-9. Clark flags that such a 'lesson' merely confirmed the learner's wishful intuitions. Hungarian liberals turned to peasant emancipation, and Croat elites became alert to the agrarian question. Others drew meaner lessons: Antoniewicz blamed 'the Jews', and Wincenty Pol lost faith in democratic methods. The peasant-versus-national-elite split is relevant to [[nationalism]] and, at a distance, to [[intellectual-vs-revolutionary]] questions about who a movement actually speaks for. The Galician nobles' appeal to a shared Polish community of sentiment is the kind of imagined community that [[benedict-anderson]] analyses, and here it visibly fails to reach the peasants.

**Violence and its remembrance.** The atrocity narratives (the Bogusz family, Tarnow's carts of bodies, Antoniewicz's devastated manors) and the Sacher-Masoch material show how a society processes such violence in multi-perspectival fiction and in sexualized fascination. Clark's hedge is that the cinematic quality does not set fiction apart from eyewitness memoir.

**Distress does not directly cause revolution.** Riots were like gout, not cardiac arrest: intermittent, local and often conventional, following a moral-economy script. The geography of hunger (Ireland, Silesian valleys, the Netherlands) differs from the geography of revolution. Poverty tends to make people 'speechless' rather than active, and revolutions are political events with a certain autonomy of politics.

**But social conflict is politically consequential.** Forest-law disputes (Heinrich and Karl Marx), the weavers, journeymen and hunger rioters all re-emerge in 1848. Some of this turbulence was anti-revolutionary, for example the Guerra dels Matiners, the Maria da Fonte revolt, Sicilian 'comunisti', and attacks on Jewish merchants. Revolutions "unlock all the tensions and resentments building within a society, not just the progressive ones".

**Fear as a constitutive element.** The line between riot and politics is hard to draw (Clark invokes the 2011 London riots). Feared armed bands pushed liberals and radicals into power, made authorities concede civil guards, and drove liberals back toward the old authorities. Fear was part of the revolution rather than an exogenous factor. For World Machines this resonates loosely with the [[history-machine]] idea of distributed components that suddenly become visible, and with how a legitimacy crisis (cf. [[gramsci-gap]]) is bridged by improvised armed institutions. Clark makes neither claim and is describing contingent political sequences.

## Notable passages

**c1 The two uprisings.** A compact statement of the double structure: a national uprising of elites and the peasant counter-insurrection that halted it.

**c2 Contested accounts.** Austrian and Polish narratives diverge on nearly every point, from perpetrators to premeditation, so the historian works from polarized sources.

**c3 Multi-vectoral upheaval.** Clark's own gloss on Galicia: political and social grievances can misalign, converge or cancel, as they would in 1848. He cites Marx's 1842 remark on the one-sidedness of parts.

**c4 Schwarzenberg's lesson.** The 'imperial peasants' anecdote and Metternich's chimera line, with the caution that lessons from history confirm the learner's hopes. It prefigures the modernizing counter-revolution's use of the countryside.

**c5 Dearth versus revolution.** The geography of hunger differs from the geography of revolution, so revolutions are political events, not the necessary consequence of accumulated distress.

**c6 Fear inside the revolution.** Feared social violence gave liberals and radicals power and then drew them back to old authority; fear was part of the revolution itself.
