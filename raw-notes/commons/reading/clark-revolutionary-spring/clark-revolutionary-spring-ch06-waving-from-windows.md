# Women in the Revolutions of 1848: Present at the Barricades, Absent from the Assemblies

Clark opens with Nordmann's lithograph of the Berlin barricade of 18 March, a composed image that puts a woman and her sons at its centre, and uses it to introduce a fact contemporaries noted: women were in virtually all the tumults of 1848. He then complicates the record (most sources are by men, and the armed Vesuviennes were a hoax), contrasts the aims of women's advocacy in Paris and Pest, shows women excluded from clubs, parliaments and the franchise, and explains the near-total failure of direct challenges to patriarchy. He closes with the routes that worked better (girls' education, the kindergarten movement, women's literary periodicals and novels) and with women writers (d'Agoult, Fuller, Belgioioso, von Arnim) as unusually valuable witnesses.

## Themes

**Participation versus legibility.** Women fought, fed, nursed, edited and sewed flags across Berlin, Paris, Milan, Prague, Bucharest and south Germany, but the sources that let historians measure this (newspapers, police records, images) are mostly male work. The surviving pictures of radical women's clubs are caricatures, and the armed Vesuviennes derive from folklore and a hoax poster by Daniel Borme, so they tell us nothing of substance about real participation. This is a case of Clark's politics of description: who is made visible, and in what genre, shapes what a state and a later historian can see.

**Women's advocacy was not one programme.** La Voix des Femmes (Niboyet) turned the liberal patriarchal order's own logic of the family as a 'social individual' against it and demanded the vote and taxation rights. The Pest 'Demands of the Radical Hungarian Women' merged the cause of women almost entirely into the nation and the middle-class and gentry rejection of aristocratic style, offering duties in place of rights. Clark ties both to the class milieus of the authors and to the fact that oppression operated on so many levels that priorities (schooling, divorce, labour, franchise) were hard to rank. George Sand and Marie d'Agoult, for their part, distanced themselves from the feminist clubs. In the 'Age of Questions' the Woman Question could not be disentangled from the others.

**Exclusion from the new institutions, and the hostile gaze.** Women waving from windows was the picture that pleased middle-class men, because it joined support for the revolution to decorum. Women who sought to act as autonomous agents met mockery, silence or hostility: the democratic clubs and the parliaments excluded them, Frankfurt greeted votes for women with guffaws, and women who watched from galleries became a passive sounding board. Tocqueville's disgust at the 500 working women at the Fete de la Concorde is Clark's exhibit of a contempt felt as 'natural', which even many activist women absorbed.

**Patriarchy as the most immovable structure.** Nothing like Schoelcher's abolition act happened for women: no enfranchisement anywhere in Europe in 1848, suffrage mostly in 1918-19 (France 1946), French divorce law unchanged until 1884, the Napoleonic Code in the Low Countries until 1905. Clark's explanation, which he says has often been remarked, is that in emergent bourgeois society gender norms had become more polarized, so the expansion of political participation carried men to places women could not reach. This is worth setting beside [[peter-gay]] and [[the-bourgeois-experience]] on bourgeois gender and private life, though Clark makes the point about political access rather than the inner life of the bourgeoisie.

**What worked: working with the grain.** Girls' education and Froebel's kindergartens (forty-four opened in 1848) offered professionalization framed as exalted motherhood, but were banned in Prussia in 1851 and the Hamburg college closed; girls' education then grew mostly under the Churches (Falloux Law, France 1850). Literary women in Spain and elsewhere used verse and novels to explore shelter versus exposure. Clark stresses that even the innocuous routes met condescension.

**Women as witnesses.** D'Agoult's history of 1848 (as 'Daniel Stern'), Fuller's Rome dispatches, Belgioioso and von Arnim are praised for detachment and for seeing men as men within rivalries, or, in Belgioioso's case, within 'a large machinery of interaction and crisis'. Clark hedges that this rests partly on social position and partly on a segregated world's estranged view, and he reports Deroin's claim about women's 'policy of peace and labour' as her claim, not as his own finding.

## Notable passages

**The evidence problem (c1).** Clark's discussion of the male-authored record and the Vesuviennes hoax is the passage that explains why the archive of female participation is distorted. It resonates modestly with [[legibility-as-machine-core]], in that some populations become legible to states and publics only through the categories, and caricatures, of others. Clark himself does not frame it in those terms.

**Paris and Pest (c2).** The Pest petition's stress on duties over rights shows nationalism absorbing other priorities (compare [[nationalism]]), and shows how a movement's aims follow its class milieu. The contrast is the sharpest illustration in the unit that there was no single women's revolution.

**Exclusion and spectatorship (c3).** The account of women hidden in the pulpit of St Paul's, and of galleries filled with women who could embody the nation but not represent it, ends on the unit's title image: 'Even as spectators in parliament, women were still waving from windows.'

**Tocqueville and the hateful gaze (c4).** Clark reads Tocqueville's disgust as partly political but rooted deeper, and calls contempt one of the most powerful weapons of the patriarchal order.

**The immovable structure (c5).** The comparison with abolition, the long timeline of legal disabilities and the polarized-norms explanation form Clark's core argument. It fits the wider book's theme that revolutionary success is uneven across the emancipations, and it should be read as his causal claim resting on a widely made observation.

**Men as men (c6).** The closing suggestion that segregation let women see the politicians as men, plus Deroin's contrast between struggle and pity, is offered tentatively and attributed carefully to the women who made it.
