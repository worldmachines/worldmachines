# Vienna's October: Habsburg equivocation, insurrection and the killing of Blum

The unit traces the Viennese counter-revolution from its fuse in the South Slav borderlands to its violent consummation. Between May and September 1848 the Habsburg court plays contradictory games with Ban Jelačić, the Hungarian ministry and the Serb insurgents, exploiting a loose command structure in which orders could be issued, deposed and disowned at will. The lynching of Count Lamberg in Buda (29 September) coincides with the failure of Jelačić's advance and pushes Vienna into armed intervention. Sending troops to Hungary then triggers the Viennese rising of 6 October: the murder of War Minister Latour, the storming of the arsenal, the flight of the court, three weeks of radical rule, and the siege and fall of the city (24-31 October). The last third follows Robert Blum, a Frankfurt deputy who came to Vienna as to the last chance of the German revolution, through arrest, court martial and execution, and ends with Ferdinand's abdication in favour of the eighteen-year-old Franz Joseph, presented as the monarchy's renewal.

## Themes

**Equivocation as counter-revolutionary method.** Official Vienna disowns Jelačić while the war ministry and court conservatives (Kulmer) egg him on; Kulmer tells him that only after he has crossed the Drava will confidence be restored, and Clark likens the warning to something from an organized crime syndicate. Clark treats the ambiguity of the Habsburg chain of command as a working instrument of the court's recovery, not merely confusion. The Hungarians, the Serbs of the Voivodina and Jelačić all claim the same borderland.

**Nationalism cross-cutting and reinforcing.** German-speaking Viennese radicals had cheered the defeat of Italians and Czechs but saw Hungarians as allies, so that for once, in Clark's words, nationalism reinforced rather than cross-cut trans-ethnic revolutionary solidarity. This is a useful qualification to any picture of 1848 as simply nation against nation (compare [[nationalism]]), though Clark presents it as an exception.

**Parliament versus the street versus the executive.** Kudlich and many progressive deputies retain a faith in the Reichstag's authority that Clark calls strange in retrospect, while Bach declares that the executive will tolerate no interference from parliament or the street. The radical deputies are horrified by revolution; the conservatives and Czechs conceal smiles of triumph. Clark notes the same schism as in counter-revolutionary Naples and the same opening gap between revolutionaries as in Paris and Berlin. Bach's journey from democrat to a presiding figure of the neo-absolutist 1850s prefigures the continuity between revolution and the regime that follows.

**Violence, meaning, and contested attribution.** Lamberg and Latour are killed in ritualized, theatrical mob violence. Clark stresses the afterlife: conservatives and moderate liberals read the killings as revealing the satanic malevolence of the insurgency, feeding a hunger for vengeance; Kudlich insists the killers were workers, not Hegelians, Jews or foreign agents. The conspiratorial account had lethal consequences (Goldmark, who tried to shield Latour, was sentenced to death in absentia). The same pattern recurs with Hübner's fabricated and distorted portrait of Blum.

**The rural gap.** The emperor's flight is met by cheering peasants who believe his promise that tithes and labour duties are finished. Clark calls the idea that a journey out of the capital leads to a world where the revolution has not happened an optical illusion, but the enthusiasm was genuine and shows how completely the revolution's leaders had failed to connect with the countryside. Kudlich's efforts to raise the peasantry come too late.

**Two parliaments, two executives.** With the centrist and conservative deputies gone to Kremsier, the rump Reichstag claims to be the sole legal constitutional organ, which Clark characterizes as a radical revolution, not a republican one. The doubling of legitimate authority resembles a [[gramsci-gap]]-style interregnum only loosely; Clark does not frame it so, and what is contested here is legality, not an old order's exhausted legitimacy.

**Renewal of the monarchy.** Windischgrätz and Schwarzenberg are the architects both of Blum's downfall and of the monarchy's renewal through the accession of Franz Joseph, who had made no concessions to the revolution and signed no constitutional oath. Continuity of the dynasty is rebuilt through a clean-hands successor.

## Notable passages

**Jelačić and the Drava (c1).** Kulmer's message and Jelačić's undisciplined invasion show the court pushing a proxy to act first and seeking sanction afterward, with Croat irregulars' atrocities then radicalizing Vienna.

**Lamberg (c2).** Clark calls sending Lamberg an act of baffling recklessness, and describes his lynching on the Buda-Pest bridge of boats; on the same day Jelačić is blocked, and the policy of equivocation gives way to armed intervention.

**Bach and the executive (c3).** The ministers' cheerful hardliners against the downtrodden moderates, and Bach's line on the executive, mark the parliament's loss of standing.

**Meanings of the Latour killing (c4).** The competing accounts show how the same violence is fitted to opposed narratives, with real legal consequences.

**The optical illusion of the countryside (c5).** The emperor's route and the peasants' cheers explain a structural strength of the old powers.

**Blum's letter (c6).** Clark reads the farewell letter as a relic that made Blum a private man who entered public life, heroism in a bourgeois key; he and the German revolution were, in Clark's phrase, extinguished together. Compare the bourgeois-domestic themes of [[the-bourgeois-experience]], though Clark does not draw that link himself.
