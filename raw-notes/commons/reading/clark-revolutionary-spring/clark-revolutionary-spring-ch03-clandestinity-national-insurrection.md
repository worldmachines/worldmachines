# Conspirators, martyrs and the making of the national idea

Clark opens with the 'cult of clandestinity': the sacralised conspiracy as a form of political action in the 1820s and 1830s. Filippo Buonarroti, Babeuf's comrade and a devotee of Robespierre, ran layered secret societies (the 'Sublimes Maîtres Parfaits', a masonry within masonry) aimed at a levelling republic imposed by revolutionary dictatorship; Georg Büchner and Friedrich Ludwig Weidig's *Hessian Courier* (1834) and Louis Auguste Blanqui's lifetime of insurrections and prison show the type of the 'entirely politicized personality', which Robert Blum's letters about love and duty also illustrate. The second half turns to Giuseppe Mazzini, the 'apostle' of national insurrection: exiled, austere, prolific, an organiser of failed risings (Turin 1833, Savoy 1834, the Bandiera brothers 1844) who nonetheless became the great propagandist of the democratic national idea. It closes with Garibaldi's transatlantic career and the mobile 'Grande Armée of liberty' of veteran fighters (Bem, Anzani) who would reappear in 1848.

## Themes

**Clandestinity as identity more than efficacy.** Clark stresses that the real impact of secret networks is nearly impossible to measure; conspirators and prosecutors alike overrated them, and some of the structures may have existed 'only in Buonarroti's head'. He suggests they are better understood as structures that sustained a 'compensatory emotion' for those stranded in the post-1815 world. This is his interpretive claim, hedged with 'perhaps'.

**Futility of the action, potency of the fear.** The *Hessian Courier* had no network, no cadres and an illiterate audience, yet it fed the 'nightmares of prosecutors and interior ministers'; it was betrayed by a spy and cost Weidig his life. Revolutionaries of this type played a limited role in 1848 but 'haunted the imaginations of conservatives, moderate liberals and police chiefs'. The gap between real capacity and perceived threat is itself a political fact.

**Second-hand memory of revolution.** The activists' grip on the Jacobin era was no weaker for never having experienced it (Blanqui knew the Committee of Public Safety from books). Blanqui's longing for 'that imagined place where revolutions are born' was performative, a theme that rhymes with [[history-machine]] questions about how past regimes persist as templates, though Clark is describing psychology and not machinery.

**Social versus national revolution.** Buonarroti saw the republic as a waystation to equality by dictatorship; Mazzini's republic was moral and unitary, 'the aggregate of all classes', a community 'akin to a religious congregation'. Clark's critique is that Mazzini's vision 'lacked granularity' and social texture, that duties were preferred to rights because rights divide, and that its hostility to individualism and cult of martyrs was later exploited by Italian fascists (Clark is careful that this was a selective memory). Compare [[nationalism]] and [[benedict-anderson]] on the print-made nation, and [[romanticism-and-political-danger]]; note that Mazzini's is a secular faith with quasi-theological vocabulary, not a fit with any single WMP concept.

**Failure as method, print as the real infrastructure.** For Mazzini 'education and insurrection' were mutually sustaining; disasters supplied martyrs, and martyrs were needed to overcome Italians' own inertia. Clark's verdict: as a conspirator he failed, as a 'promulgator and propagandist' he was 'without parallel', with twenty newspapers and Young Italy branches spanning Europe and the Atlantic. This resonates with WMP interest in news and print as the contagion channel and in how ideas assemble across distributed nodes ([[world-machines]], [[history-machine]]), and equally with [[legibility-as-machine-core]] only inversely: these networks deliberately cultivated opacity against a state trying to make dissent legible. The fit is loose; Clark does not theorise infrastructure.

**A mobile transnational milieu.** Exiles, ex-Napoleonic veterans and volunteers (Ramorino, Bem, Anzani, Garibaldi in Rio Grande and Montevideo) moved from struggle to struggle across Europe and the Atlantic. Garibaldi's fame was made in print by Mazzinian networks and the liberal press, and his refashioning as a gaucho 'antithesis of bourgeois society' gave him a hold on the romantic imagination (cf. [[the-bourgeois-experience]]). This prefigures Clark's global and continent-wide framing of 1848.

## Notable passages

The curated passages track the argument: Clark's deflationary reading of the secret networks (c1); the *Hessian Courier* as 'a study in political futility' (c2); Mazzini's moral, class-transcending 'people' set against Buonarroti's social revolution (c3); the theory that repeated failure educates the nation (c4); Clark's split verdict on Mazzini as failed conspirator but supreme propagandist, tied to Garibaldi's myth (c5); and the critique of Mazzini's duty-based, undivided national community and its fascist afterlife (c6).
