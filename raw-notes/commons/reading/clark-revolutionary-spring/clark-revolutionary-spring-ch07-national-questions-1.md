# National questions: transnational nationalism and the failure of the Italian coalition

Clark begins with the text of Mameli's 1847 anthem and shows that it is a patchwork of borrowed stock themes (the Marseillaise, Wybicki, Solomos, Petőfi), so that nationalism, for all its stress on uniqueness, was a transnational phenomenon. He then sets out why nationalism is the hardest concept to handle for 1848: the revolutions were experienced as pan-European but nationalized in retrospect. The unit goes on to the mismatch between ethnic settlement and political borders, the fuzziness of who belonged to which nation (regional, religious and social loyalties, the 'nationally indifferent'), and the moments when nationalism became a first principle that displaced social demands. The second half is a case study of Italy in 1848: war fever after the March risings, Cavour's call for immediate war, the hesitant and dynastic intervention of Charles Albert of Piedmont, the collapse of the coalition and defeat at Custoza on 23 July, and the rival post-mortems of Enrico Dandolo (a moderate monarchist) and Carlo Cattaneo (a republican federalist), together with the condescending French verdict Cattaneo met in Paris.

## Themes

**Nationalism as transnational and retrospective.** The anthems borrow from one another, and their authors were often foreigners or of mixed descent. Clark argues that later historians and 'memory-managers' absorbed the revolutions into national narratives, which makes it hard to imagine a world before the ascendancy of the nation, when national feeling was intense but 'mutable and localized, as one feeling among many'. This is Clark's own methodological warning, and it echoes the constructed-community argument of [[benedict-anderson]] and the older theorizing of [[nationalism]] and of [[johann-gottfried-herder]]. Clark is describing 1848 usage, not offering a general theory.

**Shocking but never surprising.** Clark's 'binary temporality': the surge of national emotion could feel like a sudden transformation, yet it always presented itself as recovery of something old. The resonance with [[romanticism-and-political-danger]] and [[counter-enlightenment-and-nationalism]] is real but partial, since Clark stays with the experience of patriots rather than an intellectual genealogy.

**Mismatch between map and population.** Germans, Italians, Croats, Romanians and Poles were each split, subordinated or scattered in different ways, so activists seeking language rights, civil-service access, autonomy or independence would compete over the same institutions. One could read this loosely as a legibility problem, in which a state grid drawn for dynasties could not sort populations into clean categories ([[legibility-as-machine-core]]); Clark does not use that idea, and the point here is that categories were unstable for the people themselves.

**Ambiguous belonging.** Dalmatian, Tyrolean and Bohemian identities, nobles who were neither Czech nor German, Galician peasants who called their landlords 'Poles' and themselves 'imperial peasants', patriots who had to learn their national language, and religious fault lines (Catholic 'greater' versus Protestant 'lesser' Germany; rival Italian programmes). Nation was one of several competing identity claims, not a given.

**Nationalism outranking the social question.** In Lombardy, Hungary and Galicia national mobilization pulled peasants and townspeople in ways no radical social programme could, pushing other demands 'down the hierarchy of needs'. This bears on the fissuring of the liberal–radical coalition and on how social grievances were subordinated to, or postponed by, national struggle. The word 'nation' underwent semantic inflation, a 'word through which time flowed'.

**Italy: enthusiasm, dynastic caution, coalition collapse.** War fever used the language of holy war and crusade under Pius IX; Cavour pressed the king; Charles Albert intervened for reasons of both fear and dynastic ambition, while reassuring the great powers it was a policing action; the army lacked maps and command; Naples and the Pope withdrew their contingents; Custoza ended realistic hopes of a combined Italian pressure on Austria. Counter-revolutionary moves elsewhere (Ferdinand II breaking the Neapolitan opposition in May) shaped the outcome, an instance of the continent-wide coupling that Clark stresses.

**Rival explanations of failure.** Dandolo blames municipal egoism and Mazzinian propaganda; Parisian notables, in Cattaneo's account, blamed Italian weakness of national feeling and spoke of Italy 'as if it were a colony'; Cattaneo blames Piedmontese perfidy and defends the city as the heart of Italian history. Clark treats these as partisan accounts, not verdicts, and finds in them a 'deeply fractured vision of the future'. Where the French judgement resembles a civilizing-mission view of a people not yet 'mature enough for liberty', it shows how one European public made another legible and inferior.

## Notable passages

**Nationalism as the concept that presses on 1848 (c1).** Clark's programmatic statement: steer between uncritical adoption of the nationalist perspective and overcorrection, and grasp the binary temporality of shock and inheritance.

**Map versus population (c2).** The compact statement of why national projects were bound to collide, with the German, Italian, Croat, Romanian and Polish cases.

**Who belongs to which nation? (c3).** Dalmatians, Tyroleans, Thun's 'Bohemian', and the Galician peasants who considered their landlords 'Poles', an important qualification to national essentialism.

**Nationalism as first principle (c4).** The conditions under which nationalism suppressed other political demands, with the Lombard, Hungarian and Ukrainian examples.

**Charles Albert's annexationist war (c5).** The Piedmontese king, the radicals around Cattaneo, and the plebiscite that revealed a dynastic rather than national policy.

**Cattaneo's imaginary Italy and municipalism (c6).** The dispute with Dandolo turns on republic versus monarchy, and on federalism anchored in cities versus unitary centralism, leaving the national cause with divided futures.
