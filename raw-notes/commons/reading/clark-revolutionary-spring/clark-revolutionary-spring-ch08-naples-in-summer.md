# Naples in Summer: the Compact Arc from Revolution to Counter-Revolution

Clark opens Chapter 8 with Naples, where the January 1848 constitution was greeted with illuminations and fraternization, yet by mid-May the king had shut the revolution down on the mainland, then reconquered Sicily and finally emptied both parliaments of meaning. He uses this unusually compressed case to ask what drove counter-revolution across Europe. The narrative runs through radical mobilization in clubs and streets, the expulsion of the Jesuits (an anti-clerical act that Clark argues alienated the wider population), the barricades and massacre of 15 May, the sham constitutional afterlife of 1848-9, the purges and trials, and the knock-on effects on the war against Austria in Lombardy and on Venice. The second half turns to interpretation: what the 15 May turning point did to European fear, why counter-revolutionaries hold the initiative on timing, and how to read the mass petitions against the constitution.

## Themes

**Polarization without conspiracy.** Clark rejects the view that either side needed to plot. The process was "driven not by cabals or conspiracies, but by the general disinhibition of politics, suspicion, fear and the hardening of irreconcilable viewpoints"; each side merely suspected the other. The revolution was, for Neapolitan radicals as elsewhere, "not an event but a process", which pressed against a king who was only unsettled, not overthrown, and a charter that already looked obsolete beside the Paris February.

**Fear as the hinge.** Following Charles de Mazade and an anonymous contemporary, Clark treats 15 May as "the disequilibrium of two fears": Ferdinand's success punctured the revolution's "redoubtable and violent charm" and emboldened those who hated it but had not dared to oppose it. The turning point came from Naples, not Paris, Vienna or Berlin. This is a contagion-by-perception story: the same news-driven synchrony that spread revolution also spread the discovery that it could be beaten.

**Counter-revolution sets the timing.** Revolutionaries did not control when revolutions happened; counter-revolutionaries were reacting to a fait accompli and could choose moment and manner. The crackdown showed "rigorous planning and precision in execution", not panic. Clark also insists armed force needs "an anchorage of some kind in popular feeling".

**The revolution and popular opinion drifted apart.** The Jesuit expulsion was the work of a "highly specific constituency of educated anti-clericals", not "the people"; on 15 May soldiers and lazzaroni looted and hunted liberals, and thousands of petitions from the continental south then begged the king to revoke the constitution, describing it in images of contagion and fire. Clark weighs the petitions' authenticity (some clergy- or official-coordinated, but diverse, and echoed by post-unification southern resistance) and concludes they deserve to be taken seriously, with parallels in Bavaria (anti-Jewish-emancipation petitions), Prussia (mass conservative associations) and France (the December 1848 election). Attribute carefully: Massari, Napier and Engels each report or interpret the plebs differently, and Napier's casualty figures are corrected by archival work.

**Hollowed institutions and spin.** Rather than abolishing constitution and parliament, the king left them as "mummified remnants", starving them of function, ridiculing them in the press, and intimidating liberal journals. Official announcements recast 15 May as victory over 'communists' and 'republicans', a category Massari says existed only in the imagination of the victors. Clark reads the king's reluctance to revoke the charter as "an unwitting testimony to the power of the liberal idea of a constitution".

**The conservative myth of the countryside.** Conservatives drew the lesson that beyond the cities lay "little people" whose loyalties were intact (Metternich on Galicia, Bismarck's memoir, later his 1866 remark that "the masses will always stand by the king"). Clark calls this partly wishful thinking, since the countryside proved responsive to radical appeals in 1848-9, and notes that conservatives simultaneously restricted the franchise (France 1850, Prussia's three-class system).

## World Machines resonances

The unit fits the frame loosely. Counter-revolution here is not a return to an earlier equilibrium but a reassembly of authority under new conditions (petition mobilization, spin, managed parliaments), which loosely rhymes with the [[history-machine]] idea of paradigms lurching into visibility and with the failed-interregnum logic of the [[gramsci-gap]], though Clark makes no such claim and the Neapolitan settlement lasted only about a decade. The state's need to read popular opinion, and the contest over what counted as the voice of 'the people' (petitions, illiteracy notes, official labeling of 'republicans'), touches [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and [[the-mass-as-fiction]]. Berlin, Vienna and Naples moving in lockstep echoes the continental synchrony the project cares about, and anti-Jesuit conspiracy imagery (Sue, Michelet) is a small case of how a rubric absorbs many phenomena. Treat all of these as suggestive parallels, not Clark's own framework.

## Notable passages

The polarization paragraph (c1) is Clark's explicit denial of conspiracy and his account of mutual suspicion. The fear and timing passage (c2) is the chapter's core analytic claim about why 15 May mattered beyond Naples. The 'cards up its sleeve' passage (c3) shows the popular base for counter-revolution, and the petition passage (c4) supplies the primary-source imagery of constitution as contagion. c5 generalizes to Bavaria, Prussia and France, and c6 follows the conservative myth of the loyal countryside through franchise restriction to Bismarck in 1866.
