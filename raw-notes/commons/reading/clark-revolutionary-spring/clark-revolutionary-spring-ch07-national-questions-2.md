# Nationalism as the revolutions' most contagious and most divisive force

Clark turns from the cascade of national mobilizations across the Habsburg lands to the German-Polish-Danish questions in Prussia and Frankfurt. Croatia is the extended case: a composite, pre-national patchwork (Croatia, Slavonia, Dalmatia, the military frontier) is rapidly recast as a nation around the newly appointed ban Jelačić, with a Thirty Demands programme, festivals, newspapers and an Illyrian schoolteacher, Mijat Stojanović, learning what it means to be Croat. Palacký's refusal of Frankfurt, the Prague Slavic Congress, the Posen rising, competing Ukrainian and Polish claims in Galicia, Wilhelm Jordan's 'right of conquest' speech and the Schleswig-Holstein crisis show national claims colliding over the same territory. The Frankfurt Assembly's impotence in the Malmø armistice and the September uprising split the left and drive liberals rightward. Clark closes with an evaluative argument: nationalism was the revolutions' most powerful 'phantom', producing elation and solidarity within nations while corroding solidarity between revolutionary movements and drawing revolutionaries onto geopolitical terrain where old-regime armies always outgunned them.

## Themes

**National mobilization as cascade and as fragmentation.** The unit opens with 'a chain of interlocking national mobilizations'; each movement's demands (Hungarian hegemony, Slovak language rights, Croatian unification) create the grievances of the next. Clark notes that Hungarian patriots refusing rights to minorities were 'no better or worse than any other European nationalist movement'. The multiplication of claims on the same ground is the engine of what his framework elsewhere calls the entropy of the revolutions.

**Composite pre-national polities and the making of national identity.** Croatia's 'incredibly messy, composite situation' of the era 'before modern nation-states' did not yield a 'single, homogeneous national identity'. Stojanović thought of himself as a frontiersman (Grenzer), not a Croat; parliament, museum, reading room, newspapers and dinners conducted only in Croatian taught him the nation. Clark describes this as 'both a deepening and a narrowing of vision'. This is close to [[benedict-anderson]]'s account of print and imagined community and to the constructivist strand in [[nationalism]] scholarship, though Clark makes the point through a single life rather than theory.

**The national experience as embodied, synaesthetic, and backdated.** Red hats, surkas, cockades, tricolours, music in the vernacular and a nobleman-soldier in whom 'the nation were now incarnated'. Clark says the magic lay in transforming consciousness 'while masking their novelty in a sense of continuity with a deep past'. The historical myths (Lombard League, Field of Blackbirds, Wars of Liberation) had been prepared in the histories, operas and poetry of the 1820s-40s; compare [[johann-gottfried-herder]] and [[romanticism-and-political-danger]], though Clark does not cite them here.

**Nation against history, primordialism against constructivism.** Ukrainian and Polish petitioners each claim the past. Ukrainians appeal to an imagined, survived nation against history as conquest; Poles are 'primordialists when it came to their own nation and constructivists when it came to the claims of others'. This is Clark's own wry formulation. Jordan's speech goes further: history itself, meaning armed conflict, is arbiter ('the right of the stronger'), and he presents this as democratic, not a betrayal of it.

**Institutions improvised under pressure and the gap between chamber and street.** Croatian patriots appoint their own ban ultra vires; the Posen National Committee takes town hall and improvises a provisional government and fiscal administration; the Frankfurt Assembly has an 'imperial government' but no army. Its acceptance of the Malmø armistice, the crowd at the Pfingstweide, the barricades and the lynching of Auerswald and Lichnowsky expose 'the tension between the street and the chamber'. Legitimacy without force is the recurring pattern.

**Internationalization and entropy.** Schleswig-Holstein becomes a European question through Russian, British and French reactions; Mazzinian solidarity 'was not yet dead' but its mechanisms were now 'primarily deployed within the framework of individual nations'. Blum's faction splits over the Polish question and his letter compares German fragmentation to 'a malignant tumour'. Nationalism split radicals from radicals, liberals from radicals, and peasants from the national cause.

**Nationalism as phantom and narcotic; state power as its eventual vehicle.** Clark quotes Metternich on 'fighting phantoms everywhere' and offers his own image of nationalism as heroin for the revolutions. His final argument (nationalism 'was most potent when it was yoked together with the machinery of state power') points forward to the post-1848 state. For World Machines this loosely rhymes with the [[modernity-machine]]'s coupling of legibility and state capacity, and with the way [[contested-uniformity-bayly]] treats uniformity claims, but Clark is making a narrower historical point and does not use a machine vocabulary. The Croatian and Ukrainian episodes also show new nations becoming usable by the imperial state, as when the Austrian administration used Ukrainians and Croats against Hungarians and Poles.

## Notable passages

**Palacký's letter (c1).** The refusal of the Frankfurt invitation is a compact case of a small-nation elite defending the multinational empire as Europe's protection against Russia. The claim that if Austria did not exist one would have to create it is Palacký's, not Clark's. It also shows a strategic logic of the small nations: national rights were to be pursued short of independence.

**Ukrainians, Poles and the nation that survived history (c2).** Clark's clearest analytic treatment of how national claims are argued: the appeal to a forgotten but living nation, and the Polish counter-petition denying that the Ukrainian nation exists.

**Jordan's 'healthy national egoism' (c3).** The German radical Wilhelm Jordan rejects sentimental solidarity with Poland; Clark declines to treat this as simple betrayal of the Mazzinian ideal, since Jordan claims to speak 'because I am a democrat'.

**The national experience and its effects (c4).** A general statement of nationalism's speed, contagion, reversal of centre and periphery, and inward turn of solidarity, with the Schleswig-Holstein songs as evidence.

**The phantom and the addict (c5).** Clark's assessment: elation, then fissure, with national difference trumping revolutionary solidarity and the geopolitical trap for revolutionaries. This is the unit's core argument.

**Stojanovic learns to be Croat (c6).** The individual case study of nation-building through parliament, museums and newspapers, ending with the forgetting of the Thirty Demands' solidarity with 'all other free peoples'.
