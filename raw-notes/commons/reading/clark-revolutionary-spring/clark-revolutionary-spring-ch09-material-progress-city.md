# Progress as Legitimacy: Economy, Statistics and the Remodelled City after 1848

This unit covers two sections of Clark's chapter on the post-revolutionary reinvention of the state. In the first, ministers of finance and development (Cavour, Fontes, Alonso Martínez) become the prestige figures of the new order, and economic policy replaces higher moral or ideological claims as the source of legitimacy for Napoleon III, the Spanish Bienio Progresista and the Portuguese Regeneração. Clark shows the belief that material progress would end confrontational politics, then the reversal in which political peace is prized for the sake of prosperity, and the growth of national statistics as a channel through which civil-society reformers were absorbed into government. In the second section, the same logic reshapes the capitals: Haussmann's Paris, Madrid's water system and avenues, Hinckeldey's Berlin and the Vienna Ringstraße. The Social Question's ways of seeing and mapping enter administrative practice, and Clark asks whether this is co-option or victory.

## Themes

**Legitimacy by material betterment.** Developmentalist arguments and circulatory metaphors were not new, but Clark says that what was distinctive after 1848 was their prominence in government propaganda. Napoleon III appealed to the material betterment of the French, not to a higher moral order, and Spanish ministers promised all things to all people. Clark treats the ministers and engineers as elevated to the status of gurus and technocratic saviours.

**From inoculation to inversion.** Clark distinguishes two relations between progress and peace. In the first, progress mutes revolution (the 'counter-revolutionary inoculation'). In the second, which he says increasingly predominated, political peace is valued for the sake of prosperity, and material progress becomes 'the ultimate public and political good'. He backs this with a French ministry's 1856 claim that no railway track was laid during the three years of turmoil. He is careful to add that Napoleon III's unfinished novel tells us about how the emperor hoped to be remembered, not about the objective character of the order.

**Statistics as a platform for co-option.** 'The economy' is imagined as an autonomous living entity, so consistent national data acquire heightened importance. The 1850s transition, in Clark's telling, is from a purely governmental to a civil-society-based statistical regime, with progressives and administrators merging in Spain (Junta General de Estadística, 1861) and France (Statistical Society of Paris, 1860). This resonates with [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and [[legibility-hardens-hierarchy-bayly]], where making populations measurable is the machinery of scale, though Clark stresses that the statisticians were often reformers and does not frame this as top-down imposition alone. It also fits [[modernity-machine]] themes of Progress as the telos, with the caveat that Clark is describing mid-century regimes, not a thousand-year infrastructure.

**The city as a system to be rationalized.** Paris, Berlin, Vienna and Madrid all saw barricades in 1848 (Madrid also in 1854) and all saw 1850s programmes of improvement. Clark points to a shared concern with circulation, a 'global conception of the city', slum clearance, water supply, building codes and control of spontaneous peripheral growth. The revolution reinforced the association of overcrowding, poor sanitation, disease and political upheaval, and shifted power from municipal elites to the state.

**Counter-revolution as modernizer.** Hinckeldey, hated as a policeman during the revolution, then 'dragged Berlin single-handedly into modernity' with fire service, soup kitchens, waterworks and a building code. Clark reads him as demonstrating 'the intimacy between counter-revolution and urban reform'. His death in a duel with a reactionary aristocrat, and the bourgeois public reaction, shows for Clark the gap between old-style reaction and the 'protean politics of the post-revolution'. This is close to the project's theme of reconstruction rather than restoration, though Clark offers it as an argument about 1850s states, not as a theory of order. The Vienna fortification debate shows that the lessons of 1848 could be read in opposite ways.

**Absorption of the Social Question.** Experts and hygiene committees carry ideas from progressive and left-leaning civil society into government. Hinckeldey's statistical tables, Melun's 1850 housing law and a Strasbourg commission report that 'could have been drawn from the pages of' Blanc, Guépin, Buret, Braß or Engels illustrate this. Clark offers three readings: co-option and immunization, victory of an idea, or both at once. He explicitly declines to choose between them.

## Notable passages

The passage on the belief that progress would end ideologized politics (c1) states the post-1848 wager, with Portuguese and Spanish officials quoted as saying so; the sentiments are theirs, and Clark uses them as evidence. The 'other way round' passage (c2) is Clark's own analytic claim about the inversion of priorities. The statistics passage (c3) shows the reformist-administrative merger. The Hinckeldey narrative (c4) is the sharpest case study of counter-revolutionary modernizing. The passages on the Social Question in administration (c5) and the co-option question (c6) are where Clark is most explicit about the ambiguity of the post-revolutionary state, and are useful for WMP's interest in how oppositional vocabularies become state vocabularies.
