# The June Days and the entropy of revolution

The unit has two parts. In "A revolution shuts itself down", Clark follows Paris from the improvised, disliked National Workshops through the invasion of the National Assembly on 15 May, the conservative-led decision to close the workshops, and the bloody suppression of the June insurrection under General Cavaignac. Its aftermath is refracted through Tocqueville, Hugo, Sand, Herzen and Marx. In "In the heat of the century", Clark steps back and borrows the vocabulary of Carnot and Clausius (heat, dissipation, entropy) and of Helmholtz's nerve science (latency, circulation) as a way of describing how the continental revolution lost cohesion. He hedges throughout: the science is an analogy, not a translation of politics, and the downward arc is not a mere loss of order.

## Themes

**Institutions improvised under pressure, then disowned.** The National Workshops were "rushed into existence", hated by employers, provincial taxpayers and the unemployed themselves, and disliked even by their director Émile Thomas. Clark stresses the dilemma voiced by Falloux: closing them was dangerous without economic revival, but economic confidence seemed impossible while they existed. Lamartine's railway-investment plan, which Clark says resembled the technocratic visions of the Saint-Simonians (compare [[henri-de-saint-simon]]), would have taken time. The hardliner Trélat prevailed, in a government "so divided that it was no longer capable of reining in its own members".

**Republicanism and the social question come apart.** Blanc's Ministry of Progress was shouted down and replaced by an 'investigation' into poverty. Clark reads this as the moment when "republicanism and the commitment to social causes were different and separable things". This is the fissure between liberals and radicals that runs through the book, seen here in its French, parliamentary form. The 15 May demonstrators, Clark argues, sought to "reclaim a piece of revolutionary sovereignty" and to co-determine policy on Poland. The action was chaotic and poorly prepared, but fear of the left ("communism") was itself a political force.

**Colonial violence returns home.** Cavaignac's career in Algeria (razzias, enfumades) and the perception of the June repression as an application of 'Algerian' methods show the metropole and empire linked by tools and imagery. Clark hedges that the techniques did not formally resemble those used in North Africa. Algeria then became a destination for deported insurgents, a link to the book's global dimension and to the WMP interest in how states make populations legible or illegible (compare [[legibility-hardens-hierarchy-bayly]]).

**The inarticulacy of the revolt and the reaction of intellectuals.** The insurgents lacked leaders and doctrine beyond 'Liberty or death!'. Tocqueville, Hugo and Sand, each in a different way, were troubled by the possibility that the rebels' belief that society was founded on injustice might be true. Tocqueville's refusal to help Herzen ([[alexander-herzen]]) shows the gap between political theory and practice. Sand: "I do not believe in the existence of a republic that starts out by killing its proletarians."

**Marx, and its limits.** Clark presents Marx's June 1848 reading (the moderate republicans "murdered the very revolution it claimed to be defending") as a revelation about force behind the myth of February. He also reports the historians' objection that the two sides of the barricades were recruited from the same social strata, and that Sand's phrase, a conflict between two factions of the people, is closer. Marx's deeper point, in Clark's defence, is that the potency of the myth rested on "the threat of naked violence".

**Entropy as metaphor, with hedges.** Clark warns that it "would be vulgar" to treat thermodynamics as a mirror of politics, yet notes overlaps (scientists in the Provisional Government, Bem the engineer, a post-1848 appetite for 'scientific' politics). The circulatory metaphor, as against the nervous/electrical one, makes room for blockages and unevenness. The revolutions began at different times and speeds, and this asynchrony produced blockages and counter-currents: Wallachia's envoy found a conservative Paris, Frankfurt finished its constitution as sympathy ebbed, Rome was crushed by Bonaparte's France. Connection and disconnection could intertwine (the Romanian students).

**Not a simple loss of order.** The closing paragraph is the unit's caution: waning cohesion was not only "an entropy intrinsic to the process", but the result of "new and competing forms of order-making" (liberalism, socialism, nationalism and others) whose futures were both brilliant and terrible.

## World Machines resonances (hedged)

The unit fits the WMP interest in how an order breaks down while another assembles: the moderate republic dissolves its own emergency institutions, and Clark's entropy image is paired explicitly with competing order-making, which is closer to a friction between machines than to a decay into nothing. The argument that connection across a continent depends on synchrony and on the state of news and relays echoes the WMP concern with contagion and desynchrony, though Clark treats it as an image and not a model. Elements of the [[gramsci-gap]] (an interregnum in which the old legitimacy is dying) can be glimpsed in the government that cannot rein in its ministers, but Clark does not frame it so, and here the interregnum is short and ends in a violent settlement. The shift toward 'scientific' politics gestures at legibility ([[legibility-as-machine-core]]), again only as a trend Clark says he will discuss later. The nationalism thread ([[nationalism]]) appears in the Polish cause that provoked the 15 May demonstration.

## Notable passages

**The workshops dilemma (c1).** Falloux's two-sided trap, and Lamartine's Saint-Simonian railway investment plan, show a government unable to break out of a self-reinforcing crisis of confidence.

**15 May as the reclaiming of sovereignty (c2).** Clark reads the invasion of the chamber as a demand for co-determination, rather than as a coup attempt, while noting the divisions among the leaders.

**Cavaignac and Algeria (c3).** The description of the June counter-insurgency, the colonial imagery moving in both directions, and the deportations.

**Marx in defence (c4).** Clark's careful reconstruction of what Marx saw: the end of the February dream of a united front under universal suffrage, and the exposure of force behind order.

**Heat, engines and entropy (c5).** Clark introduces the thermodynamic frame, from Carnot to Clausius's naming of entropy in 1865.

**Asynchrony and connection (c6).** The specifics of how revolutionary impulses failed to converge, with the Romanian students as an example of ambiguity in connection.

**Order-making, not just disorder (c7).** The closing hedge against a purely entropic reading of the revolution's downward arc.
