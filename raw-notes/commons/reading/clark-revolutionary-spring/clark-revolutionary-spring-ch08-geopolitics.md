# Geopolitics: the international order inside the revolutions

Clark's chapter section opens by challenging the picture of revolution as civil tumult inside states, with transnational contagion as the only external dimension. His claim is that relations between states shaped the revolutions' inception, evolution and consequences, and that the revolutions in turn reshaped European geopolitical thinking. He works this through a sequence of cases: Switzerland and the Sonderbund (where Austrian non-intervention mattered as much as later interventions), the Prussian railway and debt impasse that forced the United Diet of 1847, consuls and warships in Sicily, the papal question and the French expedition against the Roman Republic, the Schleswig-Holstein and German-union questions, the Danubian Principalities squeezed between Russia and the Ottomans, and the Hungarian war ending in Russian intervention at Világos. It closes with the great-power bargains (Nicholas I, Palmerston, Louis Napoleon) that made that outcome possible and the Anglo-Russian friction that pointed towards the Crimean War.

## Themes

**Constitutions woven into the international order.** The Vienna settlement appended the German Confederation's constitution and the Swiss Federal Treaty to a treaty among states, so domestic challenges could be framed as threats to 'European order' justifying intervention. Clark says the domestic/international distinction 'made very little sense' in this Europe. He also carries a hedge: 'European order' was an ideological abstraction, usually invoked by states pursuing their own interests. This rhymes with the [[world-machines]] idea of deep infrastructure that fuses institutions across scales, though Clark speaks only of a diplomatic settlement, not a machine.

**Interventions and non-interventions as causal.** Non-intervention in Switzerland and Austrian confidence of support shaped the opening phase; the counter-revolutionary interventions of 1849 (French at Rome, Russian in Hungary, Russian-Ottoman in Wallachia) shaped closure. Clark treats these as structural, not incidental.

**Revolutionaries thought geopolitically.** Crowds in Rome turned Viva Pio Nono into an Italian-national, anti-Austrian slogan; French radicals' frustration over Poland drove 15 May. Nationalism and street politics fed into inter-state escalation (compare [[nationalism]]).

**Contradictory restoration at Rome.** French policy claimed to protect liberal institutions while restoring a counter-revolutionary pope; the assembly turned against it too late. The papal regime that followed, in Clark's words, was not a pragmatic settlement like Piedmont or Prussia but a literal unmaking, including the reversal of relaxations on the Jews. This is a case where counter-revolution did not modernize; Clark elsewhere stresses modernizing counter-revolutions, and the contrast is worth noting for the [[history-machine]] question of restoration versus reconstruction.

**Empires classify who counts as a state.** The Tsar's St Petersburg Manifesto distinguishes 'great states' from provinces 'pure and simple', denying the Romanian revolutionaries standing. This is a nice case of an imperial power fixing what is legible as a polity (resonating with [[legibility-as-machine-core]], though the source is about diplomatic recognition, not administrative measurement). The Ottomans' initial accommodation, then reversal under Russian pressure, and Russian control of the Danube trade and tariffs show material interests (grain, dredging) under the ideology.

**Hungary: nested civil wars and improvised state capacity.** The Hungarian war was a cluster of mutually embedded civil wars, ethnic and social conflicts amplified by Habsburg divide-and-rule; yet Kossuth's government built a 100,000-strong Honvéd from almost nothing. Clark distinguishes Görgey's view of revolution as an event to safeguard from Kossuth's as an open-ended process (an argument he says recurs elsewhere). Defeat came from ethnic dissension, leadership dissension, and above all Russian intervention.

## Notable passages

The opening thesis (c1) sets out the three-way relation between geopolitics and revolution. The Vienna passage (c2) supplies the structural mechanism. The papal restoration (c3) shows intervention undoing a revolution, with Jews as an explicit casualty. The St Petersburg Manifesto (c4) shows imperial logic in the Principalities. The Görgey passage (c5) gives Clark's event-versus-process distinction. The closing bargains passage (c6) shows the interlocking cost-benefit calculations of the powers and the cosmopolitan character of the Hungarian cause.

Attribution note: the manifesto, the Franz Joseph appeal and Frederick William's letter are the actors' own words; the analysis of causes is Clark's.
