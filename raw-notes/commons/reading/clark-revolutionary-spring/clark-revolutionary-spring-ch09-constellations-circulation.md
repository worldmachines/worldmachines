# The post-1848 settlement: new political constellations and the age of circulation

This unit, the opening of Clark's account of what came after 1848, does two things. First ("New Constellations") it surveys Europe from the Netherlands, Denmark, Piedmont and Prussia to Austria, France, Saxony, Iberia and Wallachia and argues that, despite harsh repression, the political life of the 1850s was not a restoration. Constitutions, parliaments and a new centrist rapprochement between flexible conservatives and moderate liberals reshaped the field, squeezing out both the democratic left and the old ultra-conservative right. Second ("The Age of Circulation") it shows the same governments pivoting from revenue-oriented to growth-oriented policy: free-trade treaties, joint-stock deregulation, state-financed railways, telegraphs and ports, uniform administrative forms, and the concept of a governmental "programme". Louis Napoleon, a self-styled "testamentary executor" of revolution and a "Saint-Simon on horseback", is the emblematic figure, but Clark insists the pattern is pan-European. The closing paragraphs make the causal claim explicit: these policies were "a direct consequence of the revolutions".

## Themes

**Repression without restoration.** Clark opens with exile, press restrictions, expanded police and surveillance "bigger and better organized than they had been in the age of Metternich", then pivots: "Yet there was no return to the pre-revolutionary status quo ante." Even the extreme cases (Austria's rescinded constitution, the Bonapartist constitution of 1852, Saxony's coup) carried forward elements of 1848 such as peasant emancipation, municipal autonomy and technocratic reform. One quoted historian calls the Austrian decade "not restorative at all, but innovative, on a broad front". This is a strong version of counter-revolution as reconstruction; the wording and the historian's judgement are Clark's and his sources', not a machine-theoretic claim.

**The centre as the new attractor.** Across Piedmont (the connubio of Cavour and Rattazzi), Prussia, the Netherlands, Saxony (Beust), Spain (Unión Liberal), Portugal (Regeneração and rotativismo) and France, a composite centre formed from the moderate ends of old progressives and the entrepreneurial ends of old conservative elites. Its effectiveness at "controlling the middle-ground" marginalized the democratic left and the old aristocratic right, which had lost the pact that once tied the state to landed estates. This is the aftermath of the liberal-radical fissuring that Clark treats elsewhere as the "entropy" of revolution.

**Culture war as conflictual stability.** In Piedmont and the Netherlands, clerical-anticlerical feuding replaced left-right antipathy with a cleavage that could be bitter yet not destabilizing. Clark's phrase "conflictual stability" is worth carrying: a system absorbs conflict by displacing it onto a channel it can survive.

**Circulation and the interventionist state.** Post-revolutionary governments prioritized growth via deregulation and state-financed infrastructure (Portugal's public works ministry, Spain's telegraph network and extraordinary budget, Cavour's railways, the French railway boom and the Crédit Mobilier). Spanish officials describe roads and railways as "the routes of circulation". Clark also notes non-state drivers, such as the gold rushes feeding cheap credit, but says these were "channelled and focused by state-sponsored institutional change". Public works served as patronage and integration: a railway terminus for a plebiscite vote, works to manage subject nationalities.

**Saint-Simonian technocracy and Louis Napoleon.** Louis Napoleon's *Les Idées Napoléoniennes* casts government as the motor of progress and revolution as something to be absorbed into a "general fusion"; Clark links this to Saint-Simon's industrialism through the Pereires and Michel Chevalier. Clark hedges that it is "unclear whether the emperor himself had ever read the sage's works", and he stresses that similar reorientations happened elsewhere. Resonates with [[henri-de-saint-simon]] and, more loosely, with [[caesarism-gay]] and [[manuel-prophets-of-paris]], though Clark treats the Second Empire as one instance of a wider pattern rather than as a Caesarist type.

**State distinct from society; standardization and programme.** Uniform forms, unified railway networks ("The state can and must see its state railways as a whole"), crime statistics and the diffusion of the political "programme" indicate a state that conceptualizes itself as distinct from society and seeks transparency and prior commitment. Clark cautions that talk of "policy" was sometimes "a mask for pragmatic muddling through".

**Causation runs through 1848.** New parliaments removed old fiscal constraints (Prussia's State Indebtedness Law), and new income taxes drew legitimacy from new suffrage. The 1850s order is thus presented as unavailable without the revolution.

## World Machines resonances (hedged)

The unit is a close historical instance of the reassembly of an order after a breakdown: institutions improvised under pressure (constitutions, coalitions, ministries of public works) rebuilt legitimacy in a new form rather than restoring the old. It fits the idea of a [[modernity-machine]] consolidating around legibility, since uniform forms, statistics, national markets and telegraph grids make territory and population more readable to the state; see [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and [[legibility-hardens-hierarchy-bayly]]. The interregnum-then-synthesis shape loosely echoes the [[gramsci-gap]], though Clark describes a fairly quick consolidation, not morbid symptoms, and the 1850s here are a consolidation phase, not a machine transition. Bayly's parallel account of state-building and uniformity is relevant in [[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world]] and [[contested-uniformity-bayly]]. For the broader frame see [[world-machines]] and [[history-machine]]. Clark is a historian and makes none of these claims himself.

## Notable passages

The passage on **no return to the status quo ante** (c1) states the thesis and the constitutional evidence. The **Piedmont and Netherlands culture-war passage** (c2) gives the mechanism of conflictual stability and the Cavour-Rattazzi connubio. The **synthesis paragraph** (c3) states the centrist consensus and the marginalization of left and old right. The **Louis Napoleon passage** (c4) gives the ideological account of progress mediated by government and revolution absorbed into fusion. The **planning and standardization passage** (c5) shows the turn to unified networks, forms and nationalized railways. The **closing causal passage** (c6) argues that programme, transparency and fiscal capacity all depended on the revolutions having displaced conservative opposition.
