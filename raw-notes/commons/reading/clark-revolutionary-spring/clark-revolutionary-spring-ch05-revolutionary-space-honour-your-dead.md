# Consolidating a revolution: streets, cafés and funerals

The opening of Chapter 5 lists the parallel tasks facing the victors of the spring days of 1848: securing public space, retrospectively sanctioning rebellion as the inauguration of a new order, forming provisional governments, policing the streets, convening elected chambers and drafting constitutions. Clark then takes two of these in depth. "Revolutionary Space" treats the streets, squares and cafés of the revolutionized city as the medium in which the revolution was lived (and, by way of a comparison with Tahrir Square in 2011, as what counter-revolutionary authorities most wanted to erase). "Honour Your Dead" follows the funerals of the fallen in Paris (4 March), Milan, Berlin (19 and 22 March) and Vienna (17 March), reading them as rituals that turned insurgents into martyrs of a state that did not yet exist, and as occasions where the differences between liberals, democrats, clergy, monarchy and the poor first showed.

## Themes

**Space as the medium of revolution.** Crowds, flags, illuminations, singing and the sudden absence of troops made the city feel "turned inside out": the propertied watched from private interiors while those with least to lose commanded the streets. Tocqueville's horror at a great city "in the sole hands of those who owned nothing" is his, not Clark's, but Clark builds on it. Censorship's lapse put newspapers, pamphlets and hawkers (many of them women) into the streets, so that information, and the sense of simultaneity, was itself spatial.

**Cafés as synapses; differentiation versus de-differentiation.** Cafés were where news was read aloud, press campaigns coordinated and workers, barred by law from fraternities, organized. Clark argues that this composite public-commercial space did a twofold job: cafés sifted people into milieus and factions (Caffè Europa versus Caffè Buono in Naples), while streets and squares temporarily collapsed social distance. Bystanders ("stickybeaks and simpletons", in one participant's phrase) were what turned a local protest into a mass movement. Bourbon police lists of café "conspiratorial comportment" show the state understood the same geography.

**Renaming and erasure.** New authorities renamed streets and squares as memorial sites within weeks (Pest, Vienna, Milan); after the revolutions these were reversed and commemoration policed, most aggressively in Haussmann's Paris. Clark closes the section with Sisi's Cairo, a modern parallel he offers as illustration, not as an identity of processes.

**Funerals as the manufacture of legitimacy.** The Paris ceremony of 4 March reused the 1840 route to the July Column but with the whole Provisional Government visible, replacing royal monopoly on honouring service to the state with republican sovereignty. Clark's central move is the paradox that the dead were declared to have served a state that had not yet existed, so that in legitimizing insurgents the government legitimized itself. The secretary-general's plea that grieving families yield to the Republic shows the state appropriating private grief.

**Same rite, different politics.** Milan's funeral consecrated liberation from foreign rule; Berlin's improvised presentation of corpses to the king, then the hymn that defused the crowd, showed people still "imbued with the mental habits of monarchy", while the king quickly recovered his resolve. Vienna's ceremony was smaller, student-led, and unusually interfaith, with Rabbi Mannheimer separating the justice of the cause from its victory. Clark attributes the interpretations of Boerner, the anonymous middle-class observer and the clergy to them, and adds his own contrast with France.

**Fault lines in the ritual of unanimity.** Berlin planners dropped the idea of burying soldiers with civilians under military objection; Sydow framed the violence as catharsis while Georg Jung insisted the living owed the dead continued struggle; in Vienna the missing coffins of suburban poor exposed a class divide. Clark notes a "self-historicizing temperament": barricade fighters re-enacting their own fight, and a book on the Milan insurrection reviewed within a week.

## Resonances with World Machines (hedged)

The unit shows a political order being reassembled under pressure through improvised institutions and ritual, which rhymes with the interregnum described by [[gramsci-gap]], though Clark's interregnum is weeks long and about a regime, not a thousand-year machine. The capacity of authorities to name, list and police (Bourbon lists, Berlin police at Friedrichshain, Haussmann's remodelling) connects loosely to [[legibility-as-machine-core]], and the post-1848 erasure of revolutionary streets echoes the pattern in [[legibility-hardens-hierarchy-bayly]]. The mass-and-crowd language (streets as fluid, bystanders swelling protest) touches [[mass-man]] and [[the-mass-as-fiction]], but Clark's crowds are made of identifiable trades and guilds in ranks, which cuts against the flattened mass. The role of newspapers and cafés in synchronizing action across cities invites comparison with [[benedict-anderson]] on print publics, though Anderson's account is about imagined communities, not street-level sociability. Clark makes none of these connections himself.

## Notable passages

The passage on the inside-out city (c1) and the twofold function of public-commercial space (c2) carry Clark's spatial argument. The Schlögel-to-Haussmann-to-Cairo passage (c3) gives the counter-revolutionary corollary: spaces of memory are contested after the fact. The "alchemical transformation" passage (c4) is the clearest statement of how funerals legitimize a regime. The Berlin passage (c5) compares French and Prussian revolutionary behaviour towards the king, and the Jung speech (c6) states the alternative to reconciliation that would split the revolution. The Berlin re-enactment passage (c7) records the self-historicizing awareness of the actors themselves.
