# The Miller's Tale

After the Knight's noble romance, the Host calls on the Monk to answer it in kind — but the drunk Miller, Robin, shoulders past the queue, swears "by armes, and by blood, and bones" that he has a tale to "quite the Knighte's tale," and, over the Reeve's protest that it will "bringe wives in evil name," tells his fabliau anyway. The tale: in Oxford, the carpenter John, old and jealously married to eighteen-year-old Alison, lodges the astrology student Hendy Nicholas, who seduces Alison by grabbing her "by the queint" and swearing he will die of love. Meanwhile the fastidious parish clerk Absolon courts Alison hopelessly with gifts, songs, and grooming. To get John out of the house for a night, Nicholas fakes a prophetic vision — via his Almagest, astrolabe, and augrim stones — of a second Noah's Flood, convincing John to rig three kneading-tubs from the rafters so all three can "float" through the deluge. While John sleeps in his tub, Nicholas and Alison go to bed together; Absolon, arriving to woo Alison at her window in the dark, is tricked into kissing her bare backside, and returns with a red-hot coulter for revenge — branding Nicholas instead, whose cry of "Water!" wakes John, who cuts the rope, crashes to the floor, breaks his arm, and is mocked by the whole town as mad. The tale closes briskly: the wife swived, the clerk scalded, the carpenter shamed.

## Themes

**Carnivalesque inversion of the social order.** The Host has staged a hierarchy of tellers (Knight, then presumably Monk), and the Miller's drunken insistence on going next — "churl" though he is — enacts exactly the kind of low-answers-high structure the *Tales* as a whole depends on: a noble romance is immediately "quited" by a bawdy fabliau, gentility undercut by appetite and ale. The narrator's apologia that follows (turn the page if you don't want "harlotry," "Blame not me, if that ye choose amiss") is Chaucer's own device for licensing the tale's low comedy without fully endorsing it — a deliberate polyphony of registers rather than a single authoritative voice.

**Astrology and instruments of false legibility.** Nicholas's chamber is furnished with the era's apparatus of prediction — Almagest, astrolabe, augrim stones "laid fair apart" — real technologies for reading the heavens and reckoning number. But the tale turns this legibility apparatus into a tool of pure fraud: Nicholas doesn't predict anything, he fabricates a flood out of nothing to manipulate a credulous man. The instruments of measurement lend an unearned authority to what is simply theater and appetite — a small, comic version of the recurring worry that apparatus built to read the world can just as easily be weaponized to control it. Faint resonance with [[legibility-as-machine-core]], though the tale plays it for laughter rather than critique.

**"Godde's privity" as a refrain against inquiry.** Twice the tale insists a man "should not be inquisitive / Of Godde's privity" — first as John's fatalistic acceptance of his likely cuckoldry, later as Nicholas's cover story for withholding the "reasons" behind the flood scheme from the servants. The refrain is doubly ironic: it is precisely John's incuriosity and deference to authority (astrological and religious) that make him exploitable, while the audience is invited to be curious about exactly the domestic "privity" the proverb forbids.

**Bodily comeuppance as poetic justice.** The tale's climax works entirely through the body: Absolon's fastidious, perfumed courtship is punished by an degrading kiss; his outraged vanity is answered not with words but a branding iron; Nicholas's practical joke earns him a literal burn; John's credulous piety earns him a public reputation as a madman. Nothing is resolved through argument or repentance — only through slapstick, physical retribution that closes the tale in a single settling line: "the man was wood."

## Notable passages

The Prologue's brawl over precedence ("The Miller that fordrunken was all pale... With which I will now quite the Knighte's tale") stages the genre collision explicit from the outset. The narrator's disclaimer ("What should I more say, but that this Millere...And eke men should not make earnest of game") is Chaucer stepping outside the fiction to manage the reader's expectations and moral distance. Nicholas's chamber inventory ("His Almagest...Full often blessed was his merry throat") establishes the clerk as a figure of learned, seductive artifice before he ever speaks to Alison. The seduction itself ("And privily he caught her by the queint...That she her love him granted at the last") is blunt and fast, contrasting sharply with Absolon's mannered courtship later. The flood prophecy ("Now, John, quoth Nicholas, I will not lie...For sorrow of this he fell almost adown") is the hinge of the whole plot, converting astrological jargon into a private hoax. And the climactic window scene ("Dark was the night as pitch or as the coal...And Absolon went forth at sorry pace") delivers the tale's most quoted comic reversal, from which the coulter revenge and the collapse of the ruse follow directly.
