# The Court of Love

This unit opens with an editorial headnote (dating the poem, on internal evidence, to Chaucer's supposed eighteenth year at Cambridge c. 1346, and situating it against the French institution of literal, judicial Courts of Love) followed by a transcriber's note flagging that modern scholarship doubts Chaucer wrote it at all. The poem itself follows, narrated by "Philogenet," a clerk who is summoned by Mercury on pain of death to the Court of Love at Cithera, where Admetus and Alcestis reign as King and Queen. Chidden for his tardiness, he swears the Twenty Statutes of Love, prays to Venus for a lady he has only seen in a dream, and is instead introduced by the chamberwoman Philobone to Rosial, whose initial disdain melts (via a well-timed fainting fit) into promised favor. A tour of the Court's allegorical population — Despair, Hope, Liar, Flattery, Envy, Shamefastness, Avaunter, Privy Thought — is interrupted by a textual hiatus, after which Rosial confesses her love outright and bids Philogenet abide with her until May Day, when the poem closes on a choir of birds singing a profane paraphrase of the Trinity Sunday Matins to the God of Love.

## Themes

**Love administered as law.** The Court is not a metaphor for feeling but a functioning bureaucracy: an officer named Rigour, "incorruptible by partiality, favour, prayer, or gold," administers a sworn oath binding every new arrival "till death" to twenty enumerated statutes — fidelity, secrecy, patience, self-abasement before the beloved's superior nobility, even a statute prescribing the frequency of nocturnal attentions (which Philogenet, tellingly, immediately petitions to have relaxed). Devotion is thus rendered legible and enforceable, a code administered from a sovereign seat rather than negotiated feeling — the poem's own headnote insists this is "a regal, not a legal, Court," the wellspring from which lesser tribunals draw their statutes, which is itself a claim about infrastructural precedence.

**Asymmetric and forbidden knowledge.** When Philogenet turns to the statutes governing women, Rigour forbids him outright: those statutes are sealed, known only to "God and fiend," and the poem justifies the prohibition by citing Solomon and Samson as men undone by intimate knowledge of women. The Court's legibility is thus deliberately incomplete and gendered — one sex is bound by a public, readable code; the other operates by a law kept permanently opaque to the men subject to its effects.

**Imposed vows against natural desire.** The tour of the Court's mourners includes monks, friars, and nuns who lament having been forced into religious vows "in tender youth and green" by their families, against a nature that gave them "instrument in store, / And appetite to love and lovers be." This is the poem's sharpest note of protest: institutional discipline (monastic chastity) versus an appetite the poem treats as simply natural and rightfully sovereign — a fairly direct staging of desire as something that persists and presses against externally imposed constraint, in the spirit of what WMP calls [[Conatus]].

**Allegory as psychological and social apparatus.** Rather than describing courtship as private interiority, the poem populates the Court with walking personifications — Despair and Hope arguing over Philogenet's chances, Liar and Flattery conspiring in a corner, Envy fretting over any lover's success, Shamefastness silencing the timid, Avaunter boasting of conquests across a dozen countries, Privy Thought explaining that he can be everywhere his lady is at once. These figures externalize what would elsewhere be interior psychology into a cast of courtly functionaries, making the emotional machinery of love-service into something almost institutional in its own right.

**Sacred form repurposed for profane content.** The closing May Day service has each bird sing an actual verse of the Latin liturgy for Trinity Sunday — "Domine," "Venite," "Coeli enarrant," "Te Deum amoris" — redirected wholesale from God to the God of Love. The form of ecclesiastical devotion survives the transplant of its object entirely intact, which is itself a small case study in a decayed or repurposed canonical form still doing structural work after its content has been swapped out.

## Notable passages

The castle's description ("So them apace I journey'd forth among... / All was as bright as stars in winter be'n") establishes the Court as a rival built order to any earthly one, gold-plated and jewel-hidden — grandeur as institutional legitimation before a single statute is read. The recitation of the first three statutes ("The first statute that on the book was spread... / All this was whole the Kinge's ordinance") shows love rendered as enumerated, sworn law rather than felt sentiment. Rigour's refusal to let Philogenet read the statutes of women ("The officer, called Rigour... / There may no man statute of women know") stages the Court's built-in epistemic asymmetry, backed by exemplary appeal to Solomon and Samson. The monks' and nuns' lament ("Well may we curse our abbeys and our place... / Fortune, we curse thy wheel of variance!") is the poem's most plainly aggrieved passage, desire protesting an imposed vow it never chose. And the bird Matins ("On May Day, when the lark began to rise... / And then the wren began to skip and dance") closes the poem by making the liturgy itself sing Cupid's praise, form outliving — and now serving — a wholly different content.
