# Purves's Preface: Popularizing Chaucer and Spenser for the General Reader

This unit is not a story but the front matter of a Victorian popular edition: a table of contents for the combined Chaucer/Spenser volume, a transcriber's note explaining the e-text's editorial conventions (glosses, footnote numbering, spelling normalization), and the substantive item — D. Laing Purves's own PREFACE, signed and dated to his edition. Purves lays out, and defends, the editorial choices behind presenting Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales* and Spenser's *Faerie Queene* — "our two early poetic masterpieces" — to a general nineteenth-century audience "in a time of little leisure and unbounded temptations to intellectual languor."

## Themes

**Chaucer and Spenser as a single poetic lineage.** Purves justifies pairing the two poets by casting Spenser as Chaucer's "direct and really the immediate successor," the inheritor of a poetic mantle across two "eventful" but poetically barren centuries. The image of Chaucer as the "Well of English undefiled" and Spenser as the river fed by that well frames English poetry as a continuous, legible tradition rather than a series of discrete national ruptures — a historiographic move that treats literary history as inheritance and infrastructure, not just chronology.

**Legibility as editorial mission.** The preface's real argument is about *making archaic texts readable at scale*. Purves is explicit that his goal is not translation or modernization of spirit, only of "garb": preserving Chaucer's words and, wherever meter, rhyme, or meaning require it, his old forms — but discarding "the spelling of the monkish transcribers" everywhere else in favor of contemporary orthography. This is a textbook case of a text being re-engineered for legibility: stripped of the "screen of dust and cobwebs" that archaic spelling has become, so that a mass nineteenth-century readership can access it without the friction of specialist philological training. The transcriber's note appended above the preface shows a second, later layer of the same process — footnotes relocated to margins, etymological notes numbered and pushed to endnotes, Latin translated — each pass further compressing and reorganizing the original apparatus for readability. Together these two editorial layers (Purves's 1880s modernization, then a modern e-text transcriber's further simplification) trace something like a `[[decay-sequence]]` of canonical apparatus: the original manuscript glosses become footnotes, then marginal notes, then bracketed inline notes, each transformation trading fidelity for accessibility — a small, concrete instance of the `[[legibility-as-machine-core]]` logic by which infrastructure is repeatedly re-cut to serve wider, less specialized audiences.

**Abridgement as editorial violence, defended.** Purves is unusually candid that his edition cuts and compresses: thirty pages of Melibœus and the Parson's prose sermon are dropped in favor of poetry, and the *Faerie Queene* — measured against the combined length of the *Iliad*, *Odyssey*, and *Aeneid* — is compressed to two-thirds its bulk via prose-outline summary. He treats sheer length itself as a "formidable barrier to popularity," treating the classical epics as a common yardstick for scale.

**Rehabilitating Chaucer's reputation.** A significant portion of the preface is spent arguing against the "lingering tradition or prejudice" that Chaucer was merely a "coarse buffoon" who pandered to "a base and licentious appetite." Purves counters with a portrait of Chaucer as courtier, scholar, and satirist whose *Canterbury Tales* subordinate the poet's own persona entirely to his subject: "we see, not Chaucer, but Chaucer's times and neighbours."

**Editorial humility as rhetorical closing move.** The preface ends by owning the scale and fallibility of the labor — "12,000 or 13,000 notes" — asking readers to weigh inevitable errors against the "conscientious labour bestowed on the book." This closing gesture is itself part of the legitimation work of a popularizing, legibility-producing edition: acknowledging that totalizing, standardizing an archaic text for mass consumption is necessarily imperfect and asking for reader forbearance in exchange.

## Notable passages

The Spenser-as-successor passage establishes the volume's organizing premise. The modernizing-spelling passage is the preface's methodological core, and its most direct point of contact with `[[modernity-machine]]`-style legibility projects: an editor calibrating exactly how much of an archaic form to strip away without destroying what makes the text valuable. The Faerie Queene bulk comparison quantifies the scale problem abridgement is meant to solve. The defense-of-Chaucer's-character passage shows the preface's polemical stake — correcting received reputation, not just presenting text. The closing admission of fallibility across thousands of notes closes the preface on the same self-aware, laborious note it opens on.
