# The Sompnour's Tale: A Friar's Comeuppance

Stung by the Friar's tale about a corrupt summoner, the Sompnour retaliates with a prologue vision of hell as a friar's nest under Satan's tail, then tells a fabliau set in Holderness: a begging friar, having preached up donations for masses ("trentals") and kept his companion busy recording (and secretly erasing) the names of donors, arrives at the house of the bedridden Thomas. Through flattery of Thomas's wife, a long, self-serving sermon against ire (wrath) stuffed with classical exempla, and finally outright extortion for his convent's building fund, the friar wears out Thomas's patience — until Thomas, asked to grope for a hidden "gift" beneath him, delivers a spectacular fart instead. The enraged friar complains to the local lord, whose squire Jankin then "resolves" the philosophical problem of dividing the fart equally among the friar's twelve brethren via an elaborate cart-wheel demonstration, to universal applause.

## Themes

**Institutional extraction dressed as piety.** The tale is relentless anti-fraternal satire: the friar's practices are shown as a machinery of extraction wearing the mask of spiritual service. His companion's ivory writing-tablets, on which donors' names are inscribed only to be "planed away" the moment the friars are out the door, are a small but devastating detail — a mock ledger, legibility performed for the donor's benefit and immediately voided once its social function (encouraging further giving) is served. This is worth flagging for [[legibility-as-machine-core]]: a system of record-keeping that exists only to produce the *appearance* of accountability, hollowed of any actual bookkeeping function — a miniature instance of the [[decay-sequence]], where a Functional practice (real intercession, real record) survives only as Performative theater.

**Wrath preached, then embodied.** The friar delivers a lengthy homily against ire — citing an irous judge, a drunken Cambyses who kills his own courtier's son to "prove" wine hasn't dulled his aim, and Cyrus draining a river out of pique — all while extracting money from a dying man under threat of losing the church's favor. The dramatic irony is the tale's engine: the moment Thomas denies him, the friar himself erupts in the very rage he has just spent a hundred lines condemning, storming off "as doth a wood lioun." The sermon-then-reversal structure is the tale's central joke, and its material: pastoral rhetoric revealed as instrumental, not sincere.

**The fart as leveling device and the parody of scholastic proof.** The tale's climax is not the insult itself but its aftermath: a lord's squire earnestly applies the apparatus of scholastic demonstration — geometry, acoustics, precedence of honor — to the question of dividing a fart's sound and smell equally among thirteen friars via a cart-wheel's twelve spokes. The lord and company judge this reasoning "as well as Euclid, or as Ptolemy," which is the tale's real gut-punch: intellectual authority is shown happily validating an absurd proposition dressed in the right form, just as the friar's audiences accept spiritual claims dressed in pious language. It is a comedy that ultimately targets *form* mistaken for substance — the shitty-castle logic of legitimacy surviving on style long after its content is gone.

**Class inversion.** The "churl" Thomas, tormented and financially bled by a supposed spiritual superior, gets the last word through his body rather than his rank; the squire's cleverness — not the friar's learning — wins the lord's favor and a new gown. The tale sides, in its rough way, with the exploited layman against clerical extraction.

## Notable passages

The prologue's vision of friars swarming from the devil's tail ("Right so as bees swarmen out of a hive... He clapt his tail again, and lay full still") sets the tone of gleeful anti-fraternal contempt before the tale proper even begins. Within the tale, the account of the friar's companion recording and then erasing donors' names ("His fellow had a staff tipped with horn... That he before had written in his tables") exposes the mechanics of manufactured trust. The Cambyses exemplum ("Irous Cambyses was eke dronkelew... His son was slain, there is no more to say") is the friar's own homiletic showpiece against wrath, made ironic by his subsequent explosion of temper. The fart itself ("Ah, thought this friar, that shall go with me... That might have let a fart of such a soun'") is the tale's structural hinge, converting the friar's greed into physical humiliation. Finally, Jankin's cart-wheel demonstration ("My lord, quoth he, when that the weather is fair... He beareth him so fair and holily") delivers the tale's real satirical payload: reasoned form applied to a preposterous end, applauded as if it were genuine philosophy.
