# The Squire's Tale: Marvels of Sarra and the Falcon's Lament

The Host's prologue — a comic aside on his own shrewish, gossiping wife, delivered as a segue from the Merchant's tale — hands the floor to the Squire, who narrates the (famously incomplete) romance of Cambuscan, Tartar king of Sarra. Part I stages a birthday feast interrupted by a strange armed knight riding a horse of brass, bearing gifts from the King of Arabia and India: the horse itself (which can carry a rider anywhere on earth in a day and vanish or return at the twist of a pin), a mirror that reveals hidden danger and any lover's treachery, a ring for Cambuscan's daughter Canace that lets her understand the speech of birds and the medicinal virtue of every herb, and a sword that both wounds and — reversed — heals. The court's astonished, argumentative reception of these objects closes Part I. Part II drops the automata plot entirely: at dawn Canace, walking in the park, finds a peregrine falcon tearing at herself in grief atop a withered tree; using her ring she draws out the falcon's confession — a hypocritical tercelet wooed her for years, won her trust, then abandoned her for a kite, in an extended set-piece on love's counterfeiting. The tale breaks off as the Squire promises future adventures (Cambuscan's wars, Algarsife winning Theodora with the horse's aid, Camballo's tourney for Canace's hand) that Chaucer never wrote — the editorial notes confirm the poem was left "half-told" by its author, not truncated by manuscript loss.

## Themes

**A frame of gendered suspicion licenses the tale.** The Host's prologue is a small comic monologue of marital anxiety and misogyny — he catalogues his wife's vices only to withhold them, fearing "women connen utter such chaffare" (women are adept at circulating gossip). This frame doesn't recur inside the tale itself, but it sets the register: a company entertaining itself with stories about trust, concealment, and what women know that men don't — a concern the falcon's tale answers from the other side, with a *male* lover cast as the hypocrite.

**The four marvels are a fantasy of total legibility.** The mirror shows "when there shall fall any adversity," exposes "who is your friend or foe," and lets a woman see through any false lover's "newe love, and all his subtlety" — nothing hidden. The ring makes animal language and plant medicine transparent to Canace. Together with the sword's reversible wound/heal power and the horse's frictionless transport, these are instruments that collapse concealment and distance: a courtly wish-list for a world rendered fully readable and controllable, worth noting as an early literary image of the impulse [[legibility-as-machine-core]] names as a recurring civilizational project — knowledge and mobility without residue or opacity.

**The crowd's reception of the marvels dramatizes the comedy (and limits) of folk explanation.** Chaucer gives an extended, genuinely funny catalogue of the court's competing theories — Pegasus, the Trojan horse of Sinon, optics citing Alhazen and Vitellon and Aristotle, the metallurgy of hardened swords, the ring-lore of Moses and Solomon — before concluding "as lewed people deeme commonly / Of thinges that be made more subtilly / Than they can in their lewdness comprehend; / They deeme gladly to the badder end." It's a small essay on how a population confronted with an inexplicable technology reaches for whatever explanatory frame — myth, science, magic, suspicion — happens to be at hand, and defaults to the darkest reading.

**The falcon's confession recasts courtly love as rehearsed hypocrisy.** Her tercelet is a serpent hiding "under flow'rs," a performer so practiced that "save the fiend, none wiste what he meant": his oaths, humility, and grief were all craft, indistinguishable from genuine feeling until the betrayal made the performance visible in hindsight. The passage inverts the mirror's promise from Part I — where the mirror could show treachery instantly, the falcon had no such device and was undone slowly, by ordinary trust.

**Inconstancy is naturalized, then moralized, in the caged-bird analogy.** Drawing explicitly on Boethius (per the tale's own gloss), the Squire generalizes the tercelet's abandonment into a law of nature: creatures "love novelties, of proper kind," like a caged bird that spurns milk and honey the instant its door opens to fly back to worms in the wood — "no gentleness of bloode may them bind." The falcon's private grief becomes an instance of a universal appetite for the new over the tended, offered without much comfort to the wounded party.

**The tale's incompleteness is a formal fact worth sitting with.** The Squire announces three unwritten branches — Cambuscan's conquests, Algarsife's winning of Theodora, Camballo's combat for Canace — and stops. The notes are explicit that no lost ending is known to have existed; Chaucer simply left it, a fragment gesturing at a larger architecture (three siblings, three future stories) that was never built.

## Notable passages

The Host's prologue ("Now such a wife I pray God keep me fro'" through "Since women connen utter such chaffare") supplies the frame-tale's comic anxiety about women's knowledge and speech, setting up thematic counterpoint to the falcon's later exposure of a *man's* duplicity.

The knight's description of the mirror, ring, and sword ("This mirror eke, that I have in mine hond" through "It faileth not, while it is in your hold") is the tale's conceptual core — a cluster of objects promising total transparency (hidden danger, false lovers, animal speech, herbal knowledge) and reversible violence, worth flagging for its resonance with legibility as a recurring civilizational fantasy.

The court's jangling debate over the automaton ("Diverse folk diversely they deem'd" through "Of sundry doubts they jangle thus and treat") is Chaucer's comic set-piece on collective sense-making confronted with an inexplicable technology — myth, optics, magic, and suspicion all reached for at once.

The falcon's indictment of the tercelet ("Right as a serpent hides him under flow'rs" through "That, save the fiend, none wiste what he meant") is the emotional and moral center of Part II, rendering courtly love's performative vocabulary as indistinguishable, from the inside, between sincerity and craft.

The caged-bird analogy ("That alle thing repairing to his kind" through "No gentleness of bloode may them bind") universalizes the betrayal into a Boethian law of nature, the tale's closest approach to a stated moral before it breaks off unfinished.
