# The Pardoner's Prologue and Tale

The unit opens mid-frame: the Host, rattled by the tale just concluded (the Physician's, though this span never names it), rails against corrupt judges and the cruelty of fortune, then turns to the Pardoner for "some mirth of japes." The gentlefolk overrule him, demanding "some moral thing" instead. What follows is not a prologue in the usual sense but the Pardoner's own drunken confession — a extended, cynical exposure of his fraudulent trade in relics and pardons — before he delivers the tale proper: three riotous young men in Flanders set out to slay Death, are redirected by a mysterious old man to a tree where they find a hoard of gold instead, and destroy one another for possession of it. The unit closes with the frame resuming: the Pardoner, undeterred by having just confessed his own fraudulence, tries to sell his relics to the pilgrims themselves, and the Host's crude refusal nearly derails the pilgrimage before the Knight brokers peace.

## Themes

**Confession as performance, not repentance.** The Pardoner narrates his own methods with theatrical relish rather than shame — the Latin tag "Radix malorum est cupiditas" recited twice as an empty formula, the counterfeit relics (a "shoulder-bone" from a "holy Jewe's sheep," a healing "mittain") deployed with showman's precision. His self-exposure never curdles into guilt: "For mine intent is not but for to win, / And nothing for correction of sin." This is hypocrisy examined from the inside, a portrait of institutional religious authority hollowed into pure technique.

**The apparatus of extraction.** What the Pardoner describes is a whole working machinery for converting fear of damnation into money: papal bulls and seals establishing legitimacy, Latin phrases lending gravitas, physical relics offering tangible "proof," and a preaching cadence ("stretche forth my neck," "east and west upon the people I beck") calibrated for maximum yield. It is a strikingly literal medieval instance of built infrastructure for legibility — signs and instruments (seal, bull, bone, glove) manufacturing a visible, auditable simulacrum of piety so that invisible sin and grace can be transacted for coin — worth flagging for [[legibility-as-machine-core]]. The relics themselves also trace a small [[decay-sequence]] in miniature: objects that once might have carried genuine devotional function are here already Ornamental-shading-into-Absent, retaining only their institutional trappings while their sacred content has evacuated entirely, propped up by the Pardoner's performance alone.

**Radix malorum est cupiditas — the tale as trap.** The exemplum of the three rioters literalizes the sermon's theme with mathematical cruelty: greed for the gold destroys exactly the fellowship ("sworn brothers") the men had just pledged in their vow against Death. The irony is total — they set out to kill Death and become Death's instrument for each other, poisoned and stabbed by the very avarice the Pardoner claims to preach against and privately embodies. Gluttony, drunkenness, gambling, and false swearing are indicted at length before the tale begins, framing avarice as the last and deadliest link in a chain of vice.

**The Old Man.** The unnamed "olde man full meekely" who directs the rioters to the tree is the passage's strangest figure — restless, unable to die, longing to trade places with his own mother (the earth) and be let into her "gate." He functions simultaneously as memento mori, as possible allegory for Death or the Wandering Jew, and as a figure of patient endurance rebuking the young men's violence toward age. His speech is the tale's lyrical high point, oddly gentle against the surrounding satire.

**Fraud unrepentant.** The frame's return is the sharpest joke: having just admitted his relics are fake and his motives corrupt, the Pardoner immediately tries to sell them to his fellow pilgrims, singling out the Host — "most enveloped in sin" — to kiss them first. The Host's furious, scatological refusal (wishing to enshrine the Pardoner's testicles "in a hogge's turd") threatens to fracture the pilgrimage's civility until the Knight intervenes, restoring the frame's comic equilibrium.

## Notable passages

The Pardoner's set-piece on his fake relics — the crystal stones "y-crammed full of cloutes and of bones," the sheep's shoulder-bone that heals livestock, the glove that multiplies grain, and the cynical exclusion clause barring actual sinners from offering to his relics — shows the mechanism of the fraud in granular, almost procedural detail. His confession of motive ("By this gaud have I wonne year by year / A hundred marks... I preache nothing but for covetise") is the unit's ethical center, stating outright what the rest of the Canterbury Tales usually leaves to dramatic irony. The Old Man's lament ("This olde man gan look on his visage... Alas! when shall my bones be at rest?") supplies the tale's one moment of unguarded pathos. The rioters' discovery of gold where they expected Death ("See ye that oak? right there ye shall him find... down they sat them by the precious hoard") triggers the plot's fatal irony, consummated in the poisoning sequence where the youngest's murder of his two "brothers" is answered by his own death from the very wine he poisoned for them. The frame's closing exchange — the Host's crude rejection of the Pardoner's relics — supplies the unit's final, deflating punchline on institutional hypocrisy meeting plain speech.
