# Troilus and Cressida: Editor's Preface and the First Book

The unit opens with an editorial headnote situating Troilus and Cressida within Chaucer's corpus — praising it as his most artistically unified and morally elevated work, contrasting its noble Troilus, virtuous Cressida, and flawed-but-gentlemanly Pandarus with the debased versions of the same figures in Shakespeare's play, and summarizing all five books before the excerpted text of Book the First begins. That book, rendered here in a mix of verse stanzas and connecting prose paraphrase, covers the poem's opening invocation, Calchas' treasonous flight to the Greek camp and his daughter Cressida's precarious position in besieged Troy, Troilus' sudden falling in love with her at the festival of Pallas, and his eventual confession of that love to his friend Pandarus, who agrees to intercede on his behalf.

## Themes

**The editor's frame inverts Shakespeare's Troilus and Cressida.** The headnote insists Chaucer's poem is not a coarse or cynical treatment of love but its opposite: Troilus is "noble, sensitive, generous, pure-souled," Cressida is "stately, self-contained, virtuous," and even Pandarus, though he "plots away the virtue of his niece," does so out of misplaced loyalty to friendship rather than venality. The claim that "the older poet presents us in the chief characters to noble natures, the younger to ignoble natures in all the characters" sets up the entire unit's moral register before a line of the poem itself has been read.

**The poet as unworthy servant of Love, and the shape of 'double sorrow.'** Chaucer's proem casts the narrator as one debarred from love himself ("Nor dare to love for mine unlikeliness") who nonetheless serves as scribe to lovers' pains, invoking the fury Tisiphone rather than a muse of joy. The governing structure — sorrow "from woe to weal, and after out of joy" — announces a rise-and-fall arc for Troilus's fortune before the narrative has properly started, and the narrator asks blessing on all categories of lovers: the despairing, the falsely slandered, and those "at ease."

**Oracular foreknowledge and political betrayal.** Calchas' defection is driven by prophetic knowledge — "through the oracle of Apollo, [he] knew that Troy should be destroyed" — and his flight leaves his daughter exposed to the city's fury at his treason. This is the poem's one moment of large-scale historical/political stakes (a besieged city, a seer's foreknowledge of doom, a family made collateral), quickly narrowed back to the private register of Cressida's fear and Hector's chivalrous protection of her.

**Love as sudden seizure, and comeuppance for the scorner.** Troilus enters the temple mocking lovers as fools who "may no thing see," prompting the God of Love to take vengeance. The moment his eye "pierced" the crowd and "smote" on Cressida is staged as an almost violent, involuntary event, and the narrator explicitly frames it as poetic justice: the scorner is himself struck down, hardest of all, in classic courtly-love machinery.

**The paradox of desire.** Troilus's song — "If no love is, O God! why feel I so?" — works through the standard Petrarchan contradictions (a sickness that is also sweet, a fire lit by no visible cause, consent and compulsion tangled together) that Chaucer draws from Boccaccio's Filostrato (via the invented "Lollius"). It is less an argument than a diagnostic of love as a force that dissolves the sufferer's agency while implicating his own consent.

**Pandarus's proverb-craft as a preview of his manipulations.** Once Troilus confesses, Pandarus's method is entirely rhetorical: proverbs, contraries ("Eke white by black, by shame eke worthiness"), and homely images (the whetstone that cannot cut but sharpens blades) are deployed to draw out the secret and then to reframe despair as opportunity. This scene previews the "subtle manoeuvres" the headnote says will occupy the whole of Book Two — persuasion built from folk wisdom rather than moral authority.

## Notable passages

The proem (from "THE double sorrow" through the invocation to Tisiphone) sets the narrator's self-abasing, intercessory stance toward Love before any plot begins. The Calchas paragraph is the unit's only gesture toward historical/political scale, and its oracular framing gives Cressida's vulnerability real stakes. The temple scene — Troilus's eye piercing the crowd, his astonished "Oh, very god!", and the closing "Blessed be Love, that thus can folk convert!" — is the poem's central turn, converting a scorner into the deepest of sufferers. Troilus's song crystallizes the love-paradox in miniature and gestures at the poem's Italian source. Finally, Pandarus's whetstone-and-contraries speech, and his nettle-and-lily proverb ("next the darke night is the glad morrow, / And also joy is next the fine of sorrow"), establish both his persuasive method and the poem's larger promise that Troilus's present sorrow prefigures future joy — a promise Book Five will darkly undercut.
