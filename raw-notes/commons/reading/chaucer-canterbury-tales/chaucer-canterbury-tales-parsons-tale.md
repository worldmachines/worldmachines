# The Parson's Tale: Penitence Replaces the "Tale," and Chaucer Retracts

At the close of the pilgrimage, the Host turns to the Parson for the last tale and gets a refusal of tale-telling itself: no fable, no rhyme, only "moralitee and vertuous mattere" delivered as a "litel tale in prose." What follows (compressed drastically by this edition's editor, who states in the notes that the full sermon would run some thirty pages) is a prose treatise on Penitence in three parts — Contrition, Confession, and Satisfaction — threaded through with a lurid depiction of hell's torments and a catalogue of the Seven Deadly Sins, most fully elaborated in Pride's excursus on fashionable clothing and Gluttony's compact five-species anatomy. The unit ends with the "Prayer of Chaucer" (the so-called Retraction), in which the poet-narrator lists his own works, revokes the "worldly vanities" among them — explicitly including "the Tales of Canterbury, all those that sounen unto sin" — and gives thanks only for his devotional translations, closing the entire *Canterbury Tales* on an act of authorial penitence that mirrors the Parson's subject.

## Themes

**The pilgrimage of storytelling terminates in the pilgrimage of the soul.** The Parson's opening refusal — "I cannot gest… by my letter; / And, God wot, rhyme hold I but little better" — is a genre-ending move: the game the Host has run since the General Prologue (each pilgrim spinning a tale) is explicitly broken by the one teller who declines to play, converting the frame-narrative's literal road to Canterbury into an allegory of "thilke perfect glorious pilgrimage… That hight Jerusalem celestial." Fiction is displaced by treatise; the collection's last unit is generically unlike every other unit before it.

**Hell is rendered as exhaustive inversion and unending accounting.** The Parson's picture of hell works by systematically stripping away the three goods of the present world — honour, riches, delights — and showing each converted to its opposite: "in hell is no honour nor reverence; for certes no more reverence shall be done there to a king than to a knave." The torment is defined less by fire than by the impossibility of ending: "death without death, and end without end, and default without failing." This is punishment imagined as an audit that never closes.

**Pride's inward vice is legible on the body via dress.** The treatise's most vivid (and, per the editor, most "curious and valuable" for social history) passage is not doctrinal but sartorial: a tirade against both the sumptuary excess of long, fur-trimmed, cloth-wasting gowns and the opposite excess of scandalously short hose that expose "the boss and shape of the horrible swollen members." Clothing here functions as involuntary confession — pride hidden in the heart is nevertheless read off the surface, "right as the gay levesell [bush] at the tavern is sign of the wine that is in the cellar."

**Confession is specified as an exhaustive, non-partitionable procedure.** The rules governing valid shrift are strikingly administrative: sins must go to one confessor, not divided piecemeal "for shame or dread" ("let no blot be behind, let no sin be untold as far as thou hast remembrance"), must be spoken and not written, must not be embellished or minimized, and must eventually reckon every hour of a life — "there shall not perish an hair of his head, nor a moment of an hour shall not perish of his time, that he shall not give thereof a reckoning." The soul is treated as a ledger to be made completely readable to a single authorized reader (the priest) before it can be absolved — a striking, if pre-modern, exercise in rendering an interior life exhaustively legible to an institution, worth flagging for [[legibility-as-machine-core]] even though the apparatus here is confessional rather than bureaucratic or cartographic.

**The Retraction folds the author into his own doctrine.** Chaucer's closing prayer applies the treatise's logic to his own oeuvre: he distinguishes devotional translations (kept, thanked for) from "worldly vanities" — Troilus, the Book of Fame, and "the Tales of Canterbury, all those that sounen unto sin" — which he revokes. Whether genuine or a scribal/monastic interpolation (the editor's notes rehearse both views via Tyrwhitt and Wright), it stages the entire collection's ending as an act of contrition, collapsing narrator and penitent into the same figure the Parson has just finished describing.

## Notable passages

The six curated passages track the treatise's arc: the Parson's prologue refusal of "fable" for prose moralitee; the hell-passage on honour's collapse into universal indignity; the sumptuary attack on scant and disguising hosen as pride's visible "twig"; the procedural rule against dividing confession "to one man… and a parcel to another"; the Gluttony chapter, given complete because the editor judged it short enough to reproduce in full and it neatly demonstrates the abridgement method (compressed enumeration ending in the memorable "five fingers of the devil's hand"); and Chaucer's Retraction naming and renouncing his secular works, the passage that closes not just this unit but the *Canterbury Tales* as a whole.
