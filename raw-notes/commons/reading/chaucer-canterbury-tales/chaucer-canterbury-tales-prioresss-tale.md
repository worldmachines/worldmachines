# The Prioress's Tale: The Boy-Martyr of the Jewery

After the Host praises the Shipman's tale and courteously invites the Prioress to speak next, she opens with an elaborate Marian invocation before telling of a seven-year-old "litel clergeon" living beside a Jewery who learns the antiphon *Alma Redemptoris Mater* by rote without understanding its Latin, sings it constantly through the Jewish quarter on his way to school, is murdered and thrown into a privy-pit by a Jew hired at Satan's instigation, is found by his grief-stricken widowed mother because his cut throat miraculously keeps singing the hymn, and — after the provost has the murderers tortured and hanged — is revealed by the abbot to be sustained by a grain the Virgin laid on his tongue, which stops his singing only when removed at his true death; the tale closes with a direct invocation of the historical boy-martyr Hugh of Lincoln.

## Themes

**Marian devotion as the narrative's frame and license.** The Prioress opens not with story but with an extended hymn to the Virgin — "O bush unburnt, burning in Moses' sight" — casting herself as a child too, "a child of twelvemonth old, or less, / That can unnethes any word express," too weak in "conning" to praise Mary adequately. This self-abasement licenses the tale that follows: the child protagonist's naive, uncomprehending devotion (he learns the song purely by rote, ignorant of its Latin meaning) is offered as the purest form of praise, superior to learned adult worship — "by the mouth of children thy bounte / Performed is."

**Childhood innocence as sanctified, weaponized vulnerability.** The clergyon is built entirely out of devotional reflexes — kneeling at every image of Mary, reciting Ave Maria by habit, pursuing the hymn's meaning "upon his knees bare" — so that his eventual murder reads not as senseless violence but as martyrdom pre-authorized by his piety. The text repeatedly frames him through relics and gems ("this gem of chastity, this emeraud, / And eke of martyrdom the ruby bright") and through typology (a "newe Rachel," Saint Nicholas in swaddling clothes), converting the murdered child into sacred object before the miracle even confirms it.

**Anti-Jewish libel as the tale's structural engine.** The Jewery is introduced as already suspect — "Sustained by a lord of that country, / For foul usure, and lucre of villainy" — and Satan is given an explicit speaking role goading "the Jewes" into conspiracy, collapsing communal culpability ("a homicide thereto have they hired") with cosmic evil. The provost's brutal reprisal ("with horses wild he did them draw, / And after that he hung them by the law") is presented as pure justice, not atrocity. This is the ritual-murder / blood-libel legend form, and Chaucer's own closing note ties it explicitly to Hugh of Lincoln (d. 1255), grounding the fiction in a real, repeatedly recycled accusation that fueled persecution across medieval Christendom — an instance of how a devotional-literary form assembles, canonizes, and recirculates a libel until it functions as institutional common sense, a pattern the [[history-machine]] concept and the [[decay-sequence]] are built to describe (a legend moving from ostensibly functional piety toward pure ornamental/allegorical repetition, detached from any need for evidence).

**Miracle as both vindication and horror.** The tale's central image — a child singing with his throat cut to the neckbone — fuses the grotesque and the transcendent without resolving the tension: the abbot's reverent conjuring ("Tell me what is thy cause for to sing, / Since that thy throat is cut, to my seeming") receives an answer of serene doctrinal comfort (the Virgin's grain sustaining him "till from my tongue off taken is the grain"), and only when the abbot removes it does the child "softely" die a second, final death. The miracle thus authenticates the antisemitic plot rather than complicating it — divine intervention functions as the tale's proof of its own premises.

## Notable passages

The Host's courteous handoff ("My Lady Prioresse, by your leave" ... "Gladly, quoth she") establishes the Prioress's genteel voice before she pivots into devotional register. Her long Marian invocation ("O Lord our Lord! thy name how marvellous" through "Help me to tell it to thy reverence") sets the theological terms — children's praise as purest praise — that the plot will literalize. The child's rote-learning and compulsive singing through the Jewery ("This little child his little book learning" through "On Christ's mother was set all his intent") builds the innocence that Satan and "the Jewes" then react against and destroy ("Our firste foe, the serpent Satanas" through "And cut his throat, and in a pit him cast"). Finally the child's own account of the miracle, from his cut throat to the abbot's removal of the grain ("My throat is cut unto my necke-bone" through "And still he lay, as he had been y-bound"), and the closing invocation of the real martyr Hugh of Lincoln ("O younge Hugh of Lincoln!" through "For reverence of his mother Mary") anchor the fiction to an actual, historically recurring blood-libel tradition.
