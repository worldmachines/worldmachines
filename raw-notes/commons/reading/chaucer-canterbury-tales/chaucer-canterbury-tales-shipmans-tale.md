# The Shipman's Prologue and Tale

The unit opens with a short prologue in which the Host, delighted by the previous tale, calls on the Parson to preach next — then the Shipman cuts him off, smelling a "Lollard" sermon coming and refusing to let the Parson "glose" any gospel over the company. He claims the next tale for himself: no philosophy, no physic, no lawyer's Latin, just a merry story. That story is the Shipman's Tale proper: a rich merchant of Saint Denis, his sociable and expensive wife, and a young monk, Dan John, who ingratiates himself into the household by claiming false cousinage with the merchant. While the merchant is shut up in his counting-house reconciling his books before a trip to Bruges, the wife confides to Dan John that she needs a hundred francs to pay for her finery and that her husband is stingy. The monk, already smitten with her, borrows exactly that sum from the unsuspecting merchant himself — ostensibly to buy cattle — and gives it to the wife in exchange for a night in her bed while her husband is away. When the merchant returns from a profitable trip to Flanders and later visits Dan John in Paris, the monk casually mentions that he already repaid the merchant's loan — by handing the gold to the wife at home. Confronted, the wife brazens it out: she spent the money on clothes "for your honour," and offers to repay her husband the same way she was paid, in bed. The merchant, cornered by the impossibility of untangling the accounts, forgives her and merely asks her to keep better track of his money in future.

## Themes

**Every relationship in the tale is rendered as an account to be settled.** Cousinage, marriage, friendship, and lending are all treated by the characters as interchangeable instruments of credit — Dan John invents kinship purely "to have the more cause of acquaintance," the wife pledges her body "to wed" as security against a debt, and the merchant himself reduces his own trade to a maxim: "their money is their plough." The tale's comedy depends on money, sex, and reputation being rendered fully commensurable, tradable against one another at will — a flattening of qualitatively different obligations into a single fungible currency. That flattening is itself a kind of [[legibility-as-machine-core]] move: the story stages, centuries before the fact, the same impulse the [[modernity-machine]] would later formalize in double-entry bookkeeping and the counting-house — the merchant's private ritual of shutting his door to "reckon with himself," laying out his "bookes and bagges" to see whether he is "increased were or non," is precisely the technology of legibility Chaucer's plot then satirizes by showing how easily it can be gamed: the ledger can be balanced on paper while the real debt (of trust, of fidelity) goes not merely unpaid but unrecorded.

**Clerical hypocrisy is the tale's second target, set up by the prologue's anti-clerical anxiety.** The Shipman's fear of a Lollard sermon — "He woulde sowe some difficulty, / Or springe cockle in our cleane corn" — primes the audience for a tale in which the monk, sworn on his own breviary to secrecy and swearing love "by my professioun," uses the language and trappings of pious brotherhood (oaths, kisses, a portable sundial marking canonical hours) purely as seduction technique. Dan John's "diligence," his gifts to the household staff, his invented cousinage, and his oath-swearing are all instrumentalized courtesies — forms hollowed of their content, deployed for private appetite while retaining every outward sign of holy fellowship.

**The wife's closing defense collapses the tale's two economies into one image.** Faced with her husband's demand for an accounting, she does not deny receiving the gold; she reframes the entire dispute as a matter of overdue payment between debtor and creditor — "I am your wife, score it upon my tail" — before offering to settle in the same coin she used with the monk. The joke is not merely bawdy; it is that the wife has fully absorbed the mercantile logic surrounding her and turned it back on the man who lives by it, exposing how thin the line is between the household economy and the counting-house.

## Notable passages

The Shipman's refusal to let the Parson preach, and his claim on the company's ear for something lighter, sets the anti-clerical, anti-philosophical register of everything that follows: "There is but little Latin in my maw."

The merchant's counting-house scene is the tale's clearest image of legibility as private ritual — reckoning, books, bags, and a shut door — undercut by the fact that this same scrupulous man will be deceived by an account he never sees recorded anywhere.

The wife's plea to Dan John lays out the tale's premise in her own voice: a marriage found wanting against her checklist of what wives "naturally" desire, and a debt she'd rather incur from a monk than confess to her husband.

Dan John's answer and the embrace that follows fuse the oath of secrecy directly into the seduction, monastic language sliding into desire without a seam.

The merchant's own words to Dan John — "their money is their plough" — state outright the commercial logic that the wife will later use against him, that credit is the merchant's whole art and disgrace is running out of it.

The wife's final negotiation — offering her "jolly body" as security and vowing to "pay you but in bed" — is the tale's punchline and its thesis at once: every debt in this household, marital or mercantile, is ultimately settled in the same currency.
