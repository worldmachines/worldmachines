# Chaucer's Shorter Poems: A.B.C., Ballades, Envoys, and the Complaint to His Purse

This unit is not a single poem but the miscellany that closes the Purves edition of Chaucer: the *A.B.C.* (an alphabetical acrostic prayer to the Virgin), *A Goodly Ballad of Chaucer* (a daisy-conceit courtly love poem), *A Ballad Sent to King Richard* (the political complaint 'Lak of Stedfastnesse'), *L'Envoy of Chaucer to Bukton* (mock-misogynist marriage advice), *A Ballad of Gentleness*, *The Complaint of Chaucer to His Purse* (with its envoy to the new king), *Good Counsel of Chaucer* (the poem usually called 'Truth'), *Proverbs of Chaucer*, a *Virelay*, the lyric '*Since I from Love*,' *Chaucer's Words to His Scrivener*, and *Chaucer's Prophecy*. The transcriber's notes repeatedly flag that modern scholarship doubts Chaucer's authorship of several of these (the Virelay, 'Since I from Love,' the second and third stanzas of the Proverbs, the Prophecy), so the unit functions partly as an anthology of the Chaucer apocrypha alongside securely attributed late work.

## Themes

**Devotion in acrostic form.** The *A.B.C.* is a sustained Marian prayer, each of its twenty-three stanzas opening with a successive letter of the alphabet (A through Y, skipping some letters, per medieval convention), reportedly written for Blanche of Lancaster's private devotions. It rehearses the standard machinery of Marian intercession — the Virgin as advocate against the 'high Justice,' as the burning bush unconsumed, as antitype of Isaac's near-sacrifice — but the formal constraint (letter-by-letter progression) gives it a mnemonic, almost technical quality distinct from free devotional lyric.

**Courtly submission and the daisy conceit.** *A Goodly Ballad* repeats the refrain 'wholly to be under your governance' across three stanzas, then pivots to the marguerite (daisy) trope — the beloved as the sun's daughter whose closing and opening mirrors the flower's — a stock device of the French-influenced love-vision tradition Chaucer worked in throughout his career (compare the *Legend of Good Women* prologue). The bilingual code-switching into French ('*Je voudrais*,' '*Je serve Joyesse!*') marks it as a courtly performance piece for an aristocratic audience literate in both languages.

**Steadfastness lost and the world upside down.** 'Lak of Stedfastnesse,' addressed to Richard II, diagnoses a world where 'man's word was held obligation' but is now 'turned up so down' through 'meed and wilfulness' — truth put down, virtue without dominion, covetise blinding discretion. This is a complaint about institutional and moral legitimacy failing faster than anything replaces it: old bonds (word as bond, virtue as the ground of rank) no longer hold, and the envoy can only exhort the Prince to 'wed thy folk again to steadfastness.' The language of an order 'turned up so down' with no new order yet secured is the kind of legitimacy-crisis moment [[gramsci-gap]] names — old consent dying, nothing yet stabilized to replace it — though Chaucer's frame is moral-personal (truth, meed, wilfulness) rather than infrastructural.

**Comic self-deprecation and patronage.** The Bukton envoy and the *Complaint to His Purse* show Chaucer's other register: wry, self-interested, transactional. Bukton gets mock-solemn warnings against marriage ('Bet is to wed than burn in worse wise') capped by a redirection to 'the Wife of Bath' — Chaucer citing his own *Canterbury Tales* as authority within a private verse letter. The purse-complaint turns the vocabulary of courtly love-complaint ('Ye be my life! Ye be my hearte's steer!') onto an empty coin-purse, then closes with a direct petition to the new king (Henry IV, 'conqueror of Brute's Albion') — a poem that is also, transparently, a request for money, and reportedly succeeded in getting Chaucer's annuity confirmed.

**Gentility from virtue, not blood.** *A Ballad of Gentleness* runs the argument that vice cannot inherit nobility and virtue cannot be bequeathed by blood — 'gentle' status must be earned by imitation of Christ ('the firste stock-father'), regardless of 'mitre, crown, or diademe.' This is the same argument the Wife of Bath's tale makes at length; here it is compressed into three refrain-bearing stanzas.

**Withdrawal as counsel.** 'Good Counsel of Chaucer' ('Truth'), said to have been composed on Chaucer's deathbed, counsels retreat from ambition and public contention — 'Flee from the press, and dwell with soothfastness' — toward an inward constancy that Fortune's wheel cannot touch: 'Here is no home, here is but wilderness. / Forth, pilgrim!' It is Boethian consolation compressed to lyric, and its logic of defended inwardness against an unreliable, capturing world has a loose kinship with [[capture-resistance]] read at the scale of a single soul rather than an institution.

**Closing miscellany.** The *Proverbs*, *Virelay*, '*Since I from Love*,' the *Scrivener* address (chiding Adam Pinkhurst for careless copying of *Boece* and *Troilus*), and the apocalyptic *Prophecy* (priests failing their vows, lords twisting God's laws, 'the Land of Albion' turning 'to confusion') round out the unit and, with it, the volume — ending the whole edition on invocation ('Sweet Jesus, heaven's King... You bring us out of this mourning') rather than narrative.

## Notable passages

The *A.B.C.*'s opening stanza establishes the acrostic prayer's register — abject, image-dense, structurally bound to the alphabet. *A Goodly Ballad*'s first stanza shows the courtly-submission refrain in its native form before the daisy conceit takes over. 'Lak of Stedfastnesse' is quoted whole (through its envoy to the Prince) because the poem's argument only lands cumulatively — each stanza's refrain restates the diagnosis before the envoy demands a remedy. The Bukton envoy's closing stanza is curated for its self-referential gesture toward the Wife of Bath. The *Complaint to His Purse* is given entire, refrain and royal envoy together, since the poem's comedy depends on the switch from mock-erotic complaint to blunt petition. 'Truth's opening stanza is curated for its most quoted line and the philosophical stance it sets up for the rest of the poem.
