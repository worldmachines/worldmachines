# The House of Fame

This dream-vision opens with the narrator's elaborate, deliberately inconclusive taxonomy of dream-causes, then relates how, on the night of 10 December, he dreamed himself into a glass temple of Venus painted with the story of Aeneas and Dido, out onto a vast empty sand plain, and then into the claws of an enormous golden eagle sent by Jove. The eagle — loquacious, half-pedantic, half-affectionate — carries him up through the air toward the House of Fame, lecturing him along the way on acoustics, on the poet's own cloistered daily habits, and on the promise of "tidings" to come. Book Three delivers the poem's real subject: a palace built on a melting rock of ice, inscribed with names both fresh and half-effaced, inside which a goddess of monstrous, fluctuating stature dispenses fame, infamy, or oblivion to company after company of petitioners according to nothing but her own caprice — followed by a visit to the adjoining House of Rumour, a vast whirling wickerwork structure, sixty miles long, through which every tiding, true and false alike, circulates, compounds, and grows before flying back out into the world. The poem ends abruptly, unresolved, when the dreamer glimpses "a man of great authority" and wakes.

## Themes

**Fame is dispensed by whim, not desert.** The poem's structural engine is a procession of nine companies before the goddess Fame, each requesting recognition (or its opposite) for identical or opposite conduct, and each receiving an answer with no discernible logic: good workers are denied fame outright, or given slander instead of praise, purely "for that it list me not"; idle men who did nothing ask to be thought industrious and lovers, and are granted it; a man who burned a temple purely to get himself talked about receives exactly the notoriety he wanted. The scene levels virtue and vice, labor and idleness, into the same undifferentiated raw material for an arbitrary allocation mechanism — reputation as a resource distributed by pure caprice rather than merit, closer to Fortune's wheel than to justice.

**Sound as an unavoidable network.** The eagle's long, quasi-scientific discourse en route (every spoken word is "broken air" that must, by natural inclination, travel to Fame's palace exactly as a stone dropped in water disturbs the whole pond) reframes reputation as an impersonal physical infrastructure: nothing said anywhere, in any register, can fail to arrive. It is less a moral order than a plumbing system for language, indifferent to what passes through it.

**Rumour accretes by distributed, authorless repetition.** In the House of Rumour, tidings do not originate with any single teller; each hearer immediately re-tells what he has heard, embellishing it "or it a furlong way was old," so that reports snowball from mouth to mouth, true and false versions colliding and fusing at the windows, until — like a spark catching a city — the story is unrecognizably swollen before it is finally blown abroad by Aeolus's trumpet. This is history and reputation assembling themselves anonymously and incrementally rather than through any single author's intent, a dynamic with some kinship to the WMP notion of a [[history-machine]] silently accreting until it lurches into visibility, though here the process serves distortion rather than paradigm-formation.

**Permanence and decay of a name are matters of accident.** The rock beneath Fame's castle is carved everywhere with names, but on its sunward side the letters have half melted away while on the shaded northward side they remain as fresh as the day they were cut — not because the sheltered names were more deserved, but because the castle's shade happened to fall there. Reputation's durability, like its granting, is contingent on circumstance rather than on the substance of the deed — a small, literal instance of the kind of contingent, non-linear fading the WMP [[decay-sequence]] describes for institutions, applied here to individual renown.

**The poet's own cloistered life.** The eagle needles the dreamer for his ignorance of even his neighbors' business, sketching a vivid, evidently autobiographical portrait of a man who finishes his day's accounts (a clear nod to Chaucer's post as Comptroller of the Customs) only to go home and read alone until his eyes are dazed, "and livest thus as a hermite" — a rare direct glimpse of Chaucer's working life inside the allegory.

## Notable passages

The eagle's rebuke of the dreamer's isolated, bookish existence — ending, half-comically, half-ruefully, on his living "as a hermite" despite his fondness for good cheer — is the poem's clearest autobiographical aside, grounding the flight of fancy in the mundane grind of Chaucer's actual daily labor.

The sequence of the first companies before Fame ("They hadde good fame each deserved, / Although they were diversely served") states outright the poem's central proposition: desert and outcome are severed. The goddess's blunt refusals — "For that it list me not," "No wight shall speak of you" — dramatize legitimacy as pure caprice rather than judgment.

The description of the House of Rumour itself ("Was made of twigges sallow... red," its thousand holes, its ceaseless "rownings and jangles" of every category of news) catalogues the sheer indiscriminate throughput of a rumor-infrastructure that processes love and war, famine and building, health and ruin, without distinction.

The account of how a single tiding compounds as it passes mouth to mouth — each teller adding to it "or it a furlong way was old," "Till all a city burnt up is" — is the poem's most exact model of distributed, authorless amplification: no one invents the final shape of the rumor, and no one could stop its growth.

The temple-burner's confession — he set fire to the temple of Isidis purely because he "woulde fain have had a name," reasoning that infamy is as available as honest fame and cheaper to obtain — supplies the poem's starkest exemplum: fame answers to salience, not to the moral content of the act that produces it.
