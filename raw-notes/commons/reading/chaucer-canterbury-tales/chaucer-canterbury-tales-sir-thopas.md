# The Prologue and Tale of Sir Thopas (First and Second Fits)

After the preceding tale's solemnity breaks, the Host turns for the first time to the pilgrim-narrator himself — a man who has been riding apart, eyes fixed on the ground — and jokingly demands "some dainty thing" of him. Chaucer-the-pilgrim protests he knows only one old rhyme, and the Host, delighted, tells him to proceed. What follows is not a tale at all in the ordinary sense but a pitch-perfect parody of the dull, formulaic tail-rhyme romances then in vogue: the adventures of Sir Thopas, a knight of Flanders who flees an angry giant, dreams of an elf-queen, and arms himself in a catalogue of ill-fitting chivalric gear, before the text itself gives out mid-sentence in the middle of the Second Fit.

## Themes

**A rare self-portrait, delivered as mockery.** The Host's description of "Chaucer" — staring at the ground like a man hunting a hare, "elvish" in countenance, shaped in the waist much like the Host's own stout self, indifferent to "dalliance" — is one of the only physical self-portraits Chaucer gives anywhere in the work, and it arrives filtered entirely through another pilgrim's teasing voice rather than direct self-description. The joke of the frame (an aloof, unsociable figure is then handed the invitation to entertain the company) sets up the joke of the tale that follows.

**Parody through relentless accumulation.** The comedy of Sir Thopas works less through plot than through cataloguing: spices ("liquorice," "clove-gilofre," "nutemeg"), armor pieces (haketon, habergeon, hauberk, jambeaux, sword-sheath, helm, saddle, bridle), and a roll-call of genuine romance heroes (Horn Child, Ipotis, Bevis, Sir Guy, Sir Libeux, Pleindamour) are piled up in exactly the list-making manner of the minstrel romances being lampooned — and then Thopas is declared to outrank them all, "he bears the flow'r / Of royal chivalry," a claim as absurd as it is deadpan.

**Category errors as the punchline.** Nearly every chivalric detail assigned to Thopas is a small, uncommented violation of convention: a goshawk (a yeoman's bird, not a knight's), wrestling and archery (commoners' sports, not knightly accomplishments) credited to him as prowess, a coat-armor that is blank white rather than bearing heraldic devices, an absurd crest of a tower with a lily stuck in it, and — most tellingly — "sides smale," a phrase conventionally used to describe a woman's figure, applied without a flicker of irony to the hero himself. The narrator never flags these; the parody trusts the audience to recognize the wrongness on its own.

**Form enacting its own failure.** The stanza form itself keeps lurching between patterns rather than settling into a stable scheme, and the excerpt terminates mid-clause ("Till on a day - . . ."), the tale's collapse happening formally as well as thematically. The chivalric apparatus here — crest, coat-armor, sword-sheath, all lovingly itemized — survives only as decoration, detached from any working heraldic or military logic; read against [[decay-sequence]], this is convention caught at the Ornamental stage, form persisting long after function has drained out of it.

## Notable passages

The **Prologue exchange** (Host's mockery through Chaucer's own dissatisfied reply) frames the entire piece: the Host singles out the silent, staring pilgrim, describes him almost as a doll ("This were a puppet in an arm t'embrace"), and gets in return a mock-humble claim to know only "a rhyme I learned yore agone" — which is exactly what the Host, not yet knowing better, eagerly anticipates as "some dainty thing."

The **physical description of Sir Thopas** (saffron hair to the girdle, paindemain-white face, rose-red lips, cordovan shoes, a ciclatoun robe "that coste many a jane") shows the parody's method in miniature: aristocratic luxury goods named with excessive, almost commercial precision, producing a hero built entirely out of borrowed romance furniture.

The **elf-queen love-longing** stanzas turn the hero's chastity and sudden erotic fixation (triggered by a thrush's song) into a swooning, over-determined motive for adventure — "An elf-queen will I love, y-wis, / For in this world no woman is / Worthy to be my make" — before he rides off into Faery on the strength of a single dream.

The **Oliphaunt confrontation** delivers the tale's one moment of actual peril, immediately deflated: the giant threatens to slay Thopas's steed and pierce his "maw," stones fly from a sling, and "fair escaped Child Thopas" — a retreat dressed up as if it were a triumph of "his fair bearing," though nothing in the scene shows him fighting at all.

The **catalogue boast** comparing Thopas to Horn Child, Bevis, Sir Guy, and Sir Libeux, then crowning him above them all, is the parody's clearest tell: genuine romance tradition invoked only to be comically, groundlessly surpassed by this doggerel knight.
