# The Doctor's Tale: Virginia and the Corrupt Judge

The Physician tells the story of Virginius, a knight of great honor and wealth whose only daughter, Virginia, is Nature's masterwork of beauty and virtue — chaste, discreet, and so wary of temptation that she feigns sickness to avoid feasts and dances where 'folly' might be entertained. A local judge, Appius, is inflamed by the sight of her and, knowing he cannot win her by force or bribe, conspires with a clerk (or 'churl'), Claudius, to fabricate a legal claim: that Virginia is really a stolen slave belonging to Claudius. Appius rushes the case to judgment without hearing Virginius's defense and awards the girl to the clerk. Rather than let his daughter be handed over to live in the judge's 'lechery,' Virginius goes home, explains the sentence to her, and — with her explicit consent and even her plea to be struck 'soft' — beheads her, then carries her head to the court. The people, sensing the judge's guilt, riot; Appius is imprisoned and kills himself, Claudius is exiled at Virginius's own plea for mercy, and the other conspirators are hanged. The tale closes with a blunt moral tag on the wages of sin.

## Themes

**Nature as the true auteur of virtue.** The tale opens with an extended personification of Nature boasting that she formed Virginia beyond what Pygmalion, Apelles, or Zeuxis could counterfeit, working always 'to the worship of my lord.' This isn't ornamental praise — it sets Virginia up as an emblem of a perfectly made, perfectly legible creature whose worth is then destroyed by a human institution that cannot read her truly, only exploit the levers of law surrounding her.

**Law as a captured instrument.** The tale's real engine is not Appius's lust but the machinery he bends to serve it: a formal 'bill,' a hasty hearing, a 'definitive sentence' rendered by a judge who refuses to hear the accused knight's witnesses. The court's forms — petition, testimony, judgment — are all present and procedurally intact, yet gutted of their function; the outward shell of justice becomes the instrument of the very iniquity it exists to prevent. This is less a story about a bad man than about a legitimate-seeming apparatus (the 'consistory,' the judge's 'doom') hollowed out from within while its performative trappings persist — a small-scale version of institutional decay in which form survives long after function has died. [[decay-sequence]]

**Virtue as agency, not mere victimhood.** Virginia is not simply killed; she consents, bargains for a moment to 'complain' her death (explicitly likened to Jephthah's daughter), swoons, rises, and asks to be struck before shame can touch her. The tale insists on her interiority and choice even within an annihilating outcome — chastity preserved through death is framed as the maiden's own achievement, not just her father's verdict.

**Collective moral reflex against tyranny.** Once the fraud is sensed, 'a thousand people' intervene — not through counter-legal process but through direct action, imprisoning Appius and precipitating his suicide. The tale trusts popular moral instinct to correct what formal judgment could not, then closes by universalizing the lesson: no one, learned or ignorant, escapes the eventual reckoning of conscience.

## Notable passages

Nature's proud self-description of crafting Virginia beyond any artist's power establishes the maiden as an almost sacral object before the plot turns predatory. Appius's private conspiracy with Claudius shows corruption assembling itself in secret, gift by gift, well before it reaches the public court. The rigged hearing itself — the judge's promise that Virginius 'shalt have alle right, and no wrong here' immediately followed by a judgment rendered without evidence — is the tale's clearest picture of legal form used against its own substance. Virginius's speech to his daughter turns the death sentence into an act of love rather than violence, a father's grief bent into an instrument of protection. Virginia's own request — to bewail her death briefly, then her declaration 'Blessed be God, that I shall die a maid' — makes her death a chosen consummation of virtue rather than a mere sentence carried out upon her. The closing scene of popular uprising and the flat moral ('Forsake sin, ere sinne you forsake') completes the exemplum's arc from private appetite to public reckoning.
