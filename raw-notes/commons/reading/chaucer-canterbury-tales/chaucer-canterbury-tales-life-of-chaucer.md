# The Life of Geoffrey Chaucer

This unit is the editor's prefatory biographical essay on Chaucer, not a tale itself. It traces his career from a birth "generally believed" to be London in 1328, through university speculation, an Italian campaign and capture in 1359, marriage into the circle of John of Gaunt, a string of court sinecures and diplomatic missions (including a disputed but tantalizing possible meeting with Petrarch at Padua in 1373), his controllership of the Customs, his rise and fall with the fortunes of the Lancastrian faction, a hard decade of debt and dismissal, a late-life uptick in royal favour under Henry IV, and his death in 1400. The essay then pivots to describe the design, scale, and incompleteness of The Canterbury Tales, the fictional frame at the Tabard Inn, and closes with editorial notes on Middle English pronunciation (the sounded terminal "e").

## Themes

**Chaucer as linguistic unifier.** The essay opens by crediting Chaucer with fusing England's two "half-hostile" linguistic strands — the Teutonic speech of the people and the French-derived speech of the court — into a single vernacular literature, closing a rift that had persisted since the Conquest. The claim is grand and somewhat hagiographic (typical of Victorian literary biography), but it frames Chaucer's historical significance as infrastructural: he does not merely write well, he changes what a shared English idiom can do.

**A history machine displacing a "great men" narrative.** The most methodologically interesting passage is buried in an endnote (note 8): the essay explicitly contrasts the "old biographers," who built a dramatic tale of Wycliffite conspiracy, exile, imprisonment, and forced betrayal purely from inferred "autobiographic allusions" in a literary text ("The Testament of Love"), against the sober counter-history assembled by Sir Harris Nicolas from scattered administrative records — Customs rolls, Exchequer pension disbursements, Parliamentary attendance. The old narrative is vivid and personality-driven; the new one is boring, distributed, and true. This is close to [[history-machine]] in miniature: a paradigm (archival, record-based biography) silently assembling from independent bureaucratic traces until it displaces an older "great men" mode of storytelling built on romantic inference.

**Legibility infrastructure as the texture of a career.** Chaucer's adult life is narrated almost entirely through the apparatus of a legible administrative state in formation: customs controllerships requiring accounts "written out in his own hand,” pensions drawn twice yearly at the Exchequer, Parliamentary commissions, letters of protection against arrest, a wardship charge, a lease with a stated annual rent. The essay's own evidentiary method — preferring the paper trail to inherited legend — mirrors the very legibility technologies (cadastral-style record-keeping, itemized accounts, dated grants) that were being built out across this period. Both the subject's career and the essay's method sit inside the Dawn of what WMP would call the [[modernity-machine]]: [[legibility-as-machine-core]] as the shared substrate of both the poet's civil-service life and the biographer's evidentiary practice.

**Patronage as precarity.** Chaucer's material fortunes rise and fall in lockstep with the fortunes of his patron's faction — flush under Lancaster's ascendancy, stripped of both Customs offices in December 1386 when Gloucester's commission purges Lancastrian appointees, then restored to lucrative office the moment Richard II resumes power in 1389. The essay is careful to insist this was not really about merit or misconduct ("We need not ascribe... to any malpractices in his official conduct") but about factional weather. Genius and competence run on a second track beneath naked patronage politics.

**Chaucer and Italy: an argued absence of evidence.** A long, self-consciously speculative passage argues — by circumstantial literary inference rather than record — that Chaucer likely met Petrarch at Padua in 1373, reading the Clerk's mournful digression on Petrarch's death and his "taughte me this tale" framing as Chaucer speaking in his own voice through a fictional narrator. The essay flags its own method as inferential ("a moot-point") even as it argues confidently for the meeting — a useful contrast to the record-based rigor applied elsewhere to Chaucer's biography proper.

**The frame and the fragment.** The essay describes the plan of the Tales — thirty pilgrims each to tell two tales outbound and two on return, ~120 tales projected against the twenty-three actually delivered — situating it against Boccaccio's Decameron (whose plague-frame lends "grim grotesqueness" where Chaucer's pilgrimage frame strikes "a perpetual key-note of gaiety"). Chaucer began the project between sixty and seventy, guaranteeing incompleteness; death intervened before even the existing tales were fully linked.

## Notable passages

The opening claim of linguistic unification ("there was practically but one speech") sets the essay's thesis-level stakes. The Gloucester–Lancaster patronage passage shows Chaucer's civil-service life as a weathervane for court faction, ending in his December 1386 dismissal from both Customs posts. The endnote on the "old biographers" versus Nicolas's archival reconstruction is the essay's clearest moment of historiographical self-awareness, discarding a dramatic Wycliffite-exile legend once actual pension and Parliamentary records are consulted. The Clerk's Tale/Petrarch passage shows the essay's more speculative mode, reading a fictional narrator's grief as the poet's own. Finally, the Tabard Inn description lays out the frame device — Harry Bailly's storytelling contest — that structures the whole of The Canterbury Tales.
