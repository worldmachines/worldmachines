# The Tale of Melibee: Prudence Talks Her Husband Out of War

The unit opens with the frame-tale joke that sets up the prose Tale proper: the Host cuts off Chaucer-the-pilgrim's doggerel "Sir Thopas" and orders him to try prose instead, "in which there be some mirth or some doctrine." Chaucer agrees, then pre-emptively defends himself for compressing and rephrasing his source the way the four Evangelists retell Christ's Passion differently in wording but identically in "sentence" (meaning). What follows is the tale itself: Melibeus's house is broken into while he is away; his wife Prudence is beaten and his daughter Sophia wounded in five places. Melibeus wants war. Prudence, through a long counsel-scene (compressed by this edition's editor into indirect summary before returning to direct dialogue at the reconciliation), walks him through who should be trusted for advice, why vengeance is not the true opposite of wrong, and why mercy rather than punishment secures both a man's safety and his good name. The tale ends with the adversaries submitting themselves fully to Melibeus's judgment, and Melibeus, at Prudence's urging, forgiving them outright.

## Themes

**Deliberation versus reflex is the tale's whole argument.** The councils Melibeus convenes are stocked with surgeons and physicians who stay above the fray, flatterers and reconciled old enemies who fan his anger for their own reasons, and "young folk" who shout for immediate war — set against a lone old wise man who is shouted down for urging patience. The tale stages counsel itself as a contest between distributed, self-interested voices rather than a single authoritative verdict, and it is explicit that the loudest, fastest consensus (the young men's cry of "War! War!") is exactly the one to distrust.

**Prudence's method is procedural, not merely moralizing.** She does not simply tell Melibeus vengeance is wrong; she gives him a working test for evaluating counsel and for knowing when it is legitimate to change a decision already taken — echoing something close to a decision-audit: examine the source of advice, examine your own passions (anger, covetousness, hastiness) for bias, and treat any counsel too rigid to ever be revisited as inherently corrupt ("that counsel is wicked"). This procedural caution against irreversible action taken in anger or haste is the tale's real payload, more than any single quoted authority.

**Vengeance is redefined, not merely forbidden.** The physicians' maxim that "contraries are cured by contraries" is the hinge Prudence seizes and inverts: Melibeus assumes this licenses vengeance-for-wrong, but Prudence insists wrong's true contrary is not counter-wrong but goodness — war's contrary is peace, not more war. The tale's ethical work is done by refusing the equivalence between retaliation and correction.

**Mercy is framed as a transaction with divine judgment, not weakness.** Both Prudence's closing appeal and Melibeus's final act of forgiveness explicitly tie earthly mercy to a hoped-for reciprocal mercy at "his last judgement" — the household drama becomes a rehearsal of the Lord's Prayer's logic of forgiveness, collapsing private vengeance and cosmic judgment into the same moral economy.

**A textual note:** the editor's preface (cited in this unit's endnotes) admits to having "condensed the long reasonings and learned quotations of Dame Prudence into a mere outline," trading a treatise thick with Seneca, Solomon, Ovid, and Cicero for connective narrative summary. What survives here is therefore already a second-order compression of a compression — Chaucer's own prose translation of a French treatise, itself abridged again by a nineteenth-century editor protecting his verse anthology from too much prose.

## Notable passages

The **Host's outburst** ("Thy drafty rhyming is not worth a tord") is the pivot from verse to prose and is Chaucer's own joke about audience patience with doctrine versus mirth — worth preserving as the frame that authorizes everything that follows.

Chaucer's **Evangelist defense** — that Mark, Matthew, Luke, and John differ in wording yet are "all soth" in "sentence" — is a small but genuine statement of a translator's/compiler's ethic: fidelity to meaning over verbatim form. It quietly justifies not only Chaucer's own retelling but the Purves edition's further compression of the tale itself.

The **old wise counsellor's speech on war** — "War at his beginning hath so great an entering and so large, that every wight may enter when him liketh... but certes what end shall fall thereof it is not light to know" — is the tale's clearest single statement of asymmetric risk: war is easy to start and unknowable in its ending, so it demands deliberation precisely because it cannot be reversed once begun.

Prudence's **rule for revising a decision** (a counsel may be changed if the cause ceases, if new facts arise, if it was dishonestly given — but a counsel that can never be revised "for any condition that may betide... is wicked") reads almost like a decision-hygiene checklist embedded in a medieval prose treatise.

Prudence's **plea for courteous judgement** ("He that most courteously commandeth, to him men most obey"... "Judgement without mercy shall be done to him, that hath no mercy of another wight") is the tale's moral center of gravity — mercy justified both pragmatically (a good name secures more loyalty than fear) and theologically.

Melibeus's **final act of forgiveness** — received humility converting into pardon, explicitly so that God might likewise forgive the household's own trespasses — closes the tale's argument in practice rather than in further argument, resolving the drama through an act rather than another citation.
