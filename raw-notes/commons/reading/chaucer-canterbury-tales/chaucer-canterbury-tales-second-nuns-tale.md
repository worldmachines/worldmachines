# The Second Nun's Tale: The Legend of Saint Cecilia

The scout's label for this unit turns out to be accurate: this is indeed the Second Nun's Tale, and it is exactly what tradition says it is — a close translation of the Latin legend of Saint Cecilia. The Nun opens with a moralized prologue against idleness (the devil's favorite snare), moves through an elaborate Marian invocation and a punning etymology of Cecilia's name, and then narrates the saint's story: a Roman noblewoman, secretly vowed to Christ and to virginity, who is married to the pagan Valerian; on their wedding night she reveals that a guardian angel watches over her body, and converts Valerian, then his brother Tiburce, through visions of rose-and-lily crowns from Paradise. Both men are martyred; Cecilia converts the officer sent to arrest them; and she herself is finally hauled before the prefect Almachius, where she runs rhetorical circles around his threats before surviving a botched beheading to preach for three more days.

## Themes

**Idleness versus "busy-ness" frames the whole tale as a moral argument before the narrative even starts.** The prologue's stanzas on the fiend's "thousand cordes sly" waiting to catch the idle soul are not decorative — they set up Cecilia herself as the tale's answer: a woman "full swift and busy in every good working," whose sanctity is repeatedly described in terms of industriousness (the etymological play on her name as a busy bee, as "round and whole in good persevering"). Virtue here is a kind of relentless activity, and martyrdom itself becomes an extension of that busy-ness rather than a passive suffering.

**The narrator insists on her own status as mere translator, not inventor or dramatic speaker.** The extended apology — asking forgiveness for lacking "diligence" to "subtilly indite" the story, claiming to follow "the wordes and sentence / Of him that at the sainte's reverence / The story wrote" — is a humility topos, but it also does real work: it authorizes the tale's flat, legend-style plotting (visions, conversions, and debates that resolve too quickly and cleanly for realist fiction) by framing the whole performance as pious relay rather than original composition.

**Cecilia's power is entirely rhetorical and epistemic, never physical, and it consistently unmakes her interrogator's categories.** Confronted by Almachius, she refuses his binary ("Choose one of these two, / Do sacrifice, or Christendom renay") by reframing the question itself as "foolish" for demanding "two answers" to "one demand." Her famous retort that mortal power is "like a bladder full of wind" — punctured by a needle's point — and her insistence that Almachius can only take life, not grant it, both work by exposing the gap between imperial authority's self-presentation and its actual, narrow scope. The debate over idols (stone that "men may well espyen" is stone, however named a god) runs the same move: naming does not create reality, and the prefect's blindness is moral before it is literal.

**The underground Church operates as a hidden, opaque counter-network beneath visible Roman order.** Pope Urban is found "among the saintes' burials louting" — hiding among the catacombs, per the note's Latin gloss, "inter sepulchra martyrum latitantem." Conversion, baptism, and burial all happen at night, "privily," outside the reach of the prefect's law; Cecilia's own house is quietly willed into a church before her body is even cold. This is a resistance infrastructure that survives not by confronting Roman power but by remaining structurally illegible to it — a pattern that resonates with [[capture-resistance]] and [[The Thicket]]: a portal-bordered community (entry only through secret instruction and baptism) sustained by terroir-like local practices (catacomb burial, house-churches) that the imperial apparatus cannot see until it is already gone.

## Notable passages

The idleness prologue (**c1**) establishes the moral frame the whole legend answers. The translator's apology (**c2**) frames the tale's mode as devout relay rather than invention. Cecilia's wedding-night revelation of her guardian angel (**c3**) is the hinge that turns a marriage plot into a conversion narrative. The "bladder full of wind" exchange (**c4**) and the idol-worship debate (**c5**) are the tale's rhetorical set-pieces, where Cecilia dismantles imperial authority through argument alone. The botched execution and three-day preaching vigil (**c6**) is the martyrdom's climax, where bodily suffering is folded back into the tale's governing logic of tireless "business."
