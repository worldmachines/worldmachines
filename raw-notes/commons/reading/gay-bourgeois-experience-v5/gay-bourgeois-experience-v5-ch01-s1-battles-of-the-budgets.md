# Battles of the Budgets: The Political Economy of Art

Opening the chapter that gives Vol. V its title theme, Gay sets up the Victorian "pleasure wars" as a double story: first an aesthetic one, in which the collapse of a single reigning taste standard let painters go their separate, "capricious" ways; then, underneath it, an economic one, in which the real gatekeeper of high culture was not taste at all but income. The section moves from Salon reviews and a critic's manifesto for pluralism to a meticulously graded table of German wages and painting prices, arguing that "the supreme determinant was of course money—or the lack of it."

## Themes

**Stylistic proliferation is read two ways by two critics a decade apart.** Musset surveying the 1836 Salon sees only "caprice" — canvases with "neither mothers nor sisters," no schools, no common thought. Théoré, walking the 1845 Salon, describes the identical condition — French painting "without system and without direction" — and calls it not decline but virtue: "Surely this is not an evil, since originality is the precondition of art." Gay uses the pairing to name the two parties in his "pleasure wars": an emergent aesthetic individualism against a still-strenuous conservative opposition.

**Gay endorses a historical cause for the proliferation: the fall of a centralized "dictatorship over taste."** Citing the 1904 art historian Friedrich Haack, Gay locates the cause in the French Revolution and the "stylistic free trade" it released, which together broke the joint monopoly that "the state, the nobility, and the church" had held over taste for centuries. He adds his own causal layer — the bourgeoisie's mass entry into cultural consumption — and draws the section's structural claim: "the geography of taste and the geography of politics and culture were inextricably intertwined."

**A parallel, populist current runs through aesthetic theory itself.** Baudelaire, reviewing the 1846 Salon and citing Stendhal, proposes that "there are as many sorts of beauty as there are habitual ways of seeking happiness" — a psychologized pluralism directly opposed to neoclassical uniformity, which Gay says would become "a mainstay of late-nineteenth-century modernism." Taste-as-knowledge and taste-as-desire compete throughout the unit without either fully winning.

**But Gay's real argument is fiscal, not aesthetic: he adopts Ruskin's phrase and prices the hierarchy.** "The political economy of art" becomes a literal ledger: craftsmen earning some 1,500 marks a year for whom a single painting (Liebermann's *Die Gänserupferinnen*, 3,000 marks) meant two years' labor; Kleinbürger anxious about status; Gymnasium teachers paid around 2,000 marks but compensated in honorific rank ("Professor"); solid Bürgertum households at roughly 6,000 marks (the conductor Hermann Levi's salary); and a Grossbürgertum stratosphere — Prussian ministers at 30,000 marks, railroad magnates, bankers — who could commission Feuerbach (5,000+ marks), Makart (14,000 marks), Lenbach (6,000–12,000 marks, enough to build himself a mansion), or Anton von Werner, whose group portrait of Wilhelm's imperial proclamation fetched 60,000 marks.

**Status itself was a tax that squeezed even the comfortable.** The 6,000-mark households were, Gay notes, "often beset by money worries" precisely because of their rank: pianos, cuisine, engraved stationery, visible servants, sons' fraternity dues, daughters' dowries — a conspicuous consumption experienced as compulsory rather than chosen, leaving little margin for the art their position was supposed to require.

**The buying elite for major art was narrow, but visibly expanding.** Gay's close: this "cultural aristocracy" was "a sport for an elite within an elite," yet rising exhibition demand — recorded 1873 Munich prices averaging over 2,000 marks — showed importers, publishers, and department-store owners treating art purchase as something newly indispensable rather than a luxury, even as Liebermann's own price would have cost Levi half a year's salary and a Gymnasium professor more than a full year's.

## Notable passages

- **c1** stages the section's opening aesthetic puzzle through two Salon reviews a decade apart — Musset's "caprice" (1836) and Théoré's welcomed "diversity" (1845) — naming the two parties Gay calls the "pleasure wars." The proliferation Théoré defends, styles "without system and without direction" treated as a virtue rather than a defect, resonates with [[divergence-machine]]'s logic of variety-as-feature rather than bug, though here the proliferation is a mid-century art-world episode rather than an epochal machine in its own Dawn.
- **c2** delivers Gay's causal thesis via Haack: the French Revolution's "stylistic free trade" broke the "dictatorship over taste" once held jointly by state, nobility, and church, and links this to the entry of bourgeois buyers into cultural consumption. The collapse of a single legitimate taste-authority without an immediate aesthetic consensus to replace it — Musset's bewildered "no common thought at all" — has the shape of a domain-scale [[gramsci-gap]], though the gap here closes almost at once, filled not by a prolonged legitimacy vacuum but by a new, blunter organizing principle: money.
- **c3** gives Baudelaire's manifesto for aesthetic pluralism ("as many sorts of beauty as there are habitual ways of seeking happiness"), which Gay credits as feeding into modernism. Its populist embrace of dispersed, appetite-driven taste sits in productive tension with [[carey-intellectuals-and-masses]]: Carey's modernists brace defensively against a new mass readership, while Baudelaire here welcomes exactly the plurality of popular "habitual ways of seeking happiness" that Carey's later intelligentsia would come to despise — a reminder that modernism's relationship to "the mass" was not settled in one direction from the start.
- **c4** introduces Ruskin's coinage and the section's controlling method: pricing Liebermann's *Die Gänserupferinnen* against the untroubled fortune of its buyer, the railroad magnate Strousberg, launching the income-versus-price ladder that structures the rest of the unit.
- **c5** is the section's rhetorical payoff, reassembling every income figure established earlier (Levi's conductor salary, the Gymnasium professor's pay) against the painting's price to make the "political economy of art" concrete, while also registering that the buying elite, though narrow, was visibly growing.
