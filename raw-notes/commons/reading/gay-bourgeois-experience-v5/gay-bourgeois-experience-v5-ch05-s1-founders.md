# Movers and Shakers: The Founders of Bourgeois Culture

This unit opens Chapter Five of Peter Gay's *Pleasure Wars*, tracing how nineteenth-century bourgeois collectors and businessmen graduated from private acquisition into public founding—converting collections and fortunes into museums, orchestras, and cultural societies. Gay ranges across Alfred Bruyas's bequest to the Musée Fabre, Andrew Carnegie's institutes, the Frankfurt Museum and the Städel, Britain's National Gallery donors (Tate, Sheepshanks, Vernon, Gillott), Henry Lee Higginson's Boston Symphony, the Chicago Symphony's mercantile guarantors, Amsterdam's Concertgebouw and Vereeniging Rembrandt, and Berlin's self-organized Philharmoniker and Kaiser-Friedrich-Museums-Verein—cataloguing the mixed motives that turned private wealth into public culture and showing the pattern recur, with local variation, in commercial cities on both sides of the Atlantic.

## Themes

**A taxonomy of mixed motives.** Gay opens with an explicit catalogue of what drove founders to "go public": revenge on ungrateful heirs, memorials to a dead spouse, status-seeking, patriotism, the desire to immortalize a name, and—per capitalism's critics—guilt-driven restitution for "brutal acquisitiveness." Carnegie's private 1868 memorandum against the worship of money and his self-conscious 1891 rationalization of giving are Gay's proof text that even philanthropy's most confident practitioners felt this pressure; his closing, Freud-inflected aside that for many founders as for many hunters "the love of art was just a love of art" refuses to let any single motive, including cynicism, stand as the whole explanation.

**Founders, publics, and managers as a structural triangle.** Gay frames patronage as a three-cornered relationship—donors, the consuming public, and the "men in charge" (bureaucrats, conductors, museum directors)—whose friction could run either direction: some founders and institutions (the Frankfurt Museum, with its silenced audiences and banished dogs) protected tradition against "aesthetic anarchy," while others actively promoted new composers and painters. "Not all movers were shakers": founding an institution never guaranteed it would modernize taste.

**A political economy and geography of civic culture.** Across Manchester, Frankfurt, Leipzig, Boston, Chicago, Amsterdam, and Berlin, bourgeois "self-help" repeatedly substitutes for, or races, state and aristocratic patronage—Prussia's Landtag funding art by legislative appropriation even as Leipzig's Gewandhaus was raised by private Bürger money, Britain's National Gallery priding itself on owing "little to the State," Danish brewers rivaling the royal house. Gay's own aside—that "the history of Victorian bourgeois culture varied from place to place" yet the "resemblances were striking"—reads this as a genuinely comparative, near-simultaneous pattern rather than a set of isolated biographies.

**Founders as characters, not just donors.** Gay individuates his cases: the leisured, unmarried Sheepshanks versus the self-made "horse jobber" Vernon; Higginson's autobiographical letters framing music patronage as a refusal of Weber's not-yet-named "iron cage" of capitalist obsession; the Berlin Philharmoniker's founding as a "peaceful revolution" by rank-and-file musicians rather than a wealthy patron. Founding, for Gay, is as much a study in bourgeois psychology and self-narration as in institution-building.

**Class mobility inside the founding class.** The Städelsche Museums-Verein's steep membership fees excluded most of the bourgeoisie itself, but post-1871 Jewish emancipation produced a cohort—Ferdinand Hirsch first among them—disproportionately represented in the Verein's membership, which Gay reads as newly enfranchised wealth seeking integration among the "movers and shakers," one more current inside an already crowded motive-pool rather than a separate story.

## Notable passages

The analytic core is the taxonomy of donor motives in the unit's second paragraph, immediately tested against Carnegie's private guilt over the worship of money—Gay's clearest demonstration that philanthropic self-fashioning required real psychological work, not just a signed check. The "triangle" passage (donors, publics, managers) supplies the structural model Gay uses to explain why founding could cut toward either tradition or innovation. Higginson's credo—refusing the "iron cage of capitalist obsessions" a full generation before Weber coined the phrase—is Gay's fullest portrait of a founder's self-understanding. The Leipzig/Prussia contrast crystallizes the chapter's comparative method: state legislatures and private Bürger money both built culture, in the same decade, by different routes. The closing turn to the Städelsche Museums-Verein's Jewish membership after 1871 ties motive back to social history, and Gay's final aside resists reducing any of it to a single explanation.

This resonates, loosely, with the World Machines idea of a [[history-machine]]: Gay's biographical, "great-man" narration (Higginson single-handedly founding the Boston Symphony) sits over a pattern that in fact assembled independently and near-simultaneously in Frankfurt, Leipzig, Boston, Chicago, Amsterdam, and Berlin—the same bourgeois-patronage solution arrived at by unconnected local actors, closer to the "silently and distributedly" mode the history-machine concept names than to the individual-founder story Gay foregrounds.
