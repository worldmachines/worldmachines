# Guides to Pleasure: The Victorian Critic as Acknowledged Legislator of Taste

This unit opens Chapter Three of Peter Gay's *Pleasure Wars* with a brief survey of how Victorian bourgeois taste formed — self-education, status conformity (the invented Mrs. Bates and her Corot, "we let him go ahead"), or delegation to dealer-advisers like Mary Cassatt — before turning to section 1, "Guides to Pleasure," which argues that one institution rose above all others to shape nineteenth-century taste: the professional critic. Gay treats the critic's ascent as double-edged evidence about bourgeois autonomy, folds in a Freudian account of why aesthetic choice is never simply rational or free, and tracks critics' oscillation between grandiose self-importance and confessed futility, closing with the Wagner wars as criticism's most theatrical battlefield.

## Themes

**The critic's ascent is ambiguous evidence about bourgeois individualism.** Gay reads the rise of criticism as supporting two contrary readings of Victorian taste at once. Hazlitt's 1814 complaint that reputation is "stamped... by authority, not by popular consent," echoed by the *Musical Times*'s 1889 sneer at readers "of so invertebrate an order" that they cannot judge for themselves, casts the bourgeois public as docile followers. But critics like Théophile Gautier and Theodor Fontane wrote in an intimate, confessional first person that modeled private judgment as much as it dictated taste, "throwing... bridges between critic and reader."

**Clashing critical authority paradoxically liberated taste rather than undermining it.** The same enlarged market for opinion that produced "a crisis in authority" also generated, in Gay's telling, "unexpected dividends for middle-class publics": contradictory verdicts on Beethoven's *Eroica* and on Constable's *Salisbury Cathedral from the Meadows* (hailed by some, denounced by the *Morning Chronicle* as "coarse, vulgar imitation") taught readers that expert consensus was unreliable — which "widened the critics' scope" rather than discrediting them, and let bourgeois consumers "go their own way."

**Freud reframes taste as never wholly free, and pleasure as structurally regressive.** Here Gay speaks in his own voice rather than through a subject's quotation: choice is shaped by political pressure, family demand, life stage, and above all buried childhood memory — "a man's home was often his parents' castle." Building on Quatremère de Quincy and Freud, Gay argues aesthetic pleasure is conservative by nature, since habit is psychologically easier to sustain than the "mental effort" originality demands — which is why the avant-garde's departure from convention provoked "furious opposition."

**An erotic subtext runs beneath official aesthetic pieties.** Etty's protestations of innocence ("I have never painted with a lascivious motive") sit against Thackeray's charge of "a classical and pictorial orgy," and William Mulready's private sketchbook admission that art "covertly exciting" will "sell much better" shows Victorians privately aware of a sexual ground for pleasure that public discourse disavowed — part of Gay's recurring claim that "civilization... exacts some necessary lies."

**Critics styled themselves pedagogues to a philistine bourgeois public — the bourgeoisophobe posture in miniature.** Shaw's "That great booby, the public... the British bourgeoisie" and Camille Pissarro's complaint that an exhibition "reeks powerfully of bourgeois" exemplify critics defining themselves against the very class that was mostly their readership and patronage base. This is a narrower, less totalizing cousin of [[carey-intellectuals-and-masses]]'s claim that modernist contempt was intellectuals' defensive response to a new mass public: Gay's Victorian critics are still trying to *educate* their bourgeois audience into better taste rather than seceding from it, so the contempt coexists with real pedagogical investment — Shaw insists the Londoner "is not indifferent to fine art: he hates it," which is engagement, not indifference. Shaw's flattening of an entire class into one collective "booby" also rhymes with [[the-mass-as-fiction]]'s point that "the mass" functions as an invented rhetorical category rather than a sociological description — Shaw needs a single stupid antagonist to justify his own vocation as guide.

**Critics oscillated between grandiose self-conception and confessed impotence.** Having claimed, in a formula borrowed from Shelley's "unacknowledged legislators" of poets, to be "the acknowledged legislators of taste," reviewers such as Hanslick, Gautier, and Fontane also confessed their work left "no permanent mark on anyone" — Gautier's "newspaper article is a shrub that loses its leaves every evening and never bears any fruit." Gay reads this fluctuation as symptomatic of "an age of vertiginous movement," though he notes it is belied by real evidence of influence: Monet's subscription to clipping services, Bruckner's dread of Hanslick's reviews. The oscillation is criticism's [[kulturkritik]] turned reflexively on itself — a mode of cultural judgment anxious about its own legitimacy and efficacy, not only about the art it judges.

**The Wagner wars were criticism's most theatrical arena.** Hanslick's sustained campaign against Bayreuth mixed genuine aesthetic conviction (his partisanship for Brahms), personal grievance (Wagner's caricature of him as Beckmesser, almost named "Hans Lick"), and family history (his Jewish mother, set against Wagner's antisemitism). Gay presents this as the clearest case of critics deploying "bellicose style" and "military metaphors" to dramatize their vocation as guardians of culture "against barbarians on the march."

## Notable passages

Hazlitt's 1814 remark that reputation is conferred "by authority, not by popular consent" opens the section's case that critical deference could look like conformism (c1); a generation later Shaw collapses an entire public into "that great booby... the British bourgeoisie," the sharpest single line of critical contempt in the unit (c2). Gay's own Freudian paragraph — choice is never "wholly free," and "a man's home was often his parents' castle" — is the unit's clearest statement of his own argument rather than a subject's view (c3). Pissarro's diary complaint that an exhibition "reeks powerfully of bourgeois" captures the bourgeoisophobe posture in its purest, most casual form (c4). The passage tracing "acknowledged legislators of taste" back to Shelley's "unacknowledged legislators" of poets supplies the chapter's title and its thesis in one move (c5). Finally, the description of Hanslick's "bellicose style" and the critics' self-dramatizing "military metaphors" shows how war-rhetoric let critics claim they "mattered because they were defending the heights of culture" (c6).
