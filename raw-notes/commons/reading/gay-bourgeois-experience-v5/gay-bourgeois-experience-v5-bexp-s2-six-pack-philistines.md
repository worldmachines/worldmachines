# Six-Pack Philistines: A Genealogy of Bourgeoisophobia

Gay traces how scattered eighteenth-century grievances against merchants hardened, over the nineteenth century, into a self-conscious crusade of artists and intellectuals against the bourgeois public: a lineage running from Goethe's Werther through the German romantics and Nietzsche's coinages (“Bildungsphilister,” the vernacular “six-pack philistine”) to the French avant-garde's ritual chant of “bourgeois” as insult, spilling over into political extremism (left and right alike, and eventually anti-Semitic caricature) and even complicating a sympathetic critic like Marx. Gay's own argument, though, cuts against the bourgeoisophobes' self-image: the artist's supposed independence from the hated bourgeois public was historically achieved largely with bourgeois money and consumption, not against it — a claim he tests in closing against the caricature of the middle-class piano.

## Themes

**A genealogy from Goethe to Nietzsche's coinages.** Gay locates the origin of Victorian bourgeoisophobia in the Sturm und Drang, with Goethe's Werther as “probably the first modern alienated soul” convinced the bourgeois can never share the artist's passions; Goethe himself kept recycling the martyred-genius pose into old age. German romantics (E. T. A. Hoffmann's mad musician Kreisler, later claimed by Schumann and Brahms) and mid-century critics (von Marées, Billroth, Liliencron, Treitschke) widened the split, but it is Nietzsche who supplies the movement's defining vocabulary: “Bildungsphilister” (cultivated philistine) and, in imitators' hands, the “six-pack philistine” (Weissbierphilister) that gives this unit its title — Nietzsche's “herd animal” bourgeois who has been to the university but remains mediocre to the bone. This herd-versus-genius framing loosely rhymes with [[mass-man]] and Ortega's diagnosis in [[ortega-revolt-of-the-masses]], though Nietzsche's target is specifically the credentialed, tasteful bourgeois rather than the mass public as such.

**The French escalation and the epithet as ritual insult.** Musset, Heine, Gautier, Zola, Hugo, and even Proust intensify the charge until “bourgeois” becomes an almost content-free taunt. Gay's prize specimen is Zola's tirade against Barbey d'Aurevilly, chanting “Bourgeois! bourgeois!” until the word loses argumentative content and becomes pure provocation — an escalation Zola half-undercuts himself by protesting elsewhere that “we are all bourgeois,” Goethe and Diderot included. This self-defeating universalizing of the epithet resonates with [[the-mass-as-fiction]]'s point that “the mass” (here, “the bourgeois”) is as much an invented rhetorical category as a real referent, and with [[carey-intellectuals-and-masses]]'s account of the literary intelligentsia's defensive contempt for its public — though Carey's frame is contempt for a mass audience, while Gay's bourgeoisophobes are attacking a specific class fraction, often one that is buying their books and pianos.

**Aesthetic contempt fuses with political extremism.** Quarrels over taste become quarrels over politics: reactionary monarchists (Maurras) and economic critics of capitalism (Sombart) join hands with left-wing agitators in loathing the bourgeoisie, and by century's end the amalgam absorbs anti-Semitism, with “the modern Jew” cast by some as “bourgeoisdom incarnate.” Gay flags this convergence without softening it — it is where an aesthetic pose curdles into something more dangerous.

**Marx's split judgment.** Gay isolates a real tension in Marx: the philosopher of history credits the bourgeoisie with an epochal, world-building achievement (a world economy, great cities, escape from “the idiocy of rural life”), while the political agitator supplies the durable caricature — hypocritical, mercenary, a “moneymaking machine” professing pieties it systematically violates. It is the polemical half, Gay notes, that later generations remembered and generalized into an all-purpose insult, detached from Marx's own analytical framework.

**Gay's counterargument: the myth of the independent artist.** The unit's real hinge is Gay's own claim, not a subject's quotation: the shift from artists as courtiers and servants (Michelangelo, Velázquez, Racine) through Enlightenment freelancers who first faced the marketplace (Pope, Johnson, Voltaire) to the romantic ideal of an artist estranged from and superior to his public (Shelley, Beethoven) is a real historical development — but one whose “independence” was, Gay insists, “often achieved not in the face of bourgeois but with their aid,” a qualification the avant-garde itself never acknowledged.

**The bourgeois piano as test case.** Gay closes by setting the caricature of piano-mania — status-climbing families buying an instrument as a “mantrap,” daughters drilled into neurosis over tone-deaf practice — against contrary evidence: Gladstone's diary recording genuine pleasure in family music-making, and the simple economic fact that a good piano remained a luxury far into the century. It is a compact instance of Gay's characteristic method: using the historical record's mixed evidence to complicate a stereotype the bourgeoisophobes themselves manufactured and propagated.

## Notable passages

The Goethe/Werther passage anchors the genealogy at its Sturm-und-Drang origin, including Goethe's own late-life insistence (via Eckermann) that society inevitably martyrs its worthiest writers. The Nietzsche passage supplies the unit's key terminology, moving from the philosophical coinage “Bildungsphilister” to the joke-coinage “six-pack philistine” that names this section, and to Nietzsche's “herd animal” bourgeois. The Zola/Barbey d'Aurevilly passage is the rhetorical extreme of the French tradition, the epithet “bourgeois” chanted until it becomes pure aggression. The Marx passage shows Gay isolating the tension between Marx-the-historian's respect and Marx-the-agitator's caricature. The patronage-history passage is Gay's own framing argument about how artists' claimed independence from the bourgeois public actually developed, and where it was compromised. The piano passage is the chapter's closing empirical counterweight to the bourgeoisophobe caricature.
