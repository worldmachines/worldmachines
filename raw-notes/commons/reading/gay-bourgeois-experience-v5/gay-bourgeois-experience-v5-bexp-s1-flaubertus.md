# Bourgeoisophobes: Flaubert and the Modernist Myth of the Philistine Bourgeois

Peter Gay opens the chapter that gives *Pleasure Wars* its central drama: sometime around 1800 the age-old compact binding artists to the powers they served broke down, and painters, composers, and writers began making bourgeois society itself the target of their scorn. He calls the resulting story — noble avant-gardes defending "honest art" against a philistine, money-loving middle class — a "modernist myth," partisan and oversimplified though not baseless, and spends the rest of the unit testing it against its most extreme exponent, Gustave Flaubert, and against the real social texture of Flaubert's own city, Rouen.

## Themes

**The modernist myth has a kernel of truth but is a caricature.** Gay's frame for the whole volume: the nineteenth-century bourgeoisie's supposed philistinism licensed the avant-garde's self-image as an embattled minority rescuing art, music, and literature, but the "sweeping characterization" ignored the exceptions, hesitations, and mixed motives that would complicate any real cultural history.

**Flaubert is the paradigm case, and he supplies his own emblem for the syndrome.** Gay reaches for the private letter in which Flaubert signed himself "GUSTAVUS FLAUBERTUS, Bourgeoisophobus" as the name for a lifelong, near-physical loathing — bourgeois encountered in the street "made me want to vomit and to cry at the same time" — that ran from schoolboy provocations through Madame Bovary's rewarded careerist Homais to the copyist-clerks of *Bouvard and Pécuchet*.

**"Bourgeois" in Flaubert's mouth is a curse, not a sociological category.** Gay's sharpest evidentiary move is to catch Flaubert applying the word to "the bourgeois in a smock as much as the bourgeois in a frock coat," to the Paris Commune's workers ("messieurs the working people into the same bag as bourgeois"), and, as early as 1852, to "all humanity." A term stretched that far cannot do the analytic work later readers credited it with; Gay backs this with the novelist Paul Bourget's contemporary verdict that in Flaubert's mouth "bourgeois" became "a synonym for the worst villainies."

**The empirical Rouen refutes the caricature Flaubert projected onto it.** Against Flaubert's flattened "lumpen bourgeois," Gay sets a steeply stratified city — petit-bourgeois shopkeepers, comfortable rentiers, a millionaire mercantile elite — that nonetheless sustained a literary review, a new museum, reading circles, and above all François Depeaux, a cotton-and-shipping magnate who spent two decades collecting Impressionists and eventually donated fifty-three paintings to the city. The donation's two-stage fate — rejected once, celebrated a few years later — is Gay's miniature case study in the political economy of art and the uneven geography of taste that structure the rest of the volume.

**Gay's own diagnosis is Freudian: phobia as a defense against self-recognition.** The unit closes by turning the psychoanalytic lens on Flaubert himself. "No one embodied the middle-class gospel of work... more intensely than Flaubert," and his fury is read as a projection warding off the fear that he had never really escaped his own class, reinforced by Freud's "narcissism of minor difference." Gay's closing methodological claim — that even a distorted, symptom-laden perception is still a historical fact with consequences — is the move that lets the chapter treat Flaubert's rhetoric as evidence without accepting it as sociology.

## Notable passages

- **c1** states the chapter's governing thesis in Gay's own voice: the avant-garde's self-image as defender of honest art against a philistine bourgeoisie is a partisan myth, "self-serving and partisan," with only "a measure of truth" — the claim the rest of the unit tests. This framing echoes, without being identical to, [[carey-intellectuals-and-masses]]: Carey's modernists defend against a newly literate mass *readership*, while Gay's Flaubert (writing in 1850s provincial Rouen) is reacting to an established commercial class, not a newly enfranchised reading public — a difference of target and timing worth keeping alongside the resonance.
- **c2** gives Flaubert's own coinage, "Bourgeoisophobus," the emblem from which the unit (and the chapter and this note) take their name.
- **c3** is the crux of Gay's case against taking Flaubert at his word: "bourgeois" stretched to cover workers, ladies, and "all humanity" is a curse word doing the work of an all-purpose enemy, not a sociological category. The move is structurally close to [[the-mass-as-fiction]] — Carey's argument that "the mass" is an invented rhetorical category serving the critic's self-image — though Flaubert's enemy is a class label turned promiscuous rather than an invented multitude; the family resemblance is in the elasticity, not the referent.
- **c4** is Gay's counter-evidence: François Depeaux's Impressionist collection and civic donation show a real Rouen bourgeoisie capable of exactly the modern taste Flaubert denied it, in miniature the volume's recurring argument about the political economy and geography of art patronage.
- **c5** delivers Gay's own psychoanalytic verdict on the bourgeoisophobe stance itself, reading Flaubert's phobia as a defense against recognizing his own inescapable bourgeois nature — the paradigm instance, within this unit, of the self-reflexive, defense-laden cultural criticism that [[kulturkritik]] names, aimed here at the critic (Flaubert) rather than at "mass" culture.
