# The Perspiring Philistine on Trial

Two case studies show bourgeois taste conflicts playing out through public institutions in the late nineteenth century: Alfred Lichtwark's turbulent directorship of the Hamburg Kunsthalle, which climaxed in the scandal over Max Liebermann's portrait of Mayor Carl Petersen, and Gustave Caillebotte's bequest of his Impressionist collection to the French state, which triggered a bitter fight over how much of it the Luxembourg would accept. Gay reads both episodes as evidence that bourgeois taste was never a unified philistine front — the sharpest culture wars over modern art ran among the bourgeoisie itself and through the institutions bourgeois society had built to arbitrate taste.

## Themes

**Museum directors as combatants, not neutral curators.** Lichtwark, appointed to the Kunsthalle in 1886, exemplifies the "new men in authority" who shaped taste from inside public institutions. Museum directors — Lichtwark in Hamburg, Hugo von Tschudi in Berlin, Gustav Pauli in Bremen (himself "virulently abused as a 'slave to foreigners' for buying a van Gogh") — "acted as important collectors in their own right—with other people's money," turning museums built for civic education into instruments of a director's own aesthetic program. Lichtwark "transmuted his bailiwick into a superb didactic instrument," buying Renoirs and Monets and reviving neglected Germans like Runge and Caspar David Friedrich.

**Taste as national ethics: Lichtwark's civic mission.** For Lichtwark taste was not private preference but "an indispensable constituent and telltale sign of character," and collective good taste "almost literally a matter of national health"; aesthetics and ethics became "a kind of spiritual medicine." His campaign aimed to cure a newly unified Germany's provincial fragmentation by completing its Bildung, since Germans had "for too long lived for intelligence."

**A domestic bourgeoisophobe: Lichtwark turns on his own class.** Though himself bourgeois and embedded among Hamburg's Bürger, Lichtwark denounced the bourgeoisie as a "parvenu" type, "the born and sworn enemy of all artistic independence," which reduces art "to its level." Gay measures this explicitly against the French pattern — "No French bourgeoisophobe could have put it more strongly" — making the point that contempt for the philistine bourgeois was not solely an imported Flaubertian pose but could be voiced by a bourgeois institution-builder in the service of reform rather than aesthetic secession. This resonates with [[carey-intellectuals-and-masses]]'s account of a cultured elite's defensive contempt for its own public, though the fit is partial: Lichtwark's target is his own class's taste rather than "the masses" at large, and his posture is patriotic reform, not retreat into art-for-art's-sake. The broader German idiom of taste-as-national-character also echoes [[kulturkritik]]'s self-reflexive mode of cultural diagnosis.

**The Liebermann affair as intrabourgeois conflict, not avant-garde-versus-bourgeois.** Petersen himself detested his commissioned portrait as "unflattering, tasteless, a mere caricature," and the Kunsthalle kept it behind a curtain until 1905. Gay explicitly declines to read this as modernist-versus-philistine: "nothing very radical about the portrait, nothing in any way disrespectful" by later standards; the ferocity came instead from "the narcissism of minor differences," an intensified hostility toward positions that deviate only slightly from one's own. Liebermann, Gay concludes, "was the victim of an intrabourgeois conflict."

**Bourgeois philanthropy as backer, not only enemy, of modern art.** Caillebotte joins Daniel Wadsworth, J. M. W. Turner, and the Rouennais collector Depeaux as bourgeois figures whose fortunes — often made in trade — funded the public reception of art their own class largely resisted. Caillebotte used his textile-and-real-estate wealth to inflate Impressionist prices for his impecunious painter friends, then used his will to force his collection into the Luxembourg and eventually the Louvre.

**Complicating "progress versus reaction": institutional constraint, not only ideology.** Jean-Louis Gérôme's tirade against the "excrement" of Caillebotte's bequest — "this is anarchy" — supplies the era's most quotable philistinism, but Gay insists the administrators' hesitation also reflected a genuine institutional constraint (a long-standing rule against hanging more than three or four canvases by one artist), not simply "an ossified aesthetic ideology." The affair marks, in Gay's closing image, the coming "breakup of a great if shaky bourgeois compromise" that modernism was about to force into the open — a fault line opening within a settled cultural order rather than a straightforward duel between camps.

## Notable passages

The Lichtwark paragraph on Bildung and national health (c1) is the clearest statement of his fused aesthetic-ethical nationalism: taste reform as the unfinished business of German unification. The indictment of the bourgeoisie as "parvenu" (c2), paired with Gay's own aside that no French bourgeoisophobe went further, is the unit's sharpest instance of Gay's method — letting a subject's quoted voice carry the extremity of a position while Gay's framing sentence supplies the comparative judgment. The passage on the Liebermann scandal (c3) contains Gay's own analytic key term for the whole unit, "intrabourgeois conflict," and should anchor any account of why bourgeois taste battles were rarely two-sided. Gérôme's outburst (c4) is Gay's set-piece of philistine panic dressed as connoisseurship, useful as evidence rather than argument. The closing passage (c5) is Gay's own synthesis, explicitly rejecting the simple "duel between the defenders of the past and the heralds of the future" reading of the Caillebotte affair in favor of a picture of two educator factions fighting over how much risk a shared civilizing project could absorb.
