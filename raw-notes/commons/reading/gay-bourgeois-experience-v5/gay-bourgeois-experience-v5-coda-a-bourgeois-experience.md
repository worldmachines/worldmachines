# Coda: A Bourgeois Experience

This is Peter Gay's closing apologia to the five-volume series *The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud*, written as he finishes Vol. V (*Pleasure Wars*). He recounts the project's twenty-five-year genesis, defends his psychoanalytic method against critics, and summarizes the revisionist argument running through all five volumes: that the Victorian bourgeoisie was far more diverse, and far less philistine, than the standard caricature allows.

## Themes

**A project that found its subject by accident.** Gay set out in 1971 to write about the late-Victorian culture heroes who led the West into modernism (Henry James, Manet, Ibsen, Zola, Mahler). Within a few years he realized that studying these giants was keeping him from "a splendid historical subject right before my eyes, the bourgeoisie, which had been insufficiently mapped and tendentiously treated." The series that resulted — *Education of the Senses*, *The Tender Passion*, *The Cultivation of Hatred*, *The Naked Heart*, and this volume — became an inquiry into a class rather than a canon.

**The postwar eclipse of the bourgeoisie as a subject.** Gay traces how postwar social history, energized by the Annales school (Marc Bloch) and a populist, often left-leaning impulse, turned historians away from "leading actors" toward "the neglected and the despised — women, peasants, working people, religious and racial minorities." This was a genuine advance, he says, but its byproduct was a relative neglect of the bourgeoisie, a class "problematic all the way back to Aristotle." His own work started from that blank space on the map. This is a case of a discipline's paradigm shifting by accretion — a new historiographical common sense (social history from below) assembling itself and then leaving its own blind spot, which resonates with the [[history-machine]] idea of a paradigm that displaces "great men" historiography and then itself becomes taken for granted; unlike Lohse's silent, distributed assembly, Gay narrates this shift as visible and self-conscious even while it was happening.

**Evidentiary method: waiting for the private record.** The turn to psychoanalytic history needed intimate material that reticent Victorians rarely left behind. Gay's break came when his wife, an archivist, brought home a photocopy of Mabel Loomis Todd's diary — an uncommonly explicit record of an affair with Austin Dickinson (Emily Dickinson's brother) — which became the germ of *Education of the Senses*. He then went looking for, and found, comparable troves elsewhere.

**"A historian informed by psychoanalysis," not a psychohistorian.** Gay defends his reliance on Freud against two opposite complaints: "ordinary" historians think he leans on Freud too much, psychohistorians think not enough. He insists conventional history and psychoanalytic interpretation are "inextricably intertwined" in his work, not substitutes for each other, and that Freud himself denied the mind is sealed off from the world — only the id is wholly shielded; ego and superego "wrestle with their environment." He cites Freud's own observation that the Oedipus complex "has a history, a social history," differently expressed by Sophocles and Shakespeare because their cultural epochs differed. This is why Gay resists the label psychohistorian and prefers to think of himself as a historian who uses psychoanalysis as one tool among others, keeping causal weight on the external, social world.

**The bourgeoisophobe myth and its genealogy.** The coda's sharpest argument, and the hinge into Vol. V's actual subject, is that the received image of the Victorian bourgeois as a philistine who "loved money and hated art" is not a neutral historical finding but the residue of a polemical tradition. Gay traces this "bourgeoisophobia" back through Flaubert (who coined the term he borrows), to the German *Sturm und Drang* and Goethe's Werther, through the romantics (E.T.A. Hoffmann), into modernism's self-image of the artist as "alienated from, and superior to," the bourgeois public. Later historians, he argues, took this self-interested polemic literally instead of treating it as one interested party's rhetoric. This closely parallels [[carey-intellectuals-and-masses]] — Carey's claim that modernism was the literary intelligentsia's defensive contempt for a new mass public — though Gay's target is a specific class (the bourgeoisie as patrons and consumers of art) rather than "the masses" as such, and Gay is explicitly trying to rehabilitate the target of the polemic rather than diagnose the polemicists' anxiety. The move of treating an invented, self-serving caricature as literal sociological description is also structurally the move [[the-mass-as-fiction]] names in Carey ("the mass" as an invented rhetorical category); Gay's bourgeois philistine is that same kind of retrospectively fictionalized adversary. The whole bourgeoisophobe lineage — a tradition of intellectuals defining themselves through contempt for the public that supposedly cannot appreciate them — is also a specific historical instance of [[kulturkritik]], the self-reflexive mode of cultural critique, here traced from Sturm und Drang through the romantics to modernist self-fashioning.

**The revisionist thesis, volume by volume.** Gay compresses the whole series: sexual life among Victorian bourgeois was more varied and more pleasurable than the legend of repression allows (*Education of the Senses*, *The Tender Passion*); public aggression was channeled into rule-making — child labor laws, penal reform, the invention of the sports umpire — rather than merely repressed (*The Cultivation of Hatred*); the romantic reaction against Enlightenment rationalism produced an "inward turn" institutionalized in diaries, letters, and companionate marriage (*The Naked Heart*). None of this is offered as an apology for real hypocrisy, exploitation, or cruelty among the bourgeoisie, which Gay explicitly does not deny.

**Taste as the site of final fragmentation.** By the 1880s, Gay argues, whatever rough consensus defined "bourgeois" collapsed most visibly in the domain of taste — the subject of this volume. He insists that participation in high culture tracked financial resources (prices of admission, of originals versus reproductions) and differed by the political economy of patronage in each city: bourgeois who funded the arts themselves (Manchester), bourgeois who relied on a ruling house (Munich), and cities where private and state support competed (Paris, Berlin). Critics, collectors, and museum founders are named as the other decisive intermediaries who shaped what "good taste" came to mean. This geography-of-patronage argument is the political economy of art and geography of taste this project cares about in Vol. V specifically.

**Diversity over caricature.** Gay's summary self-image is pointillist: "local varieties that, once the viewer steps back, should cohere into a whole." He quotes Walter Gropius's boyhood answer to his favorite color — "multicolored — bunt" — as an emblem for the whole bourgeois class. The insistence on radical internal variety against a single legible "bourgeois type" has a loose, partial resonance with [[divergence-machine]]'s picture of variety and proliferation resisting a single legible ordering — though Gay's frame is a historiographical argument about one social class's psychology and taste, not an account of institutions or infrastructure, so the fit is suggestive rather than structural.

## Notable passages

The bourgeoisophobe genealogy paragraph is the load-bearing argument of the whole coda, tracing the philistine-bourgeois myth from Flaubert back through Goethe's Werther and the romantics into modernism's self-image. The Mabel Loomis Todd anecdote shows the contingent, archival origin of the entire series' evidentiary method. The Freud/Oedipus passage carries Gay's defense of his "historian informed by psychoanalysis" self-description against critics on both flanks. The geography-of-patronage passage (Manchester/Munich/Paris-Berlin) states in miniature the political-economy-of-art argument this volume develops at length. The social-history paragraph documents the postwar historiographical turn that left the bourgeoisie an understudied subject in the first place.
