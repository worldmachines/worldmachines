# Master Craftsmen: Sainte-Beuve and Thoré

Gay profiles two exemplary Victorian critics as disciplined "master craftsmen" of criticism—Charles-Augustin Sainte-Beuve, whose 1857 review of Madame Bovary asserted the critic's autonomous authority in more measured terms than Wilde's, and Théophile Thoré, the democratic-socialist critic who single-handedly built Vermeer's reputation—before surveying how far critical authority actually reached compared with the commercial pull of popular serial fiction.

## Themes

- **The critic as scientific craftsman, not just partisan or genius.** Sainte-Beuve modeled criticism as "historical explanation": embedding a work in its author and era, working from "solid information rather than dreamy free associations." He called himself "a naturalist of minds" aiming at "literary natural history"—criticism professionalized into a quasi-scientific discipline, weekly and disciplined (the Causeries du lundi), rather than an art of spontaneous taste.

- **The critic's bourgeois double nature.** Despite claims to Olympian objectivity, Sainte-Beuve is, in Gay's own framing, "a good bourgeois in his own way": he praises "bourgeois simplicity" and "incorruptible even temper," and common sense and hard work as the middle class's supreme virtues, and his prodigious weekly output over decades enacts the bourgeois work ethic he admires. The Goncourts denounced him as "a hopeless bourgeois" even as they conceded his gifts—Gay's recurring point that the critic who judges bourgeois taste is himself formed by it.

- **Radical politics versus aesthetic autonomy in Thoré.** A democratic activist imprisoned and exiled for his socialism, Thoré wanted "ART FOR MAN" and an art that would not "exclude so-called ordinary mortals," yet came to insist that art "has beauty for its subject matter, not ideas" and that "the subject hardly matters." His championing of Rembrandt and the Dutch "little masters" over the Italian schools, and of realism over academic mythology, fused his politics and his taste without ever fully resolving the tension between them.

- **The political economy of reputation-building.** Thoré's decades-long, near-obsessive campaign for the then-obscure Vermeer—he "had happened upon a Sphinx"—shows how a critic's advocacy slowly manufactures a canon and a market: Vermeer's prices crept from roughly $200–$2,500 in Thoré's lifetime to $8,000 by 1888, a generation after his rediscovery. Even a sympathetic expert like Fromentin walked past the "splendid Vermeers" without registering Thoré's find.

- **Critical authority was real but secondary to entertainment economics.** The unit's closing survey of critics who reached large bourgeois readerships (Sainte-Beuve, Arnold, Fontane, Brandes, Lanson, Saintsbury) is undercut by the Constitutionnel case: the paper's subscriber base was resurrected not by its critics but by paying Eugène Sue 100,000 francs for a sequel to The Mysteries of Paris. Gay leaves open whether these new readers "bothered to absorb the critics appearing in the same issue."

## Notable passages

- Sainte-Beuve's 1857 review declares the work "accountable to criticism alone" and casts the "true critic" as one who must "speak up, to greet and salute the work as it passes by"—an assertion of stature Gay calls "sturdy."
- The Causeries du lundi passage where Sainte-Beuve names himself "a naturalist of minds" pursuing "literary natural history" captures the scientizing, craftsmanlike self-image behind his authority.
- The "good bourgeois" paragraph is Gay's clearest statement of the critic's identification with the class he serves and judges.
- Thoré's 1848 outburst ("we are making living history") and his later "ART FOR MAN" credo show a critic trying to fuse revolutionary politics with aesthetic judgment.
- The Vermeer passages trace, in miniature, how criticism performs the political economy of art: advocacy, scarcity, slow recognition, and eventual price appreciation.
- The Constitutionnel circulation numbers puncture any triumphalist reading of critical influence: popular fiction, not criticism, moved subscriptions.

This resonates lightly with [[kulturkritik]]: Sainte-Beuve explicitly casts himself as "custodian of literary quality," anxious that the "condition of criticism" itself was declining in his own age—criticism turning reflexively on its own legitimacy, though his mode is scholarly-institutional rather than the more totalizing civilizational diagnosis kulturkritik usually names. The Sainte-Beuve/Thoré contrast also partly rhymes with [[carey-intellectuals-and-masses]]: Sainte-Beuve's guardianship of "standards" against "riots of imagination unchecked" sits closer to Carey's defensive intelligentsia posture, while Thoré's explicit "art for man" democratism is close to the contrary case within the very same profession—two poles of the same bourgeois critical class that Gay's bourgeoisophobes (treated elsewhere in the volume) reject altogether.
