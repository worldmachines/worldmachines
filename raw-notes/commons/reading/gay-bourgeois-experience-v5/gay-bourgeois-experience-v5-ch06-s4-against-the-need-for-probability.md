# Against the Need for Probability

Surveying painting and architecture together, Gay traces modernism's self-justifying claim that it was liberating art from “the need for probability”—from resemblance, convention, and external rule—in favor of “inner necessity”: Gauguin's break with Impressionist verisimilitude, the Fauves' and Expressionists' assault on natural color and form, and the arrival of full abstraction in Kandinsky, Mondrian, and Delaunay, with Picasso's Cubism as a one-man culmination. But the chapter's real argument is about the bourgeois public standing behind this “pleasure war”: modernist architecture needed wealthy, open-minded clients before a sketch could become a building, and modernist painting's most bourgeoisophobic practitioners were rescued, financed, and collected by bourgeois patrons. Gay closes by attacking the durable myth—illustrated through a Frans Coenen novel—that the Victorian bourgeoisie was a monolith of philistine antimodernism.

## Themes

- **"Inner necessity" against "probability."** Gauguin's diagnosis that the Impressionists remained “shackled by the need for probability” becomes the movement's founding complaint: von Hartmann's claim that “external laws do not exist,” Kandinsky's language of “inner necessity” and the artist as “superman against philistinism,” and Franz Marc's cry that art means “expression alone.” The escalation runs in one direction—unnatural color, then distorted form, then, in Kandinsky, Mondrian, and Delaunay just before the war, the total abandonment of recognizable subject matter.

- **Bourgeoisophobia as domestic and generational revolt, not simple class war.** Gauguin calls his abandoned wife a “filthy bourgeoise”; Picasso frames his whole career as needing to “kill one's father,” against a father who called him, in return, “bourgeois, bourgeois, bourgeois.” Gay reads this through mixed, partly Oedipal feeling rather than programmatic ideology. This half-resonates with [[carey-intellectuals-and-masses]]: Carey's literary intelligentsia scorned a new mass reading public defensively, from above; Gay's bourgeoisophobes mostly scorn backward, at their own class of origin and its domestic constraints, and (as the chapter goes on to show) still needed that class's money.

- **The political economy of art rescues the art that damns it.** Picasso's admirers were as poor as he was until Gertrude and Leo Stein, then the Cone sisters, then Shchukin and Morosov, bought him out of indigence; in architecture, Gaudi's Catalan bankers and industrialists, AEG's commission to Peter Behrens, and Frank Lloyd Wright's Republican Chicago clients all supplied the capital that turned iconoclastic sketches into finished buildings. Modernism required bourgeois money and bourgeois risk tolerance to exist materially, regardless of what its rhetoric said about bourgeois taste.

- **Architecture's "honesty" rhetoric as a moralized case for divergence over rule.** Ruskin, Kerr, and Mackintosh all moralize construction as “truthful” or “sincere”; Ruskin's claim that no architectural law survives “the arising of a new condition, or the invention of a new material” is a small manifesto for letting technical novelty override inherited convention. That resonates loosely with WMP's [[divergence-machine]]—proliferation and local improvisation outrunning a shared, checkable order—against the resemblance-bound aesthetic Gay calls “probability,” itself a distant cousin of [[legibility]]'s demand for a standardized, readable order; the analogy is inexact since Gay describes craft and taste convention, not state administrative capacity.

- **Proliferation, not disappearance, of style.** Franz Marc mourns that “there is no style left” in the wake of modernism, but Gay corrects him: the actual crisis was not an absence of shared style but an explosion of competing ones—literally divergence rather than collapse, playing out again in the very different local modernisms of Barcelona, Glasgow, Berlin, and Chicago.

- **Gay's revisionist thesis: no philistine monolith.** The chapter's close directly attacks the received caricature—illustrated by curator-novelist Frans Coenen's Victorian bourgeois as uniformly “stiff,” rule-conforming philistines—arguing instead that the Victorian bourgeoisie was “doctrinaire modernism, resolute antimodernism, unstable compromises” all at once. This is close kin to [[the-mass-as-fiction]]: a polemical caricature (coined largely by the bourgeoisophobes themselves) that later historians mistook for sociological description. The same self-scrutinizing, moralized argument-about-culture that runs through the architects' "honesty" debates is itself a small case study in [[kulturkritik]].

## Notable passages

- Gauguin's verdict that the Impressionists were “shackled by the need for probability” and “still... taking orders from outside,” versus the modernists who “worked from the inside out”—the chapter's organizing opposition, stated in its most compressed form.
- Franz Marc's lament that “there is no style left,” which Gay reframes: the real difficulty was not the absence of styles but that “there were so many”—proliferation, not collapse.
- The plain statement that “middle-class collectors... rescued Picasso from indigence and saved his career,” immediately followed by his meeting with Gertrude and Leo Stein—the pivot from bohemian poverty to bourgeois patronage.
- Gertrude Stein's own defense, in her early fiction, of “a material middle class with its straightened bond of family” as “the one thing always healthy, human, vital”—a bourgeois collector of radical art who never renounced bourgeois values.
- Ruskin's claim in The Seven Lamps of Architecture that no architectural law survives “the arising of a new condition, or the invention of a new material,” used to license engineers' iron and steel over inherited style.
- The closing thesis that the Victorian bourgeoisie was simultaneously modernist, antimodernist, and everything in between, against the durable myth of bourgeois-as-philistine.
