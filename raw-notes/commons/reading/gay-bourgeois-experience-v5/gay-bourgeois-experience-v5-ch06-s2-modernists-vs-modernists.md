# Modernists vs. Modernists

Gay argues that both halves of the standard modernism-versus-bourgeoisie story are oversimplified: middle-class audiences were not forced into a binary choice between academicism and the avant-garde but could settle anywhere along a graduated spectrum of "pleasing compromises," and the modernists themselves, far from a unified insurgent front, spent as much energy feuding with each other as attacking the bourgeois enemy. The chapter section moves from the art market's middle ground, through comparative national/urban tolerance for the avant-garde, into a catalogue of modernist infighting (Vienna's architects, Berlin's Secession, Mondrian on Cubism, Munch on Impressionism), and closes by tracing modernists' ambivalent, self-mythologizing relationship to both the past (romanticism, craft traditions) and the future (the proliferation of "New"/"Young"/avant-garde movement-brands), ending on the observation that this fractious "gestation of modernism" was decades in the making before nineteenth-century historicism finally lost its grip.

## Themes

**A market of graduated compromises, not a binary choice.** Gay opens by insisting that "generalizations suffer shipwreck on the rock of diversity": bourgeois collectors did not have to choose between Bouguereau and Braque. The *juste milieu* — Sargent, Tissot, minor salon landscapists bought by the state — let buyers own "modernist pretensions" while staying "safely within the accepted canon." The same graded tolerance held in music (Debussy yes, Stravinsky maybe, Schoenberg no) and theater (Ibsen yes, Strindberg maybe, Jarry's *Ubu roi* no). This is the political economy of taste at street level: consumption as a spectrum of risk tolerance, not a referendum.

**Geography and institutions shaped how much dissent was tolerable.** "Attending to the avant-garde did not mean to approve," and tolerance varied by place: French novelists could explore what English or American readers found indecent; Vienna's academic establishment was hostile enough to Schoenberg that he fled to Berlin, and later refused a post there rather than face his old audience again ("I am not yet reconciled"). This is Gay's recurring geography-of-taste argument — commercial/cosmopolitan centers versus more entrenched, tradition-bound ones — applied here to degrees of avant-garde tolerance rather than state patronage.

**Modernists turned their weapons on each other at least as often as on the bourgeoisie.** Vienna's Hoffmann and Loos loathed one another; Richard Strauss, once Schoenberg's champion, later suggested he "shovel snow instead of scribbling on music paper"; Mondrian faulted Cubism for stopping short of pure abstraction; Munch dismissed the Impressionists as mere "façade people" lacking psychological depth. Movements built on secession from an establishment routinely re-split when their own vanguard was denounced as too tame — Berlin's Secession fractured when it could not absorb the Expressionists and Nolde turned on Liebermann. Gay's line names the mechanism precisely: "One generation's unconventionality was the next generation's conformism."

**Attitudes toward the bourgeoisie itself ran the full range, not just contempt.** Against the received image of a monolithic bourgeoisophobia, Gay lists dissenters: Baudelaire praised the art-loving middle class and expected its patronage; Apollinaire pleaded, "We are not your enemies"; Manet, immune to bourgeoisophobia, wanted the Légion d'honneur more than anything and refused to exhibit with the Impressionists despite kinship (Pissarro's private verdict: Manet "believed in the establishment, he aspired to honors"). Against these stand the purists who "lived" the antibourgeois pose rather than merely voicing it — Gauguin abandoning banking and family, Rimbaud's self-destructive itinerancy — and the cheerful contempt of figures like Debussy, who joined "the pack of bourgeoisophobes" and prized the "mediocrity of the herd mind" as a foil, so that to be despised by ordinary audiences became, for many, "a virtual credential of authentic modernism."

**The Liebermann case models the "moving frontier" thesis.** Liebermann leapt once from academicism to Impressionism, founded the Berlin Secession, defended Munch — yet drew his own line at Fauvism, Cubism, and nonobjective art, admitting he might have blind spots ("the iconoclasts of one generation become the conservatives of the next") while still insisting, "No one can leap over his shadow." Modernism had no fixed content, only a receding edge that each cohort redefined and then, having arrived, defended.

**Modernism's paradoxical relation to the past and to "newness" as self-branding.** The moderns were romanticism's heirs (belief in individual genius, revolt against external pressure) and its critics (they "hunted in gangs" and had no patience for nostalgia), even as many invoked older authorities — Schoenberg's Brahms, Morris's medieval craft, Kandinsky's folk art, Stravinsky's church music — chiefly to shame contemporary "wretched hypocrisies." The proliferation of self-declared "New"/"Young" movements and little magazines (Jugendstil, La Jeune Belgique, Arte Joven, Les XX, Jüngstdeutschland, the Futurists) shows modernism institutionalizing its own claim to stand at the leading edge of time — "Il faut être de son temps" — a decades-long gestation, Gay notes, tracing back through Flaubert to Goethe's Werther, that only fully displaced nineteenth-century historicism late in the century.

## Notable passages

The opening paragraph on the *juste milieu* lays out the market mechanics of graduated compromise — audiences sliding along a spectrum from academicism to Kandinsky rather than choosing a side. The Mondrian/Munch passage and the Berlin Secession paragraph are the clearest statements of modernists-versus-modernists infighting, the section's organizing thesis. Verhaeren's 1891 image of "moving and kaleidoscopic geometric patterns" that "clash... fuse... separate and fly apart" is Gay's own chosen figure for this permanent factionalism, and pairs with his coda that this very splintering "baffled and troubled good bourgeois" — the incoherence of the avant-garde was itself part of its provocation. The Liebermann passage crystallizes the generational-succession dynamic in the memorable line "No one can leap over his shadow." The Debussy passage on the "herd mind" shows contempt for the mass audience becoming a badge of authenticity even for an artist enjoying real critical success.

This material resonates with [[carey-intellectuals-and-masses]]: Debussy's and Franz Marc's readiness to prize incomprehension by "ordinary consumers of culture" as proof of authenticity is close to Carey's thesis that modernist difficulty functioned as intelligentsia self-defense against a mass public — though Gay's modernists are shown fighting each other over degrees of purity at least as fiercely as they fought the bourgeoisie, a factionalism Carey's account (focused on a shared intelligentsia posture) understates. It also resonates with [[the-mass-as-fiction]]: Gay's insistence that "generalizations suffer shipwreck on the rock of diversity" is effectively an argument that both "the bourgeoisie" and "the avant-garde" were rhetorical unities imposed after the fact on far messier, individually motivated behavior — the "bon bourgeois" Saint-Saëns invokes is as much a rhetorical construct as Carey's "mass." The endless secession-from-secession dynamic and the branding scramble of "New"/"Young" movements loosely rhymes with the [[divergence-machine]]'s logic of proliferation beyond legibility's reach — variety treated as a feature, resisting any single standardizing canon — though this is aesthetic factionalism among elites over a few decades, not the civilizational-scale, centuries-long push-machine WMP describes. Finally, the self-appointed avant-garde periodicals and cabaret culture (Le Chat Noir, *The Blue Rider*) functioning as venues for artists to diagnose and denounce bourgeois taste is a small-scale instance of [[kulturkritik]] — cultural critique that is reflexively aware of its own status as critique, right down to modernists mocking "their own" pomposity in the same feuilletons that attacked the bourgeoisie.
