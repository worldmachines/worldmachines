# Tender and Sensual Love Reunited: The Bourgeois Ideal and the Roes' Letters

Gay opens with the print market that taught middle-class readers about love (gift-book compendia of great lovers, sentimental serials, marriage tracts), then turns to the popular caricature of the bloodless, hypocritical Victorian. He concedes the pathology but insists it was a symptom, not the rule. He argues that the reigning ideal was a synthesis of affection and passion (what he elsewhere calls the doctrine of the two currents), that people wanted it, cultivated it and often reported achieving it, and that failures show up as anguish rather than indifference. He gives a run of failures (Amiel, Holtei, John Stuart Mill), a run of successes (Michelet, Kingsley, Mabel Loomis Todd, Lester Ward, Clelia Mosher's respondents, Karl Koch, Bamberger, the Bagehots), and then a long final case, the Civil War correspondence of Alfred Roe and Emma Wickham Roe. The unit closes on a methodological warning: the record is more tepid than the lived experience, since much was never written or was destroyed.

## Themes

**Print as safe instruction in love.** Reading was the safest road to experience. Gay lists Lola Montez's *Anecdotes of Love* (1858) and Thiselton-Dyer's *Loves and Marriages of Some Eminent Persons* (1890) as didactic confections whose implicit message was that love is available, should be exciting, and might survive marriage. This is a cultural infrastructure teaching a class its emotional script (see [[infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness]]), though Gay makes the point in passing rather than as a systematic claim.

**Symptoms versus the ideal.** Gay grants the evidence for the critical picture: doctors who denied women's desire, moralists who tolerated only procreative sex, unprepared brides, asexual heroines. But he charges critics such as Meredith with mistaking "bourgeois pathology for the ideal itself". This is the same anti-caricature move as in [[the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction]] and a foil to the repressive hypothesis of [[michel-foucault]]; the hostile stance is discussed under [[bourgeoisophobia]].

**The synthesis of the two currents.** Critics disagreed whether the bourgeois erred toward sexless sentiment (Julia Duhring) or toward sensuality (Junius Browne), but both wanted the strands combined. Zola's Denise and Emil Lucka's three levels of eroticism state the same ideal. Louisa de Rothschild's self-discipline is read by Gay as evidence of "concentrated, partly unconscious strength" in the impulse she curbs. Gay attributes the theory to Freud's framework and to the sources themselves; the diarists supply the evidence, not the concept.

**Failure as anguish.** Amiel (spiritual love versus "nocturnal" imagination) and Holtei ("the angel in me" seeking another angel while "the animal demanded its like") are sufferers of the split. Holtei's "angel and animal" formulation is the cleanest contemporary statement of the problem. Gay uses them as self-diagnosing witnesses, speaking in their own language, not as illustrations of his theory imposed from outside.

**Mill's dreams.** Gay offers a frankly speculative psychoanalytic reading of Mill's letter to Harriet: the wish to find "a sincere friend" and "a sincere Magdalen" in one woman, driven back into the repressed, with Harriet's rebuke in the dream as Mill's own superego. Gay hedges ("a full interpretation of his dreams would be rash"), and the reading is his inference from thin evidence, not Mill's own claim. This is a defense against Eros in the sense of [[bourgeois-defenses-against-eros]].

**Love continuing in marriage.** Against the idea that love ends at the altar (David Copperfield's honeymoon puzzlement; Kingsley's "Fools!"), Gay cites Michelet, Gusfeldt, Todd, Ward, Mosher's respondents, Koch and Bamberger. Mabel Loomis Todd ([[mabel-loomis-todd]]) is the sharpest case of someone who "would not have known how to segregate affection from passion".

**The Roes: sacred and secular desire.** Alfred and Emma Roe, separated by his Civil War service, wrote erotic letters that they justified in religious terms (God's hand, God's word permitting their love, "holy & pure"). Gay reads their explicitness as ordinary (their moral commonplaces: woman as moral arbiter, male desire stronger, married love sacred) while their candor is telling. He also reads beneath the surface: an uneasy campaign against nagging doubt, probable coitus interruptus, tension over another child, and infantile memory (nursing, nestling on a breast) shaping adult sexuality. Their religious framing anticipates the eroticized religion treated later in the volume. Gay marks the limits of the record: what is not said, and what was destroyed.

**Method: the tepid record.** Gay suspects that the surviving evidence understates bourgeois sensual life, because of discretion, closed bedroom doors and heirs who destroyed letters. This licenses inference from silence and pushes him toward indirect evidence (institutions, sublimation, the novel). The argument is inferential and Gay says so.

**Relevance to World Machines (hedged).** The unit bears on how a class's emotional culture is built and transmitted through print and moral commonplace, and on the gap between the publicly stated ideal and private practice, where Gay argues the gap ran opposite to the hypocrisy legend. Gay is a historian, not a machine theorist, so any link to [[world-machines]] language is loose; the closest rhyme is with the idea of a dominant culture's assumptions carrying a language for individual feeling (compare [[legibility-as-machine-core]] only as a contrast: these letters are the illegible, privately guarded layer). See also [[bourgeois-privacy-as-conquest]] and [[the-bourgeois-experience]] by [[peter-gay]].

## Notable passages

**Pathology mistaken for the ideal.** Gay's core rebuttal to the caricature (c1): the critics' evidence is real but atypical, and "the pathology has proved easier to document than the felicity" because couples hid their private lives.

**The doctrine of the two currents in practice.** The passage from novelists to Louisa de Rothschild (c2) states the ideal that Gay claims most bourgeois shared and marks it as "a matter not only of hope but of experience".

**Holtei's angel and animal.** Holtei (c3) puts the split in his own words, and Gay reads it as an exact statement of the ideal and its difficulties.

**Mill's dream, interpreted.** Gay's most speculative psychoanalytic passage (c4) presents Mill as a "male feminist" with unappeased sensuality who wanted to be ordinary for once.

**Alfred Roe on holy and pure desire.** Roe's letter of February 1865 (c5) is the chapter's title source: "With you sexual intercourse & desire is holy & pure." It shows sensuality fused with sanctity and the apologetic tone Gay reads as doubt.

**The record is more tepid than the experience.** The closing argument (c6) that the Roes' ordinariness makes their candor representative, and that lost letters mean the surviving record understates the real thing.
