# Stendhal, Balzac and Bourget: the physiologies of love and the fate of enthusiasm

[[peter-gay]] traces a nineteenth-century genre, the "physiology" of love and marriage, from its brilliant founder, Stendhal's *De l'amour* (drafted 1819), through Balzac's *Physiologie du mariage* (1829) to Paul Bourget's *Physiologie de l'amour moderne* (1891). Gay reads Stendhal as an "aristocrat of experience" who combines romantic and philosophe legacies, conducts experimental science on himself as an unrequited lover, contemptuously excludes the money-minded bourgeois from love, yet argues for women's education and for marriage grounded in love and esteem. His signature contribution, "crystallization", is treated as an adaptive process rather than mere blindness. The genre then declines: enthusiasm gets "laced with cynicism, even despair", the audience narrows to the richest, most libertine strata, and Balzac's ambivalence and anxiety give way to Bourget's obsessive study of failure. Gay's own view is that these books are unrepresentative of ordinary middle-class feeling but useful as evidence about anxieties and defenses.

## Themes

**Stendhal's science of love as confession.** Gay calls *De l'amour* an experimental science with the author as guinea pig, written amid the unrequited passion for Mathilde Dembowski and rooted in his oedipal love for his mother ("I was in love with my mother", lost when he was seven). The tension between objectivity and feeling ("I am making every possible effort to be dry") is what makes it a "glorious failure". Gay reads it psychoanalytically: love as refinding, investment of libido, ego suspending reality testing. Bourget too is diagnosed through maternal loss.

**Crystallization as adaptive misperception.** Gay defends Stendhal's metaphor (the Salzburg salt-mine bough) against the charge that it merely restates "love is blind": it is a process with waves, open to revision, serving the pleasure principle and lending audacity. Stendhal thereby anticipates Freud and Proust, and Gay ties this to the risk of the "fiasco" (impotence), the ambiguous price of imagination.

**The bourgeois as anti-lover, and its limits.** Stendhal's "Happy Few" contempt for bankers, manufacturers and Americans (rational, just, "not happy at all") is an early instance of the literature of reproach; compare [[bourgeoisophobia]] and [[the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction]]. Gay notes Stendhal knew America only in his mind. Yet the same book is reformist: women's foolishness is the product of oppressive education, love needs esteem, loveless marital fidelity is "probably against nature", and love joins affection to passion (the two-currents theme Gay develops with Freud).

**The physiologies degrade and narrow.** Stendhal's heirs borrow his form but shed his energy; military metaphors decay into commonplace; the books address millionaire bankers, salon-keepers and cynics like the Goncourts, not the average middle class torn between feelings and conscience. Balzac's statistics of "women" (under a million, then four hundred thousand) and his aphorisms tying virtue to carriages show love as a luxury good of a libertine elite.

**Balzac: ambivalence, marriage-as-science, anxiety.** Balzac insists love is more than lust ("the poetry of the senses") and that one can go on desiring one's wife, yet also says marriage kills passion. He holds women sensual but passive, made by their husbands, and criticizes marriages made "the way they buy a parcel of shares at the stock exchange". Beneath the flippancy Gay finds defensive anxiety: cuckoldry as castration, wifely disillusionment as the melting of crystallization, and the double bed as surveillance. This is a clear case of what Gay elsewhere treats as [[bourgeois-defenses-against-eros]], though here a satirical, elite-Parisian version.

**Bourget: failure, misogyny, and the critique of scientism.** *Le disciple* attacks positivism (Sixte, who never loved, and his theory that "one understands all the better the passions one has experienced the least"); the *Physiologie* then, in Gay's judgment, is a "confession of bankruptcy", a specimen of the decadence it decries, though it has evidential value: love as "a ferocious hatred between two couplings", sadistic fantasy immediately disavowed, and knowledge that only augments misery by "lucid prevision". Larcher's compulsive listing and writing echoes the self-management through text of [[self-construction-through-writing-gay]], though here it fails as therapy.

**World Machines resonance (hedged).** The physiologies attempt to make intimacy classifiable and legible, which loosely rhymes with the [[legibility-as-machine-core]] impulse, but Gay's Stendhal is a case where the attempt to systematize is defeated by the subject, and Bourget explicitly ridicules "mathematical rationalism". The unit also shows a published genre shifting from enthusiasm to cynicism across roughly 1820-1890, and a gap between elite libertine print culture and the ordinary bourgeois ideals of virginity, monogamy and purity, which the physiologies rarely mention. Gay is doing history of feeling, not machine theory, so these are only parallels.

## Notable passages

**Crystallization defined (c1).** Stendhal's figure of the leafless bough coming out of the mine covered in diamonds; "all loved creatures are beautiful". It is the unit's central concept.

**Crystallization defended (c2).** Gay's argument that it is process, not blindness: adaptive, morale-sustaining, revisable. This is Gay's interpretation, not Stendhal's.

**Stendhal against the bourgeois (c3).** The "Happy Few" contempt, ennui as the signature of the Restoration, and the "useful and the positive" mindset that is love's nemesis.

**The audience shrinks (c4).** Gay's account of how the physiologies turned cynical and addressed only the topmost strata; this distinguishes their libertinism from ordinary bourgeois morality.

**The double bed as surveillance (c5).** Balzac's paranoid rationale, showing the anxieties beneath his worldliness and the closing note that love is a rare, tender plant.

**Larcher's pernicious knowledge (c6).** Bourget's aphorism that the science of women is prevision of one's misery, the terminus of the genre and Gay's link forward to Proust.
