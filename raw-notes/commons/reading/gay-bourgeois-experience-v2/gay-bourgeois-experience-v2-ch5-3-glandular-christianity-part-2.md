# Kingsley and Fanny Grenfell: the sensual theology lived in marriage

Gay continues his treatment of eroticized religion (following Coventry Patmore) with Charles Kingsley, the muscular-Christian novelist, Christian socialist and controversialist, and with his wife Fanny, whose 1876 memorial *Letters and Memories* was both a best-selling public monument and a heavily self-censored one. Reading the passages she did not excise, and then the private letters, drawings and Fanny's own 1842-43 journal, Gay reconstructs an 'erotic religion': marriage as paradise and emblem of heavenly love, 'animal enjoyments' as 'religious ceremonies', and a pre-marital regime of scourging and proposed abstinence that was itself erotic. Fanny emerges as no passive foil: anxious and submissive, but quicker than her husband to reason her way, by Scripture, to a guilt-free valuation of bodily pleasure. The unit closes by comparing Kingsley and Patmore as two very different men exploiting religion for the sake of sensuality, and by noting secular echoes (George Drysdale). Gay's verdict: they 'only succeeded in eroticizing spirituality.' This belongs to [[peter-gay]]'s larger argument in [[the-bourgeois-experience]]; the closest sister concept is [[bourgeois-defenses-against-eros]].

## Themes

**Displacement of sensuality into theology.** Kingsley's faith is, in Gay's phrase, 'an erotic pantheism restrained by the Thirty-nine Articles'. His argument from nature (God shown in works, including the body and its 'thrilling writhings') is an antique apologetic, but 'its force stemmed from his sensuality'. Marriage is for him the central Christian fact: God as father, Christ as bridegroom, human relations as 'symbols of relations to God'. Gay treats this as a case of the versatility of displacement, not hypocrisy: the erotic and the pious are fused, not one masking the other.

**Defense, conflict and reaction formation.** Kingsley's horror of celibacy and mysticism was, Gay says, a conquered youthful longing, 'a reaction formation'. His 'muscular Christianity' and his diatribes against effeminate High Church 'foppery' serve as 'corrective and cloak' for a fascination and, Gay suggests, episodes of homosexual panic (evidence: John Martineau's 'deep vein of woman'). Love needs 'wholesome fear'; marital bliss must be earned by self-castigation. The scourging he described to Fanny was 'erotic in themselves'. Gay's psychoanalytic vocabulary is his own interpretive layer, not Kingsley's.

**Public monument versus private record.** Fanny's memorial is a 'decent torso' assembled by 'wholesale excisions and massive self-censorship' yet reached sixteen printings by 1888; it is an 'unwitting informant to respectable modes of thinking'. She printed the confessions she found unobjectionable and called the erotic drawings 'exquisite drawings in pen and ink'; she also scratched out a line in a 1850 letter. The gap between what could be published and what was lived is Gay's method: the silences were 'indirections sufficiently blunt to find directions out'. This bears on the wider Gay thesis against the legend of the prudish Victorian, and sits as a natural foil to [[michel-foucault]]'s repressive hypothesis, though Gay does not use that vocabulary in this unit. See also [[the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction]] for the caricature he is undoing.

**Fanny as agent.** Her journal (1842-43, kept during a Continental exile imposed by her family) combines carnal longing ('tormented in this flame'), remorse ('an Idolator & you my God'), physical decline, and fear, then works toward a 'pious celebration of sensuality' by appeal to Eden, Job, Christ and 'to the Pure all things are Pure'. She protests his scourging on theological grounds ('Is not that flesh the work of God!') and proposes a Saturday wedding so the Sabbath dawns on their first day. Gay stresses that her fear coexists with determination; her 'innocent sophistry' is her way to a Christian version of love's two interwoven strands (a gesture at Gay's Freudian 'two currents' frame, tenderness and sensuality).

**Betrothal, probation and heaven.** Betrothal permitted extensive exploration 'short only of sexual intercourse'. Kingsley proposed a month of 'virgin bride, a sister only' after the wedding, confessing fear that 'the blaze of your naked beauty' might make him impotent, and the couple did not carry the plan through. His letters pair frank sexual fantasy with doctrine: 'What is sensuality! Not the enjoyment of holy glorious matter, but blindness to its spiritual meaning!' and projection of marital ecstasy into eternity.

**Representativeness and limits.** The drawings (a couple lashed to a cross; winged lovers rising toward heaven) find parallels in Rops, Meyer, Queiros' *A Reliquia* and, later, Huysmans, but Gay stresses Kingsley's difference in social position: a moralistic cleric, not a decadent. Some drawings hint at sadism; Gay declines to call the marriage sadomasochistic and says the issue is what Kingsley thought permissible to send. Kingsley and Patmore differ politically and temperamentally (Tory Catholic convert versus Christian-socialist Anglican empiricist who read Darwin) yet converge in exploiting religion for sensuality; George Drysdale's secularist 'reverence' for the sensual shows the strategy did not require clerical garb.

## Notable passages

**Greg on Kingsley (c1).** The reviewer W. R. Greg, who shares Kingsley's party of frankness, calls him 'a dangerous ally' in an age of 'vehement desires and feeble wills'. Greg restates the ideal of love as the union of desire and affection, and objects to heroes winning heroines 'by mesmerism'. Gay uses him as contemporary evidence that public candour was contested, not absent, and that taste regulated sexual expression.

**The memorial as unwitting informant (c2).** Gay's method statement: the historian reconstructs Kingsley's erotic religion from what the widow judged unobjectionable. Also the concise portrait of muscular Christianity as struggle, and the body as part of nature's evidence for God.

**Struggle, reaction formation, corrective and cloak (c3).** The passage where self-indulgent spiritualizing is qualified by suffering, fear and scourging, and where masculinity itself is a repeated conflict. Its attribution to Gay's own interpretive category ('We would call it a reaction formation') matters.

**Fanny and the scourging (c4).** Her diary reaction to Kingsley's mortifications: kissing away the stripes, then rejecting mortification as bearing the devil's stamp and insisting the body is God's work. Source for her agency and for the eroticized asceticism Gay identifies.

**Fanny's courage (c5).** Gay's summary judgment that she was less conflict-ridden than Charles about bodily pleasure and better at finding serviceable scriptural texts.

**'Our animal enjoyments must be religious ceremonies' (c6).** The turn from anxiety to yearning, with 'healthy materialism' in its sharpest formulation; 'his intention was to purify his bride's imagination but, unconsciously at least, he was corrupting it' is Gay's interpretation.

**Closing verdict (c7).** Kingsley and Patmore spoke for more than themselves; the religion of the sensual was sensuality. Gay's chiasmus ('spiritualize the erotic ... eroticizing spirituality') summarizes displacement as a two-way traffic. For World Machines this is a case study in how a class's dominant moral idiom (evangelical/Anglican piety) could be repurposed as infrastructure for intimate life, and in the gap between official ideology and private practice; the fit is loose, since Gay offers biography and psychoanalytic interpretation, not systemic claims. Compare [[bourgeois-privacy-as-conquest]] and [[self-construction-through-writing-gay]] for the role of letter and diary writing as the medium in which these selves were made.
