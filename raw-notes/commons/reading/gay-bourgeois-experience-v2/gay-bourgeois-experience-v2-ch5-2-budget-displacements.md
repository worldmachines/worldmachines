# Displaced Sensuality: Eating, Nature, Gardens and Water

Gay opens the second section of his chapter on displaced and sublimated sensuality by pairing music with food as arenas where erotic feeling was plainly at work, then spends most of the unit on the nineteenth-century love of nature as the most successful stratagem of indirection. He moves from culinary metaphor and the psychology of the banquet, through Flaubert, Amiel, Emerson and Carlyle, to childhood animism, gardening manuals for ladies, the water cure, and two extended case studies: Charles Kingsley's courtship letters and [[mabel-loomis-todd]]'s diaries and letters to Austin Dickinson. His claim is that the love of nature let the bourgeois both escape the implications of sensuality and intensify its delights.

## Themes

**Food as erotic and as substitute.** Oral and genital gratification are joined in speech ("honey", the German wish to devour the beloved), which Gay reads psychoanalytically as regressive incorporation, back to a stage before libidinal and aggressive drives separate. He hedges: eating is overdetermined, gluttony can signal absent love, and the great bourgeois banquet was often a substitute for sex rather than an incitement to it.

**Nature as the mother regained.** Emerson's nature is a cherished woman and first home; Carlyle's Blumine and Teufelsdroeckh's flight into "her mother-bosom" show the language of nature carrying orgasm and loss. Gay reads such texts as evidence of an unconscious wish for an oceanic, regressive reunion with the first loved object, dressed in a decent vocabulary. The interpretation is Gay's psychoanalytic gloss; the writers themselves offer feeling, not theory.

**Education into displacement.** Children were bred to eroticized nature through anthropomorphic verse, sex education by analogy to birds and flowers (Dr. Kellogg's "vegetable husbands"), and theology that made nature's miracles proof of a benevolent God. These supports made nature a legitimate repository for unruly sensual demands, easing, in Gay's words, "the path of erotic displacement away from impermissible human targets." This is an example of [[bourgeois-defenses-against-eros]] operating through shared culture, not private neurosis alone.

**Gardens and gender.** Gardening was reserved for sheltered young ladies. In Bridgeman's allegory "The Matrimonial Garden" Gay sees the female genitals protected by a moat of metaphor, marriage as walled garden; he concedes that reading it as polite pornography is heavy-handed, since its innocence is the point. Respectable women invested erotic energy in genteel outdoor labor as a substitute and an anxiety-reducer.

**Nature intensifies as well as defends.** Kingsley moves from adolescent longing to married pleasure, and his adjectives ("delicious", "mysterious") carry the charge. Religious framing lets him call the excitement legitimate; he wants to "direct it into the proper channel". For Gay nature is both defense against and source of added erotic pleasure, a point that qualifies any simple repression story and sits against the picture of the prudish Victorian in [[the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction]] and against a bare repressive hypothesis ([[michel-foucault]]).

**Water and the body.** Amiel, Whitman, Flaubert's Emma, the American hydropathic establishments, and Natalie Barney's swan-beak bath show water as primitive tactile stimulus; hydropathy institutionalized water eroticism respectably, while Barney's solitary experiment marks the heterodox edge.

**Mabel Todd's menu of pleasures.** Todd shows sensuality flowing across objects (school, church, boys, music, romance, nature, painting, sea), a transferable emotional capacity. Gay reads her steam-from-a-pipe association as unconscious phallic fantasy. After marriage nature gains memory; in the affair with Austin Dickinson landscape becomes a code of stolen pleasure and, during his dying, of undoing, where sensuality "will persist in its stratagems."

**World Machines relevance (hedged).** Gay is describing a class-wide emotional culture: poetry, manuals, periodicals, sex education and theology jointly furnish a shared vocabulary that routes desire through acceptable channels. That is an infrastructure for regulating intimate life, comparable to the material in [[infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness]], and it shows the gap between official decorum and private practice. Nothing here speaks to machine cycles directly; the resonance is with how a dominant emotional culture is sustained, and Gay does not date its rise or fall in this unit beyond noting the natural sciences beginning to erode the anthropomorphic rhetoric. Journals as tools of self-shaping connect to [[self-construction-through-writing-gay]].

## Notable passages

**Indirection and the love of nature (c1).** Gay states the thesis: sensuality's stratagems were masterpieces of indirection, and love of nature became a conspicuous tactic in the Victorian century for both escaping and intensifying sensuality.

**Overdetermined eating (c2).** The banquet as sublimation, fuel, substitute, or symptom of absent love: a compact example of Gay's caution about single-cause readings.

**Regressive reunion (c3).** The interpretive core of the nature section: the unconscious need for an oceanic reunion with the first loved object, draped in decent vocabulary.

**Bridgeman's matrimonial garden (c4).** Gay's self-aware reading of an innocent allegory as evidence of genteel women's erotic investment in gardening.

**Nature and the sensual life course (c5).** Nature as target of adolescent longing, then as savor for married satisfaction, with Kingsley as illustration.

**Todd's menu of pleasures (c6).** Todd's journal passage, with Gay's caution about self-conscious formula and his reading of her as drafting a menu of pleasures.

**Crickets and the persistence of stratagems (c7).** The closing move: disaster mobilizes defenses, including undoing, and sensuality persists in its stratagems even amid despair over Dickinson's death.
