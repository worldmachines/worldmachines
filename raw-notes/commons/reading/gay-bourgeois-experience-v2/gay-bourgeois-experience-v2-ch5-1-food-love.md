# Displaced Eros: Sublimation and the Erotics of Music (Ch. 5 opening and "The Food of Love")

The unit has two parts. The chapter's opening pages argue that love's "repertory" is elastic, and that erotic energy under pressure from guilt and culture deploys "stratagems": reaction formation, sublimation, projection. Gay leans on Freud but complicates him: sublimation is not a simple energy transfer, and excessive abstinence yields neurosis, not culture. The first section, "The Food of Love", then takes music as the exemplary refuge of displaced sensuality in the bourgeois century, moving from Mann's Hanno Buddenbrook through Amiel, Ellis, Whitman, Billroth and Mrs. Jameson at La Scala to the Wagner cult, its eroticism disguised as revelation, its critics (Statham, Pfohl, Goering) and above all Elizabet von Herzogenberg's furious account of Bayreuth in 1889. Gay's own view is that Wagnerians let cultivated bourgeois "indulge their erotic dreams" while believing the experience elevated.

## Themes

**The stratagems of desire.** Gay states the frame: much of the time spiritual and aesthetic attachments "prove to be displacements of erotic desire for which there is no conventional mode of expression." Reaction formation (lust into prudishness), displacement onto poems or symphonies, and sublimation are the defenses' products. This continues the volume's defense vocabulary (see [[bourgeois-defenses-against-eros]]) and belongs to the larger case of [[the-bourgeois-experience]] against the legend of the Victorian who simply repressed; it is a foil for [[michel-foucault]]'s repressive hypothesis in that Gay shows sensuality proliferating in disguised, culturally productive channels, though Gay is psychoanalytic where Foucault is not.

**Sublimation is messier than Freud's sketch.** Gay notes Freud "naturalized" sublimation but did not fathom it. It is often accompanied by projection (investing a landscape with the capacity to give or withhold love), and its "economic" picture, loss to sexuality as gain to culture, does not mean stricter abstinence yields better work. Freud himself said total celibacy makes "well-mannered weaklings." Gay's traveler-and-speculator image distinguishes those who merely swapped sensuality for censoriousness (the caricatured spinster) from those who enlarged their satisfactions. Neuroses, in this account, are the "shadow side" of bourgeois stratagems, not evidence of unique hypocrisy.

**A medical and literary consensus about sexual causation.** Zola's *L'Oeuvre*, the apocryphal Balzac anecdote, Lombroso, Dr. Benjamin Ball, Seved Ribbing and Strindberg show a late-century scientific commonplace that sexuality underlies culture, art and even crime. Gay presents these as an emerging consensus Freud built on, and treats individual anecdotes cautiously (the Balzac story is called "scarcely convincing as evidence").

**Music as the privileged messenger of Eros.** From Plato to Mann, music was the "only sensual pleasure without vice" (Johnson). In the nineteenth century its erotic work was democratized: musical education in middle-class homes, orchestras, conservatories, silent, quasi-sacred listening, and the virtuoso cult. Accounts by Amiel, Ellis, Whitman, Halle and Billroth show a vocabulary borrowed from religion for what Gay reads as erotic response; he shows that the writers themselves often say they lack words. Whether the erotic reading is what these authors experienced, or Gay's psychoanalytic inference, is his claim, not theirs.

**Innocence, exposure and the anxiety of parents.** Mrs. Jameson's account of an English girl at Vigano's ballet condenses a standing bourgeois worry about carnal knowledge reaching the innocent through art.

**Erotic religiosity and the Wagner cult.** Wagner rejected the label "opera" and was worshiped almost blasphemously, yet Tristan and the Walkure stage sexual congress and incest. Gay argues that the same "doctrine of distance" that let academic painters exhibit nudes under mythological drapery let Wagnerians call sensuality the sublime; the audiences' arousal ended "not by incest, or a love death, but by supper." Wagner's critics (Statham, Goering) call it nervous or opiate excitement, and Statham notes Wagner separates "the two currents of love", cultivating passion over affection, which ties to the volume's Freudian frame of tenderness and sensuality.

**The Herzogenberg document.** Elizabet von Herzogenberg's letter is analysis as diatribe: she reads "seduction" and "salvation" together, uses oral and bodily metaphors (gluttony, butcher's stall, emetic), and calls Bayreuth a "sultry sensuality with holy and solemn gestures." Gay admires her candor and shows some bourgeois could name a sensual orgy, but adds a psychoanalytic hedge about her vehemence and ambivalence. Her actual objection was to insincere and histrionic sensuality, not to sensuality itself; she preferred "fresh, naive sensuality."

## Notable passages

The chapter's programmatic paragraph (c1) links sublimation to Freud's claim that sexual drives can supply culture with force, and to reaction formation as the mechanism of prudishness. The complication paragraph (c2) separates sublimation from projection and rejects the equation of abstinence with cultural productivity. The democratization passage (c3) is the closest Gay comes here to a social-institutional claim: music education, orchestras and conservatories as "self-possessed, self-conscious bourgeois institutions" that gave sensuality a licensed outlet; this bears on [[infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness]] and, more loosely, on how a class builds cultural institutions that regulate and channel intimate life. The passage on eroticism masquerading in "higher pretensions" (c4) states that the more sensual the work, the more exalted the rhetoric. The Wagner audience passage (c5) is Gay's compact statement of the doctrine of distance. The concluding assessment (c6) is his verdict on the Wagner cult and the philistine use of art.

World Machines resonance is modest and carries hedges. The gap between an official rhetoric of elevation and the private erotic practice underneath is a case of the gap between ideology and practice, and of a class's emotional culture being channeled through print, concert halls and conservatories, but Gay is a historian of attitudes, not a theorist of machines, and he argues no transition here.
