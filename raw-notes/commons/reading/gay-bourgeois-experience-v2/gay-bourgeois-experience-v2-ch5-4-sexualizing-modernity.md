# Sexualizing Modernity: The Department Store and the Railroad as Erotic Objects

Gay closes his chapter on displaced and sublimated sensuality (after religion, nature and music) with two thoroughly modern targets of libidinal investment: the department store and the railroad. His claim is that "concrete, time-bound external forces leave their imprint on universal psychological mechanisms", so that rational novelties were sexualized in ways their inventors never intended. He reads Zola's *Au Bonheur des dames* (1883) and *La bête humaine* as the fullest imaginative records of this process, sets them beside Fontane, Dickens, Trollope, Tolstoy, Vischer, Plönnies, Whitman and Claretie, and ends by tracing how middle-class imagination moved from anxious, disguising rhetoric toward a counter-phobic assimilation of the machine.

## Themes

**Modernity as a new erotic object.** Religion supplied ancient objects for erotic investment; the nineteenth century added new ones. The point is psychoanalytic and historical at once: universal mechanisms (displacement, sublimation) attach to whatever the age builds. This extends [[bourgeois-defenses-against-eros]] into the material world of commerce and machinery, and the chapter's own closing line names these outlets as part of the middle-class "arsenal of expressive erotic vehicles".

**The department store as engine of managed desire.** Zola's Octave Mouret, a "bourgeois conquistador", builds a temple of consumption whose real aim is to have "the woman". Shopping is rendered as sexual excitement (pale faces, sweat, the rage to touch), merchandising as seduction, and the whole system as devouring (*manger* as prominent as *séduire*). Gay stresses that here sexuality and aggression are "intertwined and hard to disentangle", and that Zola's account is theatrical and overstated (stores did not crush all rivals in six years; not every shopper salivated), though it captures a real "diffuse, potent sensuality" and the enlistment of aggressive drives in the search for mastery. Zola's own motives (anxiety, frustrated sensual needs, a wish to deafen his doubts with an ethic of action) sharpen rather than blunt what he saw. The store also carries evidence about class: petty-bourgeois shopkeepers crushed, clerks in a quasi-military hierarchy, sales girls climbing a "natural stairway toward the bourgeoisie". Gay is a historian here, not a theorist of consumer capitalism; the connection to legibility and scaling ([[modernity-machine]], [[legibility-as-machine-core]]) is a rhyme, not his claim: Mouret's bookkeeping, hierarchy and volume selling are legible-scale commerce, but Gay reads them through libido rather than infrastructure.

**The railroad as punisher, servant and beloved.** The locomotive first serves as instrument of retribution, a "social superego" (Fontane's Tay Bridge poem, Carker in Dickens, Lopez in Trollope, Anna Karenina, whose suicide Gay reads as obedience to a punitive superego and rebellion against narcissistic injury). It is also a lover's accelerator (Vischer), a rhythmic emblem of libidinal energy (Dickens's Dombey passage, Plönnies), and outright erotic object (Whitman's locomotive, Claretie's stoker Hébert). Freud's remark on the shaking of carriage rides and the "compulsive link" between movement and sexuality supplies the theoretical anchor: trains were erotic causes and could become erotic objects. Gay also notes that anthropomorphic railway imagery quickly hardened into hackneyed convention.

**Ambivalence in *La bête humaine*.** Zola's novel fuses love and death in locomotives and men: Jacques Lantier's tender passion for Lison, the maiden-head joke about No. 608, and the final fatal embrace with Pecqueux. Gay, citing Lemaitre, sees the "obscure correlation" of the instinct of destruction with the amorous instinct, which is his recurring theme of ambivalence. Machines can be as sadistic or castrating as any lover.

**Extremists as evidence of ordinary tensions.** Gay disclaims representativeness (few travelers murdered on trains) but argues, on an analogy with the neurotic analysand, that extravagant writers expose the "concealed tensions and desires" of ordinary middle-class minds in the machine age. Attribution matters: the erotic readings of Whitman, Dickens and others are Gay's interpretation; Freud's symbolism is Freud's; the characters' views are the characters'.

**From disguise to counter-phobic mastery.** Early responses tamed the industrial with borrowed genres (Southey's "damnable picturesque", the sublime, as printers once imitated manuscript letters). By mid-century or the 1870s, a confident, counter-phobic attitude (Fenichel) took over, visible in the move from Turner (1844) to Manet and Monet's Gare Saint-Lazare. This is a small, well-dated case of a class culture absorbing a new infrastructure over roughly a generation, with anxiety bound first by inherited forms, then by mastery. It resonates with [[history-machine]]-style accounts of infrastructure becoming ambient, though Gay tells it in psychological, not structural terms, and says the erotic implications persisted.

## Notable passages

**Thesis (c1).** The opening states the argument compactly: nineteenth-century sensual theologies were revised editions of old texts, but modern targets for love were new, and even rational features of modernity "readily lent themselves to sexualization".

**Mouret's formula (c2).** Gay reads the very name of the store as evidence of the sexual basis of what looks like rational commerce, and the line "I have the woman" as the toughminded key to the merchandising scheme; he also flags how lyricism overwhelms Zola's analysis.

**Devouring appetites (c3).** The *manger* and *séduire* passage collapses consumption, seduction and competition into appetite, with sexuality blending into aggression and success felt as a bath (compare [[mabel-loomis-todd]]).

**Freud on railway travel (c4).** The theoretical hinge: rhythmic, accelerating, mildly threatening motion recalls infantile sensations, so the train becomes both cause and object of erotic feeling.

**The extremists as witnesses (c5).** Gay's methodological defense of reading Zola and Whitman: the extravagant reveal the concealed tensions of ordinary minds, who found the machine both exhilarating and frightening.

**Stratagems that enrich and police (c6).** The closing claim that railroad, music, nature and God together "expand—and at the same time to police" middle-class sensual love, enriching bourgeois erotic life and containing tension without resolving it. Compare [[the-bourgeois-experience]] and the broader thesis of [[peter-gay]] on a class as a family of desires and anxieties.
