# Glandular Christianity: Displacement into Religion and Coventry Patmore's Erotic Theology

Gay opens the third section of his chapter on sensuality's stratagems by naming religion as the most interesting terrain on which balked sexual impulses sought gratification in Victorian bourgeois life. He first surveys the late-century diagnostic literature (Krafft-Ebing, Havelock Ellis, Kisch, Capellmann, the Goncourts, Clara Barrus) alongside sympathetic observers (Stendhal, Amiel, George Eliot) and the dissenting William James. He then shows the rhetoric of matrimonial sanctity and the self-analysis of Annie Besant, and defines displacement as an efficacious but dubious defense. The unit closes with the first of two case studies, Coventry Patmore, whose *The Angel in the House* and later verse and aphorisms fuse the sacred and the profane; the Kingsley marriage, announced as the second case, lies beyond this unit.

## Themes

**Displacement as a generalized bourgeois mechanism.** Gay grants that erotic religiosity is far older than the nineteenth century (the saints, Bernini's St. Theresa), but argues that the century generalized it from saints to ordinary people and that self-critical bourgeois began to study it. Religion's centrality to the middle-class mind made it, in his words, an inexhaustible resource for indirect, permissible expression of erotic needs. This is Gay's own psychoanalytic framing; the clinicians he cites (Ellis, Krafft-Ebing) supply the raw vocabulary of suppression and reservoir of energy, and Freud is invoked for the mechanism of displacement and the view that religion rests on instinctual renunciation. Compare [[bourgeois-defenses-against-eros]].

**Gendered diagnosis, and its limits.** Medical and literary authorities located the danger in adolescence and the menopause, and treated women as especially prone to Schwarmerei; the Goncourts made female religion an amorous effusion with a masochistic core. Gay reports these claims as prejudice in tune with the culture, notes that men equally displaced (Dr. Siebert's confession), and shows liberal bourgeois turning the charge on moral crusaders (Ezra Heywood on Comstock's YMCA allies, W. R. Greg's aphorism about asceticism and sensual minds). Attribution matters: these are the claims of the diagnosticians, not Gay's own.

**Sympathetic and hostile readings of the link.** Gay separates anticlerical polemic from recognition without condescension: Eliot, Stendhal and Amiel saw the ties between religion and erotic feeling yet remained sympathetic; William James objected to the fashionable reduction. The gap between official ideology (marriage as sacrament, per Mrs. Duffey and, in a more radical key, [[mabel-loomis-todd]]) and the private erotic charge beneath it is the recurring structure.

**Displacement as an ambiguous defense.** Gay calls it a dubious blessing: it often constricted erotic experience and tightened repression for neurotics, yet it licensed forbidden longings by lending them religious dignity, reducing anxiety while allowing partial satisfaction. Annie Besant's retrospective self-analysis (love of Christ, then idealization of the clergyman she married) is his exhibit of the self-deception, ended by a disastrous marriage.

**Patmore: sanctified sexuality.** *The Angel in the House* (sold over a quarter million copies by century's end) looks like a compendium of clichés about male mastery and female surrender, but conceals masculine envy and a conviction that sexual satisfaction is the essential ingredient of a happy marriage, with the wedding a prelude rather than an ending. Gay reads the sacred and profane as inseparable in Patmore and argues that his 1864 conversion to Catholicism followed rather than caused his erotic theology: he read "God is Love" as effectively "Love is God." Patmore's later poems (*King Cophetua the First*, *Eros and Psyche*) and aphorisms depict divine-human union as penetration, with masochistic devotion, and the lost *Sponsa Dei* as a treatise on divine desire seen through human desire. Gay's verdict is that this is less sublimation or displacement than a way of enjoying this world by invoking the authority of the other, a hedge that keeps Patmore distinct from the unconscious cases above. Patmore's public reception, as public property, is what makes him a window on middle-class fantasy rather than an eccentric.

## World Machines resonances

The section bears on how a class's emotional culture is regulated through institutions that double as outlets: the church and the sacrament of marriage are simultaneously a control apparatus and the licensed channel for what they restrict. That fits the gap between official ideology and private practice and sits inside Gay's larger case that Victorians were not simply repressed, a foil to the repressive hypothesis of [[michel-foucault]]. It also loosely rhymes with the cultural regulation of intimacy under the [[modernity-machine]], though Gay is writing psychological social history, and nothing here supports a machine reading beyond suggestion. See also [[the-bourgeois-experience]] and [[peter-gay]].

## Notable passages

**Ellis, Krafft-Ebing and the age's consensus (c1).** Gay documents that by 1900 the association between love and religion was known to all clinicians and that displacement was older than the bourgeois century but newly generalized; religion's centrality made it a refuge and excuse for sensuality.

**The Goncourts on women's religion (c2).** Shows the gendered logic of the diagnosis: religion as licensed outlet and permission for exaltation, with masochism as a component.

**Annie Besant's self-analysis (c3).** A first-person case of displacement from Christ to the clergyman husband, and her recognition of what she had done.

**Displacement as defense (c4).** Gay's own evaluation: hypocrisy-inviting but efficacious, serving the pleasure principle.

**Patmore's conversion (c5).** The claim that sensuality eased him into Catholicism, not the reverse, and the inversion of "God is Love."

**Patmore's pan-sexualism (c6).** The aphorisms on passions as allies and God infatuated with the soul, and Gay's summary of Patmore as having the best of two worlds.
