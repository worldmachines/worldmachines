# Love and Work: Beneke's Fidelity, Bagehot's Venture

This closing section of Gay's paired portrait (see [[the-bourgeois-experience]] and [[peter-gay]]) argues that the energies of love "reach far beyond the domain normally assigned to it," so that work and politics carry the emotional signature of a man's erotic style. Gay contrasts the two men. Otto Beneke, the Hamburg archivist and antiquarian, loved defensively and stubbornly: he was pushed into courtship, remained faithful, and never enlarged his circle of attachments beyond parents, wife and city. His politics matched. He was a local patriot and old-fashioned liberal who despised Bismarck's Reich and became a cranky particularist. Walter Bagehot, once he knew what he wanted, moved toward it, and he governed his career the same way (banking, essays, Parliament candidacies). Gay then reads Bagehot's political thought as psychology: institutions matter through the feelings and symbols they arouse. The section ends by defending Eliza Wilson's claim that happy marriages are not uncommon against Thackeray's cynicism.

## Themes

**Sublimation, hedged.** Gay says work is "largely a battery of sublimations of instinctual needs," but he explicitly refuses reductionism. Sublimations are built from ready-made cultural materials and are compromises between inner urges and outer possibilities. This is the [[bourgeois-defenses-against-eros]] register: libido is displaced into public life, not abolished.

**Style of loving as style of doing.** Beneke: defensive, responsive, fidelity, gloom, the familiar. Bagehot: deliberate speed, mastery, tolerance of uncertainty. Gay takes care that this is a correlation of temperaments and not a causal derivation. Beneke's opposition to the Second Reich is "the shadow side of his loyalty," energy that hates drawn from the same source as energy that loves. Gay adds that this is not to dismiss his politics as mere resentment, since it was "not wholly unperceptive."

**Bagehot on change and productive stupidity.** Bagehot (his own claims, quoted by Gay) saw the age as one of "inextricable confusion" in which change enters even the household and sets father and son at variance. He rejected both reactionary worship of the past and rationalist utopias. He distinguished the harmful stupidity of those with no "experiencing nature" from a stabilizing stupidity, the tenacity of the common man, which preserves the social fabric. Gay's gloss is that politics became "parasitical on the study of psychology" for Bagehot.

**Public stage, private passion.** Bagehot's "efficient" versus "dignified" distinction makes The English Constitution a study of political theatre, of what people think they see and feel. For Gay this is where Bagehot's style of loving and style of thinking merge: character and work "mutually define one another."

**Happy marriages were not uncommon.** Gay concedes evidence of unhappiness (psychiatric case books, confessions, divorce courts; Thackeray's bitter question in Pendennis) but sides with Eliza Wilson's more hopeful observation, which anticipates his volume's thesis against the legend of the loveless Victorian (foil: [[michel-foucault]]'s repressive hypothesis, [[bourgeoisophobia]]).

## Notable passages

The opening (c1) states the thesis: libido has "imperialistic ventures" in unsuspected places, with the reductionism caveat. The contrast of the two courting styles (c2) is the sharpest comic and diagnostic passage, ending with Bagehot kicking Eliza's donkey to be alone with her. Beneke's anniversary entries (c3) show the mundane emotional calendar of bourgeois domestic devotion, with the formula "The language is worn and trite, the feelings are fresh and authentic." The passage on stupidity (c4) is Bagehot's conservative-liberal balance between continuity and change. The merger passage (c5) is Gay's methodological statement about character and work. The last passage (c6) is the section's thesis on marital happiness.

## World Machines resonance

Bagehot's diagnosis of an era in which "a new world of ideas is in the air" and old assumptions are questioned bears on how a dominant class emotional culture is under strain in the 1850s-1890s, loosely rhyming with the friction of transitional periods in [[world-machines]] and [[modernity-machine]], though Gay offers only an individual-level, not structural, account. Bagehot's dignified/efficient split, official theatre versus working substance, echoes the gap between ideology and practice, and his concern with symbols and habits resonates faintly with [[gramsci-gap]]-style questions of legitimacy, though Bagehot is a Victorian constitutionalist, not a theorist of interregna. The loyal, local, particularist Beneke set against an unifying Reich is an instance of a private-attachment culture resisting scale and standardization (compare [[legibility-as-machine-core]]), but that is an inference; Gay makes no such argument.
