# Refinding the Love Object: Otto Beneke's Dreams and Walter Bagehot's Letters

Gay opens with the Freudian claim that love is always a refinding of an early parental object, so that a spouse is partly a proxy and even flight from home testifies to the home's hold. He then completes the two courtship stories begun earlier in the volume. Otto Beneke's diary breaks off in April 1845 just before the engagement; Marietta Beneke's later commentaries on it credit her father's tact and her own steadfast love, while Gay argues she misread the cause of Otto's hesitation, which was resistance to leaving his first loves rather than professional setback. The bulk of the unit is Gay's close reading of Beneke's recorded dreams as wish-fulfilments with infantile roots. The unit closes on Bagehot and Eliza Wilson, whose engagement letters show a less tormented passage to a new object, written in a vocabulary of 'delicious' and 'nervous'.

## Themes

**Love as refinding, and the persistence of the parent.** Gay quotes Freud (1905, 1912, 1918) to argue that the pull of early incestuous attachments is 'invisible, unacknowledged, and tenacious' and shapes both faithful and rebellious choices. Some nineteenth-century lives never got beyond mother or father, a fate 'often masquerading as filial duty'. This belongs to Gay's larger two-currents account of tenderness and sensuality, with the hedge that union of the two is 'the ideal'. See [[bourgeois-defenses-against-eros]] for the defensive side of this material.

**Dreams as evidence of a mind the diary conceals.** Beneke's waking diary records vacillation; his dreams (the boat headed for the wheeled machine, the white lily turned red, the ring he cannot shed, the church and the blown neckerchief, the 'another being in her form') display the wish for consummation together with the wish to fail. Gay is explicit about limits: without Beneke's associations the dreams 'will forever guard some of their secrets', and literary polish, secondary revision or later exegesis may have crept in. His interpretation is therefore offered as inference ('I suggest'), not demonstration. It is a clear case of the historian using psychoanalytic method on a private text, cf. [[self-construction-through-writing-gay]] and [[infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness]].

**Two accounts of one marriage.** Marietta Beneke's retrospect (Otto was really 'cheerful, gay, free of care'; the marriage produced 'the greatest transformation in Otto') conflicts with the diary's misanthropic, renunciatory Otto. Gay does not simply choose between them: he accepts that the marriage was happy and that his bitterness lifted, but reinterprets the cause. Beneke's later dreams of a daughter and of being pregnant are noted as traces of 'obscure and ambivalent desires' and 'residual confusions about sexual identity', a speculative addition Gay flags with 'We may add'.

**Bagehot: a briefer struggle and an emotional vocabulary.** Bagehot 'kept the amorous initiative very much in his hands' and is confident he can gain a new love without betraying the old one (his mother). The letters, written only after the engagement, stress novelty, relief ('a repose of affection quite new to me'), fatigue ('The affections are always fatiguing') and a 'controlled sensuality' whose signature word is 'delicious'. Gay reads confession followed by disavowal ('Young ladies should not let their hair fly in the wind'; 'it is not right to talk so') as the shape of restrained but openly avowed passion. 'Nervous' is the second signature word, an accompaniment 'not the dominant melody'; the letters show growing confidence and relaxed defensiveness. Compare the counter-legend theme of [[the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction]]: this is a decent, bourgeois, wholly proper couple with carnal energy in the correspondence.

**World Machines resonance (hedged).** Gay is a historian of feeling, not a machine theorist. The unit does, however, show privatized emotional life being written, read, recorded and interpreted (diaries, letters, annual wedding-day celebrations), and the fatherly gatekeeping (Wilson's 'Papa quite ill'; Banks's 'soft, firm hand') of an intimate marriage market. It bears loosely on [[bourgeois-privacy-as-conquest]] and on the gap between official proper conduct and private erotic practice. The dream image of a couple in a small boat swept toward 'a gigantic and ominous machinery of gears and wheels' is a striking, but purely incidental, industrial-age image in a personal dream; it should not be read as a claim about [[world-machines]].

## Notable passages

**Refinding and the proxy husband (c1).** The opening paragraph is Gay's conceptual frame: Freud's 'refinding', the husband as 'never anything but a proxy', the extension to wives, and the point that opposite choices still betray the original claim. Freud's views are quoted, and Gay adds the extension to wives on his own account ('though Freud does not say so here').

**Marietta's note and the diary's end (c2).** Marietta finds the journal after Otto's stroke, considers burning it, and records that the diary stops because the engagement followed: Otto went to her father for counsel, and they married that October. Gay treats the stop as though Otto 'could not bear to witness his own success'.

**The boat dream (c3).** Gay's most vivid interpretive paragraph: the rain, silence, beating hearts, the accelerating boat and 'devouring machine' are read as a symbolic orgasm and death ('the little death'), with the dream's wish to fail visible alongside the wish to succeed.

**The methodological caution (c4).** Gay concedes what dreams reported in diaries cannot tell us: no associations, likely secondary revision, literary aspiration. He nevertheless holds that the day's residues are ordinary and that the dreams are repetitive rehearsals of unresolved concerns.

**The oedipal reading (c5).** Gay cites Freud that a dream wish 'must be an infantile one', notes the realistic, child-like character of the dreams and the sister-like bride, and concludes that Beneke wanted both to remain his mother's child and to unite with Marietta, and finally resolved his 'oedipal struggles'. The claim is Gay's inference and rests on the hedges of c4.

**Bagehot's 'delicious' and 'nervous' (c6).** Gay's close reading of the letters' vocabulary as a language of restrained passion, with the two contrasting registers of pleasure and anxiety.
