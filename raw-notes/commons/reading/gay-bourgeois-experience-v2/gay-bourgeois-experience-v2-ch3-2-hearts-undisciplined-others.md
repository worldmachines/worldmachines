# Fiction as Moral Instruction and Moral Anxiety: Dickens, the Saturday Review, Proal

Gay opens with Dickens, a spokesman of the middle class, and reads the Dr. Strong scene in *David Copperfield* as a condensed moral lesson: Annie Strong's “undisciplined heart” is saved from a mistaken impulse, while David learns that his infatuated marriage to Dora had the same flaw. Across Dickens, Trollope, Thackeray, Eliot, Tolstoy, Spielhagen and Adams, erotic impetuosity brings disaster and patient, disciplined waiting brings lasting happiness. Gay then turns to the quarrel over whether fiction should moralize at all. Novelists were inconsistent (Thackeray, Trollope), satirists and purifiers such as Hardy and Zola claimed a moral office for candor, censors and editors demanded restraint, and family reading markets kept Podsnappery alive. The second half is a case study of the *Saturday Review*, which spoke for educated moderate opinion. It wavered over French novels, Whitman, the Obscene Publications Act, and above all Fitzjames Stephen's review of *Madame Bovary*. Gay closes with a psychoanalytic account of reading as playful regression and “economic activity,” set against Louis Proal's 1900 treatise on love, crime and suicide, whose belief that reading invites imitation typifies anxious bourgeois censors.

## Themes

**The disciplined versus the undisciplined heart.** Gay presents the fiction of the bourgeois century as a morality of postponed gratification. Dickens's good women are “experts in serene waiting”, and the sisterly Agnes, Paula, Linda and even Dobbin are rewarded. The moral is that “civilization extracts sacrifices,” and the reality principle governs marriage choice. This bears on [[bourgeois-defenses-against-eros]]: fiction is one of the institutions that teaches those defenses and rehearses the alternative safely.

**Tantalization and reassurance.** Novelists hinted at adultery, promiscuity or incest and then showed the suspicion to be unjustified (Annie Strong, Mrs. Snagsby, Edith Dombey). Gay reads the double profit for readers as venturesomeness followed by relief, a safari that turns out to be a trip to the zoo. The official moral surface and the private thrill are supplied by the same text, a small instance of the gap between ideology and practice.

**No consensus on morality in fiction.** Art-for-art's-sake advocates (Gautier, Baudelaire, Poe, Emerson, Heine, Vischer, after Pushkin) faced moralists (Saint-Marc Girardin, Tolstoy, and Ruskin and Arnold as they were enlisted). Authors contradicted themselves: Thackeray converted to the “Satirical-Moralists”, and Trollope called himself a preacher yet was rebuked by a clergyman for not preaching enough. Gay stresses that it was often unclear who a novelist's target was (Fontane's *Effi Briest*, Gautier's *Mademoiselle de Maupin*), and that a satirist has to depict the immorality he scourges.

**Markets, editors and the family hearth.** James's “John Delavoy” and its *Cynosure* show a censorship driven by circulation. Gay ties the differing moral demands to differing markets: serialized family journals such as the *Gartenlaube*, read aloud to daughters, made Podsnappery structural. Censorship here is exercised by the market and the household, not primarily by the state. It resonates loosely with the privatization of experience ([[infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness]]), though Gay does not frame it that way.

**The Saturday Review as symptom.** Its anonymity made it sound like one voice, but it was uncertain on literature. It mocked English “prudishness” and dullness while attacking French novels, relativized France, damned Whitman, and argued that laws cannot manufacture decency while also urging that smut-sellers be burned out and fined. Gay's diagnosis is “visible confusion issuing from invisible conflict.” The review's skeptical view of human nature tied it to the anti-vice crusade (Campbell, Comstock, Bérenger) and its rhetoric about protecting “the young and unwary”. Attribute carefully: these are the reviewers' claims, and Gay reads them as symptoms of anxiety. This is closer to Gay's rebuttal of the [[the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction]] than to a confirmation of it, since the bourgeois voice here is divided against itself.

**Reading as work.** Against the censors' model of fiction as seduction, Gay offers a Freudian one. Reading is playful regression that teases the id, flatters the ego and soothes the superego, and it is “economic” because it rehearses forbidden pleasures at little risk. It can substitute for action and inhibit it. A work is a work because it “works in its readers.” Psychoanalysis is the framework here, not the Victorians' own view, and Gay says so.

**Proal as endpoint.** Louis Proal (1900) takes the opposite view: reading invites imitation, novels cause crime and suicide, and Goethe was wrong to think the young could read safely. He wants legal controls restored, including tighter regulation of drink, the press and divorce. Gay treats him as extravagant but “not yet eccentric for his age and his class”, an anxious last effort to shore up a world he thought collapsing. Foucault's repressive hypothesis ([[michel-foucault]]) is Gay's usual foil, and this unit sits on the same terrain: Gay documents intense talk about sex and reading, together with anxious control, without treating either as the whole story.

**World Machines resonance (hedged).** The unit shows one class's emotional culture being built and policed through print, lending libraries, editors, reform societies and courts, and shows its guardians in a defensive posture late in the century. This loosely echoes a legitimacy strain in the [[modernity-machine]] and the [[gramsci-gap]], but Gay makes no such claim, and the unit offers no evidence for a machine-scale argument. It is better read as a case of institutions regulating intimate life, with a notable split between the ideology of purity and the private consumption of erotic suggestion. See also [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1]] and [[peter-gay]].

## Notable passages

**Copperfield as morality tale (c1).** Gay states the thesis: the novel contrasts the penalties of the undisciplined heart with the rewards of disciplining it, and lists the Hetty Sorrels, Emma Bovarys and Anna Kareninas who yield to impulses their station and rules forbid.

**The double profit (c2).** The safari-to-the-zoo image is the sharpest statement of how suggestion and reassurance coexisted in respectable fiction.

**Confusion from conflict (c3).** After the Saturday Review's two stances on legislation, Gay names its incoherence as the visible surface of a hidden conflict.

**Reading as an economic activity (c4).** His most theoretical passage on how fiction operates psychically.

**Anxiety and the work of fiction (c5).** The censors could not see substitution or diversity of response, and Gay's account of a “work” follows.

**Proal's program (c6).** The conclusion of the survey: a repressive program and Gay's placing of it as a symptom of nineteenth-century campaigns against masturbation, syphilis and contraception.
