# The Growth of Life

Chapter 4 argues that the rise of the masses and the "raising of the level" chronicled in earlier chapters are symptoms of something larger: the world itself has grown — spatially (an instantaneous, globalized awareness of remote events), temporally (a deepened historical past uncovered by archaeology), and above all in the dimension of "vital potentiality," the sheer range of things a person can now think, want, do, or become. Ortega treats this increase in possibility, not any change in "quality," as the defining fact of the present, then pivots from it to diagnose contemporary talk of civilizational "decadence" as confused, and from there to a critique of the shared 19th-century faith of progressive liberalism and Marxist socialism that history moves toward a guaranteed good outcome. The chapter closes on the strange psychological result: modern man feels omnipotent and adrift at once, and Ortega insists that insecurity is the normal condition of a serious life rather than a symptom to cure. It ends by pivoting explicitly back to the masses.

## Themes

**The world has literally grown larger.** Space and time have collapsed as barriers to experience — Ortega's arresting example is Seville newspaper readers following, "hour by hour," an Arctic expedition, so that "icebergs passed drifting against the burning background of the Andalusian landscape." He generalizes this into a principle: anything is now effectively ubiquitous, since "things are wherever their effects are felt." Prehistory and archaeology have likewise deepened the past available to consciousness. But Ortega is explicit that mere spatiotemporal expansion "would of itself signify nothing" — physical space and time are "the absolutely stupid aspects of the universe"; what matters is what fills them.

**Vital potentiality, not comfort, is the real increase.** The chapter's governing concept is that life "consists primarily in living over the purchasing possibilities" available to it — existence is, at bottom, "the consciousness of what is possible to us." Ortega defines "world," in the sense he uses across the book, as exactly this repertory: "not... something apart from and alien to our existence—but its actual periphery." He tracks the growth of this repertory through purchasing power, career options ("shepherd, hunter, warrior, seer" versus the near-infinite modern list), sporting records, and science — Einstein's physics is cited not for being more correct than Newton's but as evidence that "the man Einstein is capable of greater exactitude and freedom of spirit than the man Newton," a claim about subjective potency rather than truth.

**A polemic against "decadence" talk.** Ortega reframes the fashionable diagnosis of Western decline: real decadence would mean "a lowering of vitality," not the partial decline of "secondary historical elements" like a particular culture or Europe's national organizations. The present age's actual problem runs the other way — it feels categorically superior to every past age, "recognizing no classical or normative epochs" — and precisely because it has no admired predecessor to imitate, it does not know what to do with its abundance: "we live at a time when man believes himself fabulously capable of creation, but he does not know what to create. Lord of all things, he is not lord of himself." Undirected, "the world today... simply drifts."

**Power and insecurity as a single condition.** From this Ortega derives the chapter's psychological thesis: "the strange duality of a sense of power, and a sense of insecurity that have nested in the soul of modern man" — like a Regent "who had all the talents except the talent to make use of them." He frames this insecurity as healthy rather than pathological: it is "essential to all forms of life," while the false "security" of 19th-century "plenitude" was really "an optical illusion which leads to neglect of the future."

**Progress as abdication.** Ortega's sharpest target is the assumption shared by "progressive Liberalism and Marxist Socialism" that history's best outcome "will be necessarily realized, with necessity similar to that of astronomy." Believing the future guaranteed, both currents "cast away the rudder of history" and stopped steering; "life has escaped from their grasp... and today meanders without any fixed course." This abdication, not any single doctrine's content, is what Ortega blames for a world that feels "empty of purposes, anticipations, and ideals" — and he explicitly ties it to "the desertion of the directing minorities" that sets up the chapter's closing pivot back to the rebellion of the masses.

This critique of automatic, guaranteed-outcome historical progress resonates with the World Machines account of the [[modernity-machine]] as a *pull* machine organized around a shared telos of Progress: Ortega is diagnosing, from inside that telos's collapse, the same abdication of agency that a legibility-as-guarantee regime produces — though he frames it as a psychological and moral failure of "directing minorities," not as an infrastructural regime running down. The chapter's closing image — an age that has lost the 19th century's false security and no longer "know[s] what is going to happen tomorrow," yet insists this uncertainty is the normal, even valuable, condition of a serious life — half-rhymes with the [[gramsci-gap]]: an old legitimacy (guaranteed progress) has died without a replacement yet named, though Ortega reads the interregnum as an existential fact about all life rather than the specific transition between two historical machines.

## Notable passages

The globalization opening (c1) grounds the chapter's premise concretely before Ortega generalizes it philosophically. The "repertory of our vital possibilities" passage (c2) gives his technical definition of "world," central to the existentialist vocabulary he uses across the book. The Einstein/Newton passage (c3) shows him converting a scientific example into evidence about subjective "potency" rather than objective truth, typical of the chapter's method. The "Lord of all things" passage (c4) is the chapter's most quotable diagnosis of the era's self-image. The "duality of power and insecurity" passage (c5) states the chapter's psychological thesis directly, including the claim that insecurity is "essential to all forms of life." The "rudder of history" passage (c6) is Ortega's most pointed political claim in the unit, aimed equally at liberal and Marxist progress-narratives.
