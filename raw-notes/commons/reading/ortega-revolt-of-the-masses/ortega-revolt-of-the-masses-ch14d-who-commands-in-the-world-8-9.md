# The Nation as a Future Enterprise: Renan's Plebiscite and the Case for a United Europe

Ortega repurposes Ernest Renan's definition of the nation as a "daily plebiscite," arguing that what constitutes a people is not shared blood, language, or past but a common program willed toward the future; he then traces the three-stage rhythm by which European nations historically formed (fusion of near neighbors, exclusionary consolidation, mature absorption of former enemies) and argues that this same rhythm is now producing "background-Europe" — a continental unity assembling itself, largely unnoticed, beneath the nations' ongoing rivalries. The essay closes on a diagnosis of the present as an "interregnum" between a spent nationalism and an unborn continental enterprise, and argues that only the deliberate construction of a unified Europe can supply the vital "enterprise" without which the European "becomes degraded, grows slack" — the alternative being that Soviet Communism, for all its separate content, could seize the role of great enterprise by sheer force of collective fervor.

## Themes

**Nation as future program, not inherited substance.** Ortega inverts the folk understanding of nationhood: blood, language, and shared past are "static... prisons," inert facts that explain nothing about why anyone would fight to defend a nation. What Renan's "daily plebiscite" captures, properly read, is that the nation is willed toward the future — "In defending the nation we are defending our tomorrows, not our yesterdays." He generalizes this into a claim about human temporality itself: nothing has sense for man "except in as far as it is directed towards the future" — even remembering is done for the sake of a future pleasure (c1, c2).

**Spain/Latin America as the negative case.** Common race, language, and history with Spanish America do not make one nation with Spain, because the "futurist plebiscite was adverse": Spain never offered a shared program compelling enough to be willed as everyone's tomorrow. Conversely a nation can exist by aspiration alone even where it fails — "a nation untimely cut off," his example being Burgundy.

**The three-movement rhythm of nation-formation.** Ortega proposes a general historical pattern (c3): first, fusion of the geographically and ethnically proximate; second, a consolidating, exclusionary period ("nationalism" properly so called) in which recent partners are still felt as foreign enemies even as trade and ideas cross the border; third, maturity, in which yesterday's enemies are absorbed as kin against a more distant "stranger." He uses this to correct the anachronism of reading "Spania" or "Mother Spain" as an already-existing national idea in the Middle Ages — the Leon-Castile fusion, not the erudite Roman-inherited notion of Spania, was the era's live national project, just as "Hellas" never became a Greek nation.

**Background-Europe assembling beneath the nations' wars.** Section 9 turns the same rhythm outward: the wars and alliances among France, England, Spain, Italy, and Germany, however bellicose on the surface, have functioned as a "living together as equals" that Rome never achieved with its subject peoples, continuously homogenizing "opinions, standards, desires, assumptions" until "four-fifths" of an ordinary Frenchman's spiritual stock turns out to be common European property, not properly French at all (c4). The nations, Ortega stresses, are largely unaware of this drift toward unity even as it happens through them.

**Diagnosis of interregnum.** The essay's pivot: European "historic sovereignty" is dispersed, no one knows "Who is going to rule, and how authority is going to be organized over the world," and the resulting condition is "scandalously provisional" — nothing done today, from sport crazes to "new art" to political violence, has roots, because "life today is the fruit of an interregnum, of an empty space between two organizations of historical rule-that which was, that which is to be" (c5). Nationalism's contemporary intensity, on this reading, is not health but a decadent "last flare" — an exclusionary defense of a principle (the nation) already fully consolidated, and therefore now merely a "blind alley" opposing the inclusive impulse that actually built nations in the first place.

**Communism as the rival enterprise.** Ortega predicts, against the complacent bourgeois consensus of his moment, that Europe could yet "grow enthusiastic for Bolshevism" — not because its doctrinal content is attractive, but because a successful Soviet "five year plan" would demonstrate the sheer vital force of men "resolutely embraced" in a shared disciplined purpose; a Europe with "no standard of a cause as great to unfurl in opposition" has nothing to answer that fervor with. His proposed counter-enterprise is explicit: only "the building-up of Europe into a great national State" — pursuing exactly the fusion-and-adherence logic he has just described for nations — can supply a program the Continent has never held: to be Europe rather than merely France, England, or Spain in rotation (c6).

## Notable passages

Renan's definition and its reframing as forward-looking rather than backward-looking (c1) is the essay's fulcrum: everything that follows — Spain/Latin America, the three movements, background-Europe — is an application of "nation = willed future enterprise" rather than "nation = inherited fact."

The philosophical aside on memory (c2) is a small but load-bearing claim in its own right: even the past, for Ortega, is instrumentalized by a future-directed will — we "make remembrance of things past" only because remembering itself serves a future pleasure.

The three-movement schema (c3) is Ortega's closest approach to a general law of national formation, and it does double duty: it explains why nationalism in an already-consolidated nation is retrograde rather than constitutive, since consolidation is properly the second, not the final, movement.

The background-Europe passage (c4) is where the essay's argument becomes genuinely structural rather than rhetorical: Ortega is describing a supranational formation assembling through the ordinary friction of war and trade, invisible to the very nations enacting it — "though unaware of it."

The interregnum diagnosis (c5) is the essay's most quotable line and the one that most directly names the condition WMP calls the [[gramsci-gap]]: an old organization of authority ("that which was") has lost its grip and a new one ("that which is to be") has not yet arrived, leaving everyday life — "even in one's inner conscience" — without roots. Ortega's version is more voluntarist than Gramsci's (he thinks the interregnum can be resolved by an act of continental will, not merely endured), but the shared diagnostic shape — dispersed sovereignty, provisional legitimacy, a wait for the next order — is close.

The closing call to build Europe against Communism (c6) reframes the whole unit, retroactively, as itself a "who rules the world" argument: Ortega is naming rival candidates — a nationalist Europe too exhausted to project a future, a resurgent Soviet enterprise, and a hypothetical unified Europe — for who gets to organize authority over "the rest of the world" in the coming century.

## Resonances

Ortega's nation-as-enterprise, restated at continental scale, sits close to WMP's [[world-machines]] framing: a "great unifying enterprise" that organizes collective life, commands adherence, and eventually exhausts itself once "consolidated" ("nations... arrived about a century ago at their highest point of expansion... now mere past accumulating around Europe, weighing it down") — a nation-machine in decline casting around for the next great enterprise (a united Europe) while a rival formation (Soviet Communism) contests the same ground. The fit is partial: Ortega's "enterprise" is a willed spiritual and political program, not an infrastructural or technological regime, and he has no equivalent to legibility or divergence as organizing logics — the analogy holds at the level of cyclical exhaustion-and-succession of organizing enterprises, not at the level of mechanism.

The "background-Europe" passage resonates with WMP's [[history-machine]]: a formation ("Europe") assembling silently and distributedly out of independent components — wars, trade, the intermixture of ideas — that the participants themselves do not register, until it becomes a fact requiring a name; the opposite of a "great men" account of how Europe came to exist.

The interregnum paragraph is the clearest resonance in the unit with [[gramsci-gap]] (see Notable passages above).

Ortega's claim that the European "cannot live unless embarked upon some great unifying enterprise" and otherwise "becomes degraded, grows slack, his soul is paralyzed" echoes, at civilizational scale, Spinoza's [[Conatus]] — striving to persist and expand as the basic drive an enterprise-less condition suppresses — though Ortega applies it to a collective's need for a shared project rather than an individual agent's ontological striving.
