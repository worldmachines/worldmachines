# Primitivism and the Technical: The Mass-Man's Ingratitude Toward Science

Ortega opens by insisting the present crisis is genuinely ambiguous—open to triumph or catastrophe alike, because history is metaphysically undetermined—before turning to his central diagnosis of the chapter: the dominant type of man today is a primitive, a *Naturmensch*, who lives inside an immensely artificial civilization (chiefly its technical and scientific achievements) as though it were a spontaneous gift of nature, and who returns that civilization no gratitude, attention, or sacrifice in exchange for its benefits.

## Themes

**History has no guaranteed direction.** Ortega rejects historical determinism: "reality hesitates, walks up and down," so the rebellion of the masses may be either "the transition to some new, unexampled organization of humanity" or "a catastrophe of human destiny." Progress is real but never secured against "involution."

**Direct action as ambiguous hygiene.** The stripping-away of "dead institutions, valuations and estimates" and "unnecessarily complicated solutions"—from frock-coats to political convention—is framed as a necessary simplification, a return to "bare essence." Ortega endorses it in principle even as the rest of the chapter catalogues its dangers.

**The mass-man as primitive inside civilization (the chapter's core claim).** The dominant type "does not see the civilization of the world around him, but... uses it as if it were a natural force," believing his motor-car "the spontaneous fruit of an Edenic tree." This is Ortega's cashing-out of the earlier phrase "vertical invasion of the barbarians": the mass-man is "a primitive who has slipped through the wings on to the age-old stage of civilization"—not an external invader but one born inside the civilization who never metabolized its principles.

**Against Spengler: technicism cannot outlive the culture that bore it.** Ortega singles out Spengler's belief that "culture" gives way to a self-sufficient "civilization" of technical efficiency, and rejects it directly: technique is "not causae sui" but "a useful, practical precipitate" of pure science, which itself depends on a living cultural enthusiasm. Kill that enthusiasm and "technicism can only survive for a time, for the duration of the inertia of the cultural impulse which started it"—a formulation close to WMP's [[decay-sequence]], where infrastructure keeps running in a Performative or Allegorical mode after the understanding that built it has emptied out; Ortega's version is narrower (it is specifically the cultural appetite for pure science that decays) but the shape—form outliving function—is the same.

**Experimental science as a historical freak, not a permanent endowment.** Pure science "succeeded in establishing itself completely only in the small quadrilateral enclosed by London, Berlin, Vienna, and Paris, and that only in the 19th Century"—rarer, Ortega says, than "the unicorn." Nothing guarantees its continuation, least of all money: "Is it seriously thought that as long as there are dollars there will be science?"

**The deepest barbarism is ingratitude, not violence.** Where louder symptoms of the mass revolt dominate elsewhere, Ortega singles out something quieter and "much more terrifying": the "disproportion between the profit which the average man draws from science and the gratitude which he returns." Post-war society has made "the man of science... a new social pariah," and even the technicians who administer science's fruits—"doctors, engineers, etc."—show the same lack of solidarity with the future of science as any consumer of aspirin.

## Notable passages

The opening paragraph on indeterminate history ("There is no call to burden this essay...drama") sets Ortega's non-deterministic frame for everything that follows in the chapter and the book: nothing here is prophecy, only diagnosis of live possibilities—a caution that resonates loosely with [[world-machines]]'s own insistence on forecasting "corrected for chaos" rather than certainty, though Ortega's is a vitalist metaphysics of drama, not a probabilistic method.

The primitivism/*Naturmensch* paragraph ("What is the significance...principles which make them possible") is the chapter's thesis statement, defining the mass-man not by class but by his relationship to the civilization he occupies.

The "vertical invasion" paragraph glosses Ortega's own earlier phrase and states plainly that the mass-man is not an outsider but someone who has "slipped through the wings" onto a stage already built—civilization here functions much like a [[world-machines]]-style deep infrastructure (institutions, technologies, assumptions) that can be occupied and used without being understood.

The Spengler rebuttal ("Technicism and science are consubstantial...unpractical activities") is the chapter's most theoretically loaded passage, arguing that applied technique is parasitic on a cultural enthusiasm for science-for-its-own-sake, and will outlast that enthusiasm only briefly, on inertia.

The "unicorn" passage on the historical rarity of experimental science underlines how contingent and reversible the whole edifice is—a single sentence, "experimental science is one of the most unlikely products of history," carries the chapter's warning.

The closing passage on the "new social pariah" names the mass-man's failure concretely: universal benefit from science paired with zero willingness to sacrifice for it, which Ortega ranks above louder disorders as the truest mark of emergent barbarism.
