# Chapter 15 — Arriving at the Real Issue

Ortega names what he takes to be the real crisis behind everything argued so far: Europe has been left without a moral code, not because the mass-man has traded an old ethic for a new one but because his settled disposition is to live under submission to none. Talk of a 'new morality' is, for Ortega, itself a fraud — a way of smuggling immorality past the censor under a respectable label. Reactionary and revolutionary postures alike turn out to be interchangeable disguises for the same underlying refusal: an appetite for unlimited rights paired with contempt for any obligation, whether toward truthfulness, courtesy, or superior individuals. That refusal extends into a contemporary cult of 'youth,' which converts what was once an indulgent credit extended to the young — exemption from having yet accomplished anything — into a permanent entitlement claimed at any age. Ortega calls the resulting posture 'universal blackmail,' worked through two complementary registers, violence and humor, both aimed at letting the vulgar man feel exempt from submission to anything. Against the era's self-flattering language of a moral transition, Ortega insists there is no such thing as amorality: refusing all rules is itself a rule, and therefore an immorality, not a neutral absence — 'a negative moral that preserves from the other its form—an empty hole.' The chapter closes by framing the whole preceding analysis as a portrait of a purely negational character, one that lives off a civilization it did not build and does not maintain, and by explicitly deferring the deeper question — what defects in modern European culture produced this human type — to what Ortega signals will come later and louder.

## Themes

**The moral crisis is a vacuum, not a transition.** Ortega rejects the flattering frame in which today's disorder is a fight between an expiring morality and a dawning one. The mass-man 'simply lacks moral ground, which is always, in essence, a feeling of submission to something, awareness of service and obligation' — there is no successor ethic incubating, only an absence dressed up in the rhetoric of renewal. This bears usefully, and only partly, on the World Machines idea of a [[gramsci-gap]]: WMP's gap presumes an old legitimacy genuinely dying and a new one struggling to be born, but Ortega is explicit that no such dawning morality exists here — the mass-man's condition is pure absence rather than an interregnum between two orders, which is precisely the frame he spends the chapter dismantling.

**Rights without obligations, under any mask.** Whether the mass-man postures as reactionary (invoking the fatherland or state to license crushing his neighbor) or revolutionary (invoking the worker and social justice to license disrespect for 'superior individuals'), the underlying move is identical: total entitlement, zero submission. This is Ortega's self-satisfied, spoilt-child psychology of the mass-man in its most explicit formulation — political content is interchangeable; only the appetite for unearned rights is constant.

**The cult of youth as a borrowed and then stolen credit.** Youth has traditionally lived on an indulgent, half-ironic credit extended by elders — exemption from obligations not yet earned. Ortega's complaint is that this temporary loan has been converted into a standing right, claimed by people of any age who declare themselves perpetually 'young' to keep collecting on rights that belong to those who have actually accomplished something.

**Amorality does not exist.** Ortega's sharpest conceptual move: there is no neutral, rule-free space outside morality. To refuse all rules is still to be subordinate to a rule — the rule of refusal — which makes the mass-man's stance not amoral but immoral, 'a negative moral that preserves from the other its form—an empty hole.' Morality survives only as an emptied shell of itself.

**A rootless culture and a parasitic character.** Ortega attributes the mass-man's amorality to modern culture and civilization themselves — Europe is 'slipping without reservation on the slope of a magnificent culture, but one with no roots.' The closing paragraphs cast the mass-man not as a rival civilization contesting the old one but as 'a mere negation... an effective parasitism,' living off what others constructed and accumulated without maintaining it. This half-resonates with the World Machines [[decay-sequence]]: a form (here, the vocabulary and posture of morality, and of civilization generally) persisting, hollowed of the function that once justified it — though WMP's decay-sequence tracks institutions and infrastructure across centuries, while Ortega is diagnosing a single generation's psychology within one 'Day' phase, not a machine's multi-century arc.

## Notable passages

The chapter opens by stating its thesis directly: Europe's trouble is not two moralities in conflict but the absence of one, with 'new morality' talk exposed as 'the most convenient way to slip in contraband.' Ortega then generalizes across political postures — reactionary and revolutionary alike reduce to the same entitled refusal of obligation, ending in the blunt observation that dictatorships of every stripe 'flatter the mass-man, kicking whatever seemed superior.' The youth-as-blackmail passage traces how a once-modest social convention (deferred obligation for the young) mutates into a mechanism for permanent exemption. The blackmail-and-empty-hole passage is the conceptual center of the chapter: it names 'blackmail of violence and blackmail of humor' as the two levers of the mass-man's submission-avoidance, then delivers the chapter's key claim that refusing morality is not an escape from morality but a hollow, negative version of it. The closing passage reframes the whole preceding portrait as an analysis of pure negation and parasitism, and explicitly withholds — for now — Ortega's answer to what defects in modern culture produced this figure, promising it will be 'shouted' rather than 'whispered' later.
