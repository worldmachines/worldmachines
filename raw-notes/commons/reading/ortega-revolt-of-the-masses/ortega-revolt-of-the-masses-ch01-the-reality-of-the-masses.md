# Chapter 1 — The Reality of the Masses

Ortega opens *The Revolt of the Masses* by declaring the single most important fact of contemporary European public life: the masses have come into full social power. He builds this claim from a plain visual observation — crowded theatres, trains, cafes, beaches — into a sociological concept, then redefines "mass" and "elite group" as psychological types rather than social classes, and closes the chapter by showing this redefinition already at work in politics as a new, lawless "hyper-democracy."

## Themes

**The rebellion of the masses as the era's central fact.** Ortega frames everything that follows as diagnosis of one phenomenon: "the coming of the masses to full social power," which he calls, without yet arguing it, "the greatest crisis that peoples, nations, and civilization can be afflicted with." He insists at the outset that "rebellion," "masses," and "social power" must not be read narrowly as political terms — the crisis is as much intellectual, moral, economic, and religious as political, extending down into "our manner of dressing and our manner of enjoyment."

**Agglomeration as the visible symptom.** Before turning to theory, Ortega grounds the argument in something anyone can see: towns, hotels, trains, cafes, and beaches that were never full before are now overflowing, so that "to find room" has become a perennial problem. He treats this banal observation as an entry point — "to be awed, to wonder, is to begin to understand" — that leads him from a merely quantitative fact (the multitude) to a qualitative one (the social mass).

**Mass and elite group as psychological types, not classes.** The chapter's central conceptual move is to strip "mass" and "elite" of any necessary link to social class. The mass-man is "someone who sets no value on himself... based on specific grounds, but feels himself 'just like everybody,' and yet is not concerned about it" — indeed, is "quite happy to feel himself identical to everyone else." The select man, by contrast, is not someone who thinks himself superior, but "the man who demands more of himself than the rest, even though he may not fulfill in his person those higher exigencies." Ortega calls this the most radical division of humanity: those who pile demands on themselves versus those who are content to be, at every instant, "mere buoys adrift." Both types can be found within any social class, including the working class and the nobility alike.

**The mass supplanting the elite groups.** Ortega reads the crowding of theatres and trains as the visible sign of a deeper shift: the mass has decided "to advance to the foreground of social life, to occupy the places, to use the instruments and to enjoy the pleasures once reserved to the few." This is not confined to leisure — he explicitly flags it as "a general mode of our time,” with politics as its most consequential arena.

**Hyper-democracy: the mass acting directly and lawlessly.** Old liberal democracy, Ortega argues, rested on a "well-tempered and abundant dose of liberalism and of enthusiasm for law," under whose shelter elite groups could still act, and under which the mass deferred political judgment to those who understood public problems "a little more" than itself. That deference is now gone: "the mass believes that it has the right to impose and to give force of law to topics born in the cafe." Ortega names this new condition hyper-democracy — rule by direct material pressure rather than through legal, deliberative channels.

**Vulgarity proclaimed as a right.** The chapter ends on its sharpest formulation: "the vulgar mind, knowing itself to be vulgar, has the gumption to proclaim the rights of such vulgarity and to impose them everywhere." Ortega glosses this with the American maxim "to be different is to be indecent," and observes that "everybody" no longer means the old complex unity of mass and select minority together — now "'everybody' is the mass alone."

**World Machines resonance.** The mass-man's defining trait — refusing to judge himself "on specific grounds" and finding contentment precisely in being undifferentiated — reads as close to an inversion of [[Conatus]]: where Spinoza's conatus is a striving to persist and distinguish oneself, Ortega's mass-man actively declines that striving and is satisfied by sameness. The fit is not exact — Ortega's frame is moral-psychological, not about capture regimes suppressing striving via statistical averaging — but the rhyme (self-flattening as a stable, even preferred, condition) is worth naming. Separately, Ortega's contrast between old liberal democracy (law, deliberation, elite stewardship) and the new "hyper-democracy" (direct mass pressure, no legal mediation) resonates loosely with a [[gramsci-gap]]-style reading — an old legitimating order (rule of law) losing its hold before anything durable replaces it — though Ortega is diagnosing 1930 mass politics, not a machine-scale institutional transition, so the resonance is suggestive rather than load-bearing.

## Notable passages

The opening paragraph states the book's thesis in full: the coming of the masses to full social power is Europe's defining crisis, a phenomenon with a name — "the rebellion of the masses." The agglomeration passage ("Towns are full of people...") supplies the empirical hook that Ortega uses to move from visible crowding to sociological argument. The definition of the mass-man ("sets no value on himself... feels himself 'just like everybody'") and the definition of the select man ("demands more of himself than the rest") together form the chapter's core conceptual pivot, recasting mass/elite as a division of psychological types rather than social classes. The hyper-democracy passage traces the collapse of law-mediated liberal democracy into a mass politics of direct material pressure. The closing passage on vulgarity as a proclaimed right crystallizes Ortega's diagnosis: the vulgar no longer defer to quality, they legislate their own vulgarity as the new "everybody."
