# Casaubon's Dating of the Hermetica and the Reactionary Survival of Hermetism in Robert Fludd

Yates opens the chapter with a large claim: Isaac Casaubon's 1614 demonstration that the Hermetic writings are post-Christian, not the work of an ancient Egyptian priest, is a watershed between the Renaissance and the modern world, though nobody speaks of a "pre-Casaubon" era. She summarises Casaubon's career and the setting of his attack (a critique of Cardinal Baronius's Counter-Reformation *Annales Ecclesiastici*, which repeated Lactantius on Hermes and the Sibyls as Gentile prophets), lays out his arguments from content and style, and reflects on how late the ordinary humanist tools of textual criticism were applied to the Greek Hermetica. She then explains why the discovery could be ignored (Campanella, Kircher, and the ghost of Bruno), and turns to Robert Fludd as the chief example of full survival of the Renaissance attitude, before closing on the Rosicrucians as a sign of Hermetism going underground.

## Themes

**A watershed that was slow to bite.** Yates says the dating shattered Renaissance Neoplatonism built on the *prisci theologi*, the Magus and his Hermetic-Cabalist magic, the non-magical Christian Hermetic movement, Bruno's extremist "Egyptian" platform, and Campanella's natural theology. Her claims are hedged: it did not take effect immediately, other factors worked against the Renaissance traditions, and Casaubon's discovery is "one of the factors, and an important one" in releasing seventeenth-century thinkers from magic. It is a contributing cause, not the sole one. This fits the [[legibility-as-precondition-not-cause]] shape of argument, though Yates has no machine vocabulary.

**Casaubon's method and its Christian premise.** Casaubon holds that the works were forged in early Christian times "to make the new doctrine palatable to the Gentiles", made up of Plato and Platonists plus Christian sacred books (Genesis, John, Paul, liturgy), with anachronisms (Phidias, the Pythian games, later Greek authors) and a late Greek style. Yates notes that his forgery theory shows how firmly the Christian reading of the Hermetica had taken hold, and that the execution of Hermes takes place inside the same Lactantian frame that had launched his influence. She adds that the Asclepius, having no Greek text, fell with the Corpus, taking with it a major ally of magic from Ficino onwards.

**The lateness of philology.** Yates expresses astonishment that the sixteenth century, with its extraordinary developments based on the misdating, had to pass before the content-and-style tools of the Latin humanists were applied to the Greek Hermetica. For World Machines this is a striking case of a founding text of an era's worldview being misdated for roughly 150 years, with the correction arriving as a scholarly footnote; it resonates with the [[modernity-machine]] and its [[Legibility]] drive (texts made checkable and datable), though Yates herself makes no such claim and the framing is ours.

**Obliviousness and the non-humanist tradition.** Bruno was burned fourteen years before the exposure appeared and Campanella was in prison; Yates argues that both came from a wholly non-humanist tradition in which Hermes and the Platonists were grafted onto mediaevalism without philological scholarship, and that Bruno would probably, like Campanella, never have lost faith in Hermes's antiquity (see [[giordano-bruno]]). Some, like Mersenne, used the new dating as a weapon against animistic and magical conceptions; others (Fludd, Kircher) ignored it; others (More, Cudworth) were modified by it.

**Fludd as reactionary Hermetist.** Fludd, dedicating his *Utriusque cosmi historia* to James I three years after Casaubon's work was dedicated to the same king, lives in a world where Casaubon "might never have been born". His pages are saturated with Ficino's Pimander, quoted seemingly from memory, with Hermes as Egyptian Moses and proto-Trinitarian; he adds Cabala (Sephiroth aligned with Pseudo-Dionysian angelic hierarchies, on Pico's model, via Reuchlin) and very probably practised magic (Agrippa; Mersenne accused him). Yates places him as a late, delayed-action expression of sixteenth-century religious Hermetism, a reactionary toward Ficino and Pico, distinct from the naturalism of Bruno and Campanella, with hints of Bruno's memory-system influence.

**Occultism goes underground.** Fludd's self-declared discipleship of the Rosicrucians, whose views and even organisation Yates calls "tantalisingly vague", illustrates her point that Renaissance Hermetism turned from a dominant outlook into the concern of secret societies and minority groups. This is a loose echo of a world machine's decay into marginal, ornamental survivals (cf. the [[decay-sequence]]), though Yates describes it as a historian of ideas rather than in those terms.

## Notable passages

The opening paragraph (c1) states the watershed thesis with its hedged conclusion. The account of Casaubon's arguments (c2) is the chapter's core evidence. The reflection on the lateness of the philology (c3) and the Fludd contrast (c4) carry Yates's interpretive weight, the placement of Fludd as delayed action (c5) is her central judgement on him, and the Rosicrucian passage (c6) supplies the going-underground thesis. Also worth knowing though not curated: Casaubon's own annotated copy of Turnebus's 1554 Greek text with Ficino's translation is in the British Museum, and Yates says holding it one realises it represents the death of the Renaissance Hermes.
