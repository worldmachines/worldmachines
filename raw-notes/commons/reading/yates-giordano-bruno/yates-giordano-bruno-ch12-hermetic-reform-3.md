# Bruno's Hermetic reform: law, Henri III, and the magical religious mission

Yates reads the *Spaccio* as a message for its own moment. The reformed constellations carry an ethic of law and social utility, an attack on the “pedants” who despise good works (Protestants, by implication, and also Spanish Catholicism), and a compliment to Henri III as a peaceable Catholic king. She then places Bruno among the religious Hermetists of the late sixteenth century as their most extreme member, and proposes that the reform of the heavenly images is itself a magical act. She ends by comparing the *Spaccio* with Picatrix's City of Adocentyn, Campanella's City of the Sun and More's *Utopia*, and suggests it may have worked as an “operative” text in Elizabethan England.

## Themes

**Law and good works as the Egyptian ethic.** Under Bootes, Corona Borealis, Hercules, Gemini and Libra the reform promotes law that protects the weak, restrains tyrants and puts learning to public use. Yates reads the attack on “pedants” as aimed at justification by faith. It carries a “double reference” to a pre-Reformation English past whose ruined temples, hospitals and colleges are lamented in tones close to the Lament of the Asclepius. On this reading Bruno's quarrel with the Oxford pedants, and George Abbot's later anti-Catholic use of him, make sense. The ethic is non-ascetic (against “Vestal edifices”) and still keeps an Altar and a priest.

**Anti-Spain, pro-Henri III.** Cassiopeia's Pride and Arrogance are expelled despite pro-Spanish Mars, so that Spain stands for another kind of pedantry, one that makes for war. The Corona Australis, with the motto Tertia coelo manet, is reserved for Henri III. Yates, with characteristic hedging (“one supposes”), takes this to be an offer to Englishmen, and to Sidney the dedicatee, of a Catholic king who rejects the Spanish-Guise faction and wants a return to the old spiritual union of Europe. The book's origins in the French embassy would lend it some weight of French authority.

**Bruno as an aberrant religious Hermetist.** Yates sets Bruno beside the non-magical Protestant Hermetism of Du Plessis Mornay, Rosseli and Patrizi's proposal to the pope. His radical step is to drop the Christian interpretation and make the Asclepius's magic “the core of Hermetism”, while still imagining reform within a Catholic framework. The Abbey of St. Victor librarian's diary and Mocenigo's deposition (against those who despise good works, and against coercion in the Church) are offered as corroboration that the Spaccio's message is Bruno's own.

**Magic as the engine of reform.** Following Corsano and Firpo, Yates suggests that the “last secret” of the *Spaccio* is that the magician manipulates the celestial images on which all things below depend, so that the reform begins in heaven. She ties this to the Koré Kosmou and to Adocentyn, whose engraved images made its inhabitants virtuous, and thereby to Hermes as magician and Hermes as lawgiver. This is a hypothesis (“I believe, may be”), not a demonstration.

**Cities of the sun and Utopia.** Adocentyn, the Asclepius's prophesied city of the restored gods, a Civitas solis mentioned by Bruno, Campanella's City of the Sun and More's *Utopia* ([[thomas-more]], [[more-utopia]]) are lined up by their shared ethic of social utility. Yates notes the odd magical atmosphere of Utopian worship. The resonance with WMP is the idea of a founding text that carries a designed social order (compare [[world-machines]] and [[legibility-as-machine-core]]). The rhyme is loose: Bruno's order is astral and ritual rather than administrative, and Yates makes no claim of the kind.

## Notable passages

- The “pedants” passage, where the good-works polemic is read as double reference to the English past ([[giordano-bruno]]'s Oxford quarrel included).
- The Henri III speech and Yates's gloss on its English audience.
- The paragraph on Bruno's radical step outside Christian Hermetism.
- The suggestion that reforming the constellation images is a magical operation.
- The Adocentyn and City-of-the-Sun comparison.
- The closing claim that the *Spaccio* may have been an operative work in the Elizabethan Renaissance, the “charge of dynamite” of the Asclepius's magic.
