# The Fludd controversies: Mersenne, Gassendi, Kepler and the end of dominant Hermetism

Yates turns from those who stayed attached to Renaissance traditions (Fludd, Kircher, the Cambridge Platonists) to those who used Casaubon's discovery to destroy them and clear the way for the Cartesian mechanical philosophy. Marin Mersenne's vast Genesis commentary (1623) is read as a summa against Renaissance magic, Cabala and animism, with Robert Fludd as its true living target. Fludd answers abusively, invoking Ficino, Pimander and the Asclepius and identifying with the Rosicrucians. Gassendi joins Mersenne, and a letter of Mersenne's shows that he has found the Casaubon weapon ("pseudo-Trismegistus"). Yates then takes Kepler's quarrel with Fludd, in which Kepler, though himself steeped in Hermetic and Pythagorean harmony, separates quantitative mathematics from number treated "more Hermetico". She closes by imagining Bruno's reaction, recounting Mersenne's hostile view of Bruno, and placing the Rosicrucians and Campanella in Paris.

## Themes

**Mersenne as a modern against the Magus.** Yates's claim is that for Mersenne the chief enemy of Christianity and of true science was Renaissance naturalism with its magics, more than the scholastics. He detects the magical core of Ficinian Platonism (ideas confused with star-images), rejects astral magic, the anima mundi and Cabalist magic, and names Ficino, Pico, Agrippa, Giorgi, Patrizi, Bruno and Campanella. Yates's own suggestion, which she hedges ("it may be suggested"), is that the unifying principle of the work is an orthodox Moses set against Fludd's Hermes-Moses equation. This is magic as an operating stance being displaced by mathematical science, which rhymes with the [[modernity-machine]]'s drive to make the world readable and measurable, though Mersenne's motive is theological orthodoxy and not a wish for legibility as such.

**Casaubon as a weapon.** Yates does not find evidence that Mersenne knew Casaubon when writing the Genesis commentary, but soon afterwards he did. If the Egyptian Moses was a fake, the corner-stone falls. The philological redating works here as a polemical event: Mersenne tells Fludd that Pimander and the other tracts lose all authority given what Casaubon has written. Yates follows Garin: after this, Hermetism no longer holds the dominant position it had since Ficino and "will sink underground" into esoteric channels such as Rosicrucian dreams. This is a case of a founding text's misdating being exposed and a worldview losing legitimacy, a partial rhyme with the [[Legibility]] theme of the [[modernity-machine]], though here the change is polemical and gradual and no clean machine hand-over.

**Kepler: two kinds of number.** Kepler was not free of Renaissance influence (mystical heliocentricity, music of the spheres, survivals of animism), and he knew the Corpus Hermeticum well, equating its teaching with Pythagorean harmony ("aut Pythagoras Hermetiset, aut Hermes Pythagoriset"). Yates is unsure whether he had read Casaubon and inclines to think the passage still reflects pre-Casaubon religious Hermetism. Yet he saw that the root of his difference from Fludd was their attitude to number: quantitative measurement applied empirically to planetary motion only, against numerological relations running through empyrean, celestial and elemental worlds and binding macrocosm and microcosm. Yates credits him with releasing mathematics from numerology. This is the clearest statement in the unit of science's separation from magic as a difference in the way of operating on number.

**Bruno and the Rosicrucians in the crossfire.** Yates speculates (in the subjunctive) that Bruno, for whom mathesis was a guide, who read Hermetic meaning into the Copernican diagram and who wrote against mathematicians, would have sided with the Hermetists against Kepler and Mersenne. Mersenne's own view of Bruno (L'Impiete des Deistes) is hostile, though he grants that an infinite world is probable. Yates agrees that Bruno's reform was anti-Christian in the sense of aiming at a pre-Christian Egyptian religion, and she connects the Rosicrucians (the 1623 Paris placards) and Campanella at Richelieu's court to the same struggle. ## Notable passages

- **Mersenne's programme (c1, c2).** The framing of the attack on Renaissance magic and Mersenne's dismissal of image-magic as meaningless, not frightening ("he has crossed the watershed"), carry Yates's thesis that the seventeenth century is the hour when the mechanical philosophy replaces the magical view of nature.
- **The Casaubon weapon (c3, c4).** Mersenne's letter and Garin's verdict show Hermetism losing its central place.
- **Kepler versus Fludd (c5).** Yates's summary of Kepler's clarity about mathematics and numerology.
- **Bruno as danger (c6).** Yates on why Mersenne saw Bruno's religious mission as the real threat.
