# Renaissance Magic as a Spur to Science: The Will to Operate, Number, and the Hermetic Sun

Yates's chapter is her most explicit statement of the science thesis. Within an unchanged mediaeval cosmos, the Agrippan Magus is a new kind of man: an operator who draws on the divine and natural order. She follows this through three channels: magic's turn to number (Pythagoras, Pico, Reuchlin, Agrippa, Trithemius, Giorgi, Dee), the real-artificial-magic tradition of mechanical marvels (Agrippa, Campanella, Dee), and the Hermetic cult of the sun that framed Copernicus and was then re-magicalised by Bruno. She closes with a large and openly hedged claim: the Magus's real historical function was to change the will.

## Themes

**Man the operator.** The cosmos is not different from the mediaeval one; what changes is Man, no longer only pious spectator but one who seeks power. Yates reads Fludd's plate of the ape of nature as an emblem: Man has lost some Dignity but gained power, by learning the chain from earth to heaven (Magia) and the higher chain to the divine Name (Cabala). Trithemius's Steganographia, cryptography fused with angel magic for telepathic messages, shows power magic at its most extreme, though Yates admits her modern comparison is a little unfair.

**Number as master-key, with caveats.** Ficino's prisci magi included Pythagoras; Pico has mathematical conclusions and links Magia and Cabala with Pythagorean mathematics; Reuchlin and Agrippa push Cabalistic computation. Yates says number is indeed a master-key to operations, but is careful that Pythagorean symbolism and Hebrew letter-conjuring will not of themselves yield working mathematics. Agrippa's scheme nonetheless leaves room for genuine mathematical sciences and mechanics: the flying pigeon of Architas, moving statues, the speaking statues of the Asclepius. Campanella's 'real artificial magic' recalls this. Agrippan magic seeks Saturnian gifts (Duerer's Melencholia) where Ficino's Venusian magic (Botticelli) avoids Saturn.

**John Dee as the exemplar.** Dee was a serious mathematician and inventor, urging applied mathematics in the Billingsley preface on Pico's authority, and also a practical Cabalist summoning angels with Kelley. For Yates these belong together in the outlook of a Magus; Dee shows how the will to operate stimulated by religious magic could pass into the will to operate in applied science.

**Hermetic alchemy, medicine and harmony.** Paracelsus is the Magus as doctor, tied to Ficino's De vita coelitus comparanda and to Hermetic-Cabalist conceptions of the Word (Pagel). Francesco Giorgi's De harmonia mundi pushes universal harmony; using number qualitatively, it nonetheless directed attention to number and so prepared for mathematical thinking, and Kepler still saw astronomy through harmonies.

**Heliocentricity and the Hermetic sun.** The prisca theologia cult (Pythagoras, Philolaus, the Egyptian planetary order, Hermes calling the sun the second god in the Asclepius) raised the sun's importance. Yates says Copernicus reached his hypothesis by pure mathematics, not magic, but introduced it within a Neoplatonic, Hermetic, contemplative frame and cited Hermes's visible god at the crucial point. Either the sun-emphasis was the emotional driver or a way to make the discovery acceptable, or some of each. Bruno then treats the Copernican diagram as a hieroglyph of divine mysteries, showing how shifting the border between science and Hermetism was.

**The will thesis.** The Greeks did not want to operate; the Middle Ages held that operating could only be devilish. Regardless of whether magic led to scientific procedure, the Magus changed the will: it became dignified and religious to operate. Yates hedges throughout ('I believe', 'may', 'perhaps both').

## Notable passages

The Fludd-ape paragraph gives the Magus-as-operator image and the Magia and Cabala chains. The number passage and Dee's summing-up show the mechanism she proposes from magic to applied science. The Copernicus passage places heliocentricity in the Hermetic frame, and the Bruno passage foreshadows his reversal. The closing passage is the thesis of the chapter.
