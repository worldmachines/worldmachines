# Yates's Egyptian illusion: Ficino's Pimander as Egyptian Genesis and Regeneration

Yates opens the chapter by setting out a deliberate method. She will summarise selected treatises of the Corpus Hermeticum as Ficino translated them under the title Pimander, and then the Asclepius (which falls in the next part of the chapter). She sets aside the modern philological problem, namely the second-to-third-century date after Christ, and asks the reader to enter "the huge illusion" of Egyptian antiquity, so as to feel the impact the texts had on the Renaissance. She gives a short critical preface: the Hermetica are composite, contradictory records of religious experience rather than a system, always set in an astrological cosmos, and divisible into pessimist (dualist) and optimist gnosis. She then abstracts two treatises. Corpus Hermeticum I, the "Egyptian Genesis", tells of the Nous, the Word as Son of God, the Demiurge and the Seven Governors, the creation of a divine Man who descends by choice into Nature, and the ascent back through the spheres. Corpus Hermeticum XIII, "Egyptian Regeneration", tells of the twelve Punishments of matter, which are the zodiac's influence, being driven out by ten divine Powers.

## Themes

**Method: reading inside the illusion.** Yates deliberately brackets the historical truth. The critical and dating problems are "irrelevant" for present purposes because Ficino could not have known them. She says the impact on the Renaissance can only be grasped by "entering with some degree of sympathy into the huge illusion" of vast antiquity. This is her own methodological stance, and it foreshadows the book's larger thesis that a misdating drove the Renaissance return to magic. The note keeps her framing distinct from Ficino's belief. Ficino believed in the antiquity; Yates asks the reader to share the belief provisionally.

**Religious approach, astrological frame, two gnoses.** Yates argues the Hermetica have unity only as records of religious experience: gnosis, personal salvation, "without the aid of a personal God or Saviour", through a religious approach to the universe. Their cosmology is always astrological (the "Seven Governors"). Following Festugière she distinguishes pessimist or dualist gnosis, in which matter and the stars are evil and must be escaped, from optimist gnosis, in which matter is impregnated with the divine and "all are parts of God". Which of these the Renaissance took up matters for what follows: the optimist strand licenses operating on nature. This is the ground of the magic in the Asclepius.

**The Egyptian Genesis as Mosaic and Christian prophecy, in Ficino's eyes.** Ficino's commentary reads the Pimander as a treatment of "Mosaic mysteries": darkness over the abyss, the creating Word, the Word as Son of God, the fall into body, the command to increase and multiply. Yates stresses that Ficino was struck by the resemblance to Moses rather than to Plato, and that he even wondered in the Theologia Platonica whether Hermes was Moses. She ties this to Lactantius's Hermes, who called the Word "Son of God". This is the source of the "odour of sanctity" around the Hermetic author.

**Yates's own argument: the Egyptian Adam is a Magus, not a sinner.** Yates then diverges from Ficino, who "significantly fails to point out" the radical differences from Genesis. In the Mosaic account Adam is not created divine or with creative power, and his wish to be like God is disobedience. In the Egyptian account the newly made Man wishes to create like the Governors, is not punished for it, is admitted to their society and receives their powers. He is "brother" to the Demiurge Word. His fall is itself an act of his power, done from love of his own image in Nature. His immortal part stays divine and creative, and he recovers it in the vision. Yates's summary is that this is "Man as Magus", and she connects it to the Asclepius on man as magnum miraculum, the passage with which Pico was to open the Oration.

**Regeneration as a grace that cancels the stars.** In the Secret Discourse on the Mountain the gnostic is freed by ten Powers replacing twelve Punishments, and Yates (with Festugière) reads the twelve as the zodiac's influence. The regeneration seems like a gift of grace cancelling stellar predestination. She notes that Ficino, who "desperately feared the stars", likely felt it to accord with St. John. This is a hedged inference about his psychology.

**Possible resonance with WMP.** The book's interest is the making of a founding text-corpus into legitimating infrastructure by a misdating. Here that appears only as the setup, in the enthusiasm of Ficino, and Yates says nothing about it beyond flagging the illusion. Any link to [[world-machines]] thinking about how eras run on unexamined foundational assumptions is a loose rhyme. The astrological cosmos as a taken-for-granted frame is a closer fit than any legibility claim. Bruno, the book's terminal figure, appears in [[giordano-bruno]]; he is not present in this unit.

## Notable passages

**The method of sympathetic illusion (c1).** Yates declares the critical problems irrelevant for her purpose and commits to approach the documents as Ficino did.

**Optimist versus pessimist gnosis (c2).** The classification that lets the Hermetic cosmos be read as either an escape from matter or a divine living nature.

**Ficino on the Genesis parallels (c3).** Yates's abstract of Ficino's commentary, ending in the picture of Mercurius as the Egyptians' Moses.

**The Egyptian Adam (c4).** Yates's core argument on how the Egyptian Genesis diverges from Moses and makes Man a divine being kin to the star demons.

**Man as Magus and the magnum miraculum (c5).** Yates joins the Genesis to the Asclepius passage on man's nature, which she says Pico later placed at the head of his Oration.

**Hermetic regeneration (c6).** The zodiac reading of the twelve Punishments and the Powers as grace against the stars.
