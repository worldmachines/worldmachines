# Bruno's Eroici furori as the Love-Poetry of the Hermetic Magus, and Bruno the Elizabethan

Yates describes the structure of the *Eroici furori* (emblem described in words, Petrarchan sonnet, allegorising commentary), shows by worked example (the stars-eyes emblem, *Mors et vita*) how a conventional love sonnet becomes a prayer to a divinity found in nature, and then argues that the real aim of the work is Hermetic gnosis veiled by Neoplatonism. Her key evidence is Agrippa's *De occulta philosophia*, where the fourth furor (of Venus) is linked to the Asclepius's 'great miracle'. She then weighs the Cabalist and Pseudo-Dionysian ingredients (the nine blind men and the nine orders of spirits), notes the Christian-sounding tone of Bruno's religion, and shows how the book is bound into the Elizabeth cult (Amphitrite, Diana, the Thames nymphs, the impresa shields of the Accession Day Tilts) and into an anti-Spanish, pro-French political message. It closes on the need to re-study Bruno's influence in England.

## Themes

**Mystical love poetry after the Ficinian two Cupids.** The dedication to Sidney explains that Bruno's Petrarchism is directed to the 'higher Cupid' (Ficino on the Symposium) and is likened to the Canticle. Yates places this in a long tradition (Dante's Convivio, Pico on Benivieni). The commentary turns the lady's cruelty into God withholding vision, and the killing glance into the mystical death of the soul. The divinity sought is a religion of 'natural contemplation' in which the divine light shines in things.

**Actaeon and the return of the Egyptian religion.** The hunter Actaeon, devoured by his own dogs (thoughts of divine things), sees 'All as One'. Yates equates this religion with the Egyptian religion of the *Spaccio*. She argues that the *Eroici furori* repeats the prophecy of its return through the 'great year of the world': the world is now at the nadir foretold in the Lament of the Asclepius, so it may expect to return to Egypt's light. This is a cyclical, returning-golden-age reading of history, which sits in tension with the progress narrative of the [[modernity-machine]] (and with any Dawn/Day/Dusk reading in [[world-machines]] terms), though Bruno's cycle is contrary-to-contrary rather than a thousand-year build and decline.

**Hermetic gnosis behind Neoplatonic furor.** Yates's central claim, hedged as 'I think', is that the heroic furors are Ficino's fourth degree of furor (Venus) read through Agrippa, who quotes the Asclepius ('man is a great miracle') and adds Cabalist Word-image doctrine. The furor thus becomes the means by which man, 'created divine', becomes the magnum miraculum again, operating with miraculous powers. She compares the Pimander trance (soul leaves the body sleeping), Milton's *Il Penseroso* and Bruno's 'contractions' from the Thirty Seals. Love is 'operative love', the basis of the Magus's magic, an instance of the will to operate on the world that Yates treats elsewhere as a spur toward science.

**Emblems as magical images.** Yates suggests the Petrarchan conceits function like the talismanic images of Bruno's memory systems, as hieroglyphs or seals in 'living, magical contact with reality'. She also compares them to the Jesuit transposition of profane into sacred love emblems (Vaenius), except that Bruno's darts come from God in nature. Interiority is stressed: divinity 'dwells within through the reformed intellect and will', just as the *Spaccio*'s reform took place within a personality.

**Cabala, Pseudo-Dionysius and the Christian tone.** The Song of Songs, the Cabalist 'death of the kiss', the negative theology of Dionysius and the nine illuminati (nine orders of spirits) show Bruno still 'within hail' of Pico's synthesis, but with the balance radically shifted to the Egyptian element. The supreme illumination comes through Circe, so it is magical. Christian religious Hermetists avoided magic, whereas Bruno, the full Egyptian Hermetist, stresses it.

**Elizabethan and political Bruno.** The nine become illuminati before the One nymph of the Thames, whom Yates identifies with 'diva Elizabetta', the unique Diana and Amphitrite. The Cena's promise of a universal empire to Elizabeth echoes Astraea imagery. Yates links the imprese shields to the Accession Day Tilts and the 1575 Woodstock Entertainment. She reads the Spaccio's politics as French-English friendship against Spain, notes Bruno lived in the French embassy, and remarks that his praise was later retracted to the Venetian Inquisitors. She contrasts his welcome at court with his hostility to the Oxford pedants, and possibly Abbot's mis-assimilation of him to Counter Reformation miracle-claims.

## Notable passages

The curated passages are: Yates's thesis sentence that the furors aim at Hermetic gnosis (c1); the great-year prophecy of a return to Egypt's condition (c2); Agrippa's fourth furor and Yates's interpretation of it as the magnum miraculum (c3); the argument that the emblems work like Bruno's magical memory images (c4); her synthesis of Bruno as a Magus with Hermetic predominance and the Reformation-by-return-of-Egypt (c5); and the identification of Elizabeth with the One who unveils the vision (c6). Note that for the great-year passage and the Agrippa quotation, the primary voice is Bruno's and Agrippa's respectively, while the interpretive claims ('The implication is', 'I think that this gives the answer') are Yates's. As elsewhere, she hedges ('possibly', 'I think', 'it may be suggested'), and the Elizabeth identification is presented as inference, with the Inquisitors' suspicion as partial support.
