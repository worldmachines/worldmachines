# Bruno's Second Paris Stay: Mathesis, the Cambrai Debate and the Failed Return

Yates follows Bruno from England back to a Paris that has changed for the worse. Guise and the Catholic League are ascendant, Henri III has lost his room to manoeuvre, and Bruno's patron Mauvissière has been recalled. She reconstructs his circle from the letters of Corbinelli to Pinelli: Italians loyal to Henri III who were looking to Henry of Navarre. Two episodes dominate the chapter. One is the quarrel with Fabrizio Mordente over his compass. The other is the public debate at the Collège de Cambrai in May 1586, where Bruno was answered by a young advocate, "Rodolphus Calerius". Yates identifies him as Raoul Cailler of the King's own spiritual circle, so she reads the rebuff as royal disowning rather than Leaguer hostility. She closes with Bruno's attempt to be readmitted to the Church and his departure for Germany.

## Themes

**Mathesis is not mathematics.** Yates treats the Mordente dialogues as a repeat of the Copernicus move in the Cena. Mordente, like Copernicus, is a technician who has stumbled on something whose divine meaning only the Nolan can see. In the *Idiota Triumphans* the compass figure is a mystical diagram to be read "after the manner of the Pythagoreans and the Cabalists". Yates ties this to Kepler's term Hermetics, meaning mathematical figures used non-mathematically. She hedges throughout ("I think", "(I believe)") and admits she has not solved the Bruno-Mordente controversy. This bears on the [[world-machines]] question of when quantity becomes instrument and when it remains symbol. Bruno's Hermetic numerology sits before the divorce of mathematics from meaning that the [[modernity-machine]] later effects, and it resonates with [[Legibility]] only as its opposite: a figure read for revelation, not measurement.

**Bruno's message survives, transposed.** The Cambrai opening oration, spoken by Hennequin, repeats near-verbatim the Cena's release from the "dark dungeon" into an infinite universe with a centre everywhere. The Paris output mirrors the English output: a mnemonic work in place of the Thirty Seals, the Mordente dialogues in place of the Cabala and Spaccio themes, and the Cambrai debate in place of the Oxford brush. It is more obscure and "crabbed" in form. Yates suggests that Catholic pedantry "quenched the genius".

**Politics of disowning.** Yates's central interpretive move is that Cailler wrote an admiring sonnet on Du Perron as a Magus united with the world-soul. Cailler belonged to the Vincennes group of religious Hermetism around Henri III, so his intervention shows the King's circle disowning Bruno. Yates frames this as Henri resigning his seat as leader of the "reform of the heavens", which was too dangerous a position. Her framing is the historian's inference; the text she leans on is Cotin's diary note.

**Sincerity and character.** Yates argues against the view that Bruno's attempt to be absolved was calculation. She holds that he never calculated, disliked Protestant contempt for works, and expected the great reform to come inside a Catholic framework. The coming dispensation is "an Egyptianised and tolerant Catholic and universal religion". She also sets out the ambivalence in his character: bombast and self-advertisement on one side, a genuine sense of mission on the other. She links the fatal return to Italy to the hopes raised by Navarre's accession (following Corsano). This relates to the wiki's existing readings of [[giordano-bruno]] as a terminal figure of the Hermetic reform.

## Notable passages

The Mordente passage (c1) gives the episode and Yates's key analogy with Copernicus in the Cena. The *mathesis* paragraph (c2) supplies the Hermetic reading of the compass, with Kepler's contrasting category. The Cambrai oration (c3) is the plainest statement of Bruno's infinite-universe message in Paris. The Du Perron and Cailler passage (c4) carries Yates's original argument about who silenced Bruno and why. The two closing arguments (c5, c6) give her account of Bruno's motives: why he provoked a debate he could not win, and why the wish to return to the Church was sincere.
