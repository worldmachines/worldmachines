# The Cambridge Platonists after Casaubon: More, Cudworth and the Wreck of Hermes Trismegistus

Yates presents the last two of her 'four examples' of what happened after Hermes Trismegistus was dated. Henry More and Ralph Cudworth, unlike Fludd and Kircher, knew and accepted Casaubon's critique, and Yates argues this removed Hermes as *priscus theologus* and so one of the main foundations of the Renaissance synthesis. She reads More's silence about Hermes in his *Conjectura Cabbalistica* as the sign of this loss, and Cudworth's laborious sorting of genuine from forged Hermetic books as its cost. Both nonetheless keep some Hermetic residue. The unit closes by saying that being correctly dated does not invalidate the Hermetica as documents of religious experience.

## Themes

**Acceptance of the dating removes the foundation.** Yates says More and Cudworth 'knew of and accepted Casaubon's critique', unlike Fludd and Kircher. She believes the consequences have never been noticed and frames her treatment as pointing to facts for others to investigate. Her hedging is explicit: this is 'a fact', but its meaning for More's Platonism is left open. This is the book's thesis (a civilisation's founding text misdated) seen from the far side of the correction. It fits a [[modernity-machine]] reading of philology as a [[Legibility]] event only loosely, since Yates writes a history of ideas, not of infrastructure.

**More: an omission as evidence.** More cites the 'fragments of Trismegist' for the pre-existence of the soul, a doctrine Christianity did not care about, so the passages are less likely to have been tampered with by pious Christian forgers. Yates stresses that this is picking and choosing, a very different thing from treating the Hermetica as ancient prophecy concordant with Genesis. The decisive evidence is that Hermes is never mentioned in the *Conjectura Cabbalistica*; More is 'a Cabalist only', not a Hermetist, whereas Fludd's *Philosophia Moysaica* and Kircher's *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* set Ficino's *Pimander* beside Genesis. Yates speculates, with a hedge ('Perhaps'), that More may really have been what Ficino used to be thought to be, a pious Christian Platonist free of the magical core that Hermes imparted to Ficinian Platonism.

**Residual Hermetism inside mechanism.** More admired Descartes, treated Cartesian mechanism as Mosaic truth preserved in Cabala, and modified it with a 'spirit of nature'. In that defence he falls back on Ficino's *Pimander* (Corpus Hermeticum X), calling the aethereal matter the Fire which Trismegist affirms is the most inward vehicle of the Mind. Yates concludes that his acceptance of mechanism is Cabalistic and his criticism of it partly Hermetic. This touches the book's larger thread of seventeenth-century mechanism emerging against a Hermetic background.

**Cudworth: forgery, salvage and circularity.** Cudworth's *True Intellectual System* deliberately avoids resting on the Sibylline oracles and the reputed writings of Hermes Trismegist. He rebuilds the old prisci theologi on better-attested sources (Iamblichus, Plutarch, the Fathers) and blames 'some pretended Christians' for forgeries. Yates notes an irony: modern scholars, who date the Hermetica to the post-Christian period, see little Christian influence in them, so Cudworth's 'rankly Christian' verdicts show how deeply the Christian Hermetist tradition shaped even its critics. She also points out a circular move: Cudworth argues that Greek learning in the Hermetica came from the Egyptians, so it need not be a Christian insertion, which shows the persistence of the Renaissance respect for Egypt as the fount of wisdom. He defends the *Asclepius* on the ground that its god-making passage is 'rankly Pagan', and disputes Casaubon's treatment of the *Pimander* as a single book.

**Bruno as the unexpected heir.** Yates observes that what Cudworth saves (the eternity and divinity of matter, the *Asclepius* and its magical religion) is what Bruno's Egyptianism embraced, by refusing the Christian interpretation of the texts. See [[giordano-bruno]]. Yates does not say Cudworth intended this; it is her observation about the overlap.

**Slow loss, incomplete invalidation.** The concluding paragraphs summarise: Fludd and Kircher kept Renaissance Hermetic-Cabalism by ignoring the dating; the Cambridge Platonists, 'bereft of the Hermetic foundation', are different from Renaissance Platonists but 'reluctant to abandon the Hermetica'. Yates says the Hermetica are not invalidated as documents of religious experience by correct dating, and that scholars still disagree about how much genuinely Egyptian teaching they contain. The resonance with the WMP frame ([[world-machines]]) is partial: it suggests a slow, reluctant decay of a founding text's authority rather than a clean cut, but Yates offers no such theory.

## Notable passages

The framing claim (c1) that acceptance of Casaubon deprived More and Cudworth of Hermes as priscus theologus; the analysis of More's omission of Hermes (c2) and what it may mean (c3); Cudworth's own denunciation of Christian forgers (c4) followed by Yates's irony about the Christian Hermetist inheritance (c5); and her closing observation linking Cudworth's salvaged Egypt to Bruno (c6).
