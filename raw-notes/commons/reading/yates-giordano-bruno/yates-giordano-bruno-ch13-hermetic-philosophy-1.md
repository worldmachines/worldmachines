# Bruno's Hermetic Copernicus: heliocentricity, earth-movement and infinity in the Cena

Yates opens Chapter XIII with the Cena de le ceneri (1584), reading its satire of the pedant doctors as a echo of Bruno's quarrel with the Oxford doctors and its praise of Copernicus as a proclamation that Bruno's philosophy is a *prisca magia* (Chaldeans, Egyptians, Magi, Orphics, Pythagoreans). She then asks what the famous passages that nineteenth-century liberals read as science breaking medieval shackles actually mean. Her answer: the returning ancient truth is not astronomical heliocentricity but a Hermetic insight into the divinity of the universe. She reads the Nolan's ascent through the spheres as a gnostic experience matching Pimander and Agrippa, the Copernican diagram as a Hermetic hieroglyph, the motion of the earth as the vitalism of Corpus Hermeticum XII, and Bruno's infinite worlds as animated beings and an extension of the Hermetic gnosis.

## Themes

**Copernicus as precursor, Bruno as prophet.** Bruno praises Copernicus as the dawn before the full sunrise of the ancient philosophy, but faults him as "more a student of mathematics than of nature". Yates notes that even Copernicus quoted Hermes Trismegistus in the Asclepius on the sun as visible god near his diagram, which she calls "the clue". This is the chapter's method: the Hermetic reading is Yates's interpretation, and she supports it from Bruno's own text and Copernicus's citation.

**Egyptian, not confessional, truth.** Yates links the image of Time bringing Truth to Light (used in England by both Marian Catholics and Elizabethan Protestants) with the "Mercuries and Apollos" who buried the truth, and with the De umbris idearum. The suppressed truth is, on her reading, magical and Egyptian, the lament of the Asclepius. She notes a double vision, already seen in the Spaccio, by which Bruno's quarrel with Oxford Protestants also reaches their Christian predecessors. She keeps the hedge that this is a reading, not an explicit statement.

**The gnostic ascent.** Bruno describes himself as having crossed the air and "penetrato il cielo". Yates compares this with the Pimander ascent through the spheres and with Agrippa's De occulta philosophia, where the magician who has "mounted higher than the heavens" can operate on celestial images. Bruno's words are almost the same as Agrippa's, "except that Bruno leaves out the angels". The expression is borrowed from Ariosto's Orlando furioso, which Yates takes as typical of Bruno's mix of magic, philosophy and poetry ("The lunatic, the lover, and the poet").

**The diagram as hieroglyph.** Yates uses Kepler's 1621 remark to Fludd ("Tu tractas Mathematica more Hermetico") to interpret the debate over the Copernican diagram in the Cena. She suggests, tentatively, that the book fetched to settle the dispute may replace the Ficino fetched at Oxford, according to George Abbot.

**Earth-movement as Hermetic vitalism.** Bruno's account of why the earth moves (renewal, vicissitude, the incorruptible substance passing through all forms) is set beside Corpus Hermeticum XII, in which living things do not die but are dissolved and renewed, and the earth is both stable and in movement. Yates adds that Agrippa quoted this in his chapters on the world soul and added that the earth is alive. Bruno's principle is the soul in things.

**Infinity as extended gnosis.** Yates acknowledges the usual antecedents (Cusanus, Thomas Digges, the principle of plenitude) but insists that Bruno's worlds are animated "great animals" and that his world-view is "vitalistic, magical". The Hermetic texts do not contain an infinite universe, but Yates finds the spirit of one (Asclepius on the space outside the world; the amplitude of the Good). She reads the De immenso as showing the Hermetic ascent landing Bruno in the infinite, with man as *magnum miraculum* extending to an infinite god.

## Notable passages

The curated passages give the thesis (the sun as a Hermetic clue), the ascent parallel with Agrippa, the Kepler comparison for the diagram, the tie between Corpus Hermeticum XII and Bruno's earth-movement, the vitalist reading of infinity, and the closing move from man as great miracle to the infinite.
