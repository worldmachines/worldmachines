# Hermes Trismegistus: the misdated founding texts of Renaissance magic

Yates opens by contrasting two Renaissance returns to antiquity, the humanist and the reforming, both correctly dated, with a third, the return to a golden age of magic, built on a radical error: the Corpus Hermeticum and Asclepius were written c. A.D. 100-300, not by an Egyptian priest older than Moses and Plato. She sketches the second-century world (Festugiere's analysis) that produced them, then traces how the Fathers transmitted the legend: Lactantius embraces Hermes as a Gentile prophet of the Son of God, Augustine condemns the god-making passage of the Asclepius while still granting Hermes great antiquity. She then turns to Ficino, whom Cosimo ordered in 1463 to translate Hermes before Plato, and to the genealogy of the prisca theologia in his Pimander argumentum, and closes on how the flood of misdated late-antique texts (Chaldean Oracles, Orphic hymns, Hermetica) rehabilitated magic as a respectable, learned pursuit.

## Themes

**A cyclic, backward-looking model of progress.** Yates says the Renaissance's forward movements drew vigour from looking backwards: progress was 'revival, rebirth, renaissance of antiquity', and truth was the early gold from which the present was a degeneration. This is the frame in which a misdated source could carry so much weight. It sits against the [[world-machines]] picture of era-scale cycles only loosely: here the cycle is a felt worldview of decline from a golden age, not a structural lifecycle of infrastructure.

**The error in dating as the hinge.** The Hermetica are pseudo-Egyptian, by unknown, probably Greek authors, containing popular Platonism and Stoicism with some Jewish and perhaps Persian influences. The Renaissance treated them as the fount from which Plato drank. Yates presents this as the central illusion of the book, and notes that the Chaldean Oracles (Zoroaster) and Orphic hymns were misdated in the same way. For WMP this is a founding-text misdating: an era's authority resting on a philological mistake, with the correction still to come later in the book.

**The second-century context.** Following Festugiere, Yates describes a well-organised Roman world whose Greek philosophy had reached a dead end (she suggests because it never took the step of experimental verification), and whose seekers turned to gnosis, intuitive Nous, the cult of the old and distant, and above all reverence for Egypt. Hermetism is a religion of the world without temples or liturgy, followed in the mind alone.

**Two Fathers, two Hermeses.** Lactantius uses Hermes as an ally for Christianity, citing the Asclepius on the second God as Son and the Pimander's Word, and sets him with the Sibyls; he never speaks against him. Augustine, using the Latin Asclepius, condemns the Egyptian statue-animating magic as demonic, yet grants that Hermes says much of God according to the truth and gives him a genealogy placing him after Moses but before the Greeks. That genealogy opens the Renaissance debate about the relative dates of Moses and Hermes. Ficino's response is to quote Augustine and then ignore him by practising magic; Bruno's, Yates says, will be bolder.

**Ficino, Cosimo and the prisca theologia.** The 1463 order to translate Hermes before Plato shows the Renaissance conviction that what is older is closer to divine truth. In the argumentum Ficino gives the chain Mercurius, Orpheus, Aglaophemus, Pythagoras, Philolaus, Plato, and Yates stresses that he drew it from the Fathers rather than from Pletho. The Pimander's diffusion (sixteen editions to 1600) shows how widely the Hermetic estimate was shared.

**Magic gains respectability.** Yates argues that the Byzantine influx of occult-tinged late-antique philosophy (Iamblichus, Porphyry, Plotinus) and the genealogy of the prisci theologi who were also prisci magi made magic a learned, reformed practice. She holds it 'almost certainly' the Corpus Hermeticum's demonstrated piety that rehabilitated the Asclepius.

## Notable passages

**The thesis (c1).** The return to a golden age of magic 'was based on a radical error in dating', to the pagan, gnostic background of early Christianity rather than Egyptian wisdom.

**Dating the Hermetica (c2).** Yates's careful statement of what the Asclepius and Corpus Hermeticum actually are, keeping scholarly hedges about native Egyptian elements.

**Lactantius (c3).** Hermes as Gentile prophet alongside the Sibyls, which made Lactantius a favourite of the Christian Magus.

**Augustine and the Magus's dilemma (c4).** The options open to Renaissance Hermetists, including the interpolation theory, Ficino's evasion, and Bruno's reversal.

**The 1463 commission (c5).** Cosimo sets Plato aside for Hermes, the strongest evidence for the authority of the misdated text.

**The genealogy of wisdom (c6).** Ficino's prisca theologia chain and Yates's claim about its importance; she also says the effect of this awestruck approach on Ficino's Platonism has not been sufficiently investigated.
