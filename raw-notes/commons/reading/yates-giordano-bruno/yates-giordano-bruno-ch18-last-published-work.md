# Bruno's De imaginum compositione: the magic memory as inner reform

Yates treats Bruno's last published work, written for the alchemy-minded Hainzell at Elgg near Zurich and printed by Wechel in 1591, as a late, dense restatement of his Hermetic programme. Formally it is a mnemonic system built on twelve central 'principles' (Jupiter, Saturn, Mars, Mercury, Minerva, Apollo with the Aesculapius group, Sol, Luna, Venus, Cupid, Tellus and others), each with lists of epithets and composed talismanic images. Yates argues, hedging with 'I believe', that the principles and epithets match the gods and virtues and vices of the *Spaccio* (the reform of the heavens), that the book's purpose is to draw only the good sides of these powers into the personality and so form a Solar, Jovial and Venereal Magus, and that the whole is a development of Ficino's *De vita coelitus comparanda*. She ends by reading the infinite universe itself as a hieroglyph of the divine rather than as scientific philosophy. See [[giordano-bruno]] for other readings.

## Themes

**The Spaccio reform re-encoded in a memory system.** The twelve principles parallel the council of gods in the *Spaccio* (Yates sets the two lists side by side), and the epithets attached to Jupiter, Saturn, Mars, Venus and Mercury are the 'raw material' for the ascent and descent of virtues and vices in the celestial reform. The memory work gives Yates a key to the *Spaccio*: the reform is a victory of the good sides of astral influences over the bad, with Orpheus and Arion's solar incantations overcoming Saturnian religious misery, Mars standing for religious wars and Mercury's bad side for 'grammarian pedants'. Bruno, she says, offers Hainzell the same panacea he offered Sidney six years earlier.

**Ficino's astral cult turned into an inner technique.** Ficino aimed to escape Saturn and Mars by cultivating Sol, Jupiter and Venus, as a therapy that shaded into a religion. Yates argues that Bruno, who knew the *De vita coelitus comparanda* by heart (via Abbot), radicalised this into a technique for forming a religious Magus, and that he accepts the magical religion of the *Asclepius* as the best religion, going further than the Christian Hermetists.

**Talismanic images and a possible key to Renaissance image-making.** Bruno mixes classical and 'unfamiliar' barbarous forms, apparently reversing the early Renaissance movement toward classical form, perhaps as part of his 'Egyptianism' (recovering magical power). Yates speculates, cautiously, that this may show how Ficino composed images and lend support to her reading of Botticelli's *Primavera* as an expanded talismanic Venus; she notes that Fludd used a related system in 1619.

**Imagination as the organ of the divine.** Bruno cites Aristotle on thinking with images and then Synesius on divine images in dreams, without noticing that the two are opposed. Yates reads this confusion as the mark of his transformation of a rational Aristotelian and Thomist mnemonic technique into a magical-religious one. Bruno's appeal to the golden calf and brazen image as Mosaic magic, Promethean clay figures and Egyptian statues, and the Genesis-Pimander equation of inner light, tie it to the Hermetic-Cabalist tradition.

**The infinite universe as hieroglyph.** Yates's closing claim is that the infinite worlds are, for Bruno, 'hieroglyphs of the divine' to be imprinted on memory in the effort to become one with the universe, not primarily philosophy or science. This resonates with WMP's interest in magic as a will to operate that precedes science, though Yates makes the claim about Bruno's intent only, not about causes of modern science.

## Notable passages

**The book's real purpose (c1).** Beyond the Spaccio parallels, Yates says the work is about composing talismanic images, one per principle, to attract only the good aspects into the personality; the count of images (Jupiter 18, Apollo group 20, Saturn and Mars 4 each) shows the intended Solar-Jovial-Venereal Magus.

**Ficino to Bruno (c2).** Yates states the line of development from Ficino's mild astral therapy to Bruno's inner technique, and places Bruno inside Christian Hermetism while noting he goes much further.

**Same panacea as for Sidney (c3).** The compact statement that the memory treatise conceals the Spaccio's reform beneath 'appalling intricacies'.

**Classical versus barbarous images (c4).** Yates's open question about whether Bruno is idiosyncratic or leaves a door ajar on Renaissance image composition.

**Aristotle, Synesius and the magicisation of memory (c5).** The clearest account of how Bruno converts a rational art into a religious-magical one, ending in the Hermetic-Cabalist justification.

**Hieroglyphs of the divine (c6).** The concluding reframing of the infinite universe as religious hieroglyph, ending on Bruno's line about seeing all things in oneself.
