# The reign of Hermes dated, Bruno re-placed, and mechanism's cost: Yates's conclusion

This is the closing section of Yates's book, following the Fludd-Mersenne controversies. She first concedes that the genuine history of science (Duhem, Padua, Greek mathematics revived) needs no Hermes, but says it cannot explain why the new interest in nature arose when it did. Her answer is a history of motives: a new direction of the will toward the world and toward operating with it, which she traces to the Hermetic tradition broadly construed. She dates that tradition's reign exactly, from Ficino's translation to Casaubon's exposure, restates Bruno as a Hermetic magician and not a proto-scientific martyr, and reads Descartes's dreams, mathesis and dualism against the Hermetic background. She ends by suggesting that mechanism's insistence on an externalised, impersonal nature is why the problem of mind was left behind.

## Themes

**A history of motives fills a gap in the history of science.** Yates does not claim that magic is science: the Magus's procedures have nothing to do with genuine science. The claim is narrower. Historians of science can trace the stages toward Galileo but not why interest in nature's workings surged in the seventeenth century, and she quotes others that the change was a change of intellectual outlook whose cause is obscure. She proposes that a movement of the will originates an intellectual movement, and that Hermes Trismegistus names the cluster behind that will: Ficinian Neoplatonism, Pico's Hermetism-plus-Cabalism, the sun as source of power, animated nature, number, the unity of the All, and the Christianising errors that made Hermes licit to study and even to operate with. Her hedges are consistent (may, perhaps, suggested); the long-view story of retreat from reason into the occult and then stimulus to genuine science is called perhaps too beautiful and coherent to be quite true.

**The reign of Hermes is datable, and the dating is the hinge.** It begins with Ficino's translation of the Corpus Hermeticum and ends with Casaubon. Within it the attitudes that led to modern science appeared; Casaubon's dating came at the right moment, when the work was done. For World Machines this is a striking case of a founding text's misdating being productive for centuries and its philological correction acting as a hinge in what [[modernity-machine]] readings treat as the building phase of legibility; the fit is loose, since Yates frames it as intellectual history, not infrastructure.

**Bruno, corrected.** Against the Berti tradition of Bruno as martyr for Copernican science, Yates states her own positive claim: Bruno was an out-and-out Egyptian Hermetist for whom Copernicanism signalled the return of magical religion, using Cabala only as subsidiary to Magia. Drain the animism and substitute inertia and gravity and his universe would become something like Newton's ([[isaac-newton]]); the long misreading of Bruno as an advanced thinker is, she says, itself evidence that Hermes helped prepare the revolution. See [[giordano-bruno]]. She adds Bacon's New Atlantis and Campanella's City of the Sun as Utopias from the same Hermetic-Cabalist stream, while noting that Bacon is a weak example since his status as father of experimental science is now weakened ([[francis-bacon]]).

**Two phases of the scientific revolution.** She proposes viewing it as an animistic universe operated by magic followed by a mathematical universe operated by mechanics, and asks whether all science is a gnosis. The Descartes episode (the dreams of November 1619, the Rosicrucian rumours, the search for a universal mathesis) is used to show the Hermetic atmosphere surrounding the birth of analytical geometry.

**The cost of externalisation.** The magician draws the world into himself; the scientist externalises and impersonalises it. Yates suggests that Descartes's dualism, with mind parked in the conarion, was the reaction against the Hermetic internalisation of the world in the mens, and that this is why the problem of mind fell so far behind the problem of matter. This resonates loosely with WMP's interest in what a legibility-optimising machine leaves out, though Yates's claim is about epistemology and mind, not social control.

## Notable passages

The will-to-operate passage (c1) is the core of the thesis and lists the components of Hermes as an explanatory umbrella. The dating passage (c2) is short and load-bearing. The long-view passage (c3) gives the Festugière-derived narrative of retreat from reason and its reversal, with Yates's own caution. The Bruno passage (c4) is her most compact statement of the book's revised Bruno. The Newton and two-phases passage (c5) gives the animism-to-mechanics reading and the gnosis question. The externalisation passage (c6) states the magician/scientist contrast and the internal quality of Hermetic attitude.
