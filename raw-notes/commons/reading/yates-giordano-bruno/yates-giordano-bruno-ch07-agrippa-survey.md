# Agrippa's Survey of Renaissance Magic as the Extreme Case of Ficino and Pico

Yates devotes a chapter to Cornelius Agrippa's *De occulta philosophia* (completed by 1510, published 1533) not because Agrippa is a deep magician (she notes that Cardanus despised the book) but because it gives the first clear synoptic survey of Renaissance magic, and because Bruno made great use of it. She walks through the three books, on natural magic in the elemental world, celestial magic (number, images, talismans, Orphic invocation) and ceremonial or religious magic (Cabala, divine names, angelic hierarchies), showing at each stage how Agrippa borrows from Ficino (*De vita coelitus comparanda*, spiritus), the *Asclepius* and *Corpus Hermeticum*, Pico's Cabala, and the *Picatrix* tradition, and how he goes well beyond them. Her conclusion is that the innocent, artistic Ficinian magic and the pious Picoan Cabala carried within them, through the prisca theologia, the power implications that Agrippa makes explicit; the Magus becomes a priest working religious miracles, in a shape close to the Egyptian theocracy of the *Asclepius*. She closes by posing, without answering, how such magic bears on sixteenth-century religious reform, and hands the question forward to Bruno.

## Themes

**A survey, not a summit.** Yates is explicit that Agrippa is derivative and popular, a tidy map of the field. Its value for her history is precisely that it collects and makes legible what Ficino, Pico and the Hermetic texts had left scattered. She also reads the earlier *De vanitate scientiarum* (1530), which declared all sciences vain, as probably a safety-device: a statement made "against" magic that in practice means only against bad uses of it.

**Three worlds, three magics.** Agrippa's cosmos is elemental, celestial and intellectual, with virtue descending from the Creator through angels and stars to elements, and the Magus claiming to reverse the flow by manipulating lower things. The three books map onto physics, mathematics and theology, and magic alone comprises all three. This is a Renaissance version of an operational cosmology: the world is a graded, sympathetic system whose levers can be pulled, which is the seed of the will to operate that Yates later ties to science. It resonates loosely with the [[world-machines]] idea of deep infrastructure that organizes a worldview, though Agrippa's is a cosmology of occult correspondences, not an institutional or technological machine.

**From Ficino's mild magic to demonic and Asclepian magic.** Agrippa quotes Ficino verbatim on star images and spiritus but, unlike Ficino (following D. P. Walker), does not avoid the demonic: he cites the *Asclepius* on demons animating statues and omits Augustine's disapproval. Yates reads him as aiming at the full demonic magic of the *Asclepius*, while noting that he hedges by allowing an evil magic of gnostic magicians and perhaps Templars.

**Mathematics, number and talismans.** Book II makes mathematical magic superior to natural magic (Pythagorean number has more reality than natural things), with magic squares, harmony, and long lists of planetary and decan images. Agrippa even prints the images of the thirty-six decan demons and allows images that represent the operator's wish, opening a field for talismanic invention. The passage where Agrippa says that only one who has "mounted higher than the heavens" can animate an image marks, for Yates, how far beyond the timid Ficino the Agrippan Magus reaches: through all three worlds to the Creator's own creative power.

**Cabala, Pico, and priestly magic.** Book III moves to Pico's type of magic (Dignity of Man, dignification, silence about mysteries), then beyond it to priestly, religious magic with the Sephiroth, Hebrew divine names and Pseudo-Dionysian angelic orders forming one continuous chain with celestial and elemental magic. Agrippa distinguishes a true magical religion based on faith from a superstitious one based on credulity, both able to work miracles.

**Magia and Cabala, and the slide toward Picatrix.** Yates says the theme is *Magia and Cabala, Cabala and Magia*, and that laid out as recipes they seem to slip back toward necromancy (a correspondent asks about "the Picatrix and the Cabala"). But she insists the Renaissance dignity of the Magus, the Hermetic philosophy and the priestly frame make it something new, close to the theocracy of the *Asclepius*.

**Magic and religious reform.** Yates raises, with hedges and no answer, whether iconoclastic rage was provoked by more magic having entered religion through Lactantius, Ficino and Pico (and Alexander VI), and hands Bruno the two-kinds-of-religious-magic solution he took from Agrippa. This sits on the seam between [[giordano-bruno]] and the counter-current of reform; it is a historical question about a founding worldview being reabsorbed into institutional religion, not a machine-cycle claim.

## Notable passages

The opening (c1) establishes Yates's stance and the safety-device reading of the vanity book. The passage on Asclepian demonic magic (c2) is her clearest evidence for Agrippa going beyond Ficino. Agrippa's own words on who can vivify images, together with Yates's gloss on the door left "slightly ajar" by Ficino (c3), give the survey's high point. The pair of summary paragraphs on Magia and Cabala as prisca magia (c4) states her structural claim. The passage on Agrippa's noble robes and the Egyptian theocracy (c5) links it back to the *Asclepius* framework, and the closing questions (c6) frame the religious stakes and the handoff to Bruno.

A WMP-facing caution: Yates is a historian of ideas, and her language is hedged ("probably", "perhaps", "evidently"); the [[modernity-machine]] link is indirect, in the sense that magic as an operative, will-driven stance toward nature precedes the mechanistic science that later dominated, a point she develops in the next chapters, not here.
