# Pico's Cabala as the higher form of magic

Yates first sketches Spanish medieval Cabala: the ten Sephiroth and twenty-two Hebrew letters as the creative Names of God, their link to the ten cosmic spheres, angelic hierarchies, and the two paths (Sephiroth and Names, the latter represented by Abraham Abulafia's letter-combinatory meditation). She then lists the techniques of practical Cabala (angelic names, Notarikon, Temurah, Gematria) and turns to Pico, who learned Hebrew from Jewish friends and valued Cabala as confirming Christianity. Working through the *Conclusiones* and the *Apology*, she argues that Pico divides Cabala into the *ars combinandi* (which she sets aside as belonging to the history of Lull's Art) and a practical Cabala that is 'the supreme part of natural magic'. This is magic that goes beyond the stars to the supercelestial world and the first cause. She ends by suggesting the Magus of the Dignity of Man oration practises both Magia and Cabala, and that this practice was probably largely subjective and artistic, as Walker argues for Ficino.

## Themes

**Cabala as theosophy tied to cosmology.** The Sephiroth are creative Names, and their correspondence with the ten spheres (Pico's forty-eighth Cabalist conclusion) is what lets Yates call Cabalist magic 'the completion of natural magic'. It taps spiritual forces that are 'yet organically related to the stars'. Angels are the intermediaries, and bad angels mirror the good ones.

**Practical Cabala is a magic, not only a mysticism.** Yates keeps a hedge throughout. She says she speaks from 'the point of view of a mystical believer in magic', that she is understanding Pico only 'so far as I am able', and that for a personality like his the line between mysticism and magic 'is difficult and perhaps impossible to trace'. Pico's disclaimers about wicked Cabalist magicians imply, she infers, that good practical Cabalists used similar methods (Hebrew names, angel names, arranged letters) but raised good angels.

**Magia versus Cabala: mediated versus immediate causation.** Natural magic works through intermediary causes (the stars, the natural spiritus, characters). Pure Cabala works in the intellectual part of the soul, through numbers via letters, and goes directly to the first cause (the twenty-sixth magical conclusion). This is why 'no Magia' can attain what it does. Yates points out that the higher magic is also more dangerous (Azazael).

**Christianizing Cabala.** Pico's seventy-two conclusions claim to confirm 'the Christian religion from the foundations of Hebrew wisdom', reading the Tetragrammaton as the Trinity and 'Iesu' cabalistically. Yates also notes his denial that Christ's miracles were done by Magia or Cabala.

**Transmission and the operative Magus.** Reuchlin's *De arte cabalistica* (1517) carries Pico's Cabala on to the angel magic of Trithemius and Agrippa, who worked it 'in a more crudely operative spirit'. Yates closes by reading the oration's Raphael-Gabriel-Michael passage as a hint of practical Cabala. She reads the complete Magus as a practitioner of both magics, whose use was probably subjective, and she extends the 'operative Magi' idea to artists such as Donatello and Michelangelo.

## Notable passages

The opening exposition (c1) gives the baseline account of the Sephiroth, cosmos and angelic hierarchies. The passage on practical Cabala and Pico's Hebrew studies (c2) supplies the techniques and the Christianizing motive. The two-branch reading of Pico's Cabala (c3) is Yates's central interpretive move, and it says that the second Cabala 'must go high up beyond the stars'. The twenty-sixth magical conclusion and her gloss (c4) state the distinction from natural magic. The Sephiroth-sphere correspondence (c5) explains why Cabalist magic can be the 'completion' of Ficinian magic. The final argument about the oration, subjectivity and artists (c6) gives her view of what the Renaissance Magus was.

## Resonances

This unit is on the will to operate and its escalation. Magic here is a claim to operate on the world at a higher causal level than the astral, which bears on the argument that Renaissance magic is a precursor of the operative stance of science, made later in the book. Yates does not make that claim here, and her Pico is largely contemplative and 'subjective', so the resonance is only half-fitting. Cabalist number and letter calculation (Gematria) treats the world as legible by numerical decoding, which rhymes with the legibility theme of the [[modernity-machine]] and [[Legibility]], though here the decoding is theological and the aim is invocation rather than administrative control. The cosmic-sphere and Sephiroth scheme is a pre-modern picture of a layered world-infrastructure, but it is a cosmology, not a world machine in the [[world-machines]] sense. The line to Agrippa and, through the wider book, to [[giordano-bruno]] marks Pico as an early node of the tradition Bruno later radicalizes.
