# Campanella after the revolt: a Bruno who survived, and a Magus born too late

This closing section of Yates's Campanella chapter places Campanella and Bruno in a single sequence that runs from Ficino, through Pico and Lactantius's reception of Hermes into the Church, to the two Dominicans. It explains how Campanella could swing from the Calabrian revolt against Spain to prophesying universal Spanish, papal and French monarchies. It then follows his later career: the release from prison in 1626, the anti-eclipse magic for Urban VIII in 1628, the loss of Roman credit, and the move to Paris in 1634. Yates sets out the Bruno parallels: French kingship, Elizabeth and Charles I's Coelum Britannicum, poetic canticles, and memory systems. She ends with the historical verdict. Campanella belongs to a world in which the Hermetic foundation had been cut away by textual criticism, and the new science of Mersenne and Descartes has no use for him.

## Themes

**Continuity of the Hermetic sequence.** Yates insists that Bruno and Campanella must be read inside the series she has built: Ficino's apology for magic, Pico's Magia and Cabala, Alexander VI's Egyptianising fresco, and Lactantius's reception of Hermes into the Church. She calls that reception a momentous step, never accepted by all and always severely criticised by the orthodox. Del Rio's 1600 book against magic marks Counter-Reformation alarm, yet Hermes was too deeply infiltrated into Renaissance religion to be easily expelled.

**Political evolution after the revolt.** Following Blanchet, Yates argues that Campanella did not decide the portents had deceived him. He concluded that some Monarch, whether Spanish, papal or French, would have to build the City. She adds two suggestions of her own. The first is that the idea of an ideal state in southern Italy spreading outward had a precedent in Frederick II's Sicilian kingdom, which she thinks probably inspired Dante's Monarchia. She frames this cautiously as a question (is it not impossible that such memories lay behind the revolt). The second is that Campanella added an Egyptian ideal, a state kept eternal by priestly magic, to the Roman and Platonic ones. The Sun ruler is priest, philosopher and king, in short Hermes Trismegistus.

**Not a liberal.** Yates says Campanella was in no sense a liberal revolutionary. His Solarian science (Copernican interest, mechanical devices) was real but held and regulated by a supreme priesthood, as in Egypt. This is a legibility-and-control picture of magic and science fused with theocracy, and it fits uneasily with a simple story in which magic loosens into science. Compare [[world-machines]] and [[legibility-as-machine-core]]. The fit is partial, because Campanella's regulation runs through celestial influences and priestly magic rather than through the administrative measurement Scott describes.

**Bruno and Campanella as variations on one pattern.** Yates lists the parallels. Both seek a Monarch as channel for reform (Henri III, Henry of Navarre and Elizabeth for Bruno; Spain, the papacy and France for Campanella). Both prophesy through solar portents, and both talk to Paris librarians and scholars about the Sacrament and a City of the Sun. Both write poetic Canticles, and both have memory systems. She calls Campanella a more successful Bruno, while noting that Bruno was more extreme and violent, and that Campanella never lived in Protestant countries or joined the cult of heretic monarchs. She raises as open questions whether the French Monarchy was always his real ideal, and whether the Spanish cult was only a lever to escape prison. Her link between Bruno's Spaccio and Charles I's 1634 masque Coelum Britannicum is a documented afterlife of [[giordano-bruno]].

**Twenty years too late.** Bruno's life fell in the crescendo of Hermetism. Campanella lived into its wane, after Casaubon-style textual criticism had dated the Hermetica accurately and removed the foundation of the whole Renaissance edifice. Mersenne finds that Campanella can teach nothing in the sciences, and Descartes declines to meet him. For Yates this marks the shift to the modern world, where science replaces magic, and Mersenne's campaign against magic is also a campaign against natural theology. Campanella's late success shows how firm a foothold Hermes had gained within religion. This is a strong case of a founding text's misdating being a load-bearing assumption, and of its philological correction as the collapse of a worldview. It resonates with the [[modernity-machine]] as the machine that displaced the Renaissance one, though Yates herself gives no machine-theoretic framing and treats the transition as intellectual history.

## Notable passages

The Egyptian ideal (c1) is the analytic core of Yates's reading of the City of the Sun. The mediaeval-imperial explanation of the political switches (c2) answers her own question about how a rebel could become a prophet of universal monarchy. The Coelum Britannicum passage (c3) documents Bruno's Spaccio reaching the Caroline court. The two-decade gap passage (c4) and the Mersenne letter (c5) give her verdict on the fall of Hermetic natural magic. The closing sonnet (c6) is Campanella's own statement of nature as the book of God, and it is the primary text she chooses to end on.
