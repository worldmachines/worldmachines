# Hermetic Magic: Astral Sympathy, the Decans, Picatrix and Ficino's Transition to Practice

Yates opens by dividing the Hermetic literature into the philosophical treatises (Corpus Hermeticum, Asclepius, Stobaeus fragments) and the astrological, alchemical and magical writings, and insists the two cannot be separated: gnosis and magic go together. She then sets out the mechanics of sympathetic magic, the Egyptian layer of the thirty-six decans, and the practical Hermetic lists of images, plants and stones. The core of the chapter is a long analysis of the Picatrix, an Arabic (probably twelfth-century, Sabean/Harran) textbook of talismanic magic, whose philosophy of spiritus and materia and whose city of Adocentyn she reads as echoing the Asclepius. She closes by asking how Ficino, so careful to reconcile Platonism with Christianity, let magic in. Her answer is the misdated prisca theologia: he returned, without knowing it, to the pagan Egyptianising gnosis of Julian's reaction, and his timid attempt to escape a Saturnian horoscope opened a flood-gate.

## Themes

**Gnosis and magic are one system.** Pessimist gnostics need magical passwords to escape the stars; optimist gnostics draw the good powers down by sympathy, invocation and talisman. Yates stresses that the method and cosmology are identical whether the magician seeks material benefit or, as in the hieratic magic of the Asclepius, religious insight. The operator works by knowing the chains of influence descending from above and building a chain of ascending links.

**The Egyptian decans.** The thirty-six decans, Egyptian sidereal gods of time absorbed into Chaldean astrology and affiliated to the zodiac, were both astrological divisions and powerful gods with images drawn from temple archives. Yates points to their high rank in the Asclepius and in a Stobaeus fragment (Hermes to Tat), and to the Liber Hermetis and Liber Sacer. She later shows the same figures in Borso d'Este's Ferrara frescoes and in Julian's Hymn to Helios and Celsus's Egyptian daemons, where they are the demons Augustine and Origen condemned.

**Picatrix as the practical counterpart of Pimander.** Never printed, it circulated widely in manuscript (Pico's library, Lazzarelli, Champier's disapproval, Rabelais's mockery, Henri III's Spanish books). Its philosophy (the One, a hierarchy of intellectus, spiritus, materia; spiritus descending and residing where it is caught) is close to the Corpus Hermeticum, yet it sits in a frankly practical context of talismans, fumigations and orations aimed at cures, success, love and escape from prison. Yates is careful to say Picatrix is not attributed to Hermes and that it is only possibly one of Ficino's authorities.

**Adocentyn as Hermetic utopia.** The Picatrix account of Hermes founding an animated, image-guarded city where astral magic enforces virtue is, in Yates's reading (hedged with "perhaps" and "may"), a picture of Hermes Mercurius Triplex as priest, philosopher-magician and lawgiver, and it dovetails with the Asclepius's Lament and prophecy of a restored Egyptian religion. This is a magic-as-social-engineering vision that loosely rhymes with WMP's interest in engineered legibility and utopian design (compare [[more-utopia]]), though here the machinery is talismanic and cosmological rather than administrative or statistical.

**Ficino's transition and the misdating.** Ficino's chief exemplar, following D. P. Walker, was the magic of the Asclepius, with Picatrix a possible source and Neoplatonists (Proclus, Iamblichus) as encouragement. The prisca theologia, the supposed antiquity and piety of Hermes, gave him an excuse to adopt Lactantius's favourable view and evade Augustine's warning. Yates argues the result is a return not to primeval revelation but to the anti-Christian pagan gnosis of Julian. This is a case of a civilisation's founding text being misdated, with large consequences; it anticipates the later Casaubon turning point that bears on the [[modernity-machine]], though the dating question itself is not argued in this unit.

**Astral magic versus astrology.** Astrology, in Yates's terminology, is deterministic and mathematical; the magic she treats works from the stars but as a way of gaining power over them and escaping fate, or as salvation. She therefore coins the term "astral magic". The operative, anti-fatalist stance is an early form of the will to act on the world, but Yates does not here connect it to science.

## Notable passages

The curated passages are: the mechanics of sympathetic magic; Picatrix on spiritus and materia; Hermes's city of Adocentyn (source text) and Yates's reading of it; the argument that Picatrix could have carried Ficino from theory to practice; and the closing claim that Ficino's Hermetic return was to Julian's pagan gnosis and that his magic is astral rather than astrological. Note that the Picatrix images and Origen's reply to Celsus are quoted matter, not Yates's own claims, and that Yates repeatedly hedges ("it is possible", "perhaps", "could have been").
