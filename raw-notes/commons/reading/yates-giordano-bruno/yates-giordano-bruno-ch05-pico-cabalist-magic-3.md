# Pico's religious magic, his condemnation, and the Borgia vindication

This part of Yates's chapter on Pico completes her account of the "double magic" of Magia and Cabala. She argues that harnessing natural magic to Cabala took magic into the supercelestial world of angels and divine powers, and so into religion itself. She then traces the Hermetic-Cabalist link at the level of religious experience, the gnostic roots of the Magus's dignity, the theological storm around Pico (Innocent VIII's commission, the 1487 retraction, Pedro Garcia's 1489 reply, Archangelo's later defence), and the reversal under Alexander VI. She reads the Pinturicchio frescoes in the Borgia Apartments as a coded statement of that reversal.

## Themes

**Magic inside religion.** Pico's Psalms-as-incantations and his claim that Magia and Cabala confirm the divinity of Christ made the cult of magic the religious cult. Yates poses a question she says later controversies inherited: should religious reform put more magic into religion or take it out? She hints, without asserting, at a bearing on Reformation iconoclasm. She keeps her hedges ("Possibly Pico simply means", "The problem is perhaps insoluble"), and admits she has no explanation of how Magia proves Christ's divinity.

**A double-edged tool.** Pico himself guarded against the risk by denying that Christ's works were done by Magia or Cabala. Yates notes that "later magicians were to take up this dangerous thought."

**The changed status of the magician.** Practical Cabala replaces the medieval conjuring of the Clavis Salomonis, as Ficino's refined magic replaces necromancy from the Picatrix. The outcast conjuror becomes the pious Magus, a shift Yates compares to the artist's rise from mechanic to companion of princes. She holds that the techniques keep a continuity with the older magics.

**Gnostic root.** Both Hermetica and Cabala are gnostic in origin (she cites Scholem and the ogdoadic ascent in the Hekhaloth literature). Pico's ten Hermetic conclusions, with the ten "ultores" set against Cabala's ten evil forces and the Sephiroth, are read by Yates ("or so I interpret it") as his perception that the Hermetist's regeneration and the Cabalist's are the same experience. The Dignity of Man as Magus rests on the gnostic Asclepius, not on patristic sources. The Renaissance magician becomes "a divine man", parallel to the divine Raphael or Leonardo. Compare the [[modernity-machine]]'s later secular will to operate: this is its theological, pre-scientific ancestor, though here the operating power is divine rather than mechanical.

**The Pico case.** Yates gives the chronology of the commission, the condemnation of the seventh magical conclusion, the retraction, the flight to France and Vincennes, Lorenzo's support and death in 1494. Garcia's reply rejected all magic as diabolical, attacked talismans, denied that "ecclesiastical magic" was comparable, and denied Cabala's antiquity. Yates treats Garcia and Archangelo as epitomising the sixteenth-century argument over magic and religious practice.

**Alexander VI and Pico's vindication.** Alexander's 1493 bulls and letter absolved Pico and his works, and the letter was printed in all editions. Yates reads the Disputationes against divinatory astrology as really a defence of Ficinian astral magic. She then argues that the Egyptianism of the Borgia frescoes (Apis, Io-Isis, Mercury killing Argus identified with Hermes Trismegistus beside Moses) shows the Pope adopting Pico's programme. She closes on Pico as the first to formulate man as Magus acting on the world "to control his destiny by science".

## Notable passages

c1 (Psalms as incantations) shows the religious cult itself as magical practice. c2 (change in the magician's status) gives Yates's sociology of the Magus. c3 (Hermetic-Cabalist experience) is her core interpretive claim about Pico's syncretism, hedged as her own reading. c4 (Magus as divine man) links the Asclepius to the dignity of man. c5 (Garcia) states the theological case against magic that structured later debate. c6 (Borgia programme and Pico's significance) offers her hypothesis about the frescoes and her closing verdict, tying the Magus to science. Where WMP asks how deep infrastructure shifts, this unit offers a case of a founding-text misdating ([[world-machines]]) working through papal patronage, though Yates makes no such theoretical claim herself.
