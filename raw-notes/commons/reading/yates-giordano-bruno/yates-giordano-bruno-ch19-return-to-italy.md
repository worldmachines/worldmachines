# Bruno's Return to Italy, Trial and Death: Mission, Egyptian Cross, and the End of the Philosophical-Martyr Legend

Yates's closing chapter asks why Bruno walked into Venice in August 1591, and answers that Mocenigo's invitation to teach memory was a pretext: Bruno believed a new day was dawning. Henry of Navarre's rise, Francesco Pucci's and Francesco Patrizi's Hermetic-flavoured irenicism, and Pope Clement VIII's favour to philosophers seemed to promise a liberal, reformed Catholicism into which his 'Egyptian' message could be absorbed. She then reconstructs the arrest (26 May 1592), the sources for the trial (Spampanato, Mercati's *Sommario*, the lost *processo*), Bruno's theology as a non-Christian Hermetist, his view of the cross as a stolen Egyptian sign, and the final condemnation and burning of 17 February 1600. She concludes that the legend of Bruno as martyr of infinite worlds and heliocentrism cannot stand, and ends with suggestive (explicitly tentative) links to Shakespeare's Berowne, Boccalini and Galileo's *Dialogo*.

## Themes

**The mission behind the return.** Yates reads Bruno as never anti-Catholic: the Catholic religion 'pleased him more than any other' but needed reformation, and he was to assist it. Mocenigo's delations (reported with a 'flavour of misrepresentation') preserve the familiar Hermetic-Egyptian theme that the world is at its lowest ebb and about to return to a better state, with Bruno as prophet and would-be 'capitano'. Yates hedges: she does not think him greedy, but he wanted recognition of his prophetic role. Messianic self-conception is tied to Hermetism's 'divinising' experience (Lazzarelli's *Crater Hermetis*, the 'Mercurius-Christ'), which she thinks conducive to 'religious mania'.

**Navarrism and religious Hermetism as a shared hope.** Bruno was 'not alone in hoping great things of the King of Navarre'. Yates sets him beside Pucci (also returned in 1592, beheaded 1597), Rosseli's Cracow commentaries on the *Pimander*, and Patrizi's 1591 *Nova de universis philosophia*, which urged a pope to teach Hermetic philosophy instead of coercion. She suggests, with the caveat that detailed research is needed, that Navarrism may have drawn eirenic hopes from varieties of Hermetism, coinciding with what Dagens called the golden age of religious Hermetism. The Del Bene/Corbinelli/Pinelli circle is the likely channel of Bruno's Navarre knowledge.

**Padua as preparation and Bruno's unsuitability.** At Padua he dictated *De vinculis in genere* and copied Hermetic seals; following Firpo, Yates treats this as preparation for a mission for which his rages and temperament unfitted him, unlike Campanella's realism.

**Bruno's theology and the Egyptian cross.** His confession to the Inquisitors is that of a Neoplatonic Hermetist, unorthodox on the Son, with the Holy Spirit as *anima mundi*. Refusing to read the Hermetic *Filius Dei* as the Second Person is, for Yates, 'the root theological reason' his Hermetism becomes purely Egyptian, with the Egyptian religion as the true religion, not a *prisca theologia* foreshadowing Christianity. Ficino's *De vita coelitus comparanda* treats the cross on Serapis's breast as a star-capturing form and presage of Christ; a slight inversion (Christians 'stole' and weakened it) gives Bruno's position. This licenses him to embrace all magic, where Ficino had to keep his natural and non-demonic. Yates presents this source suggestion as a question ('One wonders').

**Why he was condemned.** The lost *processo* means no certain reconstruction; the interrogations were mostly theological and disciplinary. Yates argues that Bruno's philosophy, including Copernicanism (a symbol of the new revelation, a return to Egyptian natural religion and magic), was inseparable from his religion, so the Church was 'within its rights' to include philosophical points. Contrast with Galileo, whose views rested on genuine mathematics and mechanics. Yet on moral grounds Bruno's position stays strong as descendant of the Magi who stood for Dignity of Man, liberty and toleration.

**Speculative afterlives.** Berowne in *Love's Labour's Lost* as echo of the *Spaccio*, Prospero as benevolent Magus, Boccalini's *Ragguagli di Parnaso*, and the formal resemblance of Galileo's *Dialogo* to the *Cena de le ceneri* are offered as pointers for future study, not conclusions.

## Resonances

The chapter is a case study in a would-be [[world-machines]]-era reformer: Bruno as terminal figure of the Hermetic 'return' worldview at the moment when the [[modernity-machine]] hardens (see [[giordano-bruno]]). Yates's separation of Bruno from Galileo (magic and gnosis versus mathematics and mechanics) rhymes with the framing of a legibility regime taking hold, though Yates does not speak in machine terms, and the Church's right to treat philosophy and religion as inseparable is the opposite of a clean science-religion split (compare [[scripture-and-nature-parting-eisenstein]], which is about print, not Bruno). Hope in a prince's conversion as a hinge for civilisational reform is a period-specific bet on political events, not a WMP claim.

## Notable passages

**Mocenigo's report and the prophet of return (c1).** Bruno's alleged talk of the Church using force rather than love, of impending general reform, and of himself as 'capitano'. Yates reads it as the return-to-Egyptian-state theme with mixed prophetic motives.

**The real reason for returning (c2).** Yates's thesis: Navarre's ascendancy and Patrizi's papal success seemed to herald a liberal turn, so Bruno 'was a religious Hermetist' after his fashion.

**Bruno's Hermetic theology (c3).** The non-Christian reading of the Filius Dei explains pure Egyptianism.

**The Egyptian cross (c4).** Ficino's cross-of-Serapis turned into the true religion's seal; a proposed major source of Bruno's Egyptianism.

**Bruno and Galileo, mission and philosophy (c5).** Copernicanism as symbol of the new revelation; the death-by-philosophy legend dissolved.

**Boccalini and the Parnassus echo (c6).** A tentative link between the *Spaccio* and Boccalini's political mythology.
