# Bruno's sources and the Hermetic One: Lucretius, Cusanus, the pedants

Yates closes her account of Bruno's philosophy (the second part of the chapter on his Hermetic philosophy) by asking where the "new vision" of an infinite universe with innumerable worlds came from and what it meant to him. Her answer is that the immediate source is Lucretius, but that Bruno transformed him by adding magical animation and by making the infinite an image of infinite divinity. She then reads Bruno's own genealogy of wisdom, his metaphysics of the All in the One, his selective use of the Hermetica, the syncretist mix of his reading, his anti-Aristotelianism, his hatred of the humanist grammarian pedant, and the religious-tolerance thread in the Cena de le ceneri, with a glance at the imagery of the Cena and De la causa dedications. The unit is a portrait of [[giordano-bruno]] as an "Egyptian" Hermetic Magus whose cosmology, ethics and religion are one thing.

## Themes

**Lucretius transformed into Hermetism.** Yates argues that Bruno took Lucretius's infinite space and inhabited worlds but reversed their spirit: the godless, cold Epicurean universe becomes "a vast extension of Hermetic gnosis, a new revelation of God as magician", to be received by a Magus who must expand himself to infinity. This is Yates's interpretation, resting on what she calls Bruno's misunderstandings of Copernicus and Lucretius. Half-fits with a modern reading of Bruno as a scientific precursor: here the infinite universe is a religious and magical figure, not a physical hypothesis.

**The genealogy of the Wise as a prisca magia.** Bruno's Wittenberg sequence (Egyptians and Chaldeans, Persian Magi, Indian gymnosophists, Orpheus, Greeks, Italians including Lucretius, Germans including Albertus Magnus, Cusanus, Copernicus, Palingenius) places Copernicus and Lucretius inside a single occult lineage. Yates infers ("to my mind") that Lucretian infinity counted for Bruno as extended Egyptian wisdom. She also traces the sphere with centre everywhere to a pseudo-Hermetic twelfth-century treatise, transferred by Cusanus to the universe. A recurring pattern in the book: a founding lineage is assembled from misdated or misread authorities, a prisca theologia in the form of a golden-age genealogy, in tension with progress narratives (a loose rhyme with the [[modernity-machine]] story, though Yates makes no such claim).

**All is One: the philosophy conducive to magic.** Quoting De la causa, principio e uno, Yates shows nature descending and intellect ascending by one ladder from unity and back to unity. She names this "the philosophy conducive to magic": the Magus depends on ladders of occult sympathies, and when it becomes a religion it is the religion of the pseudo-Egyptians in the Spaccio. Bruno speaks of philosophy and religion as one.

**A religious Hermetism without Trinitarian scaffolding.** Bruno chooses the pantheist, optimist treatises (Corpus Hermeticum XII and V), accepts metempsychosis, and ignores the Ficinian and Christianising habit of finding Trinities in ancient theologies, unlike Patrizi or even Agrippa. Yates says he explains this by speaking as a pure natural philosopher. She suggests his Hermetism perhaps comes nearer than the Christian Hermetists' to the true sense of the texts as a gnostic religion of the world, yet, like everyone, he does not know their real date and believes he is returning to Egypt before Moses.

**Syncretism and Aristotle.** Bruno also draws on Plato and the Neoplatonists in Ficino, Averroism, Avicebron, Albertus Magnus, Aquinas, Agrippa, Telesio and Paracelsus. His anti-Aristotelianism is against the pedant who cannot see occult truths (the Copernican theory as a Hermetic seal), while Aristotle himself is a half-diviner; mathematics is likewise pedantry to be transcended by the Nolan.

**The grammarian pedant and humanism.** Yates argues the pedant of the Cena and Candelaio expresses Bruno's hatred of humanist studies for usurping philosophy, a reversal of Erasmus's satire on scholastic pedantry, and that a pedant's language lacks magical and incantatory power. The pedants of the Cena link to those of the Spaccio: religious intolerance of Protestants or Puritans. Bruno's Hermetism belongs to the sixteenth-century religious Hermetism tending toward toleration or an eirenic solution; the long Cena passage on inherited zeal is Bruno's statement of it, and Yates calls him "a missionary of conciliation".

## Notable passages

The opening passage (c1) is Yates's thesis on Lucretius transformed into Hermetic gnosis. The genealogy passage (c2) shows Bruno's own occult lineage and Yates's reading of it. The All-in-One passage (c3) gives Bruno's ladder of nature and intellect. The religious-Hermetism passage (c4) is her account of what Bruno kept from and dropped from the Hermetica. Passages c5 and c6 carry the anti-Aristotelian and anti-humanist argument, in Yates's phrase a reversal of Erasmus. Further Cena and De la causa dedication passages (the palace, the solar lion, the mole and night-birds) are pictorial and only lightly curated; they tie to Yates's remark that poetry, painting and philosophy were one for Bruno.
