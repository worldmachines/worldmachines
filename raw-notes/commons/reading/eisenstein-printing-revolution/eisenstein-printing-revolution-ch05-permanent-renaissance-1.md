# The Permanent Renaissance: Print's Fixity Against the Periodization Debate

Opening Part II and Chapter Five, Eisenstein confronts the periodization problem head-on: historians agree that script gave way to print and that Western culture became “modern” sometime around the fifteenth century, but, in Pierce Butler's 1940 formulation, cannot demonstrate any connection between the two changes beyond their sharing a century. Rather than adjudicate the endless, inconclusive debate over when the Middle Ages ended and modernity began—a debate she treats as definitionally hopeless, since “medieval” and “modern” are labels that cannot be operationally defined—she anchors her inquiry in the one empirically traceable event of the period: the Europe-wide spread of printing workshops in the second half of the fifteenth century. From there she turns to the chapter's actual thesis, previewed in its title: printing did not cause the Italian classical revival, which predated Gutenberg, but it “mutated” that revival by supplying the fixity that made it, uniquely among prior classical revivals, permanent rather than transitory—reopening and revising Erwin Panofsky's celebrated distinction between the Renaissance's cultural permanence and the transience of the “two medieval renascences.”

## Themes

**The periodization debate is a trap she declines to enter.** Historians of the Renaissance locate the shift to modernity with Petrarch and Giotto, well before printing; medievalists and early-modernists split their disciplines at the fifteenth century by academic convention rather than argument; and appeals to a vague “transitional age” (Huizinga's unanswered “What actually was the cultural transformation we call the Renaissance?”) let both sides “pass over in silence an actual transformation in order to argue about a hypothetical one that cannot be clearly defined.” She proposes instead to answer “What was peculiar to the transitional age itself?” directly: “printing deserves to be put first,” because the shift from script to print, unlike “medieval” or “modern,” names tangible objects and dateable, traceable activity—printers' workshops appearing in city after city across the Continent within a few decades.

**Why an acknowledged “radical transformation” stays invisible.** Even scholars who call printing's advent the most important event in the cultural history of mankind fail to give it a place in their periodization schemes, and Eisenstein explains the paradox: print's sui generis effect was not simply to add new titles (which a later scholar might count) but to reproduce old ones in far greater quantity, so that “different spirits from different times were being simultaneously released.” Because print's capacity to increase access—rather than any novelty of content—did the transformative work, a mind conditioned to hunt for new titles or new “mutational elements” finds, and duly reports, that nothing of significance was introduced in the fifteenth century.

**Print's fixity, not Renaissance culture, explains permanence.** Panofsky's formula—the Carolingian and twelfth-century revivals were “limited and transitory,” the Italian Renaissance “total and permanent”—is, in her account, correct as description but wrongly explained. Current debate treats permanence as an inherent virtue “somehow exuded by Renaissance culture,” invoked circularly to justify calling the Quattrocento revival epoch-making, without ever asking why the Carolingian and twelfth-century revivals, which had their own genuinely novel features, proved less durable. She proposes typographical fixity—the preservative powers of print—as the missing causal term Panofsky's formulation never supplies.

**A “total rationalized view” of antiquity needed a century of printing, not Petrarch's lifetime.** She directly disputes Panofsky's claim that Renaissance humanists achieved a distanced, historicized view of the classical past comparable to focused perspective's “distance between the eye and the object.” A totalized view of any past civilization, she argues, presupposes uniform reference guides and a shared, fixed spatiotemporal frame—exactly what scribal culture's “multiform maps and tangled chronologies” could not supply. Evidence follows from quattrocento practice itself: humanists mistook an Attic stele for Michelangelo's work and Carolingian minuscule for ancient Roman lettering, and classical images were slotted into the “memory theaters” of the ars memorandi rather than fixed chronological locations. Only after roughly a century of print sorting and standardizing this material did a shared reference frame—and with it a genuinely distanced view of the past—become possible; the same reorganizing process, she adds, eventually placed the medieval and Gothic past “behind museum walls” too.

**Permanence belongs to the technology, not to any one cultural revival.** She closes by turning Panofsky's own illustration against him: he reads Roman/Italic typefaces (descended from Carolingian models opposed to Gothic) as testifying to “the enduring quality of the Italian Renaissance” against a transitory “Gothic” medievalism. Eisenstein counters that Gothic type itself proved just as durable—dominant in German, Dutch, and Scandinavian printing for centuries, legible to any twentieth-century reader who learned to read from a German newspaper—so what actually endures is a property of mechanical reproduction as such: the permanent imprint comes “not because it drew on one style of lettering rather than another but because it was impressed by type and not by a human hand.”

## Notable passages

- The Butler paradox (c1) is the puzzle the whole chapter exists to dissolve.
- The “printing deserves to be put first” passage (c2) is her explicit methodological pivot away from periodization debates and onto a traceable event.
- The “different spirits…simultaneously released” passage (c3) explains why an admittedly radical transformation evades detection by conventional literary-historical methods.
- The Panofsky permanent/transitory passage (c4) states the claim the chapter exists to reattribute, from Renaissance culture to typographical fixity.
- The “total rationalized view” passage (c5) is her sharpest evidentiary rebuttal, dating the totalized historical frame to a century after Petrarch's lifetime.
- The closing Gothic-type passage (c6) generalizes the argument: permanence is a property of print, not of one favored cultural revival.
