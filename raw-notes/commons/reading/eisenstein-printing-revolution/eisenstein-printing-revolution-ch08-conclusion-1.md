# Conclusion: Trading ‘Modernity’ for the Communications Shift

Opening the book's concluding chapter, Eisenstein recalls its own epigraph — an unnamed account locating “modernity” first in the Renaissance and Reformation and then, more fashionably, in the Scientific Revolution of Copernicus, Galileo, and Newton — only to declare the whole quest futile, since “modernity” is a moving target that later ages keep redefining. In its place she restates the book's method: track the effects of “a definite communications shift” (script to print) across the contested sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The rest of the unit applies that method to the historiography of the Scientific Revolution — explaining paradoxes of timing, crediting printers alongside genius and social change, rebutting Marxist class-struggle accounts — and closes by setting up a parallel between the restoration of Ptolemy's astronomy and Erasmus's restoration of Jerome's Bible, which anticipates the chapter's title, “Scripture and Nature Transformed.”

## Themes

**Retiring ‘modernity’ as the unit of analysis.** Eisenstein argues that debating which movement (humanism, Reformation, science, even industrialization) first produced “modernity” is self-defeating, since each generation's achievements come to look conservative in hindsight (she predicts Copernicus, Galileo, and Newton will eventually be read the way Renaissance humanists already are). Substituting a concrete communications shift for an abstract modernizing process lets her sidestep periodization fights and instead identify “features which were not present in earlier epochs and which altered the textual traditions” of each movement — a methodological move that resonates with the World Machines habit of replacing a vague zeitgeist-label with a specific infrastructure (see [[modernity-machine]]; the resonance is structural, not doctrinal — she names print an “agent,” never a sole cause).

**Print, not new “thinking caps,” explains the timing paradox.** Copernicus's cosmology, Harvey's physiology, and Galileo's mechanics all preceded the telescope and microscope. Rather than accept vague appeals to scientists suddenly “putting on new thinking caps,” Eisenstein points to a prior, traceable change: “the technical literature upon which astronomers relied had undergone change” through altered output and intake — i.e., print reorganized the evidence base before any new instrument existed. This is a precondition, not a cause, of the breakthroughs themselves — close to [[legibility-as-precondition-not-cause]].

**Fixity arrests and reverses scribal corruption.** Applied to cartography and astronomy, the “preservative powers of print” cut both ways: at first, corrupt maps and data were merely duplicated and error perpetuated. But once corrected, positions of stars and coastlines could be “fixed permanently,” decisively arresting and eventually reversing “an age-old process of corruption.” She explicitly warns against the misleading analogy of uniform inertial motion for what is really a reversal — a fixity/standardization dynamic close to [[Legibility]] and [[legibility-as-machine-core]].

**Coordination pools data beyond any prior institution's reach.** Printers who sponsored field trips, instrument advertisements, and technical handbooks let astronomers, geographers, botanists, and zoologists circulate identical, labeled images and receive corrections back from scattered readers — a feedback loop that outstripped even the Alexandrian Museum and replaced a “closed sphere” cosmology with an “open-ended investigatory process.” The same dynamic (many diverse texts assembled “side by side”, output and intake both rising) touches [[printing-press-and-information-overload]], though here the emphasis falls on productive synthesis rather than overload.

**Printers belong with professors and friars, not only with merchants.** Eisenstein rejects Marxist historiography that opposes an “avant garde of early capitalists” to a “rear guard of Latin-reading clerks”: in the age of scholar-printers, commercial and clerical culture were closely intertwined, so class-struggle narratives obscure more than they explain. She extends this to political geography — printing flourished in small federated realms (Venice, then Amsterdam) rather than centralizing nation-states — and to social composition: Aldus Manutius's pocket octavo targeted scholar-diplomats, not a mass market, so the “altered worldview” shows up first among Latin-reading elites, not a rising new class.

**Setting up the Scripture/Nature parallel.** The unit ends by pairing Copernicus (“the second Ptolemy,” emending the Almagest) with Erasmus (redoing Jerome's Vulgate): both pursued traditional restorative goals — purifying an inherited text — by untraditional, print-enabled means, and both were propelled into unconventional new directions precisely by seeking old ones. This is the hinge the chapter title names, and the unit ends here rather than developing it further.

## Notable passages

The unit opens by re-quoting the book's own “opening citation,” the conventional account crediting the Renaissance, Reformation, and above all “the Scientific Revolution” with making “modernity,” admitting frankly that “what is not clear is how it all came about.” Eisenstein's whole strategy answers that admission by substituting print's communications shift for further modernity-talk.

The clearest statement of that substitution follows shortly after: debating which movement was first to be modern is set aside in favor of “features which were not present in earlier epochs and which altered the textual traditions upon which each movement relied” — the book's method compressed into one sentence.

She then applies it to a specific puzzle: naked-eye astronomy and pre-microscope physiology produced revolutionary results because “the technical literature... had undergone change,” not because scientists acquired new mentalities — print, not psychology, explains the timing.

A parallel passage extends the fixity argument to cartography: printed maps eventually fixed stellar and geographic positions permanently, so that “an age-old process of corruption was being decisively arrested and was eventually reversed,” even though corrupt copies were duplicated for a time first.

Her historiographical polemic is sharpest against Marxist class narratives: she insists there are “perfectly good reasons for associating printers with merchants and capitalists” but “none for detaching them from association with professors and friars — especially in the age of scholar-printers, when close collaboration was the rule.”

The unit closes on the Copernicus/Erasmus pairing: both “set out to fulfill traditional programs” — emending the Almagest, emending the Bible — “but both used means that were untraditional,” so that “they broke new paths in the very act of seeking to achieve old goals,” the line that seeds the chapter's Scripture-and-Nature theme.
