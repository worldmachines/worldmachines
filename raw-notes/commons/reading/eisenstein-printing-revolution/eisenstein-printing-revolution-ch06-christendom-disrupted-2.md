# Print, the Vulgate under Siege, and the Nationalization of Scripture

Eisenstein argues that the enthusiastic welcome given to printing as a "peaceful art" concealed its real effect on Christendom: because Christianity is a textually transmitted heritage, it was uniquely exposed to what happened when texts stopped being copied by hand. The chapter traces how print, independent of any particular reformer's actions, opened a "two-pronged attack" on the medieval Latin Vulgate — one prong scholarly (Greek and Hebrew philology undermining Jerome's authority among an educated elite), one prong popular (vernacular Bible translation reaching lay readers directly) — and follows the consequences through Trent's defensive machinery, English royal policy's contradictions, and the eventual "nationalization" of Scripture that tied the rise of vernacular literatures to the rise of religious and linguistic nationalism.

## Themes

**Print, not Protestantism, forced the confrontation.** Eisenstein's boldest causal claim is that "it was, then, printing, not Protestantism, which outmoded the medieval Vulgate and introduced a new drive to tap mass markets" — regardless of what happened in Wittenberg, Zurich, or Geneva, the church would eventually have had to reckon with copyediting, trilingual scholarship, and expanding book markets. This is an argument about print as forcing agent and precondition, not sole or sufficient cause: she is careful to frame Luther, Zwingli, and Calvin as occasions on which a print-driven reckoning arrived, not as its origin.

**A two-pronged attack, one workshop.** The elite track (grammar and philology "forcing their way into the schools," Greek and Hebrew scholarship displacing theology) and the popular track (ordinary readers "knowing their Scripture as well as most parish priests," vernacular catechisms expanding) issued from the same institution, the scholar-printer's workshop (Robert Estienne, Richard Grafton), even though the two tracks did not always converge — Luther attacked Erasmus as "more of a grammarian than a theologian," and Thomas More broke with Erasmus over lay evangelism even while agreeing with him on Greek studies.

**Catholic containment as rear-guard war.** Trent's response — withholding new Bible editions, restricting lay reading, inventing the Index and Imprimatur — is read by Eisenstein as the first in "a series of rear-guard actions" against forces print had already released, a "long war" she says had not fully ended even by Vatican II. She distinguishes this from the simpler claim that Catholics and Protestants merely "exploited" print in the same way: the real story is that new functions of print (not just dissemination) is what post-Tridentine policy was built to contain.

**Ambivalent state policy under pressure.** Henry VIII's 1543 settlement — banning unlicensed and annotated English Bibles while simultaneously ordering weekly public reading from an English Bible in every parish — shows a state working at cross purposes with itself once print had raised the stakes of vernacular access; Eisenstein notes the irony that public reading "probably worked … to increase the market for English Bibles" it was meant to restrict.

**The universalizing impulse that fragmented Christendom.** The chapter's central paradox: the drive to spread "glad tidings" to everyman, implemented through print, required nationalizing Scripture into mutually unintelligible vernaculars, so that "it is no accident that nationalism and mass literacy have developed together." Hans Kohn is quoted (and partly endorsed, partly sharpened) for the claim that vernacular Bible translation dignified emerging national languages and dethroned Latin just as it was becoming the era's cosmopolitan scholarly tongue. Medieval Latin, by contrast, kept Catholic Europe — and "Latin" America — linguistically unified in a way no single Protestant vernacular ever could; J. H. Hexter's complication is preserved rather than smoothed over: Calvinism's own claims were "as universal, quite as catholic" as the Papacy's, yet Calvin's French Bible was still foreign to the English and Scots Puritans who used the Geneva Bible.

**Anti-elite spillover beyond religion.** The same vernacularizing, anti-Latin rhetoric that powered lay Bible reading was redeployed against professional and political elites — Culpeper against the Latin-gatekept College of Physicians, Lilburne against lawbooks written in "Latin and old French." Eisenstein reads this as anticipating Enlightenment appeals to a general reading public, but with an important difference: unlike the later cosmopolitan philosophes, these translators "played insistently on chauvinistic themes," reworking a patriotic defense of the vernacular rather than a universalist one.

For World Machines: the vernacular-Bible material anticipates [[benedict-anderson]]'s print-vernacular account of nationalism by several centuries, though Eisenstein's frame is confessional (sacred vernaculars competing with a sacred Latin) rather than Anderson's secular print-capitalism, and her linkage of print to [[nationalism]] runs through religious fragmentation specifically. The "printing, not Protestantism" argument is also a clean instance of print functioning as forcing precondition rather than sufficient cause, an "agent" in Eisenstein's own vocabulary — worth reading alongside [[legibility-as-precondition-not-cause]]'s more general point that infrastructural change can make a confrontation inevitable without determining its particular trigger or victor. Note, too, that this unit's evidence cuts against a simple standardization story: the same press that produced identical Bibles within a language also multiplied incompatible sacred vernaculars across languages, fragmenting Christendom even as it fixed each fragment's text.

## Notable passages

- The opening irony (c1) states the chapter's frame: a "peaceful art" that did more to inflame religious warfare than the actual arts of war, because a textually transmitted faith was uniquely vulnerable to a revolution in how texts were made and transmitted.
- "Printing, not Protestantism" (c2) is Eisenstein's sharpest causal claim, insisting the Vulgate's fate was forced by print-driven scholarship and markets independent of any particular reformer.
- The Trent passage (c3) is her own framing statement (not a quoted opponent) that Catholic policy targeted new functions of print beyond mere dissemination, inaugurating a "long war" between Rome and the press.
- The two-pronged-attack passage (c4) lays out the chapter's organizing structure: an elite philological assault and a popular vernacular assault on the Vulgate's authority, both from the same printing workshops.
- The Kohn passage (c5) carries the "nationalization" of Scripture argument, attributing the insight to Kohn while sharpening it into her own claim about the coincidence of vernacular dignity and falling print costs.
- The mass-literacy/nationalism passage (c6) delivers the chapter's most quotable line — "nationalism and mass literacy have developed together" — and frames it as the paradoxical cost of trying to make Scripture universally accessible.
