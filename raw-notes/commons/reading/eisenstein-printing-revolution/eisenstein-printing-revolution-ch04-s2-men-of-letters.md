# The Rise of a New Class of “Men of Letters”

Eisenstein argues that the printing trade produced a distinct, understudied social class — the professional “man of letters” — running from the cosmopolitan, often pseudonymous Republic of Letters housed inside sixteenth-century printers’ workshops to the alienated, commercially dependent novelist and poet of the nineteenth century; along the way she disputes Karl Mannheim’s sociology of the intelligentsia and closes by defending, against contemporary “obituaries on the Age of Gutenberg,” her claim that print’s effects have been continuous and cumulative since the fifteenth century.

## Themes

**A neglected social class.** Eisenstein opens by noting that historians have written about “alienated intellectuals” in Tudor-Stuart England and the “desertion of intellectuals” in Bourbon France without treating men of letters as a distinctive social class produced by print itself. Robert Darnton’s work on Grub Street and the “low-life of literature” in eighteenth-century France is the exception; the fuller picture — spanning early sixteenth-century Venice, Elizabethan London, and the clandestine book trade in forbidden and smuggled texts (vernacular Bibles, Galileo’s evasions) — “remains, like those of a gigantic iceberg, submerged.” The story of the rise of a “fourth estate,” from the earliest printing-trade occupations to the journalists who governed France in 1848, “also remains to be told.”

**The Republic of Letters as a real institution behind a fictive geography.** Its inhabitants preferred Latinate pseudonyms or anonymity to their own names, and its imprints issued from deliberately placeless addresses — Utopia, Cosmopolis, Eleuthera, Philadelphia — that were partly evasions of censors and partly branding. Behind the elusive geography, though, sat concrete infrastructure: real foundries, workshops, and profits, and printing houses (Bomberg and Manutius in Venice, the Amerbachs and Oporinus in Basel, Plantin in Antwerp, the Wechels in Frankfurt) that functioned as “miniature international houses,” sheltering “infidel Jews and Arabs, schismatic Greeks, and a vast variety of dissident foreigners” alongside wandering scholars. The Republic’s working language shifted from Latin to French over the seventeenth century, and its eighteenth-century capital, by most accounts, was Amsterdam rather than Paris or Rotterdam.

**Authorship as a print-linked category, against Mannheim.** Following Febvre and Martin, Eisenstein treats “man of letters” as a neologism — the term has no meaning before print. She then takes on Karl Mannheim’s sociology of the intelligentsia directly: he describes intellectuals as cut off from “any vital and functioning segment of society,” confined to “the secluded study.” Eisenstein disagrees pointedly — sixteenth-to-eighteenth-century men of letters (Erasmus, Benjamin Franklin, and later autodidact printer-authors like Mark Twain) were frequently printers themselves, working with a composing stick in hand, so that the printing shop — not the university, court, or salon Mannheim catalogues — was their actual “vital and functioning segment of society.” The “secluded study,” she insists, is a later, retrospectively projected image.

**The professional author’s structural ambivalence.** Poets, playwrights, and novelists occupied a more indirect relation to the trade than jobbing printer-authors, caught between royal or aristocratic patronage and publishers’ stipends, between “divine art” and “mercenary métier.” This was “a highly volatile, unstable status group” with no place in traditional systems of rank, oscillating between “arbiters of taste” and sellers of a commodity. Commercial success required courting a susceptible readership — adolescent bookworms, apprentices, “a wide spectrum of feminine readers” — and increasingly depended on scandal rather than respectability. The resulting figure, alone with his quill pen, was structurally alienated: he promoted leisure for a living, and “fought for a commercial success that he despised.” This alienated-author figure is the class history underneath [[print-culture-and-the-individual-author]]’s claim that print manufactured, rather than merely discovered, the solitary suffering author; Eisenstein supplies the patronage-to-market economics that produced it.

**Diverging nineteenth-century paths.** Eisenstein traces a bifurcation: commercial fiction writers chasing a mass readership on one side, and artists retreating into secluded “ivory towers” for the sake of a posthumous place in the literary anthology on the other — a “museum culture” alternately assaulted and nostalgically defended. Print’s own preservative power compounds the problem: each new generation inherits an ever-larger “burden of the past,” an “overpopulation of Parnassus” that its own strident voices only add to. This split rhymes with [[carey-intellectuals-and-masses]]’s account of a literary intelligentsia defensively repudiating a newly mass reading public, though Carey’s frame is the later, narrower 1880–1939 crisis and diagnoses one side of the split (elite contempt for “the masses”), where Eisenstein treats the whole oscillation between ivory tower and marketplace as built into authorship’s commercialization from early print onward.

**Cumulative, uneven, still-unfinished effects.** She closes by rejecting recent “obituaries on the Age of Gutenberg”: print’s effects have been “exerted always unevenly, yet always continuously and cumulatively from the late fifteenth century on,” with no point at which they diminished, notwithstanding the “distracting effect of new electronic media.” This uneven-but-continuous, additive change resonates with the [[modernity-machine]]’s slow, accumulating Dawn-phase construction — though Eisenstein is explicit that she is describing one communications medium’s persistence, a historian’s claim, not a theory of civilizational infrastructure. She frames the whole excursion — reaching well beyond her early-modern remit — as a preliminary sketch, not a conclusion, ahead of Part II’s closer look at specific developments.

## Notable passages

The opening move (Grub Street) puts the printing trade’s social consequences on the historian’s agenda by naming what is missing: a social history of the “men of letters” themselves, with Grub Street itself still “awaiting its historian.”

The Republic of Letters passage shows Eisenstein’s method at its best: she takes a self-consciously fictive, cosmopolitan label and grounds it in “real foundries, workshops, and offices,” refusing to let the group’s own mythology substitute for institutional history.

The Mannheim exchange is a clean example of her habit of quoting a fellow scholar in order to disagree with him: she preserves his “secluded study” image intact before reversing it by pointing to printers’ workshops as the intelligentsia’s actual “vital and functioning segment of society.”

The “divine art” / “mercenary métier” passage names a status ambivalence that, in her account, predates industrialization: paper mills and steam presses intensified a tension already built into commercialized authorship rather than creating it from scratch.

The “alone with his quill pen” passage is the essay’s clearest portrait of the isolated, market-dependent author — the figure her argument has been building toward since the opening paragraphs on the neglected social class of men of letters.

The closing thesis is Eisenstein’s own methodological signature: change uneven in its distribution but continuous and cumulative over time, never fully arrested, offered explicitly as a preliminary sketch rather than a final verdict.
