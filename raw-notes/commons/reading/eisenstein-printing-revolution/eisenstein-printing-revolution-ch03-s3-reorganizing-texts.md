# Reorganizing Texts and Reference Guides: Rationalizing, Codifying, Cataloguing

Eisenstein argues that early printers’ editorial and commercial decisions — how to lay out, index, and catalogue a text — reorganized not just books but the thinking of the readers who used them; she traces the claim through the historical rarity of full alphabetical order before print, printers’ competitive drive to sell indexing and cataloguing as features, the paradox that “restoring” an ancient legal text to full internal coherence could strip it of living authority, and closes by arguing that print’s most consequential innovations — pagination, running heads, title pages — were also its least conspicuous.

## Themes

**Print did not invent the impulse to rationalize, but only print could scale it.** [[marshall-mcluhan]]’s claim that scanning printed lines reshaped thought is, for Eisenstein, “at first glance somewhat mystifying,” but she makes it plausible by showing concretely how format guided cognition: full alphabetical order was rare and idiosyncratic before print (a thirteenth-century Genoese encyclopedist felt he had to explain and take personal credit for ordering “amo” before “bibo”; the library at York was catalogued in verse, prompting the observation “a poem is a far cry from a card index”). She rejects the common explanation that cheaper paper alone caused the shift to alphabetization: paper lowered costs, but a hand-copied index still had to be recopied from scratch each time, so no scribal system could make a standard ordering rule stick across copies. Only print’s capacity for identical, competitively marketed duplication — Peter Schoeffer’s boast of “more complete and better arranged” indexes, Robert Estienne’s trilingual catalogues praised as “a miracle of lucid arrangement” — made systematic cataloguing not just possible but commercially necessary.

**She concedes medieval precedent while insisting it could not “really launch” without print.** Concordances, marginal glosses, diagrams linking bodily organs to heavenly bodies, and parchment reference-tabs all predate the press; she even endorses C. S. Lewis’s claim that medieval people were already organizers and codifiers who “would most have admired the card index.” But she immediately warns against overreading such anticipations: “their sense of order was not based upon its use,” and “a unique bilingual lexicon cannot do the same work as hundreds of trilingual reference guides.” This resonates, partially, with [[legibility-as-precondition-not-cause]]: a rationalizing impulse and even some of its techniques pre-exist print as latent cultural material, but print is what converts scattered habit into standing infrastructure — though her frame is a technology unlocking a pre-existing urge rather than exactly the precondition/cause distinction that page draws, so the fit is a rhyme, not an equation.

**Commercial and intellectual motives fused into what she calls “an especially powerful, almost irresistible drive” toward order** — the “early capitalist” and the “medieval schoolman” wanting, in C. S. Lewis’s phrase she quotes approvingly, “a place for everything and everything in the right place.”

**Ramist “method” became a marketable print format, not just a pedagogical doctrine.** Peter Ramus’s dictum that any subject could be broken down topically and expounded by analysis was “enthusiastically adopted by publishers and editors” because it suited the new profitability of textbook writing; the word “methodus” itself, once dismissed as barbarous by early humanists, proliferated in treatise titles a full century before Descartes.

**Rationalizing an old code for coherence could drain its living authority.** The unit’s sharpest passage is the Corpus Juris case: manuscript access to the Digest was so restricted (Budé reportedly viewed the guarded Florentine Codex “only through a grate”) that the text survived a century of print in fragmented, glossed disarray. Its 1553 publication let Jacques Cujas finally correct and fully index it — but the more “stylistically coherent and internally consistent” the restored text became, the less relevant it grew to living jurisprudence, exactly “as was the case with Ciceronian Latin, when complete restoration had been successfully applied to the letter of the ancient code, its living spirit vanished for good.” Fixity and rationalization are not unambiguous goods in her account here — perfecting a text’s internal order can be what finally embalms it.

**The same rationalizing format reorganized public and legal life, not only scholarship.** Competing London law printers (Pynson, Redman, Rastell) raced to issue better-indexed, alphabetically tabulated abridgments of the statutes; Rastell’s “Tabula” for the Great Boke of Statutes gave many readers their first systematic view of parliamentary history at all — evidence, in her words, of “how a rationalized book format might affect vital organs of the body politic.”

**The most consequential changes were the least conspicuous.** She closes by naming pagination, punctuation, section breaks, running heads, and above all the title page as innovations that “helped to reorder the thought of all readers, whatever their profession or craft” — mundane infrastructure, in [[modernity-machine]] terms, for a broader shift toward [[Legibility]]: making textual knowledge searchable, citable, and comparable at a scale scribal culture never sustained.

## Notable passages

The alphabetization case (the Genoese “amo before bibo” anecdote) is the unit’s clearest evidence that ordering-by-letter was still a novel, self-conscious invention on the eve of print rather than an ancient default. The Ramist passage shows organization becoming valued “almost an end in itself” and merchandised as such by publishers. The Corpus Juris passage is the unit’s most consequential curated quote: it is where Eisenstein’s usual case for fixity and rationalization runs into its own cost, a paradox she states and lets stand rather than resolves. The Rastell/statutes passage extends the argument from scholarship into “the body politic,” and the closing passage restates the unit’s thesis in its most McLuhan-adjacent form, tying directly back to the opening claim that book format reorganizes thought.
