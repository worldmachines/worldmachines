# The Preservative Powers of Print: Fixity and Cumulative Change

Eisenstein argues that of all the effects of printing, preservation is the most consequential: scribal transmission could never reliably preserve a text against copyist corruption, decay, and loss, but print achieves preservation not through a tougher writing material (paper is in fact less durable than parchment) but through sheer multiplied quantity, and this typographical fixity is what first makes cumulative, permanently-recorded change possible across language, law, authorship, and the very idea of progress.

## Themes

**Print inverts the durability calculus that governed scribal preservation.** Before printing, a manuscript was "fairly impermanent unless it was stored away and not used," vulnerable to "moisture and vermin, theft and fire," and copying itself produced textual drift; the old rule held that "Much is preserved when little is written; little is preserved when much is written." Print breaks this rule while using a *worse* substrate: paper, not parchment. Quantity, not quality of material, becomes the preservative mechanism — enough dispersed copies survive even though any single one may perish.

**Fixity converts a transitory classical revival into a permanent one.** Once Greek type fonts had been cut, "neither the disruption of civil order in Italy, the conquest of Greek lands by Islam, nor even the translation into Latin of all major Greek texts saw knowledge of Greek wither again in the West" — unlike earlier medieval revivals that had lapsed. This is Eisenstein's "permanent Renaissance" claim: recovery becomes irreversible once it is typographically fixed, feeding the modern "knowledge industry" and its ever-expanding bibliographies.

**Fixed vernaculars become the linguistic scaffolding of nationalism.** Which sixteenth-century vernaculars got primers, catechisms, and Bibles printed determined which provincial dialects sedimented into "national" literary languages and which disappeared into a "spoken provincial dialect." Print homogenized what fell within a language's "fortified walls" while sharpening the boundary with neighboring vernaculars, and a print-standardized "mother's tongue" learned in childhood entangled linguistic roots with national rootedness. This anticipates [[benedict-anderson]]'s print-capitalism account of imagined national communities and resonates with [[nationalism]] as a side effect of a printing technology rather than a purely political program — though here the vector is a printer's commercial choice of which text to typeset, not the newspaper-and-novel simultaneity Anderson emphasizes. It is also a one-way, printer-and-crown-driven standardization rather than the actively contested, resisted uniformity [[contested-uniformity-bayly]] traces for the nineteenth century.

**Fixity hardens law, doctrine, and precedent into something harder to reverse.** Once statutes, charters, breviaries, and edicts existed as identical, checkable printed copies — Eisenstein's example is the 1533 "Tabula" showing exactly how often Magna Carta had been reconfirmed — custom becomes auditable in a way "immemorial custom" transmitted orally or in manuscript never was, and religious and legal disputes accordingly become "more permanent." That auditability is a narrow, local instance of what [[Legibility]] and [[legibility-as-machine-core]] name at civilizational scale, though Eisenstein's claim here is about specific texts (statutes, breviaries, charters) becoming fixed and checkable, not about state-scale measurement or control — a distinction [[legibility-as-precondition-not-cause]] already insists on, since Eisenstein herself calls print an "agent," never a sole cause.

**Fixity invents the modern author, and with it plagiarism and copyright.** Medieval writing recognized only scribe, compiler, commentator, and author (Bonaventura's fourfold scheme), with no category for wholly original composition; attaching a permanent name to a permanent text is what makes priority disputes (Gutenberg versus Coster), patents, and "possessive individualism" over a literary "common" possible — "the terms plagiarism and copyright did not exist for the minstrel." This is squarely the territory of [[print-culture-and-the-individual-author]].

**Print-made permanence also reshapes the private self.** Cheaper paper let individuals record private lives and correspondence, but Eisenstein is careful to credit presses rather than paper mills with making it "possible to preserve personal ephemera intact," and she links the ancient "drive for fame" to a specifically "print-made immortality." This touches the interiority-infrastructure theme of [[infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness]], though her emphasis is on durable, public, name-attached traceability of a life rather than inward subjective depth as such.

**Cumulative, permanent record-keeping is the precondition for an idea of progress.** Before printing, "exact determination of every previous step" (Sarton) was simply impossible, so no technique could be "securely established, permanently recorded, and stored for subsequent retrieval." Eisenstein's thesis sentence is explicit: "Permanence introduced a new form of progressive change. The preservation of the old, in brief, was a prerequisite for a tradition of the new." This is the unit's clearest statement of print as a machine-construction dynamic — fixity is the mechanism by which [[modernity-machine]]'s legibility apparatus accumulates rather than merely recurring episodically.

**Wisdom-veneration outlives the conditions that produced it.** Even after fixity made exact retrieval and dating of old texts possible — and so should have dissolved retrieval-narratives about lost ancient wisdom — Rosicrucians and Freemasons went on claiming that Descartes and [[isaac-newton]] had merely rediscovered a "magical key to nature's secrets" once known to ancient pyramid-builders. Eisenstein reads this as a residue of scribal epistemology surviving well into the age of print, not a counterexample to fixity's effects.

## Notable passages

The chapter opens by cataloguing exactly how scribal transmission failed to preserve texts — corruption by copyists, "moisture and vermin, theft and fire" — before naming preservation as print's master feature (c1). It then states the scribal-era durability rule and shows print reversing it through quantity rather than material quality (c2). Jefferson's letter to George Wythe, quoted at length, is Eisenstein's prime piece of evidence that preservation-through-publicity (multiplying copies) succeeded where preservation-through-lock-and-key (vaults) had failed, and that this democratizing logic was "central both to early modern science and to Enlightenment thought" (c3). Steinberg's paraphrase, which Eisenstein endorses, makes the unit's most concrete causal claim: print fixed which sixteenth-century vernaculars survived into national literary languages and which did not (c4). The paragraph on individual innovation traces fixity's role in producing modern authorship, patents, and priority disputes — "It is no accident, I think, that printing is the first 'invention' which became entangled in a priority struggle" (c5). Finally, the passage on Sarton and "exact determination" is the unit's thesis statement connecting fixity directly to the possibility of cumulative progress (c6).
