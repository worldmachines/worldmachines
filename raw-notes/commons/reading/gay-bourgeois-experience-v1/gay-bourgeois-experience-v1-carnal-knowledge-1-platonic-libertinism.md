# Platonic Libertinism: The Bourgeoisie's Unofficial Sex Education

Opening Chapter 5 of *Education of the Senses*, Gay argues that respectable nineteenth-century children and adults absorbed real, if fragmentary and often mistaken, sexual knowledge entirely outside any sanctioned curriculum — from overheard parental intimacy, siblings' and playmates' bodies, servants and obscene schoolboy jokes, court reporting and journalism, popular fiction, and above all the spectacle of city life. The official story of protected bourgeois innocence, he shows, coexisted everywhere with a dense informal traffic in erotic information that parents could neither suppress nor safely ignore. Amiel's coinage “Platonic libertinism”, describing his own voyeuristic thrill on a visit to Paris, gives the section its title and its organizing image: erotic knowledge acquired at a distance, by eye and ear, without physical contact.

## Themes

**Erotic knowledge is acquired, not innate — and it is acquired informally.** Gay opens with Freud's 1916 dictum that “love is the great educator” to frame sexual desire as biologically rooted but socially shaped: fantasy elaborates or distorts “provocative scenes one has witnessed, noises one has heard, stories one has read,” and this schooling runs through Flaubert eavesdropping on his parents, the “Wolf Man’s” primal scene, and ordinary children startled by a naked mother or a sibling's genitals. “Carnal knowledge,” as Gay puts it, “begins with intimations of the body” rather than instruction.

**Concealment fails, and children draw their own — often accurate — inferences.** The stork story, and Freud's patient “Little Hans” working out from his mother's groans and the doctor's bag that “the stork is coming today,” then confronting basins of “bloody water” after his sister's birth, shows the gap between what parents supplied as an official explanation and what children actually reconstructed. This is Gay's recurring point about bourgeois “learned ignorance”: the pretense of not-knowing was a defense maintained past the point of its own plausibility, on both sides.

**Print culture negotiates, rather than simply enforces, the boundary of the sayable.** Lizzie Eustace hiding French novels behind a Bible for Lady Fawn's benefit, and Mr. Brooke recommending the “a little broad” Smollett to newly married Dorothea in *Middlemarch*, dramatize a graduated, class- and status-coded permissiveness rather than blanket prohibition. The 1883–84 priority dispute between Goncourt and Zola over which novelist first depicted menarche shows the frontier of fictional decency being actively contested and inched forward, not fixed.

**Cities are the paramount school of the senses, and Amiel supplies the century's name for it.** Prostitutes patrolling railway stations and dance halls, Berlin bordello windows performing “half-exposed” morning toilette for passing schoolboys, and Amiel's own confession of being “seduced by the curiosity of the senses” in Paris all exemplify what Gay, following Amiel, calls “Platonic libertinism”: erotic curiosity satisfied by looking rather than touching. This is a vivid instance of Gay's broader “doctrine of distance” — desire indulged precisely by being held at arm's length, converting looking into a licensed, quasi-respectable substitute for touching.

**National self-flattery displaces domestic anxiety about sensuality onto other countries.** Taine praises English girls' frankness against French “doll”-like ignorance; the German family magazine *Daheim* reverses the charge, casting Parisian and American mores as scandalously precocious. Comparative-morality journalism functions as a socially acceptable outlet for the same prurient curiosity it claims to condemn.

**Ambivalence, not simple repression, governs the whole system.** Gay's closing formulation — the respectable “wanted to know and did not want to know” — states directly the ambivalence he treats elsewhere as constitutive of bourgeois character. This whole unit reads as a rebuttal-in-advance of a purely repressive account of Victorian sexuality: what it documents is not silence but a proliferating, informally circulated discourse about sex running beneath and around official reticence, which resonates with [[michel-foucault]]'s argument (in the *History of Sexuality*) that the nineteenth century multiplied discourses on sex rather than merely repressing them — though Gay's frame is personal-psychological (defenses, ambivalence, private diaries) where Foucault's is institutional and discursive (medicine, pedagogy, the state).

## Notable passages

The opening paragraph (**c1**) sets the book's method: carnal knowledge is pieced together from witnessed scenes and misinformation, not taught, and “error is as effective a spur to sexual stimulation as truth.” The Sigrid Undset vignette (**c2**) — a small boy urinating like a “water-cart” and declaring “no girl could do a thing like that” — is Gay's chosen instance of how ordinary childhood encounters, not scandal or trauma, transmitted the “charged meaning” of sexual difference. The stork/Little Hans passage (**c3**) is the clearest statement of learned ignorance's failure: children reason past the official cover story using direct sensory evidence. The Amiel passage (**c4**) supplies the section's controlling concept and its own titular phrase, “Platonic libertinism of the eyes and ears.” The Trollope/Eliot passage (**c5**) shows respectable fiction's own graduated, hypocrisy-adjacent rules for what could be read and by whom. The closing Goncourt line (**c6**), “the world will come to an end the day that young girls stop laughing at scatological jokes,” crystallizes the chapter's thesis that officially prudish bourgeois culture ran on an ambivalent, half-willing tolerance of exactly the knowledge it claimed to forbid.
