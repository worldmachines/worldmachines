# The Castrating Sisterhood: Fatal Women, Castration Fear, and the Anatomy of Anti-Feminism

Gay reconstructs a nineteenth-century wave of castration imagery and "fatal woman" iconography — running from a children's cautionary picture-book through Edmond de Goncourt's vagina-dentata nightmare to the femmes fatales of Rossetti, Stuck, Munch, and Beardsley — and argues that both this artistic flood and the era's organized political anti-feminism were overdetermined responses to women's emancipation, rarely reducible to simple misogyny or naked castration terror.

## Themes

**Castration was a live cultural idiom, not just a private neurosis.** Gay opens by showing the metaphor circulating everywhere — Amiel's "equivalent of a castration" for depressive spleen, a Swedish critic's "black death of castrating morality" — before turning to Heinrich Hoffmann's 1845 *Struwwelpeter*, whose cheerful tales of mutilated children (Konrad's thumbs cut off "at the root") show the theme reaching into the nursery. Gay's point is methodological: because the material seemed lighthearted and readers didn't recoil from it, that doesn't mean the underlying fear wasn't real and terrifying at "some deep level."

**The vagina dentata and Gay's psychoanalytic method.** Edmond de Goncourt's 1883 Bastille Day dream of a dancer whose genitals are "armed with the most terrible jaws" is read as a textbook manifest/latent split: Goncourt's own denials (the teeth only reminded him of his own bad teeth) cannot conceal an oedipal wish — Bastille Day as the day the son symbolically slays paternal authority — compromised by fear of maternal retaliation, so that "the triumphant boy would have encountered the biting mother" instead of the father. Gay backs this with the Goncourt brothers' earlier dream record (1855-56) and situates the fear of woman as a near-universal mythic pattern (Medusa, vagina dentata folklore across cultures), rooted in infantile dependence on the mother and the boy's dread that female genitals model his own possible castration.

**A mid-century turn from the fatal man to the fatal woman.** Byronic demonic men dominate the romantic imagination through mid-century; after roughly 1850 they are eclipsed by a "flood" of murderous women — Salome, Judith, Delilah, Cleopatra, the vampire, the Sphinx — recycled from religious and classical sources by Rossetti, Burne-Jones, Pater (on the Mona Lisa's vampiric antiquity), Vigny, Liebermann's *Simson und Delila*, Stuck, Munch, and Beardsley. The Oedipus/Sphinx motif tracks the same shift in miniature: Ingres's 1827 Oedipus is a conquering hero, but Heine's 1839 poem and Moreau's paintings recast him as the Sphinx's passive victim — a figure Gautier calls "a little Hamlet," acted upon rather than acting.

**Method: great artists as sharper witnesses, not just private pathologies.** Gay explicitly rejects the move — which he calls attractive to "most historians of our day" — of dismissing Munch's or Moreau's disturbed personal histories as merely private testimony irrelevant to culture at large. His counter-claim is a general historiographical stance: major artists, however personally isolated or ill, "often see more sharply, feel more precisely, and reveal more accurately contemporary currents, including their unconscious dimensions" than lesser, more representative contemporaries.

**Anti-feminism as overdetermined, rarely simple misogyny.** Gay pivots from art to the suffrage debate and insists the "Antis" were not typically woman-haters (citing Brian Harrison's research); their sober "separate spheres" argument was religiously and scientifically respectable on its own terms, even if latent anxiety can be suspected beneath it. He locates a specific, non-castration fear in "clubland" — men's colleges, clubs, professions — where the real threat was not being castrated but being forced to grow up and abandon largely unconscious, homoerotic adolescent bonds. Female anti-feminism complicates the picture further: Anna P. See's farce *When Women Vote*, the 1889 "Appeal Against Female Suffrage" (signed by Mrs. Humphry Ward and others), and Beatrice Webb's later confession that her own lack of personal disability left her indifferent to feminism, all read through Anna Freud's "identification with the aggressor." Eliza Lynn Linton — an agnostic, independent, financially self-made woman who nonetheless attacked "The Girl of the Period" (1868) with career-making venom while also cataloguing women's real legal wrongs — is Gay's showpiece for how one person could hold genuinely progressive views on women and still trade in anti-feminist caricature.

**The unifying claim.** Gay's close ties the two halves together: the Sphinxes of Stuck, the viragos of Punch, the Delilahs of Gautier, and Linton's Girl of the Period occupy different aesthetic and political registers but "inhabited one psychological world" — a bourgeois culture managing the same anxiety over women's changing status through art, journalism, and political argument alike, none of it reducible to a single cause. This is Gay's characteristic move against reductionism: the class's official, reasoned ideology (separate spheres as a natural, scientific division of labor) and its private, often unconscious anxiety (castration, the devouring mother) run on parallel tracks rather than one simply masking the other — a version of the gap between official doctrine and private experience that runs through the whole book. It also reads as a genuine, if partial, counter-example to Foucault's [[michel-foucault]] "repressive hypothesis": nothing here is silent — the century is instead extravagantly productive of anxious discourse and image around female sexuality and power, even while it enforces a public "separate spheres" doctrine. The episode also has the shape of a [[gramsci-gap]] in miniature: as the old certainty of natural gender hierarchy loses its grip under feminist pressure, the interregnum throws off exactly the "morbid symptoms" — lurid art, panicked dreams, overwrought editorials — that a dying-but-not-dead order produces before any new settlement is reached; the fit is partial since Gay's frame is one class's internal gender order rather than a civilizational machine transition.

## Notable passages

- The **Struwwelpeter** passage shows castration and mutilation as an idiom even children's culture could carry cheerfully, which Gay treats as evidence the anxiety ran deep rather than being confined to adult neurosis.
- **Goncourt's dream** is the unit's primary document: a first-person vagina-dentata nightmare recorded on the anniversary of Bastille Day, which supplies the chapter's raw material.
- Gay's own **interpretation of the dream** — the oedipal wish against paternal authority compromised by fear of maternal retaliation — is the clearest statement in the unit of his interpretive method applied to a private document.
- The paragraph on the **turn from fatal man to fatal woman** supplies the chapter's periodizing thesis and its inventory of painters and poets, the backbone of the claim that a "flood" of femme-fatale imagery arrived after 1850.
- The **clubland** passage is Gay's sharpest qualification: some apparent fear of women was really reluctance to leave homoerotic adolescent male bonds, a fear of growing up rather than of castration.
- The closing line — that Stuck's Sphinxes, Punch's viragos, Gautier's Delilahs, and Linton's Girl of the Period "occupied very different aesthetic universes, but they inhabited one psychological world" — is Gay's thesis statement, tying the art-historical and political halves of the unit together.
