# Fortifications for the Self: Hypocrisy as a Search for Safety

Opening Chapter 6, Gay pivots from the public, social forces shaping bourgeois sensuality back to the individual — to hypocrisy, the family, and the diary as the three private mechanisms the nineteenth-century middle classes used to build "fortifications for the self" higher than any other class in history. Section 1, "A Search for Safety," then asks what the age's notorious reputation for hypocrisy actually means: Gay surveys the charge's long pre-Victorian pedigree, catalogs the century's own obsessive hypocrite-hunting (in life, literature, and criticism), traces how sexuality in particular was managed through euphemism, editorial bowdlerization, and posthumous archival cleansing, and finally uses Freud to argue that "hypocrisy" is too blunt a diagnostic term — what looks like duplicity is often an unconscious, structurally necessary compromise between instinctual drives and an internalized parental voice.

## Themes

**Hypocrisy is older than the bourgeoisie, but the nineteenth century perfected it.** Gay traces cant and reticence back through Sir Thomas Herbert (1634), Milton, La Rochefoucauld's definition of hypocrisy as "the homage that vice pays to virtue," Molière's Tartuffe, Sterne, and Thomas Morton's 1798 invention of Mrs. Grundy. The bourgeois century's distinction is not novelty but concentration: "they combined to make the most concentrated culture of Tartuffes the world had ever known."

**A dense company of hypocrite-hunters made "hypocrisy" the century's own self-diagnosis.** Froude, Flaubert, Heine, Ibsen, Baudelaire, Whitman, Ruskin, Nietzsche, Marx, Veblen, Shaw, and Freud himself all turned denunciation of cant into a genre, targeting figures like Anthony Comstock. Gay treats the list itself as evidence of how thoroughly the accusation saturated bourgeois self-understanding, while noting the era also produced calculated defenders — the *Saturday Review* accused the hypocrite-hunters of a hypocrisy of their own, a "prurient curiosity about wickedness" dressed as moral exposure.

**Sexuality was hypocrisy's most exposed nerve.** Thackeray's self-censoring preface to *Pendennis*, Charlotte Brontë's complaint that he kept "the mermaid's tail below water," nervous publishers softening Thomas Hardy (Leslie Stephen, Mowbray Morris, H. M. Alden), and translators of *Anna Karenina* progressively de-euphemizing "Ya beremenna!" from evasion (1886) to "I'm with child" (Garnett, 1901) to "I am pregnant" (Maude, 1918) show reticence operating as a craft-level editorial and translational practice, not just private shame.

**Expurgation extended past death into the archive itself.** Biographers and heirs treated the historical record as a shrine rather than evidence: Ednah Cheney's approving account of Louisa May Alcott destroying her own journals, Georgina Hogarth and Mamie Dickens cutting Dickens's letters "remorselessly," and — most absurdly — Alexander Carlyle bowdlerizing his uncle Thomas Carlyle's private travel journal, changing "wh-re" to "female" and the crowd wanting to "smell" the Prince of Prussia to wanting to "see" him.

**Freud supplies Gay's diagnostic vocabulary, and also its limit.** Freud's claim (1898, 1915) that civilized morality forces everyone into a structural, often unconscious "cultural hypocrisy" lets Gay stop moralizing about Victorian duplicity and start historicizing it. But Gay presses the problem further: if hypocrisy is both universal to civilization and potentially unconscious, the term loses the moral charge that made it useful as an accusation — "the unconscious hypocrite is simply man in civilization" — and the historian's real task becomes measuring its degree, forms, and defensive function rather than its bare existence.

**The chapter's real argument is architectural: hypocrisy as fortification.** Gay's closing move (via Walter Bagehot's line that "the real tyranny is the tyranny of your next-door neighbor") reframes the dread of public opinion as *transference* — an internalized parental voice, first heard as literal scolding in childhood, now projected outward onto society at large. The "search for safety" the section names is not evasion of the world but retreat to where the anxiety originated: the family, and from there the sheltered private sphere (diary, home) the rest of the chapter goes on to explore.

## Notable passages

The opening thesis paragraph names the chapter's controlling image directly: no class was ever "more strenuously, more anxiously devoted to the appearances, to the family and to privacy" or built "fortifications for the self quite so high" — the phrase the chapter title comes from, tied explicitly to the coming turn toward Mabel Todd's diary.

The Santayana passage on the "Genteel Tradition" frames hypocrisy as itself "an educator of the senses," and credits Whitman, Henry James, and William James with having broken the genteel tradition's "illegitimate monopoly" over what could be hoped for — Gay's own frame of an education/miseducation of the senses recurring in a different register.

Thackeray's *Pendennis* preface — "no writer of fiction among us has been permitted to depict to his utmost power a man" — is Gay's clearest first-person evidence of self-censorship as lived professional constraint, not just external prudery imposed on writers.

The two Freud paragraphs (1898's "we are, all of us... hypocrites nowadays" through the 1915 claim that a person forced to live by precepts foreign to his drives "may objectively be called a hypocrite... whether he is clearly conscious of this incongruity or not," and Gay's own follow-up complicating what a term can mean once it covers unconscious civilization-wide compromise) form the chapter's theoretical spine — the point where Gay converts a moral accusation into a historical-psychological problem.

The closing paragraph — the internalized "voice of the Other," first heard in childhood scolding and later "given back to the world magnified and distorted" — delivers the section's payoff: hypocrisy and reticence as a defensive retreat to safety, staged "precisely where insecurity had begun: at home."

This account of a class's private psychological fortifications against public exposure resonates loosely with how [[modernity-machine]] frames legibility-management as infrastructure built to contain disorder — though here the machinery is domestic and psychoanalytic (family, superego, transference) rather than institutional or technological. Gay's insistence that "hypocrisy" be historicized rather than moralized also runs in a parallel lane to [[michel-foucault]]'s critique of the repressive hypothesis: both refuse the simple claim that Victorians merely repressed sex, but Gay's route stays psychoanalytic — internalized defenses and unconscious compromise — where Foucault's is discursive and power-analytic.
