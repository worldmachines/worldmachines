# Sex in Mind and Body: Medicine against Women's Higher Education

Gay follows the decades-long transatlantic and eventually German controversy over whether university education is compatible with womanhood, from Edward Clarke's 1873 *Sex in Education* and Henry Maudsley's 1874 tract — both holding that a fixed store of "vital force," if diverted to the female brain by strenuous study, would wreck the reproductive organs and threaten "race suicide" — through the feminist counter-literature that answered him fact for fact (the Comforts, Julia Ward Howe's anthology, Caroline Dall, Mary Putnam Jacobi), and on to Arthur Kirchhoff's 1897 survey of 120 German professors, closing with Max Planck's genteel restatement of the same doctrine: that nature has "prescribed" woman's vocation as wife and mother.

## Themes

- **A social fear translated into physiology.** Clarke and Maudsley do not argue that women are inferior; they argue, with the era's favorite idiom of economic accounting, that a finite quantity of "vital and constructive force" must be spent either on the brain or on "the ovaries and their accessories,” never both. This lets a fear of female competition present itself as a disinterested medical finding — Gay's recurring point that bourgeois anxiety is managed by dressing itself in the authority of science.
- **Menstruation as the chosen battleground.** Clarke's case history of the pseudonymous "Miss D-----," a Vassar student whose menstrual troubles he blames on her studies, becomes the era's paradigm anecdote; the debate keeps returning to "the catamenial function" because it is the one physiological fact that can be made to stand for woman's whole difference from man.
- **Feminist rebuttal as counter-science, not mere protest — with its own ambivalence.** The Comforts, Howe's contributors, Caroline Dall, and above all Mary Putnam Jacobi meet Clarke on his own empirical ground and get the facts right, but Gay notes they too show "a measure of denial," understating how disagreeable the period could actually be in their eagerness to prove women's fitness — an ambivalence on both sides of the argument rather than simple villains and heroes.
- **The same anxiety persists across decades and borders, in ever more genteel form.** By the 1890s the crude "race suicide" rhetoric of Clarke has given way to Arthur Kirchhoff's ostensibly neutral survey of German professors and to Max Planck's mild insistence that nature has "prescribed" woman's domestic vocation; the register softens but, per Gay's closing sentence, "the fear of woman and the fear of change met and merged" underneath it throughout.

The whole chapter reads as an unintended demonstration of the point [[michel-foucault]] makes about the nineteenth century's *scientia sexualis*: rather than silencing discussion of sex and the female body, the bourgeois establishment multiplied official, expert discourse about it — Kirchhoff's solicitation of 120 professorial opinions on women's fitness for study is exactly this apparatus in miniature. The resonance is only partial, though: Gay locates the drive in individual psychic defense (anxiety, ambivalence, "learned ignorance") rather than in Foucault's diffuse institutional power/knowledge, so the two accounts complement rather than duplicate each other.

## Notable passages

- Maudsley's 1874 declaration that "There is sex in mind as distinctly as there is sex in body" is the axiom the whole chapter interrogates — once granted, it licenses every subsequent argument that women's minds require different, lesser treatment.
- In the Miss D----- case, Clarke's "economic, quantitative analysis" of vital force diverted from "the ovaries and their accessories" to the brain is the unit's clearest instance of pseudo-science laundering a social fear as physiological fact.
- Carey Thomas's 1908 recollection of "the clanking chains of that gloomy little specter" shows the controversy's real psychological weight for the women it targeted, thirty-five years after publication.
- Mary Putnam Jacobi's rebuttal is vindicated by the facts, yet Gay uses it to make his own point that the feminist literature had its own "measure of denial" about menstrual discomfort — the chapter's most explicit statement of anxiety cutting both ways.
- Otto von Gierke's answer to Kirchhoff, ending in the cry that we must "take good care above all... that our men remain men," is the moment Gay flags as the debate's anxiety "finally break[ing] through" its rhetorical camouflage.
- Max Planck's contribution, and Gay's own closing sentence that "the fear of woman and the fear of change met and merged," supplies the chapter's explicit thesis statement.
