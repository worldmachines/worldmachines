# An Age of Factitious Innocence

Opening the chapter “Learned Ignorance,” Gay argues that nineteenth-century bourgeois men and women married in a state he names “factitious innocence”—not blank ignorance but an ordinary psychosexual amnesia (oedipal repression, the latency moratorium, a second, less thorough forgetting after puberty) that bourgeois culture then compounded with its own reticence about sex. He documents the costs through a run of diaries, autobiographies, and medical case histories—Emily Lytton, Annie Besant, Effie Gray, George Sand, Natalie Barney, Sigmund Freud's patients—drawn from Britain, France, Germany, and the United States, and then complicates the received melodrama of the innocent bride and the brutal groom: much of that story, he argues, is wishful fantasy, since men were frequently just as unprepared, and just as likely to suffer wedding-night fiasco, as their wives.

## Themes

**Factitious innocence as a psychoanalytic artifact.** Gay grounds bourgeois sexual ignorance in ordinary development, not merely social conditioning: the child's amnesia after the oedipal crisis, the “long sexual moratorium of the latency years,” and a second, incomplete forgetting after puberty leave even attentive brides and grooms without conscious access to what they once “correctly observed” or “anxiously invented” about sex. “Innocence once forfeited can never be reclaimed,” he writes, but nineteenth-century respectability produced its own simulacrum of the real thing.

**“Learned ignorance” as an unconscious cultural defense.** Gay is precise about mechanism: this was “not a deliberate policy” but “an unplanned, if highly adaptive defense,” “unconsciously desired, informally imparted, and assiduously fostered” by physicians, clergy, and teachers who “were wholly implicated in, and representative of, their culture” rather than correctives to it. His formula—“the bourgeois century repressed better than it knew”—treats the ignorance itself as a symptom the culture could not see past, distinct from Foucault's “repressive hypothesis” [[michel-foucault]]: where Foucault reads Victorian reticence as a productive discourse that multiplies talk about sex even while forbidding it, Gay locates the same reticence in real repression and real informational deficit—silence as lived amnesia and structural neglect, not (only) as discursive strategy. The resonance is genuine but partial; Gay would likely insist his account is more psychologically literal than Foucault's.

**Testimony of the ignorant.** First-person cases carry the indictment: Emily Lytton (later Lutyens) blaming “the conventional protection” for nearly costing her to a fifty-three-year-old seducer, and calling the whole edifice a “fictitious barrier” and “hollow pretence” in words that anticipate Gay's own thesis; Annie Besant recalling she married “with no more idea of the marriage relation than if I had been four years old”; Effie Gray's six-years-unconsummated marriage to Ruskin; George Sand's letter warning her half-brother against brides “raised as saints” and “handed over like fillies.”

**Revising the “brute in the bedroom.”** Gay names the standard melodrama—the terrified virgin raped by an exigent husband—and grants it real cases (Wood-Allen's vaginismus patient, Sicard de Plauzoles's language of marital rape) before undercutting it: “there is as much wishful fantasy in these vignettes as gross reality.” Evidence of habitually patient bridegrooms, of Hammond's temporary-impotence cases, of Millais's and Kingsley's anxious wedding-day letters, and of a Freud patient's husband who faked the bloodied sheet with red ink, all show men “often burdened with misinformation” and just as capable of paralysis as their brides. “Marriage was a trial for men as it was for women, in more senses than one.”

**A transnational bourgeois pattern.** The chapter insists the pattern was not English alone: French convent girls kept “in the dark about physiology,” Wedekind's German adolescents still told the stork brought babies, and M. J. Exner's 1914 survey finding 91.5% of American college men got their “first permanent impressions about sex from unwholesome sources” recur to the same structure—official reticence, unreliable informal transmission, and physicians who reinforced rather than corrected it (the Oxford doctor who assured undergraduates that “nine out of ten women are indifferent to or actively dislike” sex, and “the tenth… will always be a harlot”).

## Notable passages

The chapter's conceptual core is stated twice: first as the developmental mechanism (amnesia after latency and puberty leaving the “inexperienced groom and bride” without access to “what they had correctly observed and what they had anxiously invented,” culminating in “factitious innocence”), then as the cultural diagnosis (“learned ignorance” as unplanned, unconscious, “assiduously fostered” defense). Emily Lytton's own letter is the sharpest lay statement of the thesis, condemning “a fictitious barrier that has rarely any existence” in language that could serve as Gay's epigraph. George Sand's sermon to her half-brother supplies the sharpest image of the class mechanism from the other side of the Channel: daughters “brought up…as saints” and “handed over like fillies.” The chapter's pivot away from the standard narrative turns on the paragraph beginning “I have no doubt that such traumatic domestic dramas occurred… But there is as much wishful fantasy in these vignettes as gross reality”—and lands on the almost comic case of Freud's patient whose husband, having failed to consummate the marriage all night, poured red ink on the sheet rather than face the hotel maid.
