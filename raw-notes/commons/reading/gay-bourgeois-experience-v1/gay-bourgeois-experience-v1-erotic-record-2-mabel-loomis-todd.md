# Mabel and David Todd: Erotic Candor and the Limits of Angel or Whore

This unit is the opening case study of Gay's chapter on Mabel Loomis Todd: using her private journal and the couple's letters, he shows that marriage did not tame the Todds' sexuality but intensified it, that Mabel Todd devised (and then unwittingly disproved) her own folk theory of contraception, that she met her pregnancy with candid ambivalence rather than instant maternal rapture, and that her husband's rhapsodic letter on hearing she might be pregnant fused frank sexual admiration with the sanctification of motherhood — evidence, for Gay, that the received picture of the bifurcated bourgeois male (wife as angel, mistress as whore) does not hold for this couple. The section ends on a narrative hinge — the Todds' move from Washington to Amherst — that signals this erotic idyll is about to be tested.

## Themes

**Marriage as intensifier, not sedative, of desire.** Gay opens by inverting the stereotype directly: the Todds' "appetite grew with its regular and lawful satisfaction," glossed via Shaw's line that marriage is "the most licentious of human institutions." Mabel Todd's own diary entries (rapturous descriptions of nightly ardor, her husband as more "passionately my lover" after marriage than before) are offered as primary evidence, not authorial paraphrase — the whole chapter's method is to let the diary do the arguing.

**The diary as a private instrument of candor.** The material exists only because it was never meant to be read: numerical codes for how often the couple made love ("two symbols signifying...intercourse twice"), euphemisms ("a little Heaven just after dinner"), and admissions too intimate for print elsewhere. This is Gay's "privatization of experience" in action — a zone of erotic self-documentation walled off from the public reticence the same class enforced in its official rhetoric. The material is also the natural rebuttal to a `[[michel-foucault]]`-style repressive hypothesis: rather than sex being administratively silenced, it is exhaustively spoken, just in a deliberately private register — the repression, if any, is a matter of audience, not of desire itself. Worth noting as a partial, ironic echo of `[[modernity-machine]]`-style legibility: even in retreat from public visibility, the Todds instrumentalize and record their intimate life with near-actuarial precision (dated entries, tally marks for frequency, retrospective note-taking on "illness" cycles) — a private legibility regime applied to eros itself, not the public administrative kind the framework usually names, but a recognizable cousin of the same impulse to make experience countable.

**A homemade theory of fertility, and its disconfirmation.** Mabel Todd's confident personal doctrine — that conception could only occur if intercourse continued past her own climax, combined with the era's misguided belief that mid-cycle is the infertile period — led her to experiment on May 15, 1879, believing herself safe. The resulting pregnancy (Millicent) is presented by Gay as a small, almost comic parable of bourgeois self-assurance about the body colliding with biological reality, absent reliable medical knowledge.

**Ambivalence about impending motherhood.** Far from folding smoothly into a sentimentalized maternal identity, Mabel Todd resisted announcing her pregnancy, described feeling "almost trying grief," and explicitly ranked her love for her husband above any future love for her child: wife-love was "my air & food & water," mother-love merely "incidental." Gay uses her unusual frankness to suggest that many less candid bourgeois women privately felt the same, undermining a simple ideology of natural, automatic maternal devotion.

**Collapsing the angel/whore dichotomy.** David Todd's ecstatic letter on hearing of the pregnancy praises his wife's body in frankly sexual terms (her hips "admirably built," untouched by "tight-lacing") in the same breath as declaring her "sacred, holy mother." Gay reads this fusion — sealed by the couple's mutual salutation "husbandlover"/"lover-husband" — as direct counter-evidence to the "received wisdom" that nineteenth-century bourgeois men split women into angels and whores; here the wife is neither, and it is she, not he, who often "inspired the husband to his finer flights."

## Notable passages

- The opening paragraph states the chapter's thesis in miniature: marriage did not dim but enhanced the Todds' "sexual ardor," glossed with Shaw's aphorism and Mabel Todd's own diary language about her husband's "fresh springs of deepest tenderness."
- A run of letters and diary fragments captures the couple's hunger during separation — food-and-sex imagery ("figs and grapes," "ice cream"), Mabel Todd's "it is just dreadful to sleep alone," and David Todd's self-censoring struggle not to "inflame" himself and his wife by writing too ardently.
- Mabel Todd's own homemade contraceptive theory — timed around her sexual climax and menstrual cycle — is quoted at length and then punctured by Gay's dry aside that its "dramatic disconfirmation" arrived nine months later as her daughter Millicent.
- Her private reckoning with the prospect of motherhood ranks "wife love" as necessary as "air & food & water" against a merely "incidental" mother-love, an unusually candid admission Gay treats as symptomatic of wider, less-voiced bourgeois female ambivalence.
- Gay's closing analytical passage names the chapter's stakes explicitly: the Todds' letters and the "husbandlover"/"lover-husband" salutation "contradict" the standard image of the bourgeois male who divides women into angels and whores.
