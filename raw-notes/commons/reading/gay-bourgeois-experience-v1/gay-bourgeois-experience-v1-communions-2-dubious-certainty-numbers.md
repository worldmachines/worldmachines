# The Dubious Certainty of Numbers

Having established through private diaries and letters (the Todds, the Lymans, Lester Ward) that bourgeois erotic life could be candid and passionate, Gay turns to the century's own statistical instruments — Iwan Bloch's informal canvass of educated women, J. Matthews Duncan's clinical lectures on sterility, and above all Clelia Duel Mosher's 1892 survey of forty-five educated American wives — to ask whether that candor was exceptional or typical. He concludes, with careful qualification about the fragility of the evidence, that it was broadly representative: Mabel Todd's distinction was her eloquence, not her experience.

## Themes

**Numbers as a check on impressionistic sources, and an admittedly dubious one.** Gay frames the turn to sexology's surveys and statistics as a necessary corrective to what diaries alone can establish about a whole class, while insisting from the outset — in the phrase that gives the section its title — that such numbers are themselves "fragmentary, partial, sorely in need of skeptical interpretation."

**Pre-Mosher sexology already undercut the anesthesia stereotype.** Iwan Bloch's ad hoc interviews and J. Matthews Duncan's statistics on 504 sterile women (only a minority of whom would even answer his questions) converged on the same finding well before Mosher: educated women reported sexual desire and orgasm at rates comparable to men, and Duncan's own clinical vignettes treated the sensual wife as unremarkable rather than pathological.

**The Mosher Survey is at once the best evidence available and a compromised sample.** Gay treats Mosher's forty-five respondents — self-selected, disproportionately educated, married to educated men, mostly of Mabel Todd's own generation — as mapping only "the upper limits of Victorian sensual flexibility and liberalism," not the whole class, and he says so repeatedly rather than letting the data's authority go unquestioned.

**Orgasm statistics expose a gap between admission and experience.** More than a third of respondents reported reaching orgasm "always" or "usually"; Gay reads the internal contradictions of individual answers — a flat denial of ever having a "venereal orgasm" followed, one question later, by an inadvertent description of exactly that experience — as proof that shame about *admitting* pleasure ran well ahead of the absence of pleasure itself.

**"Spirituality" functions as the culturally sanctioned vocabulary for physical pleasure.** Respondents overwhelmingly justified intercourse as spiritual union, completeness, or a "bond," rarely as enjoyment in its own right; Gay does not read this as evidence against pleasure but as the only available language through which bourgeois women could legitimate a pleasure their culture would not let them name directly — a close cousin, in this section, of what Gay elsewhere calls bourgeois defenses and the privatization of experience, though he does not invoke those terms explicitly here.

**Gay's own historiographic reflexivity about the numbers.** He continually flags the survey's weaknesses — small sample, ambiguous questions, self-selection, the unknown profile of those who refused to answer — while still using the data, cautiously, to generalize; the section's closing move ("the dubious certainty of numbers is not quite so dubious on this crucial point") is a qualified, not a triumphant, claim.

**Payoff: Mabel Todd as representative, not exceptional.** The statistical detour folds back into the book's central argument about her: her sexual candor typifies her class and generation rather than setting her apart from it, which is the section's real historiographical stake — using aggregate data to license inference from a single rich case.

## Notable passages

The opening move (c1) states the problem baldly: private testimony is elusive, and the century's "addict[ion] to surveys and comparisons" supplies flawed instruments to check it. Duncan's clinical vignette (c2) is the earliest hard evidence in the chapter that an orgasmic, desiring wife was medically unremarkable. The Mosher orgasm statistics and the No. 23 case (c3) are Gay's clearest demonstration that reticence about naming pleasure coexisted with the pleasure itself. The parade of "spiritual union" justifications (c4) shows the discursive cover under which that pleasure was voiced. Gay's own weighing of how far to trust the sample (c5) is the section's most explicit piece of historiographical self-awareness, and its close (c6) converts the survey's very limitations into license for the book's larger claim about Todd.

Gay's material here works as empirical ammunition against a crude reading of the [[michel-foucault]] "repressive hypothesis" — the numbers suggest desire and orgasm were common, not simply suppressed — though Foucault's own argument was subtler than blanket repression, turning on the *proliferation* of discourse around sex rather than its simple absence; Gay's "spirituality" rhetoric is arguably itself an instance of exactly the discourse-multiplication Foucault described, even as it served here to license private pleasure rather than merely police it. The sexological survey apparatus itself — an attempt to render the most private and least standardizable of experiences legible through numbers, and then admitting the attempt only partly succeeds — also rhymes, faintly, with [[modernity-machine]]'s drive toward legibility; the section is in effect a case study in that drive meeting its limit against subjective, non-portable experience.
