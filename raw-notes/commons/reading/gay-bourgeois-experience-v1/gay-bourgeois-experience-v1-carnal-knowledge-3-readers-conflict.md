# Readers in Conflict: Pornography, Obscenity Law, and the Shared Unconscious of Censors and Pornographers

This section (Gay's chapter on "Carnal Knowledge," §3) moves from the nineteenth-century pornography trade and the legal campaigns against it to a psychoanalytic reading of pornography itself, and finally to the anti-pornography crusaders — arguing that censors, pornographers, and ordinary readers were all, in different ways, "readers in conflict." Gay first documents the trade's scale (Holywell Street, the Palais Royal, Comstock's meticulous seizure tallies) and the vague legal tests — Lord Campbell's Obscene Publications Act, Chief Justice Cockburn's 1868 Hicklin standard, France's 1819 law, Paragraph 184 of the German code — that let prosecutors treat serious literature (Flaubert, Baudelaire, Zola, Swinburne, Dehmel) under the same rubric as trade smut, producing real chilling effects even where prosecutions failed. He then uses private diaries and letters — his signature method — to show how differently individual bourgeois readers experienced the identical category of "licentious books": Amiel and Gladstone were seized by compulsive shame, while the young Ohio bachelor Thomas Beer wrote to his brother in proud, unembarrassed catalogue of his "tough collection." The chapter's psychoanalytic center of gravity is an argument that pornography is organized by infantile, "polymorphously perverse" fantasy, above all the splitting of women into "angel" and "whore" that recapitulates the small boy's unintegrated image of his mother — a split so charged that son-mother incest remains pornography's one nearly unbroken taboo, displaced instead onto mother-surrogate or father-daughter substitutes. Finally, turning the same psychoanalytic lens on the crusaders, Gay argues that Comstock and his allies were locked in the same unconscious economy as the pornographers they hunted — "extremes met" — and closes by naming the century's genuinely characteristic posture not prudery nor libertinism but an anxious, self-contradicting liberalism.

## Themes

**The trade was vast, cross-border, and effectively unstoppable.** Holywell Street and the Palais Royal anchored international networks that outran customs enforcement and survived repeated prosecutions; Comstock's own tallies of destroyed material (tens of thousands of pounds of books, hundreds of thousands of pictures) inadvertently prove the scale of what they failed to suppress. Access was stratified by price rather than policed away: the "most notorious" luxury editions stayed with "rich amateurs" while cheaper trade material reached everyone else.

**Legal tests for obscenity were vague by design or default, and that vagueness did the damage.** Lord Campbell's language ("calculated to shock the common feelings of decency in a well regulated mind") and Cockburn's 1868 Hicklin formula gave "troubled moralists and ambitious prosecutors... more room for maneuver," and comparable French and German statutes were no more precise. The result was not the suppression of pornography as such but the prosecution of serious writers under the same statutes as hack pornographers — Gay's real point being that legal "obscenity" was never a stable category, only a site of continuous, self-serving redefinition.

**Prosecution produced self-censorship independent of conviction.** Flaubert was acquitted, yet the "looming threat of fines and prison" made writers flinch: Swinburne withheld *Lesbia Brandon*, lending libraries like Mudie's quietly blacklisted naturalists, Vizetelly went to prison for an *insufficiently* expurgated Zola translation, and Louis Desprez died shortly after a month's sentence for a "serious novel about rural life" — martyrdoms that fed the avant-garde's fury against bourgeois respectability.

**Reading pornography was radically various — "one man's classic was another man's pornography."** Amiel's diary records paroxysms of self-reproach for an addiction to "licentious books" he could not resist; Gladstone confessed guilt for reading Petronius on the day of his child's baptism. Thomas Beer's 1887 letter to his brother is the chapter's rare positive specimen — proud rather than ashamed — and his actual list (Rabelais, the Decameron, the Heptameron, Aristotle's Master-Piece) is revealing precisely because it is so tame: canonical works were redeemed for respectable readers by what Gay elsewhere calls their **doctrine of distance** — "their coarseness was redeemed by their distance, their sensuality by their wit."

**Pornography's content follows an infantile, not an adult, logic.** Gay reads its stock features — the impossibly endowed male, the insatiable woman, the omnipresent voyeur, the impatience that cannot "wait" (Freud's primary-process thinking) — as a "plagiarism of infantile fantasies." Its central operation is the splitting of women into the "purest and the most defiled," recapitulating the boy's unintegrated image of a mother "he wants to kill in the afternoon" and "marry in the evening"; Freud's 1912 essay on the "psychological debasement of the sexual object" supplies Gay's clinical vocabulary for why the addict of pornography can only desire where he does not love. Tellingly, son-mother incest is pornography's most reticent taboo — evaded through substitutes (a middle-aged landlady, an "excitable housekeeper") — while father-daughter incest circulates far more freely, a telling asymmetry in what even a shameless genre can afford to picture directly.

**Censors and pornographers were, in Gay's psychoanalytic reading, mirror images — "extremes met."** Comstock's obsessive, almost loving cataloguing of "filth," Sydney Smith's aphorism about rat-catchers who love to catch rats, and Charles Reade's 1864 coinage **the prurient prude** all serve Gay's claim that purity crusaders were not, as they supposed, authorized spokesmen for bourgeois opinion but "a caricature of the prevalent bourgeois concerns with the mastery of passion and the segregation of clean from dirty thoughts."

**The chapter's real subject is bourgeois ambivalence, not repression or liberation.** Gay's closing judgment is that the "most characteristic bourgeois perspective" was "an uncertain, far from consistent liberalism," readers and legislators alike who "vacillated, hesitated, and contradicted themselves" — a small, sharply drawn instance of the larger argument that the bourgeois was less a settled ideology than a family of desires and anxieties in permanent friction with its own defenses.

## Notable passages

The **Holywell Street raid tally** (383 books, 12,346 prints, thousands of pounds of type) opens the chapter's demonstration that the trade's sheer material scale outstripped any regime of suppression, private or legal.

**Cockburn's 1868 Hicklin test** — "whether the tendency of the matter charged... is to deprave and corrupt those whose minds are open to such immoral influences" — is quoted because it is the operative legal fiction that let "serious literature" and "unmistakable pieces of sexual exploitation" share the same dock for the rest of the century.

**Thomas Beer's 1887 letter** to his brother William is curated because it is Gay's rare positive case: a young bourgeois bachelor boasting, rather than confessing, about his erotic reading — the private-document method (diaries, letters) that grounds Gay's whole revisionist thesis about bourgeois sexuality.

The **madonna/whore passage**, ending on Freud's 1912 line about "the psychological debasement of the sexual object," is the chapter's psychoanalytic keystone: it is where Gay explains pornography's degradation of women as a displaced, unresolved oedipal split rather than simple misogyny.

**Sydney Smith's rat-catcher aphorism and Reade's "prurient prude"** are curated together because they show that the psychological mirroring of censor and pornographer was a bourgeois commonplace well before Freud gave it "scientific grounding" — Gay is tracing a lay insight, not imposing an anachronistic one.

The **closing sentence** — "censors and liberals alike were readers in conflict" — is curated because it states the chapter's thesis in its own words and gives the unit its title.

## World Machines resonances

The anti-pornography societies' passion for exact enumeration — Comstock's "27,584 pounds of books," "203,238 obscene pictures," "1,376,939 catalogues, songs, circulars, and poems" — reads as a small, telling instance of a **legibility** project (per [[modernity-machine]]): turning private moral disorder into countable, administrable quantities, in the same spirit as the cadastres and timetables the Modernity Machine used to make a population governable. The resonance is partial, though: Gay's point is not state capacity but psychological projection — the crusaders' quantifying zeal is itself read as symptomatic, not merely administrative, and the trade obstinately refused to be mapped ("It is not possible to reconstruct a dependable map across space and time of nineteenth-century pornography"), so the legibility project fails on its own terms.

The chapter is also a natural point of contact with [[michel-foucault]]'s repressive hypothesis, since Gay's own evidence — that the crusaders' war on obscenity generated an obsessive discourse *about* obscenity, and that "the fantasies of those most obsessed with the evil of pornography were distorted, slightly sublimated mirror images of the very fictions they were dedicating their lives to extirpating" — is exactly Foucault's claim that repression proliferates rather than silences sexual discourse. But the mechanism differs: Foucault locates the effect in power/knowledge and the discursive apparatus itself, while Gay locates it in individual unconscious projection (reaction formation, disavowed desire), a psychoanalytic rather than discursive explanation for the same observed pattern.
