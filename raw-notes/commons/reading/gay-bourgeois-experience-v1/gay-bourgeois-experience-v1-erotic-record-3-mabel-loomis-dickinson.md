# Mabel Loomis Todd's Affair with Austin Dickinson: A Test Case for Bourgeois Respectability

This unit is the third section of Gay's chapter on Mabel Loomis Todd, using her long, passionate, and widely-known-yet-officially-secret affair with Austin Dickinson (Emily Dickinson's brother) as a case study in how the nineteenth-century bourgeoisie actually managed erotic life. Gay narrates the affair's course — from Todd's arrival in Amherst in 1881, through a reckless flirtation with Austin's son Ned, into a consuming decade-plus liaison with Austin himself, sustained until his death in 1895 — and then reads it against three registers: Mabel Todd's own psychology (narcissistic hunger, oedipal father-idealization, self-justifying antinomianism), her husband David's active complicity, and the town of Amherst's collective, semi-public toleration. The chapter's payoff is a reframing of Victorian "hypocrisy" itself: not a simple mask over repression, but a socially engineered set of "routes of evasion" that let respectable people buy erotic satisfaction at an affordable price.

## Themes

**The affair as "a test case for the middle-class social style."** Gay frames the Todd–Dickinson liaison not as an aberration but as diagnostic: a secret "that everyone shared," conducted with elaborate subterfuge inside an otherwise showcase-respectable household. The scandal is instructive precisely because it was absorbed rather than expelled by bourgeois Amherst.

**Narcissism and self-justifying antinomianism.** Gay reads Todd's diaries as evidence of a woman whose eroticism was fused with a conviction of her own exceptionalism — her affair "confirmed her extraordinariness" rather than the reverse. She explicitly defends George Eliot's "unhallowed union" with Lewes and arrives at a doctrine — "the spirit is greater than the letter," fit only for the rare "one in a thousand" — that Gay calls, pointedly, doctrine "a Nietzsche would not have been ashamed" of. Her contempt for "middle-class pharisees" is less a rejection of bourgeois norms than a self-flattering exemption from them.

**Complicity and "learned ignorance" as domestic defenses.** David Todd's role is the unit's strangest material: he encouraged the affair, carried messages, and warned the lovers of his return by whistling a tune, deriving what Gay suggests was vicarious erotic satisfaction from it — itself entangled with his own promiscuity and, Gay hints, unintegrated homosexual and incestuous impulses that surfaced starkly in his senile old age. Their daughter Millicent's defense was the mirror image: a practiced refusal to "wonder," because "to conjecture would have been to know." This is Gay's "learned ignorance" in miniature — a household-level defense mechanism that lets shared knowledge remain technically unspoken.

**The oedipal argument: idealized fathers as templates for erotic choice.** Gay generalizes from Todd's lifelong idealization of her father Eben Loomis (a fellow astronomer, like her husband) to argue that a daughter's unresolved oedipal attachment could be, for "the fortunate," sublimated into "a school for love" rather than "a road to emotional shipwreck" — citing Alcott, Beecher, Sanger, and Victoria Benedictsson as comparanda, and Dora Montefiore's memoir of the "children's hour" as a positive instance of the same eroticized father-idealization.

**Hypocrisy reconsidered as negotiated, illegible space.** The unit's structural argument, and its clearest statement of Gay's larger thesis: bourgeois moral boundaries were "wide, often ambiguous bands of prohibitions," and what moralists denounced as hypocrisy was in fact "a way of carving out space for the passions—within reason." Todd's affair cost her something real, but the price was "one she could afford to pay" — this is the "privatization of experience" the source pack flags, made concrete as a social mechanism rather than an abstract slogan.

**The cost borne by the child.** Millicent Todd is repeatedly named "witness and sacrifice"; Gay invokes Freud's observation that "the guilt of the innocent is the hardest of all to bear" to explain her lifelong, displaced sense of responsibility for a household disorder that was never hers to cause.

## Notable passages

The opening frame — "But her intimacy... also came to be profoundly problematic... a secret that everyone shared, a test case for the middle-class social style" — sets up the whole chapter's argumentative use of the affair as evidence, not anecdote.

The whistling passage (Millicent's father warning the lovers of his return, and her sixty-years-later insistence she never wondered why) is the unit's sharpest illustration of "learned ignorance" as a lived, embodied defense rather than a mere figure of speech.

The antinomian self-justification passage — Todd's defense of George Eliot and her own "revolutionary doctrine" — shows Gay tracing the psychological machinery (narcissistic investment, not mere rationalization) behind bourgeois transgression.

The oedipal-idealization passage generalizes the case study into a claim about nineteenth-century women's erotic development more broadly, treating idealization of the father as double-edged: potentially "baneful" but also, when "suitably sublimated," an authentic "school for love."

The closing passage on boundaries and "routes of evasion" is the unit's thesis statement: hypocrisy re-described as social technology for managing desire, cashed out in the flat, almost economic final line about an affordable price.

For World Machines: this is close kin to the "repressive hypothesis" territory the [[michel-foucault]] page covers — Gay's whole point is that official Victorian propriety and actual erotic practice diverged systematically, and that the divergence was itself socially managed rather than accidental. The fit is not exact: Foucault's claim is discursive (repression as itself a productive proliferation of sex-talk), while Gay's claim here is behavioral and social-historical (a community's tacit, differential tolerance of a known affair). There is also a loose, partial resonance with [[modernity-machine]]'s legibility concept: Gay explicitly says the bourgeoisie's moral boundaries were never "easily legible or in any way definitive" despite their official, seemingly-legible codes — an illegibility that was functional, not a failure of the code. This is worth naming but shouldn't be overstated: Gay is describing interpersonal and communal norms, not the state-scale measuring/ordering infrastructure the Modernity Machine concept centers on.
