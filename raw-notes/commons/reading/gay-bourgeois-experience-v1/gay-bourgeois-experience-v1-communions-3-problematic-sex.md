# The Problematic Sex: The Nineteenth-Century Dispute over Female Desire

This chapter reconstructs the nineteenth century's peculiar and unresolved argument over whether women possess a genuine sexual appetite — a question Gay shows had been effectively settled the other way for millennia (the Zeus-Hera-Tiresias myth held woman the *more* lustful sex) until "the bourgeois century made Woman problematic." Surveying doctors, sexologists, novelists, moralists, and finally Freud, Gay maps a running quarrel between those who affirmed female sensuality (Debay, Droz, Nystrom, the Goncourts, Bartlett) and those who denied or minimized it (Acton, Krafft-Ebing, Alcott, Greg), with a large, self-contradicting middle ground (Becklard, Blackwell, Napheys, Otto Adler) whose own evidence usually undercuts its official conclusions. The chapter closes by installing Freud as the pivotal, though only partial, resolution: he insisted on innate female sexuality and built a method for reading it beneath its cultural disguises, but he remained, in Gay's phrase, "not simply the subverter of his culture" but "also its child."

## Themes

**A historical reversal, not a timeless prejudice.** Gay is emphatic that nineteenth-century doubt about female desire was itself a novelty, not an inheritance: from Rome through the Renaissance and Enlightenment the received wisdom (the Tiresias myth, Philo Judaeus's reading of Eve, the moralists' "eternal Eve") assumed woman the lustier sex. The chapter's real subject is therefore not sexual ignorance in general but a specific, dated invention — "reopened a hundred years or so earlier" than Bloch's 1907 survey — that runs in tandem with the century's "panic-stricken crusade against masturbation." This periodizing move is Gay's signature: he wants a *manufactured*, historically locatable anxiety, not a perennial one.

**Two camps, and a large, contradictory middle.** One line of physicians and writers (Siebert, Moreau, Ribbing, Roubaux, Scott, Droz, Debay, Nystrom, Amiel) treated female appetite as simply real, sometimes wrapping the claim in maternal or spiritual rhetoric to make it respectable. The opposing line (Acton, Krafft-Ebing, H. Newell Martin, Alcott, Greg, Platt) built what Gay calls the "gospel of female sexual anesthesia," in which normal women are constitutionally cool and only "aberrant" ones desire. Between them sit compromise figures — Becklard's "natural inclination" restrained by social role, Elizabeth Blackwell's "Christian physiologist" defense of sexual pleasure as divinely granted but subordinate to mental love, Napheys's simultaneous claims that women's appetite is "moderate" and that "only in very rare instances" do women feel a tenth of male desire. Gay treats this incoherence itself as data: a sign of **defenses** and unexamined anxiety operating beneath the appearance of clinical objectivity ("Their books were dictated more by unexamined preconceptions and unconscious fears than by medical observations").

**Bourgeois class construction through sexual doctrine.** Dr. Debay's physiology is notable not only for affirming female pleasure but for locating refined, mutually satisfying sexuality specifically among the urban, literate, and "idle" — his code for the prosperous bourgeois — against a supposed rural sexuality of male brutality and female indifference. Sexual sensitivity becomes a mark of class distinction, a "human," civilized attribute the middle class claims for itself. This is the chapter's clearest instance of a class defining its own culture partly through a doctrine of the body.

**Ideology contradicted by its own evidence — the case of Otto Adler.** Dr. Otto Adler's 1904 treatise on women's "defective sexual susceptibilities" estimates frigidity at anywhere from ten to forty percent, yet his own fifteen case vignettes mostly show intense, if often unconsummated or displaced, desire. Case XIV — a woman who masturbated to orgasm from childhood but felt nothing in intercourse despite being "sexually excited to a high degree" — becomes Gay's set-piece for the **gap between official ideology and private practice**: a "disjunction between experience and expression" shared by patient and physician alike, in which real erotic capacity is present but unreadable to a doctrine that has already decided what it will find. Acton's own confident claim that "the best mothers, wives, and managers of households know little or nothing of sexual indulgences" gets the same treatment: read against the grain, it describes women who *believed* something was wrong with them, not women who lacked desire.

**Freud as partial resolution.** Freud enters as the one who could finally treat professions of coldness as camouflage rather than fact, using dreams, slips, and symptoms to decode desire that respectable culture had pushed out of awareness — Gay's version of Freud as the great listener where his predecessors "did not know how to listen." But Gay pointedly refuses to let Freud stand outside the culture he diagnosed: Freud never resolved "the riddle of femininity," underestimated ordinary bourgeois erotic happiness because his consulting room selected for its casualties, and his own theory of a universal, ineradicable "man's fear of woman" is presented as both an insight into and a symptom of the very anxieties the chapter has been cataloguing.

## Notable passages

The opening reconstruction of the Zeus/Hera/Tiresias myth establishes that antiquity, unlike the bourgeois nineteenth century, took woman's greater sexual appetite as settled fact — the necessary baseline against which Gay's "Woman problematic" thesis makes sense. Debay's physiological treatise, read closely, shows a specifically bourgeois idiom of sexual refinement being constructed against imagined rural coarseness. Acton's "gospel of female sexual anesthesia" gives the doctrine's most quotable, and most consequential, statement. Otto Adler's Case XIV is the chapter's evidentiary centerpiece, and the closing paragraph on Freud states Gay's own analytic stance most directly: that Freud "offered most of the solution but, at the same time, perpetuated some of the problem," a formula that could stand for Gay's method throughout — using each era's own experts against their own certainties.

The chapter's whole procedure — an escalating, self-multiplying archive of expert discourse (medical treatises, marriage manuals, case histories, surveys) all trying to name and adjudicate a single forbidden-yet-endlessly-discussed topic — resonates with [[michel-foucault]]'s argument that modern sexuality was produced by proliferating discourse rather than simple silence; the brief notes this is Gay's natural foil, and here the fit is real but partial. Gay does not go where Foucault goes: he wants a genuine repressive doctrine (the anesthesia gospel) sitting on top of a genuine private sexuality (Mabel Loomis Todd's diary, Adler's own excitable patients), an authentic-experience-under-ideology model that Foucault's account of discourse as productive, not merely repressive, would resist. There is also a fainter, more speculative echo of [[modernity-machine]]'s legibility project in the century's drive to render female desire statistically knowable — Adler's freely invented ten-to-forty-percent frigidity estimates, the repeated appeals to "a really dependable standard of judgment," the era's surveys (Mosher's) — though Gay's own point is that this legibilizing apparatus mostly failed, producing certainty-shaped noise rather than knowledge; the resonance is with the ambition, not its success.
