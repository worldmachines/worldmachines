# The Flight into Knowledge

Gay surveys the international boom, from the 1890s through the 1910s, in literature on sexual enlightenment: expert treatises (medical, philosophical, sociological) and popular parental tracts alike, all proclaiming an unprecedented emergency of ignorance and demanding frank instruction of the young. Reading this literature against itself, Gay argues it is more revealing about bourgeois culture's anxieties — over class respectability, national and racial fitness, professional authority — than about sexuality as such, because the reformers, even as they denounce silence and euphemism, practice both; their "flight into knowledge" is compromised at every turn by the reticence it claims to overcome.

## Themes

**The rhetoric of manufactured emergency.** A dense international infrastructure of societies and congresses — the American Federation for Sex Hygiene, the White Cross Society, Germany's Society for Fighting Venereal Disease, Senator Berenger's League against License in the Streets — each insisted that a centuries-old "policy of silence" had only just been broken by their own generation. Gay is skeptical of the claimed novelty: ignorance and misinformation about sex did not originate with the bourgeois century, yet educators competed to dramatize their own urgency and to feel superior (or inferior) to counterparts abroad. The intensity of the alarm, more than the content of the pedagogy, is the historical symptom.

**Reticent candor — the book's key irony.** "These calls for frankness are reticent; condemning euphemisms, they use little else." Most of the literature evades exactly the topics it purports to address, producing "a compromise between the concealment it condemns and the candor it professes, with candor more often than not the casualty." Mary Wood-Allen's morning-glory allegory and Edward Lyttelton's account of intercourse as a "seed of life... entrusted by God to the father" exemplify this: sentimental, oblique, evasive precisely where explicitness was promised. Freud's blunt counter-motto — "J'appelle un chat un chat" — is Gay's foil, marking how far even sympathetic, well-meaning reformers like Lyttelton fell short of Freud's directness (Lyttelton, notably, never questions the era's fear of masturbation, and does not realize girls masturbate too).

**Purity over pleasure — knowledge as anxiety-management.** With rare exceptions (Mantegazza, Havelock Ellis), the enlighteners wrote to prevent vice and "pollution," not to enlarge erotic life; Sylvanus Stall and Mary Wood-Allen's devotional "Self and Sex" series, endorsed by clergy and by Anthony Comstock alike, treats carnal knowledge purely as prophylaxis against "perdition." This is a clear instance of the defenses and "learned ignorance" Gay traces through the volume: information deployed to contain anxiety rather than to expand experience.

**An alliance of fear, not belief.** Darwinists, bishops, fundamentalists, Anglicans, militarists and social-purity crusaders converged on the same programme for incompatible reasons, united by shared dread — syphilis weakening armies, "race suicide," the swamping of "desirable" stock by "inferior" — rather than shared conviction. Hermann Rohleder's slogan, "Civilization is syphilization," crystallizes the fusion of sexual hygiene with national and racial survival anxiety that runs under the whole movement. This resonates with (while narrower in scope than) [[michel-foucault]]'s account of the nineteenth-century "incitement to discourse" around sex — Gay, like Foucault, finds not silence but a proliferating, anxious, institutionally organized talk about sex; where Foucault reads this discourse as producing new mechanisms of power, Gay reads it more psychologically, as bourgeois defenses managing class and status anxiety. The proliferation of congresses, societies, and standardized didactic tracts for classifying and correcting sexual ignorance also faintly echoes [[modernity-machine]]'s drive toward legibility — making an intimate, disorderly domain governable through publication, comparison, and pedagogy — though the parallel is loose: these reformers sought moral discipline, not the modernity machine's characteristic apparatus of measurement and administration.

**Children already know.** Dr. Hugh Cabot's insistence that "there is no such condition as innocence in these children" anticipates Freud's own view that childhood sexual curiosity is nearly as exigent as an inborn drive. For Gay, this is the chapter's most subversive observation: the real question reformers faced was never whether children would learn about sex, but from whom — and the evasive, purity-driven literature they produced was poorly suited to the task it claimed to serve.

## Notable passages

The opening thesis states Gay's method directly: the literature is diagnostic of "the vitality of bourgeois repressions now under scattered fire" rather than a reliable guide to sexuality itself. The catalogue of national societies culminates in the chapter's title phrase, "the flight from ignorance became a flight into knowledge," showing how uniform and self-congratulatory the reform rhetoric was across countries. The reticence/candor passage supplies the chapter's sharpest formulation of its central irony. The Freud/euphemism passage stages the contrast between Freud's declared bluntness and the reformers' sentimental evasions (the morning-glory story is the clearest specimen of the latter). The closing passage names the "ill-assorted" coalition of Darwinists, bishops, fundamentalists and militarists and delivers Gay's verdict: "ignorance claimed its allies, in the bourgeois century, even among the crusaders for knowledge."
