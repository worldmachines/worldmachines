# Maize, Potatoes, and the Dietary Revolutions of the Eighteenth Century

This unit closes Braudel's survey of the world's dominant food plants with maize: its well-documented Mesoamerican origins, its load-bearing role in Inca, Aztec and Maya civilization, its slow two-century diffusion around the globe alongside the potato, and the eighteenth-century "dietary revolution" in which both crops finally became staples of the European (and Congolese, Chinese, and colonial American) poor. It closes on a vignette from Mexico City in 1697, where a bread riot lets Braudel read the word "bread" itself as a marker of social, and possibly racial, hierarchy against maize.

## Themes

**Archaeology fixes what texts cannot.** Braudel opens by dismissing eighteenth-century naturalists' speculation about an Asian origin for maize and instead builds the case from fossilized pollen beneath drained Mexico City and from stratified cave deposits in the Tehuacan valley, where the modern cultivated plant can be watched shrinking, layer by layer, back into a small, self-seeding wild ancestor. This is Braudel's evidentiary method in miniature: material life's history is read out of soil and pollen before it is read out of chronicles.

**Material life underwrites monumental power and human misery in the same breath.** Maize's near-effortless cultivation (fifty days' labor a year, in Braudel's account) freed peasant populations for the pyramids of the Maya and Aztecs, the terraces of the Inca, and the "cyclopean walls of Cuzco" — but the same surplus leisure, in Braudel's telling, was conscripted into theocratic-totalitarian public works and left the maize-eating peasantry chronically malnourished ("An unsatisfactory daily bread... None of these is an adequate food"). Braudel's dry aside — asking who is to blame for the misery and answering "Man of course. But maize as well" — treats a crop as itself a historical agent operating within the limits of the possible, not a neutral input.

**Diffusion is slow, local, and invisible until it suddenly isn't.** Maize and the potato both reach Europe within decades of Columbus but do not become continent-wide staples until the eighteenth century — two hundred years of scattered, independent local adoptions (Andalusia by 1500, Béarn by 1523, Venetia by 1539, the Balkans under at least ten different regional names) before what Braudel treats as a single "revolution." This shape — long, distributed, near-invisible accumulation across disconnected local instances, then a sudden visible threshold of dominance — rhymes with WMP's [[history-machine]]: a paradigm assembling silently from independent components before it "lurches into visibility." The rhyme is partial: Braudel's diffusion is agronomic and demographic, driven by famine and yield rather than an institutional paradigm coalescing, but the curve's shape (long invisibility, sudden dominance) matches.

**Peasants used invisibility strategically to escape extraction.** In the Balkans, maize cultivation was deliberately kept "off the main roads" and restricted to garden plots specifically to evade seigneurial dues and taxation. This inverts [[legibility-as-machine-core]]: here the cultivators, not a state apparatus, are the ones managing visibility — staying illegible to a proto-fiscal gaze rather than being rendered legible by one. It is a pre-modern, bottom-up version of the same underlying stake (visibility as vulnerability), not the Modernity Machine's cadastral imposition of legibility from above, so the resonance is more a shared logic than a shared mechanism.

**The potato had an accidental, literal capture-resistance.** Because it ripens underground, the potato could survive an army camped on the field all summer, and because it was administratively unclassified, it initially escaped tithes — making it the crop of choice in nearly every major eighteenth-century European war Braudel lists, from the War of the Augsburg League to the aptly named "potato war" (the War of the Bavarian Succession). This is a productive but strictly botanical/administrative rhyme with [[capture-resistance]]: the potato resists extraction as a passive structural property of tubers and tax categories, not through anything like agentic autonomy or self-organization.

**Material life and the market economy interlock at the peasant's own table.** The unit's clearest statement of Braudel's three-storey house in miniature: maize let the peasant eat cheaply while freeing wheat — the higher-value crop — to be sold at roughly double the price, with Venetia exporting 15–20% of its cereal crop as a result. Subsistence and the market economy are not sequential stages here but simultaneous, interlocking uses of the same harvest.

**Food differentiates world regions and, within them, social and racial hierarchy.** Braudel's comparative method plays out globally — maize's reluctant reception in the Congo (initially "for pigs," not men), its slow penetration of China only once eighteenth-century population growth forced cultivation of marginal hill land, its rejection in south-east China where rice still ruled — and locally, in the closing Mexico City vignette, where a 1697 bread riot lets Braudel float the "white bread, white man" equation: demanding "bread" under the Viceroy's window may itself have marked the demonstrators as white, since maize cakes, not bread, were what everyone else ate.

## Notable passages

The Tehuacan excavation passage is the unit's methodological anchor: cave deposits yield "the whole retrospective history of maize" from a fragile-eared wild ancestor forward, grounding origin claims in stratigraphy rather than naturalist speculation.

The pyramids-and-misery passage is the unit's sharpest rhetorical turn, moving from crediting maize with making "the giant Mayan or Aztec pyramids... the cyclopean walls of Cuzco" possible to the deadpan indictment "who is to blame? Man of course. But maize as well" — Braudel crediting a crop with agency in shaping a civilization's labor regime.

The Balkans passage shows maize cultivation deliberately kept off main roads for two centuries specifically to dodge taxation and seigneurial dues, only becoming a field crop once it no longer needed to hide — the clearest illustration in the unit of subsistence agriculture managing its own visibility to power.

The potato-and-war passage catalogues the European wars in which potato-growing spread precisely because armies could not destroy an underground crop, closing on the aptly named "potato war" (the War of the Bavarian Succession, 1778–9).

The Venetia passage — "The peasant ate maize and sold his wheat, for which he could get about double the price" — states in one sentence the unit's core structural claim about how a subsistence crop subsidizes a market one.

The Mexico City passage closes the unit on Careri's 1697 eyewitness account of a bread riot, with Braudel's own speculative gloss that "bread" versus maize cakes may have tracked a racial line between whites and mestizos, Indians, and black slaves.
