# Houses Throughout the World: Materials, Tradition, and the Rural–Urban Divide

This unit is the housing half of Braudel's Chapter 4 ("Superfluity and Sufficiency: Houses, Clothes and Fashion"), taking dwellings — not yet clothes or fashion — as the second great index of the line between luxury and sufficiency. Moving continuously from Prague and Santillana to Persia, Brazil, Cuzco, the Yucatan, Canton, and Peking, Braudel argues that houses everywhere are slow-changing repositories of custom and material constraint, and then traces how, within Europe especially, changing building materials, fire regulation, village mobility, and urban wealth gradually differentiate rich housing from poor and country from town.

## Themes

**Houses are Braudel's own instance of the longue durée: tradition as an almost immovable force.** He opens by insisting that housing forms change "only very slowly, if at all," citing sixteenth-century Prague, Santillana, and wooden Beauvais houses still standing centuries later. Masons rebuilding fire-ravaged Valladolid after 1564 reproduce archaic Muslim design "unconsciously"; Persian houses of 1694 all share one plan; a Brazilian peasant hut observed in the 1930s is called "an ageless document, valid for centuries before the present day"; the nomad's tent "has come down through the centuries without change." This is the same deep-time register Braudel uses elsewhere for material life as a whole — a genuinely close cousin of [[world-machines]]' notion of deep, slow-moving infrastructure that organizes life beneath the level of events, though here the infrastructure is literally physical (walls, roofs, plans) rather than institutional or cognitive.

**Geography sets "long-lasting limitations," but "does not explain everything" — materials are also a social hierarchy.** Stone, brick, wood, and earth each impose regional ceilings on what can be built, yet within those ceilings, choice of material tracks wealth and status: stone reserved for "the houses of princes and gods" at Cuzco and in the Maya kingdoms, marble only for the imperial palace at Peking, tiled roofs marked as "a sign of wealth" in Europe well into the eighteenth century. This is Braudel's characteristic move — material constraint plus human/social agency operating jointly (his "limits of the possible" logic, though the phrase itself isn't used in this unit) — and it is what licenses his genuinely world-scale comparison across Islam, China, India, and the Americas rather than a Eurocentric account.

**Building materials were governed, not simply chosen.** Village houses in Alsace needed communal or seigneurial authorization to draw wood from controlled forests; Wetterau villages were forbidden straw and irregular shingles for fire risk; the king of Sardinia's administration in 1772 tied disaster relief to a switch from thatch to tile or slate. Braudel's own gloss — "the use of stone and tile was only achieved in some places by coercion or subsidy" — describes authorities standardizing vernacular building against local practice for safety and control. That's a modest, material-scale echo of [[legibility-as-machine-core]] (rendering a domain uniform and inspectable to manage risk), though it operates through seigneurial and municipal fiat rather than the accounting/cadastral apparatus the Modernity Machine is usually keyed to.

**Villages are mobile, and their remains decay into heritage rather than simply vanishing.** Archaeological excavation shows villages growing, contracting, and shifting site over centuries; some desertions were total and final (the German *Wüstungen*), others just a few kilometres' relocation of "furniture, people, animals, stones." What survives now mostly survives as musealized fragment — Dracy's excavated cellars, the Innsbruck museum's mountain-house interiors, a Savoy chimney no longer used because the house became a holiday home. A functioning peasant habitat ending up as a preserved or excavated display piece is a loose but suggestive rhyme with the [[decay-sequence]] (Functional sliding toward Ornamental/Invisible) — loose because Braudel's houses decay through abandonment and rediscovery rather than through a canonical institution hollowing out while its form persists.

**Housing enacts and produces class difference, culminating in the eighteenth-century separation of home from work.** Parisian garrets housed the poor at ever-higher, ever-worse floors while the rich occupied hôtels like the Musée de Cluny or Wallenstein's Prague palace; before the eighteenth century, master, workshop, and household were one unit (a Paris merchant's shop below, family above, apprentices higher still — the same in London and, Braudel stresses, in Canton and Peking too). Only with new luxury does the wealthy home split from the workplace, assigning domestic space to "the woman" and productive space to the man. The chapter's final movement, the "urbanized countryside," shows the same class logic running outward: town fortunes buy rural land and villas from the Paris basin to the Bosporus to the outskirts of Naples, so that rural building, too, becomes an expression of urban wealth.

## Notable passages

The opening statement of housing as a longue-durée object (tradition and precedent "always present," illustrated through Persia, Brazil, and the nomad's tent) is the unit's clearest statement of Braudel's own framing and anchors the first curated passage. The second passage runs from "geography explains why, but it does not explain everything" through the Cuzco/Maya/Deccan comparison, capturing both the limits-of-the-possible argument and the world-scale method in one continuous span. The third passage, on Wetterau's straw ban and Sardinia's tile-or-slate condition, is the clearest case of authority reshaping vernacular building. The fourth, on excavated deserted villages and the *Wüstungen*, documents village mobility and the musealization of the rural past. The fifth, opening the "new luxury" of the eighteenth century, marks the historical hinge where home and workplace split for the European (and, Braudel notes, Chinese) urban rich.
