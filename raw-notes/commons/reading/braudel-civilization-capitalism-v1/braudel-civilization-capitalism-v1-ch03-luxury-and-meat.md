# Superfluity and Sufficiency: Luxury, Diet, and the Long Retreat of Meat

Braudel opens with luxury as a definitional problem — an elusive, constantly redrawn line between the diet of the majority and the ostentation of the privileged — then narrows to food specifically: Europe's own refined cuisine arrived "belatedly" compared to China and Islam, ordinary Europeans nonetheless ate meat in startling abundance from the mid-fourteenth to the mid-sixteenth century, and that abundance then reverses into a three-century retreat after 1550 that only lifts by about 1850; the whole argument is set inside a world tour comparing European meat-eating against China, Japan, India, the Ottoman lands, Egypt, and colonial Brazil and Argentina.

## Themes

**Luxury is not a fixed set of goods but a moving, relational category.** Sugar, pepper, flat plates, forks, glass windowpanes, chairs, handkerchiefs, oranges — each was luxury in its moment and ordinary soon after; Braudel insists the "dialectic" between average and exceptional "can never be perfect" and must be redrawn per period, per country, per civilization.

**Desire, not need, is what moves both classes and civilizations.** Braudel assembles Mauss, Bachelard, and Rueff to argue that "production is the daughter of desire" rather than necessity, and generalizes the rich-versus-poor "class struggle" over luxury into a struggle *between* civilizations, each "incessantly eyeing" the others' goods and accelerating exchange. This reads as a civilizational-register cousin of [[Conatus]] — striving beyond bare subsistence as the engine of activity — though Spinoza's conatus is a metaphysical drive of individual persistence, while Braudel's "desire" is a collective, materially conditioned appetite that only some societies can act on (he ties its suppression, via Dobzhansky, to "the many as manured soil" for the luxury of the few).

**Sophisticated cuisine reached Europe late, and always stayed an elite affair.** China's culinary tradition is over a thousand years old; equivalent European refinement doesn't appear until fifteenth-century Italy, then France (formalized only as late as Menon's 1746 *Cuisiniere bourgeoise*). Throughout, elite cookbooks describe nothing like what "most people ate": the French peasant "sold more than his surpluses" and "never ate his best produce," selling wheat and poultry while eating millet, maize, and salt pork — a pattern Braudel finds mirrored in China (rich/poor culinary disparity greater than anywhere else in the world, per Sir John Barrow).

**"Carnivorous Europe": for two centuries, ordinary people ate remarkable quantities of meat.** Contra the assumption that hardship deepens the further back one looks, Braudel argues 1350–1550 was unusually favorable for common living standards — Black Death labor scarcity drove up real wages — and meat consumption on that scale was "not... reserved to the very rich": Montaigne's seven-dish German inns, Saxon ordinances guaranteeing craftsmen four meat courses, herds of 16,000–20,000 oxen pouring into German cattle fairs.

**After 1550 the trend reverses for three hundred years, reconstructed from scattered witness testimony rather than any single observer's account.** Peasant and gentry witnesses across Swabia, Brittany, Normandy, and Nuremberg independently describe the same collapse — Heinrich Müller's Swabian peasants now eating "almost worse... than day-labourers... in the old days" — which Braudel triangulates against price series (Paris, Hamburg, German averages) into a continent-wide "extraordinary step backwards" lasting to about 1850. He grounds the cause in what he elsewhere calls "the limits of the possible": population growth pressing on a fixed land base forced a trade-off between arable and pasture (the Melun figures — 14,400 arable hectares against 814 of pasture — make the constraint explicit), so cereals, not meat, took the available land. Braudel piecing together this decades-invisible conjuncture from disconnected local testimonies and account books — no contemporary saw "meat consumption is falling across a continent," only their own kitchen — is a strong instance of what this project calls a [[history-machine]]: the pattern assembles silently and distributedly and only becomes visible in aggregate, in Braudel's own synthesis, three centuries after the fact. (Lohse's concept is about historiographical paradigms rather than the socioeconomic object itself, but the structural shape — distributed, unrecognized components lurching into visibility only in retrospect — is the same.)

**Europe's meat-eating "privilege" was real but comparative and unevenly distributed, part of a genuine world-scale survey.** Braudel sets Europe against Japan ("the only meat they eat is game"), India (meat "regarded... with horror"), China (meat rare, minced into vegetable dishes, mandarins eating "only tiny quantities"), Turkey and Egypt (a sheep or a third of a sheep per Istanbul resident per year), and colonial Brazil/Argentina (a new meat-abundant frontier via *carne do sol* and *charque*, echoing Europe's own vanished 1350–1550 abundance). A Peking missionary makes the material logic explicit: Chinese population pressure on a fixed land base leaves no land for pasture, hence "no manure for the fields, no meat on the tables, no horses for battle" — ten oxen in France for every one in China. This comparative-material method — independent civilizational systems, each shaped by its own local land/population ratio, producing radically non-convergent food regimes rather than converging toward one norm — rhymes with this project's [[divergence-machine]] (variety and proliferation beyond any single legible standard), though Braudel's divergence here is pre-modern and ecological/demographic rather than the specifically modern institutional push-machine WMP names.

## Notable passages

**The catalogue of yesterday's luxuries** (c1) runs from sugar and pepper through flat plates, forks, glass windowpanes, and the still-rare chair — "Indian troops stationed in southern Italy during the Second World War were amazed... there were chairs in all the houses!" — Braudel's clearest demonstration that luxury is defined only against a moving baseline.

**"This was a conflict waged not only by classes, but by civilizations"** (c2) is the unit's most explicit theoretical claim, closing on Rueff's line that "production is the daughter of desire" — Braudel's answer to why luxury matters beyond etiquette: it is a driver of exchange and rivalry, not a footnote to it.

**The peasant who "never ate his best produce"** (c3) — selling wheat, eating millet and maize, taking poultry and lambs to market rather than the table — is the concrete evidence behind Braudel's insistence that elite cookbooks describe no one's actual diet but the elite's.

**Montaigne's seven-dish German inns** (c4) is the anchor fact for "Carnivorous Europe": meat abundance in the fifteenth-sixteenth centuries wasn't confined to princely banquets but reached ordinary travelers and inns.

**Heinrich Müller's 1550 Swabian testimony** (c5) — peasants now eating "almost worse... than day-labourers and valets in the old days" — is the first of several independent witnesses Braudel stitches together into the continent-wide retreat of meat after 1550.

**The Peking missionary's population-pressure explanation** (c6) — "no manure for the fields, no meat on the tables, no horses for battle," "ten oxen in France for every one in China" — is Braudel's most direct statement of material constraint (land, population, "the limits of the possible") determining diet across civilizations rather than culture or taste alone.
