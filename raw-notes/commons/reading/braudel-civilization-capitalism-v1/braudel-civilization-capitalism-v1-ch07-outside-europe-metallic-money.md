# Outside Europe: Early Economies and Metallic Money

The unit surveys how a monetary economy took hold, unevenly and at different depths, in Japan, the Ottoman/Islamic world (with a detour into Persia), India, and China across the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries. In each case coin recruits some functions of older exchange media — rice, cowries, bitter almonds, sea-shells, silk, caixas of copper or iron — without fully displacing them, and Braudel follows the resulting hybrid systems along two axes at once: the internal social depth of monetization (who actually touches coin, and for what) and the external flow of bullion, which he argues terminates less in China, despite its reputation as a silver "suction pump," than in India.

## Themes

**Monetization layers onto older systems rather than erasing them.** In Japan, gold, silver and copper coin "barely touched the masses"; rice remained money enough that "loads of herrings were still exchanged for loads of rice," even as peasants increasingly paid dues in copper on newly cleared land and daimios paid samurai retainers in gold and silver. In India, silver rupees and virtually absent gold mohurs sat above a much wider circulation of copper, bitter almonds (a Persian import functioning as "primitive money"), and, in the south, sea-shell money beneath gold pagodas. In China, caixas of copper and lead, strung on cords, handled daily life while silver ingots — cut with scissors and weighed on pocket scales — handled large transactions; rice, salt, grain and silk had all at various points been serious monetary competitors to coin.

**Bullion moves along a long chain, and Braudel disputes where it pools.** The Ottoman Empire drew Western coin to Istanbul only to melt and re-mint much of it as larins bound for Persia and Indian rupees; Persian merchants by Tavernier's time preferred Spanish reals, smuggled onward toward India. A 1695 traveller's account pictures "all the gold and silver which circulates in the world" converging on the Great Mogul "as if to its centre," via Ottoman, Persian and Red Sea routes. Manila-galleon silver did flood into China through Chinese junks eager for Mexican reals ("plata sa sangre," 'silver is blood'), but Braudel explicitly rejects the common historians' description of China as a silver "suction pump," arguing from the sheer purchasing power of a single piece of eight that China, unlike India, was never inundated with the world's bullion.

**Monetary policy is an instrument of extraction and control, not a neutral medium.** Ottoman Istanbul fixed commodity prices and customs duties by price list; Mogul princes profited by requiring recent recoinage, so that "local money was at a premium"; the Chinese imperial government owned all copper mines and deliberately released silver or copper to rebalance their exchange rate, in the Jesuit correspondent's words making "the respective value of silver and money rise and fall"; Japan's shogunate ordered the 1695 devaluation explicitly "in the hope of 'increasing money.'"

**China's central paradox: a precociously abstract currency abandoned for a physically primitive one, yet judged more coherent than India's.** Paper money, Braudel's "stroke of genius," ran from the ninth to the fourteenth century and let China reserve silver for external trade even while exporting it — the noted paradox of a silver exporter settling internal accounts in paper. Ming-era hyperinflation (seventeen paper caixas equal to thirteen copper ones in 1378; a thousand notes for three caixas by 1448) killed it off, leaving China to run on strung copper caixas and hand-weighed, hand-cut silver ingots, a system European observers found at once primitive and remarkably precise. Braudel's closing judgment sets this in relief: China was "more primitive and less sophisticated" monetarily than India, but its system had "much more cohesion and obvious unity," and — unlike India — China was not the world's recipient of coinage from everywhere.

**A resonance worth naming, held loosely.** Japan's monetary world is described as "at least threefold; governmental, merchant and urban the stuff of revolution" — distinct orders of exchange coexisting uneasily within one polity. That rhymes, at a much smaller scale and synchronic rather than millennial, with [[world-machines]]'s picture of coexisting infrastructures whose friction is the real substance of conflict; Braudel's threefold split is a single early-modern state's internal stratification, not a Dawn/Day/Dusk succession, so the fit is partial. Similarly, the recurring state projects to standardize and read currency — Ottoman price lists, Mogul recoinage, China's managed silver/copper ratio — gesture toward [[legibility-as-machine-core]], though here legibility serves fiscal extraction rather than the modern cadastral/timetable apparatus the concept was built to describe. And the sheer persistence of rice, cowrie, bitter-almond and sea-shell moneys beneath and beside the official metallic layer, never fully absorbed into it, has a family resemblance to [[divergence-machine]]/[[capture-resistance]] themes of local media that resist standardization — though Braudel reads this as economic backwardness, not deliberate resistance, and the text does not itself make that normative claim.

## Notable passages

The Japan passage ("The monetary economy blossomed in japan..." through "...forbade them to think, let alone speak, of money") is the clearest single statement of the unit's core method: monetization measured by who is and isn't touched by it, down to a moral prohibition among samurai against even discussing money.

The Levant passage on melted-down coin sent onward to Persia and India, and the 1695 traveller's testimony on the Great Mogul as bullion's "centre," together carry Braudel's argument about the direction of the world's specie flows — the reader should notice these are curated as two distinct but connected passages rather than one, since the intervening material shifts from Ottoman-Persian mechanics to the India-focused traveller's global panorama.

The paper-money passage and the closing comparative verdict bracket the China material: the first names the institutional innovation and its paradox (an exporter of silver settling accounts in paper), the second delivers Braudel's considered ranking of China against India — less sophisticated, more coherent, and not a magnet for the world's coin the way India was.
