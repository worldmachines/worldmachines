# Daily Bread: World Diets and the Wheat System

This unit opens Braudel's Chapter 2, 'Daily Bread.' The scout's label ('Wheat: Cultivation and Yields') fits only the unit's back two-thirds: Braudel first surveys the world's diets in general - why most of humanity ate vegetable food rather than meat, and how diet marks civilization and social status - before narrowing to wheat as one of three 'plants of civilization,' then tracing wheat's Old World geography, its inseparability from companion grains and livestock in a fixed rotation system, and its stubbornly low, only slowly rising, seed-to-harvest yields across early modern Europe.

## Themes

**Diet is a function of population density, not taste, and a marker of civilization.** Any given surface of land feeds far more people under cereal cultivation than under stock-raising, so population pressure alone pushes societies toward vegetable diets; thin populations (Mongols, Nagay Tartars, pre-seventeenth-century Europe) can instead afford meat. Braudel treats food choice as diagnostic of a whole social order - 'Tell me what you eat, and I will tell you who you are' - and stages the point through cross-cultural friction: Christian Muscovites eating bread scorned by nomadic meat-and-milk Tartars, or Han Chinese ts'ai-cuisine set against Mongol and Manchu slab-meat eating in Peking.

**Wheat, rice, and maize are near-permanent structures, not mere crops.** Braudel frames the 'plants of civilization' as choices made once, early, and almost irreversibly - an initial commitment 'on which everything or almost everything would thereafter depend,' producing 'almost ineradicable structures' that organize not just material but 'sometimes his spiritual life.' This is Braudel's material-life determinism at its starkest: a founding agricultural choice, once locked in, sets the boundaries of what a civilization can be for centuries.

**Wheat was never grown alone - it was one term in a locked regional system.** 'Wheat' is misleadingly singular: European fields interleaved it with spelt, millet, barley, rye, and oats, with rice and pulses (chickpeas, beans, peas) filling in as functionally equivalent 'cereals,' sometimes priced identically to wheat itself. Rotation - biennial fallow in the south, triennial wheat/oats/fallow in the north - bound grain to livestock through manure, producing, in Ferdinand Lot's words which Braudel quotes approvingly, 'a complicated system of relationships and habits' that was 'so firmly cemented together that no fissure was possible.' Regional rotation patterns persisted for centuries 'like old writing showing through on a palimpsest' - changing the system meant overturning the whole interlocking apparatus at once, which is why England's shift to forage-crop rotation took roughly a century to spread even after 1750.

**A miniature 'world machine.'** This wheat-rotation-livestock-manure complex - centuries-durable, self-reinforcing, and yielding only grudgingly to change - resonates with [[world-machines]]'s notion of deep, interlocking infrastructure that organizes an era's life and resists localized disruption. The fit is partial: this is a material-agrarian infrastructure rather than an institutional-civilizational one, and its eventual 'revolution' (English forage rotation) is a regional technical shift diffusing province by province rather than a millennium-scale Dawn/Day/Dusk cycle. But the pattern - centuries of lock-in, broken only by slow, uneven diffusion - is a genuine rhyme.

**Yields were the hard ceiling on early modern material life.** The chapter's central empirical claim is that the seed-to-harvest ratio for wheat was usually about 5:1 (4:1 net of the following year's seed) across three centuries, with sharp regional collapse (Poland: 2-4:1 for most sixteenth-seventeenth-century harvests) and only a slow multi-century climb, tracked through Slicher van Bath's four country-groups and four yield-phases (A-D), correlated with population density - the Netherlands/England/Ireland cluster eventually reaching yields above 10:1 while eastern Europe lagged. This is Braudel's 'limits of the possible' rendered as hard data: population, prices, and even the possibility of towns were bounded by a biological ceiling that barely moved for three hundred years.

**A revisionist coda: 'backward' livestock regions may have out-performed 'good' grain land.** Braudel closes by endorsing J. Mulliez's argument that the fallow of livestock-rich regions (Brittany, the bocages, the sea-coasts) was misread as waste by eighteenth-century agronomists, and by historians after them; such land in fact fed its (smaller) population more efficiently than prime cereal country, since grain-growing and grazing were structurally complementary rather than competing land-uses. Braudel appends a personal aside - he would rather have lived in the pastoral Bray than the grain-rich Beauvaisis - as a marker of how far this revises the standard hierarchy of 'advanced' versus 'backward' land.

## Notable passages

The diet-and-civilization passage (c1) states Braudel's opening thesis in miniature: population density determines the vegetable/meat balance, and that balance in turn marks out a civilization's identity.

The 'plants of civilization' passage (c2) is the chapter's structural hinge - the moment Braudel narrows from diet-in-general to the three founding cereals, wheat among them, that he treats as quasi-permanent structures.

The Ferdinand Lot quotation (c3) is Braudel's own compressed statement of the wheat-livestock-manure system's rigidity, and the strongest textual basis for the world-machine resonance above.

The Maurizio quotation (c5) is Braudel's explicit longue duree marker, stating outright that diet is one of history's slowest-moving structures.

The yield-ratio passage (c4) supplies the chapter's key number - roughly five grains harvested per grain sown - that underwrites every claim about population ceilings and famine risk elsewhere in the chapter.

The Mulliez passage (c6) is Braudel's late course-correction, cautioning against reading 'fallow' and 'backward' as self-evident categories, and worth reading alongside his aside about the Bray versus the Beauvaisis.
