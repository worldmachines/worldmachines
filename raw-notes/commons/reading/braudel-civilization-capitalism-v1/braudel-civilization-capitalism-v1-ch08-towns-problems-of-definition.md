# Towns and Cities: Defining, Measuring, and Peopling the Urban World

The unit opens Chapter 8 ("Towns and Cities") with Braudel's transformer metaphor and Marx's line on the town/country antagonism, then moves through four connected arguments: towns can only be defined by a bundle of recurring functions rather than by scale ("Towns: the problem of definition"); any numerical threshold for "town" is arbitrary and produces wildly different urbanization statistics depending on which country's convention is used ("Minimum size, combined weight"); the division of labour between town and country is not a fixed, one-directional relationship but a constantly renegotiated and mutually constitutive one, with towns sometimes preceding rural settlement entirely ("The ever-changing division of labour"); and towns, structurally unable to reproduce their own populations before the nineteenth century, survive only by continuously absorbing newcomers — overwhelmingly the poor, but also skilled elites ("The town and its newcomers: mainly the poor").

## Themes

**A town is defined by function, not by size.** Braudel's opening move is to strip away demographic thresholds and instead list what is structurally inseparable from "town" wherever it appears: division of labour, a market that generalizes food supply for even the humblest resident, a form of coercive power, a place at the centre of communications networks, self-consciousness, and — decisively — position in a hierarchy of other towns, since "a town never exists unaccompanied by other towns." This is the basis for his declaration, against Philip Abrams' criticism, that "a town is always a town" across radically different times and places — not because all towns are alike, but because they all "speak the same basic language" of functions.

**Measuring the urban threshold is an unstable, comparative exercise — a small taste of the legibility problem.** Once Braudel tries to put a number on "town," the exercise collapses into incommensurable national conventions: France's official cutoff is 2,000 inhabitants, Britain's is 5,000, Gascon proposes 600 households for the sixteenth century, and late-medieval Germany granted city status to 3,000 places averaging only 400 people each. The same raw data, sorted by threshold, yields a 25% or a 40% urbanization rate for 1801 Britain. This mirrors — at a much smaller scale and without a state apparatus behind it — the difficulty any classification scheme has in rendering messy social reality countable; it resonates loosely with [[legibility-as-machine-core]], though Braudel's is a historian's retrospective and admittedly failing attempt at commensurability, not a contemporary administrative project imposing one measure by fiat.

**Town and countryside are mutually, not sequentially, created.** Against the assumption that rural settlement necessarily precedes urban growth, Braudel cites Jane Jacobs' claim that Neolithic towns like Jericho and Çatalhöyük preceded their own countrysides, and multiplies New World cases — Buenos Aires (refounded 1580), Morris Birkbeck's blow-by-blow account of an American frontier town being founded before any farms exist around it, and Irkutsk (1652) founded ahead of the country districts that would feed it. He names this outright as the rule of "reciprocity of perspectives": "mutual creation, mutual domination, mutual exploitation." This non-teleological, co-emergent picture of two entities that make each other rather than one preceding or causing the other has a real, if partial, rhyme with [[Configurancy]] — Braudel's version is a concrete socio-economic dialectic rather than Rao's broader relational ontology, and it still resolves into asymmetric domination ("mutual exploitation") in a way pure configurancy would resist, but the refusal of a fixed origin story is genuinely shared.

**The town/country boundary is porous and reversible, not a clean division of labour.** Even where towns hold a legal or craft monopoly, agriculture persists inside city walls (vineyards guarded jealously, pigs kept in Venice until 1746, artisans leaving their trades at harvest time in Flanders, England, and Florence) and industry migrates back out to villages seeking cheaper, unguarded labour. Braudel also notes the relationship runs in both directions over time: "the countryside was the abyss that swallowed up urban capital," as merchant wealth from Venice to Lyons periodically drained back into land purchases. The pattern is organized differently outside western Europe — Russian, Indian, and Chinese villages absorbed most industrial tasks themselves, largely bypassing urban–rural competition — which is one of several places in the unit where Braudel explicitly compares Europe against China, India, Russia, and Japan rather than generalizing from Europe alone, a comparative instinct that echoes (without needing a direct link) WMP's own insistence on tracking one pattern across multiple civilizational instances.

**Towns cannot reproduce themselves; they exist only by continuous, stratified immigration.** Before the nineteenth century, urban mortality exceeded urban births, so every town depended on a constant inflow of newcomers, sorted into two very different streams: a mass of poor labourers recruited from specific regions into specific trades (Savoyards as decorators, Auvergnats as water-carriers, Limousins as masons, and so on down a whole occupational geography), and a smaller stream of "high-quality recruits" — merchants, master craftsmen, mercenaries, professors, doctors, engineers, architects, painters. Paris's underside makes the stakes concrete: some 20,000 deaths a year, 4,000 of them in the poor-house, and 7,000–8,000 abandoned children out of roughly 30,000 births around 1780, carried to the foundling hospital by professional child-carriers who "often" find one dead in the box en route.

## Notable passages

The opening "electric transformers" metaphor and Marx epigraph (c1) set the chapter's stakes before any argument begins: towns are the site where the founding division of labour between field and city both originates and keeps accelerating history.

"A town is always a town" (c2) is Braudel's explicit thesis statement, defended against a named critic (Abrams), and it is the passage that licenses everything comparative that follows — the claim that wildly different towns across Europe, China, and elsewhere share one structural grammar.

The France-2,000-vs-Britain-5,000 passage (c3) is the unit's cleanest demonstration that the whole urbanization debate is a classification artifact: the same population, counted two ways, yields two different percentages.

Birkbeck's frontier-town account (c4) is the unit's most vivid empirical case for towns preceding their countryside — a store, a tavern, a blacksmith, and a schoolmaster arriving before the surrounding land is even farmed.

The Irkutsk/"reciprocity of perspectives" passage (c5) is where Braudel names his own organizing concept outright, generalizing the Siberian and Argentine cases into a rule about mutual town–country creation.

The common-labourers passage (c6) is the unit's most concrete evidence for urban dependency on immigration — a near-taxonomic listing of which French province supplied which trade, down to how the Savoyards organized their own dormitories.
