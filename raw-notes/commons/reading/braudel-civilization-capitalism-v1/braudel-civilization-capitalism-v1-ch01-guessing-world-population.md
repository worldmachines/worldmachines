# Guessing the World's Population, 1300-1800

Opening *Civilization and Capitalism*, Braudel makes population the necessary starting point for any study of material life, then spends this unit reconstructing approximate world population figures for 1300-1800 from wildly uneven regional evidence: usable statistics exist only for Europe and China, while India, Africa, the Americas and Oceania are guessed at through backward extrapolation and contested ratios. From this patchwork he extracts what he calls "the most important and disturbing fact" of the book: population roughly doubled worldwide over the period, a genuinely global synchrony that existing Eurocentric explanations (falling urban mortality, hygiene, younger marriage) cannot account for, and for which he cautiously proposes shared climatic fluctuation as the best available unifying hypothesis.

## Themes

**Population as a first-class historical pointer, not a footnote to it.** Braudel opens by insisting that raw numbers are themselves an index of a society's success or failure, mapping a "differential geography" of crowded and empty regions that has changed little over centuries even as the *rhythm* of growth has changed entirely - from the pre-modern alternation of demographic "ebb and flow" to today's continuous rise. This ebb-and-flow logic is also where he states, in embryonic form, his *limits of the possible*: population "crosses 'critical thresholds'" and, when it outruns a society's capacity to feed itself, is corrected by the "extremely crude" mechanism of epidemic and famine - a structural ceiling rather than a policy failure.

**A world of radically uneven evidence.** Outside Europe and China, Braudel is candid that there is "nothing, or almost nothing" - no India, no non-Chinese Asia, no Oceania beyond a flat two-million guess, nothing certain on America. He stages this starkly through the Rosenblat/Berkeley School dispute over the pre-Conquest Americas: one method (regressive extrapolation from present-day figures) yields 10-15 million, the other (extrapolation from partial Mexican records) an already-revised 25 million for Mexico alone, with wildly different implications for how catastrophic the post-Conquest collapse (disease above all - smallpox, measles, plague, later yellow fever) actually was. Africa's canonical "100 million" figure turns out to trace back to a single unsupported 1696 guess by Gregory King, endlessly repeated thereafter - a small case study in how a number can calcify into false certainty.

**Method: ratios and "order of magnitude," not precision.** Facing historians' discomfort with such thin foundations, Braudel sides with the statisticians' approach: assume that ratios between the world's population masses change only slowly, anchor the calculation in the one relationship solid enough to bear weight - the rough numerical equivalence of Europe and China - and apply coefficients (of four or five) to extrapolate a world total, accepting a wide "zone of possibilities (and errors)" rather than a false single figure.

**The central, disputed finding: population doubled worldwide, and this is not a European story.** Whichever low or high estimate he selects, Braudel finds the same trajectory: world population increased substantially between 1300 and 1800, unstopped in the end by crisis, disaster or "massive mortality." Because the growth appears in China, Russia and the Americas as much as in Europe - places where the standard European explanations (medical advance, falling infant mortality, later marriage) plainly do not apply - he insists the usual Eurocentric account has to be revised or abandoned, reframing the puzzle as one of *simultaneity*: why did demographically unconnected societies grow at the same time?

**Climate as the candidate mechanism, offered as a hypothesis rather than a proof.** Braudel proposes synchronized climatic change - the "little ice age," glacier advance cutting the Viking sea route to Greenland, Chinese droughts and locust plagues occurring alongside French unrest under Louis XIII - as the one explanation capable of operating everywhere at once, prior to and independent of any economic integration between regions. He is careful to hedge this: climate's effects on harvests run through "very devious routes" specific to place and crop, and he explicitly warns against over-simplifying a genuinely complex system.

## Notable passages

- **"Numbers as a first-class pointer"** frames the whole chapter's premise before any statistics arrive: population figures are read as a diagnostic of a society's condition, and the deep shift Braudel wants readers to notice is not the level of population but the change from cyclical "ebb and flow" to modern continuous growth.
- **The admission of near-total data scarcity** ("Nobody knows the total population of the world...") sets the epistemic terms for everything that follows: only Europe and China offer "reasonably safe" ground.
- **The Europe-China equivalence** is named directly as "probably one of the most visible structures in world history over the last five or six centuries" - the single load-bearing ratio the whole world estimate is built on.
- **The doubling of world population, 1300-1800**, is Braudel's headline claim, stated as a fact that survived "economic crises, disasters nor massive mortality."
- **The climatic-coherence passage** proposes that shared climate, not trade or conquest, may have unified the globe's biological history "long before the voyages of discovery, the industrial revolution or the interpenetration of economies."
- **"The simultaneity is the problem"** is Braudel's own summary of why space, colonization and economic explanations fail on their own: they explain *where* growth could happen, not *why* it happened everywhere at once.

Two loose resonances with World Machines vocabulary, both partial: the way Braudel's population curve is assembled - independent, mutually distrustful regional estimates (Rosenblat vs. Berkeley School, Beloch vs. Mombert, successive revisions as new documents surface) that only cohere into a usable global picture once triangulated - rhymes with the [[history-machine]] pattern of a paradigm assembling silently and distributedly from scattered components until it becomes visible, though here the "paradigm" is a statistical estimate rather than a historiographic shift. And the chapter's search for a deep, non-institutional substrate beneath visible events - climate and demography operating as a kind of prior infrastructure to economy and politics - echoes the [[world-machines]] idea of deep infrastructure running beneath an era's visible institutions, though Braudel's substrate is natural/biological rather than technological or institutional, and he treats it as hypothesis rather than settled structure.
