# Money and Credit as a Universal Human Technique

This unit is Braudel's discussion of paper money and instruments of credit — bills of exchange, bank notes, scriptural (book-keeping) money — circulating alongside metallic coin. It argues two things at once: first, that credit instruments are ancient and nearly universal, attested in Babylon, Greece, Rome, the Islamic world, and China long before their post-medieval 'rediscovery' in the West; and second, that money and credit resist a clean definitional boundary, so much so that economists can plausibly claim either that 'everything is money' or that 'everything is credit.' Braudel traces the halting, multi-centered emergence of the bank note (goldsmiths' notes, the Casa di San Giorgio, the Bank of Stockholm, the Bank of England), the mingling of paper, bonds, and IOUs in everyday circulation, and closes by treating money and credit as a 'language' every society is forced to speak — one that unified the world only into a hierarchy of favoured and handicapped economies.

## Themes

**Credit is definitionally simple — a promise deferred in time — but its everyday forms long predate paper money.** Braudel opens with a spare definition ('I will do something for you, you will pay me later') and then a run of concrete, pre-monetary examples — a lord advancing seed-corn against the harvest, a tavern-keeper's chalk marks, a baker's notched tally-stick, merchants buying standing corn before the sheep were shorn — establishing credit as older and more 'natural' than the paper and book-keeping devices that later scandalized observers.

**The 'old practices' section is explicit world-scale comparison, and its point is corrective.** Braudel traces notes and cheques to Babylon twenty centuries before Christ, then to Hellenistic Egypt (Alexandria as 'the most popular centre of international transit'), Rome's argentarii, the Islamic merchant world (documented in the Cairo geniza), and China, which 'was using bank notes by the ninth century AD' — centuries before the West's thirteenth-century 'rediscovery' of the bill of exchange via the Crusades. The move parallels the corrective Braudel makes elsewhere in the book against 'Western nationalism' (e.g. over gunpowder): financial technique, like military technique, is not a Western invention but a recurrently rediscovered inheritance.

**Each generation greets paper money as a scandal or a miracle, which Braudel treats as a recurring cultural reflex rather than evidence of genuine novelty.** The Italian who set up 'with a table and an inkstand' in Lyons around 1555 and made a fortune is remembered as 'an absolute scandal'; David Hume in 1752 wanted to suppress England's paper currency outright; Sébastien Mercier wished Paris were 'modelled on the Bank of London' instead of watching porters haul cash bags through the Rue Vivienne. Braudel's antiquity-tracing 'old practices' history is implicitly an answer to this recurring amazement: nothing here is really new.

**No single origin point for the bank note — Braudel deliberately multiplies candidates.** Stockholm (1661, suspended 1668), the Bank of England (1694), English goldsmiths' notes (common from the 1650s, one goldsmith alone circulating £1,200,000 by 1666), the Casa di San Giorgio's biglietti (from 1586), Venice's banks di scritta (from the fifteenth century) — the bank note 'was born almost spontaneously from commercial usage,' out of urgent necessity (Charles I's 1640 seizure of merchants' bullion). The Bank of England's real innovation, per Law, was issuing credit in notes that 'far exceeded actual deposits,' which 'increased the quantity of money.'

**Chronic metal shortage is the mechanism that forces the whole system into being — and Braudel reaches for a machine metaphor of his own to describe it.** Metal money is 'slow to fulfil its functions or absent altogether... it had to be pushed into circulation or somehow replaced'; the resulting 'artificial manufacture' of money is what John Law and other bank promoters grasped as a discovery 'better than the alchemists.' Braudel's own image for this is mechanical: it was 'the slow pace of the heavy metal money, its failure... to keep the engine running, that created the necessary profession of banker'; the banker is 'the man who repaired or tried to repair the mechanical breakdown.' Money and credit, in this passage, are quite literally an engine that periodically fails and needs a repairman.

**The unresolved question — is everything money, or is everything credit? — is where Braudel locates 'the problem of modern capitalism.'** He frames the definitional debate between metal specie, substitute money, and instruments of credit as the terrain on which capitalism 'first flexed its muscles, found its instruments and in seeking to define those instruments became \u2018conscious of its own existence\u2019' — an explicit statement of his three-storey model (material life / market economy / capitalism), with credit and paper money as the market economy's technical vocabulary shading directly into capitalism proper. He stages both sides without resolving them: the pre-1760 'nominalist' mercantilist view that money is itself wealth, mass and momentum accelerating exchange (Cantillon's 'general reconciliation,' in which barter, substitute money, and credit are all 'pseudo-money — or even real money'); and the Schumpeterian view that money is only ever 'a credit instrument, a claim to the only final means of payment, the consumer's good.' Braudel's verdict: 'the brief can legitimately be argued either way.'

**The unit's most ambitious move is to describe money and credit as a self-elaborating universal technique — a claim that rhymes with, but is broader than, this project's own infrastructure vocabulary.** 'Like ocean navigation or printing, money and credit are techniques... a single language, which every society speaks after its fashion, and which every individual is obliged to learn' — unlike literacy, innumeracy is fatal to survival, not merely to 'high culture.' Crucially, Braudel says these techniques 'operate principally upon themselves, come into being of their own accord and are transformed by their own progress': the bill of exchange appears in the West in the twelfth century because money needed transporting across the Mediterranean to the fairs of Champagne; further instruments (endorsement, banks, discounting) appear once fixed fair-dates can no longer keep pace with an accelerating economy. This self-propelling, demand-driven elaboration of technique — visible in aggregate only after the fact — has a loose kinship with [[history-machine]]'s account of infrastructure assembling silently from distributed pressures until it 'lurches into visibility,' though Braudel's subject is a near-universal human technique present in every society rather than an era-bound historiographical paradigm. It also resonates with [[world-machines]]' notion of deep infrastructure — institutions, technologies, and assumptions that 'determine human life... century to century' — except that Braudel's money-and-credit 'language' is explicitly not confined to one historical era or one machine's Dawn-Day-Dusk arc; it is, in his telling, a permanent feature of any society 'with a claim to development,' which sits awkwardly with WMP's era-specific machines even as the infrastructural, self-organizing character of the claim rhymes strongly.

**Money's world-scale reach produces hierarchy, not equality — the unit ends on this note.** 'Money gave a certain unity to the world, but it was the unity of injustice': developed economies acquire wider ranges of monetary instruments, and 'each society had its place, some favoured, some backward, some heavily handicapped.' Braudel illustrates with an eighteenth-century Marseilles merchant unable to find silver or discounting facilities in a just-occupied Crimea, and closes with two epigrams bridging four centuries: Van Ouder Meulen (1778) on nations destined to become powerful or destitute, and Scipion de Gramont (1620, via 'the seven sages of Greece') that 'he who has none [money] wanders dead among the living.'

## Notable passages

The definition-and-examples passage (c1) establishes credit as prior to and independent of paper money — the conceptual anchor for everything that follows. The Babylon-to-China passage (c2) is Braudel's explicit world-scale correction against treating credit instruments as a Western invention. The 'mechanical breakdown' passage (c3) is the unit's most quotable image, casting the banker as an engineer called into being by metal money's chronic failure — notable for its literal machine language. The Schumpeter passage (c4) crystallizes the unresolved 'everything is credit' position that Braudel sets against the mercantilist 'everything is money' view, and names the debate as 'the problem of modern capitalism.' The money-as-language passage (c5) is the unit's broadest theoretical claim and the strongest, if qualified, resonance with this project's infrastructure vocabulary. The closing hierarchy-and-injustice passage (c6) reframes the whole unit's world-scale comparisons as evidence of structural inequality rather than neutral diffusion.
