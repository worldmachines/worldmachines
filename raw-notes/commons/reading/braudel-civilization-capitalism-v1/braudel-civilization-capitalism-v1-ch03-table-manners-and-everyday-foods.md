# Table Manners and the World Trade in Everyday Foods

The unit opens with the extravagance of the post-fifteenth-century European elite table — princely gluttony, adulterated meat sold down the social scale, and the grand houses' left-overs feeding a quarter of a town — before turning to how a table was physically laid: the halting, centuries-long spread of individual forks, glasses, plates, and napkins, cross-checked against depictions of the Last Supper in Western painting. It then pivots from luxury to necessity, surveying five everyday-food commodities at world scale — salt, dairy products and eggs, seafood (climaxing in the Newfoundland cod trade), pepper and spices, and finally sugar — tracking how each moved between subsistence, regional trade, and, in sugar's case, full plantation capitalism.

## Themes

**Elite luxury and stratified consumption.** The chapter's opening vignettes — a thousand-ecu turtle, a wild boar 'larded with foie gras' and barely tasted by its guests, hazel grouse and ortolans by the thousand at a princess's wedding — are framed by Braudel as 'the whims of princes,' but the same passage insists on the underside: caterers kept the best meat for the rich and sold the poor 'the worst,' padded with bones "ironically called *rejouissances*," while a quarter of a town lived off palace left-overs sold at the *regrat*. This is material life read as social stratification, not just abundance.

**The longue durée of table manners.** Braudel treats the fork, the individual plate, and the personal wineglass as tracers of how slowly custom changes even among the wealthy: the fork spread from Venice from the sixteenth century, was still refused by Montaigne (who bit his fingers eating too fast), mocked as 'diabolical' by a German preacher, and was still being forbidden to Louis XIV's own nephews at court — the Princess Palatine boasts of eating with 'her knife and fingers' her whole life. English inventories show no table forks before 1660, and general use only arrives around 1750. This is Braudel's *longue durée* made visible at the scale of a place setting: a technology available in Italy in the 1500s takes roughly two centuries to become customary among the French and English elite, and wooden plates persist in the German countryside into the nineteenth century.

**Painting as evidence for material life.** Braudel reads Last Supper paintings as a dataset, not devotional objects: 'no fork appears before 1600 and almost no spoons either' in depictions of Christ's table, which instead show trenchers of bread, wooden or pewter discs, and a single oversized knife shared among guests — Jacopo Bassano's 1599 Last Supper is flagged as one of the first to show a fork at all. It is a characteristic Braudelian move: iconography interrogated as an archive of everyday material culture rather than doctrine.

**World-scale comparison as method.** Braudel's own framing dominates the second half of the unit: salt was "a major source of income to states and merchants, in Europe and China alike"; the Chinese and Japanese categorically reject dairy while it is central to Islamic, Indian, and European diets; Europe eats eggs constantly while "the vegetarian Far East" barely touches them; only Japan among Asian societies fishes at a scale comparable to Europe. Pepper's own history is explicitly comparative — "Islam, China and India shared the taste" for spice as an antidote to dietary monotony, with a Hindu writer quoted on rice's 'insipidness.' This comparative sweep across Europe, China, Japan, India, Islam, and the Americas is the same world-scale method the project's own context names as central to Braudel's approach.

**The limits of the possible, unevenly exploited.** Fish supply shows that proximity to resources does not guarantee their use: the New World's Caribbean shoals, Newfoundland's banks, and Canadian salmon rivers went largely unfished by the societies living near them, while distant European fleets crossed an ocean to exploit Newfoundland's 'miracle' of self-renewing cod. Technology and custom, not raw abundance, set the *ceiling* on what a given material-life regime could actually draw from its environment — Braudel's 'limits of the possible' operating as a cultural and technical constraint rather than a purely natural one.

**From material life to market economy to capitalism, inside one chapter.** The unit is a compact demonstration of Braudel's three-storey house. Diet and manners (what people ate, how they held a fork) are the near-immobile floor of material life. Regional trade in cheese, milk, and herring — Auvergne cheese, Gournay butter, Dutch dairy 'invading the markets of Europe' — is the market economy: organized, price-sensitive, but still local or continental in scale. Sugar is where the argument climbs to the third storey: a plant grown for medicine in Sassanid Persia becomes, via Cyprus, Madeira, and Brazil, a plantation monoculture requiring capital, credit, refining industries concentrated in a handful of ports (Antwerp, Amsterdam, London), and — explicitly — "a large labour force (in America the black slaves)." Sugar's transformation from an apothecary's rarity into a commodity people "devour... out of gluttony" is capitalism's reorganization of material life, and it is inseparable here from chattel slavery: sugar cane's monoculture left colonies unable to feed themselves, requiring imported cod and salt beef just to keep the plantation system running. This is a stark historical anchor for what [[capture-resistance]] and [[Conatus]] name in the abstract — a capture regime (slavery) suppressing the enslaved's conatus wholesale to feed a new world commodity chain — though the register is different: WMP's framework describes modern informational and institutional capture, not the direct physical bondage Braudel is documenting; the rhyme is in the structural logic of extraction-through-suppression, not the mechanism. The plantation's rollers, quintal weights, charter-parties, and bottomry loans are also an early, partial instance of what [[legibility-as-machine-core]] names — standardizing and accounting for a commodity at scale — well before the Modernity Machine's stated 1600 'Day,' since Braudel's own account has this apparatus assembling gradually from the 1520s Brazilian sugar boom onward.

## Notable passages

The turtle-and-wild-boar vignette (c1) opens the chapter on elite extravagance and ends on Braudel's own dry judgment, 'These were the whims of princes.' The fork-and-manners passage (c2) is the unit's clearest *longue durée* case study, tracking one utensil's two-century crawl from Venetian novelty to French and English norm through named eyewitnesses (Montaigne, the Princess Palatine, Thomas Coryate). The Last Supper passage (c3) — deliberately anchored just after an interleaved figure caption, so the quote doesn't straddle it — states Braudel's method of reading paintings as material-culture evidence. The salt passage (c4) shows trade indifferent to the confessional and political map, moving 'regardless of wars' from Catholic salt-pans to Protestant fishing fleets. The pepper passage (c5) gives Braudel's explicit world-scale comparison (Islam, China, India) and the Hindu writer's line on spice curing dietary monotony. The Ortelius quotation on sugar (c6) is the chapter's sharpest single line on capitalism remaking material life: 'What used to be a medicine is nowadays eaten as a food.'
