# The Energy Budget of Pre-Industrial Europe: Wind, Water, Wood, and Coal

This unit is not confined to windmills and water-wheels, as the scout's label suggests — it is Braudel's full inventory of the energy sources available to Europe before the eighteenth century: watermills and windmills, sailing ships, wood and charcoal, and finally coal, closing with a quantified attempt to rank them by horse-power. The throughline is that pre-industrial Europe possessed real mechanical ingenuity — the vertical water-wheel, the tower mill, multi-implement gearing — but remained chronically starved of transportable energy, and that starvation, not a lack of invention, was the binding constraint on growth until steam.

## Themes

**The "first mechanical revolution" was a slow accretion, not a rupture.** Braudel opens by downgrading the eleventh-to-thirteenth-century mill boom from a single revolutionary event to "a whole series of slow changes" in the number of wind- and watermills, each producing only two to ten horse-power. He treats the Roman shift from horizontal to vertical water-wheels (first century BC) as the real stroke of genius, later generalized across Europe by the twelfth and thirteenth centuries and extended from grinding grain to fulling cloth, forging iron, sawing wood, and pumping mines. The mill becomes, in his phrase, "a sort of standard measure of the energy supply in pre-industrial Europe," and he cross-checks estimates (Domesday Book, Galicia, Poland) against Lazlo Makkai's calculation of 500,000–600,000 mills across Europe on the eve of the industrial revolution.

**Windmills diffused later and more unevenly than watermills, following a real geography.** Braudel traces windmills to Iran or Tibet (seventh–ninth century AD), their spread via Islam, and their conversion to a vertical-sail form in Christendom from the twelfth century — a diffusion so regionally staggered that windmills reached Don Quixote's La Mancha late enough to still look like monsters, while the Dutch turned the *Wipmolen* into drainage infrastructure for reclaiming land from the sea. This is Braudel's world-scale comparative habit in miniature: China's mills stayed horizontal "for centuries" while the West's vertical conversion is treated as the technically decisive break.

**Wood, not coal, defined the material civilization of pre-eighteenth-century Europe.** The chapter's most quotable line — "Civilizations before the eighteenth century were civilizations of wood and charcoal, as those of the nineteenth were civilizations of coal" — anchors a long account of wood as structural material (buildings, ships, tools, gears, even wooden clocks) and as fuel, alongside the forest depletion this caused: shrinking French royal forests, Swedish deforestation of Pomerania during the Thirty Years War, iron-works that had to shut down for want of charcoal. Braudel insists forest wealth had no value except as it was drawn into an economy of woodcutters, charcoal-burners and carters — "worth nothing unless it was used" — a material-life argument about how a resource only becomes economic infrastructure once a labor system metabolizes it.

**Coal's arrival was technically early but socially delayed, in both England and China.** Coal and even coke were known in Europe and China centuries before their systematic industrial use; coke succeeded first not in ironworks but in Derbyshire brewing (giving Derby beer its "paleness and sweetness"), and only reached metallurgy around 1780. China's case is more pointed still: Braudel notes coal and cast iron were known there from the fifth century BC, yet the "tremendous precocity" never converted into a coke-based industrial breakthrough even at China's thirteenth-century peak. His verdict — "Technology is only an instrument and man does not always know how to use it" — turns the coal story into an argument about social and institutional readiness, not raw technical possibility.

**The closing arithmetic makes the "limits of the possible" a literal energy budget.** Braudel ranks pre-industrial Europe's power sources — animal traction first (~10 million horse-power), then wood (4–5 million), then water-wheels (1.5–3 million), then manpower, then sails (at most 233,000) — and concludes that "lack of energy was the major handicap of ancien regime economies," not for want of machinery (which "existed and was constantly being perfected") but because energy could not yet be transported to where it was needed. This is Braudel's material-life argument in its purest quantitative form: the ceiling on pre-industrial growth was physical and infrastructural before it was ever a matter of ideas, closing with his borrowed formula (from Pierre Léon) that "first came evolution (a slow rise) and then revolution (an acceleration): two connected movements."

## Notable passages

The opening paragraph frames the whole unit's low-key thesis about incremental mechanization rather than sudden revolution. The wood/coal "civilizations" line is the chapter's most durable formulation and the one most likely to be cited elsewhere. The Derbyshire coke-for-beer passage is a concrete, almost comic illustration of how new energy technologies diffuse through unpredictable, non-obvious channels before reaching their "natural" industrial application. The China passage is Braudel's most explicit statement of the puzzle his whole comparative project is built around — why technical precocity did not produce an industrial revolution outside Europe — stated here as bluntly as anywhere in the volume. The closing horse-power ranking and its verdict on transportable energy are the unit's analytical payoff, converting all the preceding narrative into a single comparative table. The sail tonnage passage (Europe's merchant fleet reportedly quintupling from ~650,000 to 3,372,029 tons between 1600 and 1786) is included as an example of Braudel's characteristic move of anchoring an argument in a shaky but suggestive statistic, flagged in his own text as "a figure to be treated with the usual caution."

The unit's insistence that these energy infrastructures — mills, forests, coal basins, ports — are the physical substrate beneath institutions and practices resonates with [[world-machines]]'s notion of a machine as deep, interlocking infrastructure organizing life across centuries, though Braudel's register here is material and energetic rather than institutional or ideological. The closing "evolution, then revolution" formula — decades of unremarked, distributed technical accumulation (gear-wheels, jacks, transmission belts) that only later crystallizes into a visible industrial revolution — also rhymes with [[history-machine]]'s picture of a paradigm assembling silently from independent components until it lurches into visibility, though Braudel's slow-burn account has no single Lohse-style lurch moment; the acceleration he describes is itself gradual, dated loosely to the 1730s–40s rather than a sharp rupture.
