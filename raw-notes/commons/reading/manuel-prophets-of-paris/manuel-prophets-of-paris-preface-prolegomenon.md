# Preface and Prolegomenon: The Prophets of Paris

Manuel opens with a methodological preface defending his 'history of ideas' as portraiture rather than the tracing of 'disembodied moral and philosophical traditions' — he insists on introducing 'the bearer of the idea even when he disturbs the flow of abstractions,' combining biography with intellectual history and confessing a taste for psychological and sociological determinants. The Prolegomenon proper then makes the book's real argument: that Turgot, Condorcet, Saint-Simon, Fourier, and the Saint-Simonians/Comte, though scattered across a century and impossible to file under any single discipline (philosophe, mathematician, system-builder, founder of sociology), constitute a coherent lineage of Parisian 'prophets' bound by discipleship, rivalry, and a shared psychological and intellectual profile.

## Themes

**Prophecy as a category that supersedes discipline.** Manuel explicitly rejects labeling these men by their professional titles — Condorcet 'the mathematician and Girondist politician is subordinated to the visionary of a utopia of science,' and Comte 'would have bridled at the idea that he was a "sociologist."' The unifying frame is instead the Hebrew prophetic tradition: moral teachers 'sparing neither kings nor commoners' and 'revealers of a divine historical design.' This is a genealogical claim as much as a rhetorical one — Condorcet as Turgot's 'chevalier,' Comte's Judas-like betrayal of Saint-Simon, the running blood feuds between Fourierists and Saint-Simonians for 'the alienated souls of Europe and America.'

**Crisis-consciousness as the genre's engine.** 'They were all crisis philosophers,' each rehearsing the whole of human history to prove that 'the moment of their speech was the instant of the great divide.' Manuel is careful to note the anachronistic familiarity of this rhetoric to a modern reader — its tone has since become 'the inevitable hackneyed accompaniment of even the most casual social or political utterance' — while asking us to recover its original 'poignancy.'

**Future-intoxication and the devaluation of the present.** The prophets 'were intoxicated with the future,' treating the present as an 'unendurable' burden to be destroyed 'as fast as possible' to hasten the coming order. Manuel glosses this with Montaigne's 'frantic curiosity' and Pascal's 'we never live; but we hope to live,' framing the prophets as permanently adolescent, forever 'panting to grow up.' Their self-conception as men 'born too soon' — Moses figures who may only glimpse the promised land, 'heralding the future, inaugurating the dawn of real humanity' — casts them as inhabitants of an interregnum: the old political and moral order already discredited, the new religious-social order not yet deliverable. This is a period consciousness that resonates strongly with the [[gramsci-gap]] — legitimacy exhausted, birth withheld, only 'morbid symptoms' and prophecy available in the meantime.

**A deliberate turn away from the state.** Sociologically, Manuel identifies a shared 'deflection of their primary concern from the state and the definition of power to social relationships and religion.' Amid revolutions and constitutional churn, the prophets treated the state as 'a scaffolding, a tool, a superstructure, a covering — not the heart of the human condition,' something to be 'circumvented' rather than seized, in favor of excavating 'the core of human nature' directly. None of the five, in Manuel's reckoning, ever regarded man as 'primarily a political animal' — a striking anti-statist premise for thinkers usually read as architects of new social orders.

**Pathology and genius held together.** Manuel does not flinch from calling the group 'schizophrenic, megalomaniac, and paranoid,' comparing their symptoms to 'ordinary asylum patients,' even while insisting this diagnosis coexists with 'a touch of genius' and genuine closeness 'to humanity and its pain.' The book is explicitly framed as 'a frank attempt at rehabilitation' against a posterity that dismissed Saint-Simon as a crackpot and neglected Fourier ('the greatest and the most neglected').

**Unresolved forecasting: which prophet was right?** The Prolegomenon closes by refusing to adjudicate among the prophets' rival futures — Fourier's world of 'endless delights' versus Comte's 'ordered, successfully repressed or sublimated society' — suggesting 'their day may pass' and that the present moment is itself 'only a prolegomenon.' This branching, undecided posture toward long-run outcomes, paired with the book's insistently oracular subjects, gives the unit an odd kinship with the forecasting mindset the [[world-machines]] project takes toward contested futures, though Manuel offers no resolving mechanism of his own.

## Notable passages

The five curated passages trace this arc: the definition of 'prophet' as a category that absorbs and outranks professional title; the crisp statement of crisis-philosophy as method; the Montaigne/Pascal-glossed account of future-intoxication; the anti-statist sociological thesis; and the closing chorus of prophetic reassurance (Saint-Simon's 'golden age,' Fourier's exhortation amid 'the Napoleonic slaughter,' Enfantin's 1832 address to unbelievers), which Manuel undercuts with a final, skeptical aside that the 'thunderers who reveled in the spectacle of death and destruction may have known the hidden nature of man better than these sons of la douce France.'
