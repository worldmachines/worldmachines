# Fourier: Life of a Salesman, Death to Philosophy

This unit opens Manuel's treatment of Charles Fourier (1772–1837) with a biographical sketch — "The Life of a Salesman" — that reads his obsessive personality, his commercial career, and his chance encounters (a fourteen-sou apple, a shipment of hoarded grain) as the raw material out of which the entire system of passionate attraction and the phalanstery was spun; it then turns to "Death to Philosophy and Its Civilization," laying out Fourier's foundational method of *ecart absolu* — total, deliberate rupture from every prior philosophical and moralist tradition — and his resulting verdict that civilization cannot be reformed, only abolished.

## Themes

**The autodidact as system-builder.** Fourier read almost nothing after a conventional classical education; his doctrine was assembled instead from introspection into his own desires, from newspapers, and from the talk of fellow travelers, restaurant scandalmongers, and shopkeepers. Manuel treats this as methodologically load-bearing, not incidental: Fourier's claim to authority rests on direct acquaintance with "the infamies of commerce" rather than treatises on political economy, making him a strange mirror of the Enlightenment savants he despised — a system-builder who scorned system-building's traditional epistemic base.

**Obsession as generative, not merely pathological.** Manuel resists the easy diagnosis of madness, cataloguing Fourier's compulsive measuring, cataloguing, and hoarding of trivial facts (a small hotel's proportions becoming the architectural template for a phalanstery) as the same faculty that produced his "infinitely detailed" system. The chapter's psychoanalytic undertone — Fourier's phalanstery as a "type-case" sublimation of unappeased personal need, embracing "the totality of mankind" because he had "read himself into the whole keyboard of psychological types" — frames the system's exhaustive inclusiveness (every one of 810 psychological combinations, sixty-odd categories of adultery) as compensation rather than decoration.

**Ecart absolu: rupture instead of reform.** Fourier's own methodological statement — that he undertook "to stand in constant opposition to" the established sciences precisely because millennia of moral and philosophical accumulation had produced no useful invention — is the doctrine's hinge. Manuel is explicit that this is not one Enlightenment critique among others but a rejection of the entire premise of gradual amelioration: civilization is "a prison," and where the philosophes sought to improve conditions inside it, Fourier meant to break the bars. This positions Fourier's radicalism as categorically different from Condorcet's or Saint-Simon's melioristic progress-narratives, even though all three shared a millenarian confidence in imminent transformation.

**Passions as fixed, repression as the variable of history.** Against natural-law moralists who prescribed what men *ought* to desire, Fourier insisted the passions were constant across all historical epochs and that civilization's whole history is a study in their varying repression. This inverts the reformist logic: since the passions cannot and should not be moderated (moderation is, in Fourier's vocabulary, "a gross, pejorative word"), the only honest project is to build social machinery — the phalanstery, with its combinatorial completeness — capacious enough to gratify every psychological type rather than discipline any of them. Read against `[[Conatus]]`, Fourier's insistence that passions are drives "which God has bestowed on us as our fundamental drives" and must be given "absolute free expression" is a strikingly literal precursor to the idea that capture regimes function by suppressing conatus through averaging and moral standardization — Fourier names family, marriage, and commercial civilization themselves as the repressive apparatus.

**Rivalry with Saint-Simon as a fight over convergence versus proliferation.** Manuel's account of Fourier's furious polemics against the Saint-Simonians turns on a real doctrinal fork: the Saint-Simonians pursued a *moral* revolution — remaking human nature toward obedience and hierarchical love — while Fourier insisted on taking human nature exactly as given, in all its variety of passion, and engineering a social combinatorics rich enough to absorb every deviation without correction. This is legible as an early instance of the `[[divergence-machine]]` logic avant la lettre: rather than making desire legible and governable, Fourier's phalanstery treats maximal variety of passionate expression as the design goal, explicitly refusing the standardizing, hierarchy-imposing move he accuses the Saint-Simonians of making.

**Utopia as irrefutable by design.** Manuel notes, via Burke, that there is no way to refute a utopia: every empirical objection to the phalanstery's feasibility is met by redirecting attention to the undeniable miseries of existing civilization. This structural move — indict the present rather than defend the blueprint — explains both Fourier's polemical energy and his followers' inability to ever test the system "precisely as Fourier prescribed."

## Notable passages

The apple-price episode (Fevrier's Restaurant, fourteen sous) is Manuel's chosen origin myth for the whole system, deliberately paralleled with Newton's apple to dramatize Fourier's self-mythologizing as a discoverer of a physical law of passionate attraction equal in stature to the law of gravitation.

The *ecart absolu* passage — Fourier's declaration that he set out "to stand in constant opposition to" the sciences that had never once relieved poverty — is the unit's methodological keystone, later explicitly linked by Manuel to Nietzsche and Rimbaud as a recurring gesture of total philosophical rupture.

The Venus/Praxiteles outburst ("I'd rather break the arms of all the philosophes / than those of my Venus") captures Fourier's refusal to trade away the erotic core of harmony for philosophical respectability — love, not labor, was for Fourier civilization's central deformation.

The declaration that "civilized society was a prison" and that philosophy's project of amelioration was therefore worthless captures the unit's clearest statement of Fourier's absolutism against reform, distinguishing him sharply from the progress-oriented prophets treated elsewhere in Manuel's book.

The closing indictment of civilization as "a reign of violence and cunning" sustained only by executioners and prisons crystallizes Fourier's view that civilized order is coercion in disguise — a claim Manuel connects, via the instinctual-repression reading of class conflict, to ideas Freud would only gesture at decades later.
