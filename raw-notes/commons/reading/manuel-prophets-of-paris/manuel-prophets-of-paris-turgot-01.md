# Turgot: The Architect of the Idea of Progress

Manuel opens his study of the "prophets of Paris" with Turgot's two 1750 Sorbonne orations, arguing that this reluctant, administratively-minded abbe-turned-minister — not Voltaire, not Rousseau — produced the first fully modern version of the ideology of progress, and that his scattered fragments, encyclopedia articles, and reported conversations, though never assembled into the universal history he talked of writing, cohere into a genuine system: a sensationalist psychology of the innovating mind, a theory of genius as history's engine, a theory of language as the vessel and record of advancing thought, an ethnological proof drawn from the "savage" world, and a taxonomy of four unevenly-developing progressions.

## Themes

**A prophet in spite of himself.** Turgot is an unlikely founder of a prophetic tradition — a shy, orderly royal minister, "worlds away from the spluttering prophets of London," who delivered his founding texts as a nominal eulogy of Christianity before the Sorbonne's Faculty of Theology. Manuel makes much of the irony that the church's own celebrants, defending Christianity by cataloguing its historical utility, thereby "robbed Christianity of transcendence" and helped birth a rival, earthly religion of progress on ecclesiastical ground. Turgot's later career as Louis XVI's reforming minister — the six edicts of 1776, the guerre des farines, his eventual fall — is presented as his philosophy of history acted out in the world: an assault on the "dead weight of the past" (feudal guilds, mortmain, frozen endowments) in the name of perpetual innovation.

**Novelty against the rut of sameness.** Building on Locke-Condillac sensationalism, Turgot posits an almost-innate human drive to combine sensations into novelty, opposed by an institutional "spirit of routine" that seeks to "stall man in the rut of sameness." World history becomes "a war eternal between these polar principles," a secularized version of the war between good and evil. Unlike Montesquieu's equilibrium-seeking mechanistic polity, Turgot wants maximal openness to change; his illustrative foil is the frozen, over-scientized but liberty-starved mandarinate of China, and his general suspicion extends to any sect, academy, or corporate body that ossifies once it gains power.

**Genius as the dynamic agent of history.** Progress requires a human intermediary who can perceive and articulate the novel: the genius. Turgot treats genius as a near-mechanical constant — "spread among mankind like gold in a mine" — so that population growth mechanically increases the yield of geniuses, and the central task of "the good society" becomes maximizing genius's actualization rather than wasting it. Manuel traces a direct line from this idea to the elaborate genius-subsidy schemes of Condorcet and Saint-Simon.

**Language as vessel and record.** Genius's perceptions survive only if an adequate language exists to contain them; political catastrophe (barbarian conquest, the "Babel" following Rome's fall) can silence genius for centuries by degrading its linguistic vessel. Language is also itself a fossil record of mental development, moving from grunt to image to sentence to the ideal endpoint of the unambiguous mathematical symbol. Here Manuel flags Turgot's embryonic anticipation of Comte's law of three states — theological, metaphysical, positive — decades before Comte systematized it.

**Ethnology as living proof of stadial history.** Contemporary "barbaric" or "savage" peoples are read not as different cultures but as frozen earlier stages of the one universal path every nation traverses at uneven pace — "all nations start from the same point, proceed to the same goal... but at a very uneven pace." Political geography, given a time dimension, becomes universal history: isolated societies form local constellations of progress that expand and eventually collide into one coalescing, uniform world.

**The anatomy of four unequal progressions.** Turgot's law of unequal development separates technology (cumulative, durable, advancing at an even tempo because artisanal knowledge survives political collapse), speculative science (the coming "vanguard of the battalions of progress"), the fine arts (non-cumulative, bounded by fixed human sensibility, peaking irrecoverably in the Augustan age), and moral behavior (progressive toward Stoic-Christian ideals of gentleness and utility). This typology, and its central term "les progres" in the plural, passes directly into Condorcet's Esquisse.

## Notable passages

The founding-moment passage on the Sorboniques frames Turgot's discourses as literally inventing "the first important version in modern times of the ideology of progress" — the seed of what World Machines calls the Modernity Machine's shared telos of Progress, its central *pull* attractor, born, tellingly, inside a theological faculty (`[[modernity-machine]]`). The line that "in the very bosom of the old religion of sin, death, and salvation was born the new religion of earthly immortality" crystallizes Manuel's thesis that the Enlightenment doctrine of progress is a transposed eschatology.

The passages on the "basic drive in human nature to innovate" and the "war eternal" between novelty and routine read as a period-piece version of `[[Conatus]]` — a striving to persist by expanding through ever-new combinations, opposed by institutions that seek to freeze it into sameness. The genius-as-gold-mine metaphor shows how thoroughly Turgot mechanizes even his humanist exception to determinism: genius is a natural constant to be mined, not a mystery to be revered.

The passage on the theological-metaphysical-positive stages is the clearest textual hinge to Comte, later installments of these same "prophets of Paris," and to the WMP interest in how a `[[history-machine]]`-style paradigm can assemble in fragments — Manuel notes Turgot's doctrine survives only in scattered essays, encyclopedia articles, and secondhand report, never in the single systematic treatise Turgot imagined but never wrote, yet these fragments cohere into a doctrine with "a reasonable measure of consistency" that later thinkers systematize into law.
