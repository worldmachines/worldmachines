# Children of Saint-Simon: The Triumph of Love

This unit follows the Saint-Simonian movement after the master's death, as Olinde Rodrigues's proselytizing sect hardens under Enfantin into a schism-ridden church whose doctrine of the "rehabilitation of the flesh" scandalized Paris even as it diagnosed a real "malady of the age." Manuel moves through four connected arguments: Enfantin's theology of love and the sexual schisms it provoked; the Saint-Simonians' apocalyptic reading of contemporary civilization as morally and economically bankrupt; their redefinition of man as a tripartite being of reason, action, and feeling (the "social trinity"); and their adaptation of Kant's concept of Antagonism into a doctrine of humanity's inevitable, arithmetic progress from war to universal association.

## Themes

**Rehabilitation of the flesh as theology.** Enfantin's church did not preach promiscuity but a typology of love — intimate (constant or fickle, but self-consistent), convenient (between mismatched natures), and religious (the priest's love, which comprehends both) — administered by a "hierarchic priesthood of love" empowered to correct emotional maladjustment. Manuel reads this less as libertinism than as an assault on the Christian mind/body dualism: once the Saint-Simonians (following hints already in Saint-Simon himself) denied that duality, "a pronouncement of instinctual emancipation had to follow." The claim that "[a]s long as one vital drive in man's nature was curbed there could be no true happiness" reads as a striving-to-persist-and-expand argument close to Spinoza's **[[Conatus]]** — fittingly, since Manuel notes the school's own debt to a romanticized, de-geometrized Spinozism transmitted through Eugène Rodrigues's translation of Lessing.

**The malady of the age as historical interregnum.** The Saint-Simonians' cry "Progress is in danger!" frames their generation as living, in Manuel's paraphrase of their own language, in "a twilight world when that-which-had-been no longer held men's allegiance and that-which-would-be was not yet believed" — an affective description of the same structural gap named elsewhere as the **[[gramsci-gap]]**: the old legitimacy dead, the new not yet born, producing (in their diagnosis) industrial anarchy, atomized science, and an art capable only of satire or elegy. Their opening address renounces the "war machines" of spiritual-versus-temporal antagonism and competitive liberty alike, seeking instead a doctrine that ends civilization's self-cannibalizing conflict.

**The social trinity.** Against both the Enlightenment's one-dimensional rational-utilitarian man (Turgot, Condorcet) and Bichat's finite-energy typology, the Saint-Simonians — drawing on Vico, Kant, Herder, Lessing, and ultimately Plato — insisted man is irreducibly tripartite: scientist, industrialist, and "artist" (their generic term, extended to all moral teachers), corresponding to reason, action, and feeling. Crucially, these capacities were held to be infinitely and simultaneously expansive rather than traded off against one another, and the sentient/emotional capacity — atrophied in the present crisis — was elevated above the merely "glacial" scientific one. This produces the romantic cult of the artist-poet-priest (Obermann, Vigny's Chatterton, Hölderlin, Beethoven) as the ideal personality type of the coming order, displacing Turgot and Condorcet's scientist-genius.

**From antagonism to association.** Via Cousin's lectures on Kant and Hegel and Michelet's translation of Vico, the Saint-Simonians absorbed Kant's 1784 essay on Antagonism — the idea that sociability and asociability in tension drive moral progress — and stripped it of Kantian caution, turning it into an optimistic, quasi-arithmetic law: antagonism contracts and love expands as human association widens from family to tribe to nation to a coming multinational religion of humanity. This is elaborated as an alternating rhythm of "organic" epochs (integrated, harmonious, if sometimes founded on "false scientific assumptions") and "critical" epochs (destructive, analytic, but historically necessary) — a heartbeat-of-history model that anticipates the **[[world-machines]]** framework's own Dawn/Day/Dusk periodization and functions here as a **[[history-machine]]**-style paradigm for reading the whole of the past.

## Notable passages

Enfantin's November 1831 lesson on the "three faces of love" is the doctrinal hinge of the rehabilitation-of-the-flesh chapter — the passage the schisms were fought over. The merged "malady of the age" passage is doubly valuable: it states the interregnum diagnosis in the Saint-Simonians' own affectively charged terms and then quotes their opening 1828 address renouncing "war machines" of antagonism outright. The tripartite-man passage is the anthropological keystone of the whole social trinity, explicitly infinitizing the three capacities against Bichat's zero-sum psychology. The Kantian-antagonism passage documents the direct textual transmission from Königsberg to the Salle Taitbout. And the organic/critical epochs passage is Manuel's clearest statement of the school's cyclical philosophy of history, the version that (as he notes) fed most directly into Marx.
