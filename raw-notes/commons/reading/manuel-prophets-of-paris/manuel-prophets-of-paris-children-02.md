# The Saint-Simonian Religion: Hierarchy, Love, Trial, and Aftermath

This unit covers the mature, institutionalized phase of Saint-Simonism: its doctrine that society should be reorganized as a hierarchy of capacity administered by expert-priests and a single directing bank; its plan for an "education for love" to complete humanity's stunted emotional development; the dramatic 1832 trial in which Enfantin defended the "rehabilitation of the flesh" before a hostile court; and the movement's slow dissolution into bourgeois respectability, with some disciples (Chevalier, the Pereire brothers) becoming pillars of Second Empire finance while Enfantin himself spent three more decades as an increasingly marginal mystic of universal love.

## Themes

**Hierarchy of capacity as a legibility project.** The Saint-Simonian motto — "Each according to his capacity! Each capacity according to its works!" — presupposes that every individual's aptitude is "clearly definable by a hierarchy of experts," who then slot each person into an "optimum spot," a designed "cubbyhole in the perfect system." Manuel stresses that this abolished inherited property not out of egalitarianism but to clear obstacles to a total, expert-administered sorting of society — a technocratic dream of perfect measurement and allocation that Manuel explicitly likens to the welfare state, central banking, and university/army bureaucracy of his own century. This is a striking instance of the drive to render an entire social organism legible and orderable — a fit case for [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and the [[modernity-machine]]'s optimization for control-through-measurement, even though the Saint-Simonians framed it as love and organic order rather than bureaucratic control.

**The unitary bank as utopia of finance capital.** Saint-Simonism imagined the entire industrial mechanism as "one vast enterprise" run by a single directing bank that could "weigh accurately the various credit needs of all branches of industry," with a central budget of the "totality of the annual products of industry" — essentially GNP before the concept existed, adjudicated by "competent chiefs" who are recognizable prototypes of twentieth-century planning commissioners. Manuel notes this bankers' dream anticipates the actual centralized credit institutions of modern capitalist and communist economies alike, regardless of their divergent theories.

**Hierarchy defended against the charge of totalitarianism.** Manuel takes pains to distinguish Saint-Simonian hierarchical order from fascism or Stalinism: their power rested on persuasion and love, never terror, and "they never spilled a drop of blood in their lives." Yet he concedes the amorphous, unbounded categories of "crimes against science" and "moral crimes" prefigure the vocabulary of later totalitarian denunciation — an ambiguity he treats seriously rather than resolving cleanly.

**Education for love and the theology of hierarchy.** Because industrial and scientific progress had outstripped humanity's emotional development, the Saint-Simonians proposed a triplex education (sympathy/art, reason/science, activity/industry) built on a repurposed Catholic apparatus — confession recast as psychoanalytic "consultation," preaching ex cathedra, dogma for the unphilosophical masses. Order itself was reconceived not as Montesquieu's equilibrium of forces nor as naked coercion, but as "a creative force, the spontaneous expression of love enlisted under the banner of progress" — a doctrine that dissolves the space between authority and affection.

**The trial as theater of doctrine.** The 1832 prosecution could not settle whether the Saint-Simonians were a political conspiracy, a protected religion, or common swindlers. Enfantin turned his defense into a sermon, distinguishing the "rehabilitation of the flesh" from bourgeois "disorder of the flesh," indicting marriage, property, and prostitution as the true sources of sexual corruption, and staging his own hypnotic "eye" as proof of the primacy of feeling over legal reason — a self-conscious theatrical enactment of the doctrine itself, method mirroring message.

**Routinization and the fate of the faithful.** After prison, the movement fractured into biography: Chevalier became a Second Empire senator who once called himself one of the "hierarchical men" — Manuel's gloss, "we would now say organization men," marking the sect's charismatic hierarchy hardening into ordinary bureaucratic careerism. Enfantin himself never fully recanted, pursuing the Suez Canal and a philosophy of selfhood dissolved into universal love, right up to the disciple Delaporte's "cool awakening" that the Father was "only Enfantin." This arc — prophetic capacity-hierarchy curdling into ordinary technocratic careers — traces something like a compressed version of the [[decay-sequence]], and the movement's whole life-cycle (crisis religion filling the space after the old church's authority died and before a stable secular order matured) sits comfortably within a [[gramsci-gap]] reading.

## Notable passages

The motto passage ("Each according to his capacity!") crystallizes the doctrine's technocratic core: capacity assessed by experts, reward proportioned to works, and personality preserved only by being correctly slotted. The finance-capital utopia passage shows how thoroughly this hierarchy was meant to extend into economic administration via a single directing bank. Manuel's own aside on totalitarianism is quoted at length because it is his most explicit editorial intervention, refusing easy anachronism while still naming the resemblance. Enfantin's courtroom speech on "the rehabilitation of the flesh" is the movement's most vivid self-articulation, and the description of the Menilmontant costumes (white for love, red for labor, blue for faith) captures how completely the sect's cosmology was worn on the body.
