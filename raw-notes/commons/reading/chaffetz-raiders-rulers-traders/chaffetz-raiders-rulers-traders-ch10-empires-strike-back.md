# The Steppe Closes: Qing China and Russia's Conquest of the Horse Frontier

This unit tells, in parallel, how the two great land empires flanking the Eurasian steppe — Qing China in the east and Muscovite Russia in the west — finally succeeded, in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, at what no earlier settled power had managed: permanently subordinating the horse-breeding peoples who had menaced them for two thousand years. The Qing, themselves former steppe horse traders (the Jurchen/Manchu lineage of Nurhaci and Hong Taiji), combined genuine patronage of Mongol herders with genocidal annihilation of the recalcitrant Oirats. Russia, built on two and a half centuries of Tatar tutelage under the Golden Horde, absorbed steppe military culture wholesale through the Cossacks and used demographic weight, agricultural colonization, and calculated alliances to pick off the Crimean khanate, the Kalmyks, and the Kazakhs one by one. The chapter closes with both empires' cavalry frontiers meeting on the Ili River, and with a coda — Qianlong's curt dismissal of George III's trade embassy — that quietly signals where the next threat to Qing power will come from: not horses, but ships.

## Themes

**Scale finally answers the steppe.** The Qing succeeded where the Tang, Song, and Ming had failed not through superior tactics but through sheer accumulated resources: a population that had grown from fifty million to two hundred million, a bureaucracy capable of running famine relief for Mongol herders fifty-five times in one reign, and a logistics operation for the Oirat campaigns (thousands of tons of grain and dried meat, multi-year provisioning at Hami and Turfan) that the author explicitly ranks above Louis XIV's or even Napoleon's contemporary European campaigns. This is a demographic-and-administrative argument, not a purely military one: the settled machine wins by outlasting and out-resourcing the steppe, echoing [[legibility-as-machine-core]] in its granular monitoring of "rainfall and grass height" and its stud-farm bureaucracy (the Court of the Imperial Stud, 8,880 square miles of managed grazing) that replaces independent pastoralist horse-supply with hired ranch hands under direct state control.

**Annihilation as policy tool.** Where patronage failed to secure loyalty — as with the distant Oirats, cut off from Qing subsidy and forced into raiding to keep their own herdsmen loyal — the Qing did not merely defeat but exterminated: smallpox, systematic massacre, and enslavement killed more than a million people, an episode the author flags as "perhaps the first large-scale and historically attested example of genocide." The lesson drawn is structural: a horse-breeding people could not be forced into "unwilling political submission" by force alone, only eliminated by the unmatched resources of a vastly larger settled state. This is the endpoint of the friction the book has traced throughout — when negotiation and co-optation fail, the settled machine's answer is erasure rather than accommodation.

**Contingent loyalty as the standing weakness of steppe polities — and Russia's use of it.** Across both Qing and Russian sections, the same structural vulnerability recurs: horse-breeders' allegiance to their own khans was never fixed. "If they so chose, horsemen could simply saddle up and abandon their chiefs." Crimean khans were overthrown by their own followers dozens of times across three centuries; the Oirat khans had to keep offering plunder or markets to hold their federation together at all. This is a clear instance of [[capture-resistance]] — the same autonomy that let steppe peoples evade any one khan's total control also meant no khanate could achieve the disciplined permanence needed to resist a patient, resource-rich neighbor. Autocratic Russia is shown explicitly exploiting this: subsidizing rival clansmen, absorbing defectors (Cossacks, later Kazakhs-as-Cossacks), and simply outwaiting the fragmentation that steppe polities inflicted on themselves.

**Russia builds itself out of the steppe it conquers.** The chapter's Russian half stresses continuity, not conquest from outside: Moscow's princes were Tatar tax collectors for two hundred fifty years before they were rulers; Russian cavalry organization, tack, and even loanwords (kopeck, from the Russian for "spear," on coins bearing a mounted horseman) are steppe in origin; and the Cossacks — themselves freebooters modeled explicitly on the Kazakh/Tatar pattern of flight from settled authority — become Russia's cut-rate cavalry, grazing free on the steppe rather than costing the state stabling and fodder. Russia's expansion is less an outside power crushing the steppe than a steppe-adjacent power absorbing steppe methods and turning them against steppe rivals, culminating in the destruction of the Crimean khanate, the catastrophic 1771 flight of the Kalmyks (which killed roughly half their population), and finally the peaceful absorption of the Kazakhs once no independent steppe rival remained to play them against Moscow.

**A world-machine transition glimpsed at the edges.** The unit closes by turning away from horses entirely: Qianlong's 1793 rejection of a "seaborne embassy" from George III ("tremble and obey") is framed as the first skirmish in a contest the Qing did not understand they were entering — against "expanding maritime empires" that horse power could not touch. Coming immediately after the most complete land-based, horse-based victory the book describes, this is a pointed structural irony, gesturing toward [[world-machines]]: the steppe-versus-settled frictional order that had organized Eurasian history for two millennia is being resolved even as a wholly different axis of contest (maritime, commercial, eventually industrial) is quietly opening underneath it.

## Notable passages

The Oirat destruction is narrated with unusual bluntness for the book, moving directly from Qianlong's ten commemorative victory poems and Castiglione's celebratory copperplate engravings to the campaign's actual outcome: mass death by disease, massacre, and slavery. The juxtaposition of imperial self-congratulation (poems, propaganda prints sent to Versailles) against the scale of the killing is the passage's argumentative weight — triumph and atrocity are presented as the same event, not sequential ones.

The Qing's famine-relief apparatus for the Mongols is offered as the positive counterpart to that same imperial capacity: a state rich and organized enough to track grass height and rainfall and to feed a starving pastoral population fifty-five times over a single reign, thereby buying loyalty that the Tang and the Yuan's successors had never secured. This is the "carrot" that makes the eventual "stick" against the Oirats comprehensible as policy rather than mere cruelty — patronage for the compliant, annihilation for the unreachable.

The generalization about horse-breeding polities' "contingent" loyalty functions as the chapter's connective thesis, tying together the Oirat federation's fragility, the Crimean khanate's constant internal overthrows, and the Cossacks' and szlachta's repeated failed bids for independence. It states outright the mechanism Russia exploited for two centuries.

The account of the Kalmyk flight closes out the Russian steppe narrative on a note the author explicitly marks as historically final: "the last great migration of a steppe nation in history," undertaken to escape Russian encroachment and ending in the deaths of roughly half those who set out. Its placement — immediately before the chapter pivots to Russo-Qing rivalry over Central Asia — marks the symbolic end of independent steppe agency in the book's narrative.

The Qianlong-George III exchange, though brief, is positioned as a hinge: an old world-machine's supreme victor (a horse empire that has just annihilated its last steppe rival) dismisses, uncomprehendingly, the herald of the one that will actually displace it. The author's closing line — "The horse-breeding Manchus underestimated how hard it would be to keep these expanding maritime empires at arm's length" — reads as a direct authorial signal that the book's horse-centered history is approaching its own limits.
