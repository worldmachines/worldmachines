# Riding the Whirlwind

This chapter follows the last great arc of Eurasian mounted conquest, from Timur's return to Samarkand in 1404 and his aborted invasion of Ming China, through his descendant Babur's exile and founding of the Mughal Empire in India, to the professionalized cavalry-states of Akbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan, and Aurangzeb, and finally to Nader Shah's catastrophic sack of Delhi and the rise of Ahmad Shah Durrani. Across two hundred and fifty years the same structural logic recurs: a raider consolidates a horse-breeding base, uses plunder or land revenue to fund an ever more expensive and technically sophisticated cavalry, is compelled by the economics of that cavalry into unceasing campaigns, and sees his conquests unravel within a generation once campaigning stops or a succession crisis intervenes.

## Themes

**Horse power compels conquest as much as it enables it.** Chaffetz's central argument is that by Timur's era the steppe army had "lost its low-cost status": artillery, year-round campaigning fed by fodder trains, and fixed per-rank horse quotas made cavalry armies expensive machines that had to keep conquering and extracting plunder or taxes simply to sustain themselves. Timur's own troops were paid in *ulufat* (named from the word for alfalfa), and his refusal to demobilize for thirty years was as much a political necessity — denying his sons and generals the leisure to plot against him — as a strategic choice. The same logic drives Aurangzeb's "perpetual-motion machine" of endless campaigns in the Deccan, and ultimately Nader Shah, whose conquest of India "did nothing more than ride the whirlwind": the plunder of Delhi's Peacock Throne and Koh-i-Nur still could not cover the cost of the cavalry that won them, and his empire collapsed into bankruptcy and assassination.

**Control of horse-breeding territory is the strategic keystone of empire.** Iran under the post-Mongol Timurids and Afghanistan under the Mughals function as the indispensable "springboard" resource base — India itself is described by Babur as "a land of no good horses," and it is precisely this deficiency that both draws conquerors in (a horse-poor, wealthy target) and forces the Mughals into permanent dependence on imported Turki, Arab, and Iranian stock via Kabul and Kandahar. Losing control of the supply chain — as the later Mughals lost Afghanistan in 1737 — is fatal in a way losing revenue-rich territory alone is not. This is the same logic, at a much larger and more monetized scale, that shaped Song China's dependence on Central Asian horse markets in earlier chapters: legible institutions can substitute for horse-rich homelands only up to a point.

**Institutionalization converts steppe plunder-cavalry into settled bureaucratic power.** Akbar's regime marks a genuine transition: officers are paid not with battlefield loot but with land revenue assigned in proportion to a graded schedule of horses-per-rank, state stables are staffed by a hierarchy of *daroghahs*, *mushrifs*, vets, and grooms, and horses themselves are inspected, ranked, and promoted like soldiers. This is [[legibility-as-machine-core]] applied to cavalry logistics — turning an inherently mobile, personalistic steppe resource into an auditable instrument of a settled fiscal state, letting the Mughals conquer and hold agricultural India on a scale the pasture-bound Mongols never could. The Mughal court's own language absorbs this fusion: *riyasat* names both horsemanship and statecraft, and courtiers describe themselves as "present in the stirrup."

**Horse-poor peripheral peoples convert into new military aristocracies through raiding.** The Rajputs, originally cattle and camel herders, and the Marathas, originally lowland horse thieves from the horse-poor Western Ghats, both build cavalry power from stolen and locally bred stock and parlay it into durable, semi-autonomous military states that alternately serve and resist Mughal authority — a pattern of [[capture-resistance]] against incorporation into the settled empire, most vivid in Holkar's steppe-derived boast, "My home is the back of my horse, and where his head is pointed is my country." The Portuguese Estado da India, meanwhile, shows a non-steppe actor inserting itself into the same horse-trade economy as a geopolitical lever, arming the southern sultanates against Mughal encroachment purely through control of the Hormuz–Goa shipping route.

**The chapter closes on a turning point in the wider Eurasian pattern.** By the time of Ahmad Shah Durrani, the narrative notes that "the initiative was slipping away from the horse-breeding nations of Central Asia, Iran, and Afghanistan, in favor of the great, sedentary empires" — the Qing having, unlike the Ming, "cracked the horse-supply problem." This is the hinge into the following chapter's account of Qing China and Russia, and reads as a [[gramsci-gap]]-like transition point: the old steppe-conqueror playbook (raid, consolidate, conquer, extract, campaign forever) still functions individually — Ahmad Shah still briefly entertains invading China — but the surrounding conditions that made it dominant are dissolving underneath it, visible only in retrospect as a [[history-machine]]-style pattern repeating across independent actors (Timur, Babur, Nader, Ahmad Shah) rather than as any one ruler's unique genius.

## Notable passages

The chapter opens and closes on ritualized spectacle and its collapse. Timur's *töy* at Samarkand — horse races twenty miles around, prize horses and silver saddles, foreign ambassadors watching — is staged explicitly to project invincibility before he announces the invasion of China; the passage's dramatic irony is that the same horse-based confidence that built his empire also blinds his court to its fragility. That fragility is dramatized directly in the death-announcement scene: a solitary rider gallops through the pasturelands crying "Amir Timür is dead!" without stopping to answer questions, and the seven-year invasion dissolves before it starts. The scene works almost as a fable of the whole chapter's argument — conquest empires built on one irreplaceable rider's momentum stop moving the instant he does.

Babur's paradoxical line about Kabul and India — he "regretted leaving Kabul behind for the plains of India—'a land of no good horses,' as he put it—that lack was precisely why he was able to conquer it" — crystallizes the chapter's thesis about horse scarcity as both a strategic weakness and an invasion incentive in one sentence.

Akbar's minister Abul Fazl's description of the emperor's obsession with horses as "an almost supernatural means for the attainment of personal greatness" is the chapter's clearest statement of horsemanship as the master metaphor and literal mechanism of Mughal statecraft, echoed later in the double meaning of *riyasat*.

The Portuguese Estado da India passage, built on the 1527 letter to Dom João III and Afonso de Albuquerque's line that "everyone who possesses a Persian horse can govern throughout the Deccan," shows how thoroughly the horse trade itself — not just horse-mounted armies — could function as an independent instrument of geopolitical leverage, wielded here by an outside maritime power rather than a steppe empire.

Finally, the account of Nader Shah's campaign — his treasury exhausted supplying horses even before the invasion, the record-shattering indemnity extracted from Delhi still insufficient to avert bankruptcy, and his assassination followed immediately by his heirs "predictably" killing one another — delivers the chapter's summary judgment in a single clause: Nader "had done nothing more than ride the whirlwind."
