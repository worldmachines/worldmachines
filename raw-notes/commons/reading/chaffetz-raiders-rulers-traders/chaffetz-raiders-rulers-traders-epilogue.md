# Epilogue: The Last Refuge of the Horse

The book ends not in antiquity but in mid-twentieth-century Kabul, at a royal buzkashi match staged by King Zaher Shah, and uses that scene as a lens onto the twentieth century's near-total destruction of Eurasian steppe horse culture — a destruction Afghanistan alone, for a time, escaped. The epilogue functions as a coda to the whole book's argument: having traced three millennia of horse-borne raiders, rulers, and traders reshaping Eurasian empires, it shows the world-historical machine finally grinding that culture down to a single, isolated, embattled remnant.

## Themes

**Legibility remaking an anarchic tradition into national spectacle.** Zaher Shah takes an unruly, boundary-less steppe brawl over a goat or calf carcass — "played like the children's game King of the Hill," with "no rules and no defined playing field... no prizes or even teams" — and converts it into a rule-bound, timed, refereed sport on a fixed field with uniforms and prizes. This is legibility-making in miniature: the king and his court impose measurable form (a hundred-yard field, forty-five-minute halves, a "circle of truth," scoring) on a practice that had previously resisted any such capture, precisely so it can be watched, understood, and mobilized as a unifying symbol for a fractious multiethnic, multilingual state. The riders' discomfort — bolting past barriers, resisting livery and fixed teams — registers the friction between the older, capture-resistant form of the game and its new legible, spectator-ready incarnation. This is a small-scale instance of the decay sequence: a once-functional steppe practice (herding contest, martial training, status display) sliding toward the performative and ornamental as it becomes state theater. [[legibility-as-machine-core]] [[decay-sequence]]

**A throne trying to manufacture unity out of horse culture.** Afghanistan's violent history of twenty-one rulers in rapid succession, and its live tension between rural/religious conservatism and urban modernizing aspiration, is explicitly named as the backdrop for Zaher Shah's choice of buzkashi as a "national game" — even though only Uzbeks in the north had ever played it. The move is a deliberate act of historical mythmaking, reaching back to the "As´vakas," the ancient "masters of the horse," to claim a two-millennium continuity across a country riven by language, sect, and tribe. This is nation-building through invented/curated tradition, using the horse as the one shared cultural referent left standing. [[modernity-machine]] [[gramsci-gap]]

**Soviet and Iranian state-building as horse-killing.** The epilogue's darkest material is its account of forced sedentarization: Soviet collectivization in Turkmenistan, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, and Uzbekistan turned herds into state property, eliminated the need for horses, and produced catastrophic losses (Kazakhstan's livestock fell from thirty-six million in 1928 to three million in 1932). Anthropologist Anatoly Khazanov's anecdote about a Soviet work brigade needing four trucks to do what one horseman and his son used to do — capped by the foreman's deadpan "But in those days the herd belonged to the horseman" — crystallizes the book's implicit argument: ownership and personal stewardship (a kind of conatus, a striving bound to a particular herd and a particular rider) sustained the horse economy, while collectivized, depersonalized ownership (by everyone, and therefore by no one) destroyed it, down to herders themselves butchering their horses for meat. Iran's parallel campaign against the khans was explicitly aimed at breaking political power via disarmament and forced settlement, with the same ruinous economic result. Both are instances of a modern legible state apparatus proving fatal to a way of life organized around mobility, personal bonds, and evasion of central control. [[capture-resistance]] [[Conatus]] [[modernity-machine]]

**Afghanistan as the last thicket.** Because it alone avoided this social engineering (until 1978), Afghanistan became the refuge for horse breeders fleeing Soviet Central Asia and Iran, who brought their finest bloodstock and reconstituted a horse culture — the great Turkmen-descended horses, the meticulously trained chapandaz and their buzkashi mounts — even as the old transregional horse trade (Bukhara, Turfan, Lahore markets; the caravan routes destroyed by British bombing in 1919) had collapsed under twentieth-century border closures. The wealthy khans and beys who now bought the horses replaced the vanished Indian maharajas, but the impulse — horses as "a precious reminder of their steppe traditions" — survived intact. This portrays Afghanistan, in its historical isolation and light state presence, as a refuge habitat where an older mode of life persisted after being extinguished everywhere else — the book's closing embodiment of what elsewhere in this project is called a thicket. ## Notable passages

The account of buzkashi's construction as spectacle (paragraph 7) is the epilogue's clearest small-scale case study in turning an ungovernable practice legible — precise field dimensions, a scoring circle called "the circle of truth," fixed team sizes, and timed halves imposed on what had been essentially unstructured combat. It sets up the following paragraphs' account of riders instinctively defying those boundaries, "since in their native steppe the play sprawled without boundaries" — a reminder that legibility is imposed, not natural, and meets resistance.

The Khazanov/foreman exchange in paragraph 17 is the moral center of the epilogue: a single deadpan line — "But in those days the herd belonged to the horseman" — does the work of an entire argument about why collectivized property regimes cannot replicate the stewardship that personal ownership and mounted vigilance provided, and why removing horses (and thus the herder's direct capacity to defend and control the flock) led directly to the animals' disappearance, "eaten by wolves" or butchered outright.

The closing paragraph is the book's final image: a Westerner who claims to have "played buzkashi in Afghanistan" almost certainly only watched, because the countryside game has no field and no boundary — to see it at all requires riding into "the eye of the storm," among "thousand-pound horses" biting and kicking, "raging riders" swinging plaited leather whips, for "many hours or even several days." The book ends with foreign observers necessarily left behind, watching the riders "vanishing into the freedom of the limitless steppe" — a final, literal enactment of the horse culture's capture-resistance: the outsider cannot keep pace, cannot fully witness, and the steppe closes back over the scene as it always has.
