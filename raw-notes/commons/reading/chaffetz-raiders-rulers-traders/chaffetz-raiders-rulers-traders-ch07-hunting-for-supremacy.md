# Hunting for Supremacy

This chapter argues that hunting was not a leisure pastime on the Eurasian steppe but a rehearsal for statecraft and war: the mass encirclement hunt (nerge, or battue) trained horsemen in the coordinated maneuvers of steppe warfare, and skilled chiefs used the hunt's hospitality and display to build the followings that became empires. Tracing the roughly two centuries after the Tang dynasty's collapse (907–1071 and beyond), the chapter follows this "hunt-to-empire" pattern across Eurasia: the Khitans in China, the Seljuk and other Turkmen in Iran and Anatolia, and the Ghaznavid and mamluk dynasties in India, each converting steppe horse power into rule over settled populations, and each eventually succumbing to the same cycle when a fresher wave of steppe conquerors arrived.

## Themes

**The hunt as social and military infrastructure.** Long before it was strategy, the hunt was the basic organizing ritual of steppe life. Camp routine freed men for weeks- or months-long expeditions (women and children managed the herds), and epic literature like *The Book of Dede Korkut* treated hunting as the central heroic activity, prone to jealousy, blood feuds, and status contests ("Is the skill in the horse or in the man?"). A great chief's hunt, with its lavish hospitality and choicest kills reserved for guests, functioned as a recruiting and alliance-sealing mechanism — "great chiefs used hunting to establish vast steppe empires." This is the chapter's throughline: sociality organized around the hunt is a proto-political technology, a pattern of distributed practice quietly assembling into statecraft before any single ruler "invents" an empire — a dynamic resonant with the WMP idea of a [[history-machine]] crystallizing out of independent components rather than great-man decree.

**The battue/nerge as dual-use technology.** The chapter's technical centerpiece is the crescent-and-encirclement hunting tactic (nerge in Mongol, battue to Western writers), which required discipline, patience, and coordinated flanking to trap fast prey without spooking it into premature flight. Chaffetz shows this same maneuver was steppe warfare's core tactic — surrounding an enemy, harrying it with arrows, and letting panic dissolve its cohesion once an apparent gap opened. He quotes a modern scholar's line, "War is the continuation of hunting with the same means" (echoing Clausewitz), and the Khitan emperor Taizong's own words: "Our hunting is not simply a pursuit of pleasure. It is a means of practicing for warfare." The tactic's effectiveness recurs at Manzikert (1071) and against Igor of Suzdal's Rus in 1185 — a single piece of organizational know-how migrating seamlessly between hunting ground and battlefield.

**Founding empires through the hunt.** Abaoji's rise among the Khitans (907) is framed explicitly through hunting ritual: he celebrates his election as khaghan with an elaborate hunt, then converts hunting infrastructure (the walled hunting park at Huangdu) into the seed of an imperial capital, before declaring himself emperor — a title none of his steppe predecessors (Hun chanyu, Turkish khaghan) had dared claim. The chapter treats this as the template for a repeatable cycle: horse-breeding hunter-bands, freed from Tang subsidy and pasture after 907, migrate outward and, wherever a talented chief emerges, convert hunting cohesion into conquest, whether the Khitans in China, the Seljuks in Iran and then Anatolia, or Mahmud of Ghazna and later mamluk sultans in India.

**Divergent settled responses: walls, absorption, and importation.** The chapter contrasts how different agrarian civilizations coped with steppe pressure. China renewed the Great Wall as a durable, maintained boundary (even under Khitan and Jurchen rule), while Iran let its Wall of Gorgan crumble and forgot who built it or why, leaving no hard frontier once Baghdad's caliphal border lords absorbed Turkmen as slave-soldiers (mamluks) and pastoral levies — with catastrophic ecological and agricultural consequences once "millions of horses" flooded in. India took a third path: because its climate could not sustain large steppe herds, ruling dynasties (the Ghaznavids, then the Delhi mamluk sultanate) imported horses commercially rather than importing whole horse-breeding populations, avoiding the herder-farmer conflict that destabilized Iran and producing a comparatively durable synthesis that even survived the Mongol storm. This divergence — legible, maintained boundary versus permeable, eventually overrun frontier versus market-mediated adaptation — maps suggestively onto [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and the differential fates of infrastructure under pressure.

**The fragile arithmetic of steppe rule.** Every conquest regime in the chapter follows the same arc: after several generations of ruling settled land, steppe elites sinicize or Persianize, lose their taste for the steppe, and grow estranged from the horse-breeders whose loyalty put them in power — at which point a fresh wave of steppe conquerors displaces them (Khitans by Jurchens, Jurchens by Mongols; Seljuks fragmented by Turkmen revolt and then displaced by the Kipchak-backed Khwarazm Shah). This is presented as a structural, near-mechanical cycle rather than a series of contingent failures, and it previews the chapter's closing image of the hunter becoming the hunted — a decay sequence for conquest legitimacy itself, evocative of WMP's [[decay-sequence]] and [[gramsci-gap]] framing of interregnum collapse.

## Notable passages

The opening pages ground hunting in daily steppe life and its literary memory, using *The Book of Dede Korkut* to show hunting's centrality to status, masculinity, and social bonding among horse and rider alike — the affective substrate beneath the later political argument.

The description of the nerge/battue (the crescent encirclement, the "token clemency" of letting an animal escape to lull the rest, the psychological mastery required of both horse and rider) is the chapter's technical core, and Chaffetz explicitly ties it to the identical tactic used to break enemy armies at Manzikert and against the Rus — the same organizational form doing double duty as sport and as war college.

Abaoji's story dramatizes the moment a steppe chief converts a customary hunt-celebration into declared emperorship, complete with an invented capital and hunting park organized by cosmological color-symbolism — an origin story for empire built entirely out of hunting ritual and steppe custom rather than any settled precedent.

The paragraph on the Wall of Gorgan versus the Great Wall crystallizes the chapter's comparative-civilizational argument: two nearly contemporaneous frontier monuments, one maintained and renewed for a millennium, the other allowed to crumble and its very builders forgotten, with radically different downstream consequences for Iran versus China.

The closing paragraph reprises the hunting metaphor to summarize the whole chapter's argument about fragility: steppe-derived regimes had to balance protecting farmers against keeping horsemen occupied with hunts or wars, "but their regimes were fragile, because their horse-breeding followers shifted their loyalties when a promising new leader appeared" — the chapter's final line, "events in Mongolia would soon turn them into prey," pivots directly into the rise of Genghis Khan in the following chapter.
