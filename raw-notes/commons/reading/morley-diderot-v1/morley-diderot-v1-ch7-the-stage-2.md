# Diderot as dramatic critic: the Paradox, the classic stage, and the genre sérieux

Morley moves through Diderot's writing on the theatre. He begins with Diderot's favourites: Terence, praised for moderation and taste though lacking verve, the English comic writers (more verve than taste), Moore's The Gamester and Lillo's George Barnwell as models of bourgeois tragedy, and Shakespeare as a giant. He then argues that a great critic of drama must study acting, and gives an extended account of the Paradox on the Player, with Garrick as Diderot's illustration. Next he turns to Diderot's attack on the declamatory French classic stage (acting, scenery, costume), places it in the older quarrel of Ancients and Moderns, and dismisses the 'Serbonian bog' of dramatic rules. The unit closes with Diderot's proposed genre sérieux and bourgeois tragedy, which Morley judges a theory that produced no school, and with a verdict that Diderot's scheme was neither classic nor romantic and that only Nathan the Wise speaks the eighteenth century's own mind in dramatic form.

## Themes

**Attribution of the argument.** The praise of Terence, the Saint Christopher image, the Paradox, the Garrick anecdote, and the genre sérieux are Diderot's views as Morley reports or quotes them. The verdicts (that the Paradox is unusually well finished, that the theory of types is self-defeating, that dramatic rules are a 'grotesque monument of sterility') are Morley's own, in a Victorian liberal critic's voice.

**Acting as a serious part of dramatic criticism.** Morley holds that no one can be a great dramatist or critic of the drama without studying stage representation, and contrasts Goethe and Lessing with Hazlitt, who could not bear to see King Lear acted. A play must be judged as a play, not merely as a poem.

**The Paradox on the Player.** In Morley's summary, the first-rank player has judgment, self-possession and penetration but no sensibility; extreme sensibility makes mediocre actors, and the absolute want of it prepares sublime ones. Coolness, not fury, produces the characteristic stroke, in actor, poet, painter and musician alike. Garrick running through a series of passions in a few seconds without feeling them is the illustration. Morley also reports Diderot's subsidiary points: stage truth is conformity to an ideal model, not to nature as it is; rehearsal balances the troupe; make the denouement an action, not a narrative; fewer gestures, more silence, less emphasis.

**Return to nature against the French classic stage.** Diderot, per Morley, refused the comparison between French classic tragedy and the Greeks: the French dialogue is emphasis, wit and glitter, far from nature. The same demand for truth extends to scene-painting and costume (no gilding, no panier). Morley places this in the Moderns and Ancients quarrel (La Motte, Fontenelle) and notes that Voltaire's tragedies restored the conventional drama and that he found the Greeks too colloquial.

**Dramatic rules as a swamp, and Diderot's escape clause.** Morley condemns the long controversy over Aristotle's catharsis and the imputation of ethical or social purpose to the dramatist, but credits Diderot with the concession that no general principle cannot be infringed by a man of genius. He also credits him with generalising from the facts of nature and art, not reading his own ideas into works.

**The genre sérieux and its failure.** Diderot's third dramatic kind treats the duties of men as comic matter. Morley observes that it formed no school; Beaumarchais and later French bourgeois comedy depart from it (adultery, romance, Spanish structure). His two objections are that mere reproduction of reality does not move us, citing Burke, and that a drama steeped in Duty leaves too little room for imaginative genius. Replacing individual character with class types would bring back the pseudo-classic abstraction (he asks what is gained by trading the typical king for the generalised shopkeeper), and Morley dissents from the Romantics' placing of Diderot among the Flamboyant.

**Encyclopædists and the classic spirit.** Morley argues that an Encyclopædist, who disdained the Dark Ages, could not mount a real antagonism to the classic school, since romanticism drew life from the medieval; yet he suggests that in the eighteenth century classicism meant making man the centre of the universe. This is Morley's historical generalisation, and it is dated Victorian periodisation. It faintly rhymes with [[enlightenment-monism]] and the humanist side of the Enlightenment, but the unit does not argue the connection.

## Notable passages

**Shakespeare as Saint Christopher.** Diderot declines to compare Shakespeare to the Belvedere Apollo, preferring an unshapely colossus through whose legs one can pass. Morley calls it unsatisfactory recognition, yet better than Voltaire's 'drunken savage'. This reveals Morley's own measure of Voltaire against Diderot.

**The Paradox stated.** The core claim of the Paradox in Morley's own condensed paraphrase, with Diderot's three-step formula about sensibility.

**Garrick.** The face running through the whole ladder of emotion between two folding doors, with the Little Baker's Boy and Hamlet on demand. The concrete evidence for the thesis that performance is craft and not feeling.

**French classic critique.** Diderot's argument that tragedy is the most imperfect of literary kinds, grounded in truth to nature and unforced dénouements.

**The genre sérieux.** Diderot's case for a serious comedy of duties, in Morley's paraphrase, one of the central statements of the bourgeois drama.

**Morley's verdict on illusion and Duty.** The Burke quotation and the claim that the genre's structure repels dramatic genius.
