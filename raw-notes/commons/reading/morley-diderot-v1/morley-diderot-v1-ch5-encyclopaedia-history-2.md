# Who built the Encyclopædia, and what for

Morley, taking up Voltaire's own tribute to the contributors, walks through the roll of the Encyclopædia's writers: the famous (Montesquieu, Turgot, Rousseau, Buffon, later Haller and Condorcet), the specialists (Forbonnais, Quesnai, Holbach, Duclos, Morellet), and the unglamorous workhorse Jaucourt. He then describes the flood of unsought volunteers, gives Voltaire's frank and active support (including his complaints about weak articles), reports D'Alembert's reply that censorship forced bad theology articles and a system of covert cross-reference, and closes with Morley's own judgement: the work was not mainly a siege-engine but aimed at "practice, not literature but life", with Diderot holding that man must be the common centre of the whole undertaking.

## Themes

**A distributed, multi-hand enterprise.** Morley's catalogue is in effect a map of the labour behind the work: celebrities who sent one article or left it to a deputy (Buffon, via Daubenton), specialists who supplied whole fields (Holbach on chemistry and mineralogy from German sources), and a large unsought periphery. The editors were "constantly embarrassed" by choosing among competing articles on one subject. Voltaire's tribute, as Morley opens it, stresses that the contributors worked without common understanding or party spirit; Morley's own list also notes Turgot leaving when he saw the scheme "coloured by that spirit of sect". This is a picture of a knowledge project assembled from many independent hands, which resonates loosely with [[composable-knowledge]] and with the [[history-machine]] idea of a paradigm assembling distributedly, though Morley frames it as a literary-moral roll of honour, not as infrastructure.

**Humble editorial labour.** Jaucourt is Morley's emblem of the unseen work of editorship: writing, rewriting, reading, correcting, thirteen or fourteen hours a day. Diderot's own words (quoted by Morley from the preface to vol. viii) credit him with getting the work across the line. Morley's moral emphasis, "modest and unwearying helpers", is his own.

**Voltaire as ally and quality-controller.** Per Morley, [[voltaire]] greeted the first volumes from Potsdam as the glory of France and the shame of its persecutors, offered small contributions, and pressed for method (etymologies, definitions, examples, clearness, brevity) while criticising declamatory journeymen. Morley praises his good faith in complaining to the editors rather than of them.

**Compliance with prejudice, and the veil that failed to hide.** D'Alembert concedes the bad theology and metaphysics articles but blames censors and the privilege; Diderot's quoted method is to state a national prejudice respectfully and then overturn it through cross-references to articles with solid principles. Morley's verdict is his own and pointed: this is a deplorable necessity, but let no one cast a stone who is not free of "hypocritical conformity and mendacious compliance"; he endorses [[nicolas-de-condorcet]]'s remark that the shame belongs to the ecclesiastics and ministers whose tyranny made dissimulation necessary. The practice reads as a legibility game between a censoring incumbent and writers who encode dissent in the reference apparatus; it touches [[illegibility-as-elite-defense]] only from the opposite side (here the authors, not the elite, work by indirection), so the fit is partial.

**Aim: practice, not literature.** Morley rejects the claim that the Encyclopædia was merely a siege-engine, saying that is true of only one part and not the most important, yet insists that the French revolutionary school subordinated mere knowledge to the practical raising of society from the paralysis imposed by civil and ecclesiastical authority. The tools built for attack were equally suited to disinterested research in a happier season. This is the core of Morley's thesis (a positive-practical turn preparing change) and bears on [[intellectual-vs-revolutionary]]: the Encyclopædists are men of battle, but Diderot keeps the post-battle reconstruction in view.

**Man as centre.** In the passage Morley quotes, Diderot argues that without the contemplative being the universe is dumb, and that the work should make man the common centre from which all lines radiate. Morley hears here "the voice of the new time", and notes Diderot's linked claim that an Encyclopædia is the work of centuries, addressed to posterity.

## Notable passages

**Jaucourt, the slave of A to Z (c1).** Morley's portrait, with Diderot's quoted tribute: the self-effacing chevalier who revelled in drudgery and refused every solicitation to leave.

**The volunteers (c2).** Clergymen who were unbelievers in their hearts, unknown ladies on costume, and the arc from Lenglet's birth (1674) to Morellet's death (1819), which Morley calls an arc of western experience.

**Voltaire's counsel (c3).** Enthusiasm ("the greatest work in the world") alongside blunt method advice and the quip on Hell; shows a collaborator who edits by candour.

**D'Alembert, Diderot and the dissimulation (c4).** The key passage on enforced conformity, Condorcet's judgement, and Diderot's own explanation of the cross-reference technique; D'Alembert's "what we have thought from what we have said" is the line Morley calls bitter and humiliating.

**Not literature but life (c5).** Morley's rebuttal of the siege-engine reading and his statement of the practical aim.

**Man at the centre (c6).** Diderot's humanist organising principle for the work, as quoted by Morley, with Morley's own gloss that the weight of the burden steadied him. Dated note: Morley's 1878 hostility to the siege-engine reading is a response to critics of his day, not a settled verdict.
