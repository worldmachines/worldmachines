# The Church at mid-century, the Encyclopædia as symbol of revolt, and Diderot's claim to attention

The unit holds Morley's 1878 Preface (a September 1878 note on Franklin, Raynal and the Polly Baker speech is appended) and Chapter I, "Preliminary." The Preface says the book closes Morley's studies of the literary preparation for the French Revolution, explains that it is more descriptive than the Voltaire and Rousseau volumes (more "transcript or analysis", since Diderot's circle cannot be assumed known), and sets out to counter Taine's "one-sided exaggerations" by bringing out the social significance and positive quality of the Encyclopædists. Chapter I opens with the Gallican church around 1750 confident of renewed unity, then jumps forward to the Feast of the Supreme Being. Morley argues that the earlier heretics lacked the "social idea", that the Encyclopædia joined the scientific idea to the social one, and that its optimistic doctrine of human plasticity was what made it dangerous to the Church. He rebuts Carlyle's dismissal and ends by making a case for Diderot as the one real thinker among the great illuminators.

## Themes

**The Church's false dawn.** Morley's own narrative: around mid-century Voltaire is at Berlin, Rousseau is copying music, the Encyclopædia is thought of as a bookseller's project, the Jansenists have shrunk, and the Church has beaten a proposed tax on its property. A churchman might think abuse and superstition were on the eve of their "most triumphant days". Morley's irony is retrospective ("We now know how illusory"): in about forty years Paris had the Feast of the Supreme Being and the Goddess of Reason.

**Scientific idea plus social idea.** Morley's central diagnostic claim. Earlier heretics from Abelard to Bruno and Vanini were isolated and often submissive, and had only the scientific idea; the Church was crushingly able to handle them. He credits the Church with real success in moral and social direction for centuries, and rejects as a "gross fallacy" the view that only physical science improves social condition. The new phase (Diderot's) was "animated at once by the scientific idea and by the social idea", aiming at a "new social synthesis resting on a purely positive basis". Morley says it lacked only the historic idea. This is a Comtean frame, and Morley states it as his own verdict.

**The Encyclopædia as symbol, protest and provisional spiritual power.** For Morley the Encyclopædia is a protest against the old organisation as much as the old doctrine. Its moral is that human nature is good, the world can be made habitable, and evil comes from bad education and institutions. He calls it a "wonderful gospel" then, a truism now, and the antecedent of all social improvement, since conviction that human lot is "indefinitely modifiable for good" precedes any effort to modify its conditions. Churchmen resented it because the Church taught a fallen, depraved man, and because curiosity about the earth and the spirit of improvement undercut both doctrine and abuse. He calls it an "informal, transitory, and provisional organisation of the new spiritual power", winning authority by a "more penetrating eye for social exigencies". The Encyclopædia is thus framed as an institution of knowledge as well as a book, which resonates loosely with the WM interest in knowledge infrastructure, though Morley's interest is moral and political rather than infrastructural, and nothing here discusses its mechanics (arts, trades, classification).

**Against Carlyle and the reaction.** Morley disputes "our veteran humorist" (Carlyle) that the Encyclopædical tree "has borne no fruit", quotes Goethe's rebuke of those who hold Diderot cheaply as "Philistine", and calls the post-Revolution spiritual reaction (Wordsworth, Carlyle) tainted by Philistinism. He claims the movement is "in full progress" again in his own day (materialism in the science of man, humanitarian legislation, naturalism in art), reading eighty years of transcendental reaction as a mere "parenthesis". This is a Victorian progressivist reading of history as a resumed advance, and it is dated as such; it rhymes with a history-machine or cycle framing only in that Morley sees long underlying movements outlasting an interruption.

**Why Diderot.** Morley cites Comte's praise and says Diderot alone of Voltaire, Rousseau and himself had the idea of scientific method, feeling for doctrine and "large organic and constructive conceptions", "true philosophic meditation". He grants Diderot is inferior in literary expression to Voltaire and Rousseau, and superior in breadth of artistic principle: a realistic, sympathetic criticism and an appeal from classic convention to common life. ## Notable passages

**The Church before the storm (c1).** The opening panorama of confidence, including the persecution of Protestants, is the setup for Morley's irony and shows the incumbent institution at its apparent high point.

**Scientific idea without social idea (c2).** The load-bearing explanation of why earlier heresy failed and why the Church remained legitimate for centuries; it also contains Morley's concession that the Church's directing function was "so immeasurably more important" than physical discovery.

**The new phase and its symbol (c3).** States the thesis in one paragraph: the insurrection joined scientific and social ideas, and "The great symbol of this new comprehensiveness in the insurrection was the Encyclopædia." Compare [[intellectual-vs-revolutionary]] for the question of intellectuals preparing a political upheaval, though Morley is not framing it that way.

**The cheerful doctrine (c4).** Human nature is good, evil is institutional, and character is plastic; Morley treats this as the engine of every later reform. A critic of [[enlightenment-monism]] or the [[counter-enlightenment]] would aim at exactly this premise, but Morley does not engage such critics here beyond Carlyle.

**The Church's chagrin and the new spiritual power (c5).** Spells out the structural clash: secular knowledge displaces sacred lore, and the old spiritual power has become an accomplice of the temporal. This is an incumbent institution defending itself against a rival authority, framed by Morley in Comtean terms of spiritual power.

**Diderot as thinker (c6).** Morley's own ranking of Diderot over Voltaire and Rousseau on the criteria of method, doctrine and observation; flagged as Morley's evaluation, partly borrowed from Comte.
