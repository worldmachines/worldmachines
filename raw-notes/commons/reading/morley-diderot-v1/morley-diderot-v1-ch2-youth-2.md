# Diderot's character and his marriage (to 1743 and after)

This stretch of Morley's biography is almost wholly character-study rather than argument about ideas. Morley (in his own voice, backed by anecdote and by testimony from Diderot, Marmontel and Morellet) presents Diderot as a man whose warmth of feeling survived family quarrel, poverty and constant exploitation; then sketches his intellectual formation and the pull of knowledge over drudgery; and closes with the story of his marriage to Antoinette Champion (1743), the temperamental mismatch that followed, and his liaisons with Madame Puisieux and Mdlle. Voland. Morley ends by refusing to assign censure and by denying that the household irregularities can be charged to the new religious opinions.

## Themes

**Character read through conduct, and conduct read through character.** Morley opens on Diderot's estrangement from his father. His claim is that Diderot kept faith with old love despite a conflict of wills, quoting Diderot's own account of going to Langres confident of his father's goodness ("I thought rightly"). Morley turns this into a general moral about piercing the veils of egoism to the reality of sentiment and motive. This is Morley's gloss, not Diderot's.

**Pity as practical, delicate generosity.** Morley calls pity Diderot's "divine gift" and offers two stories: the woman and the snuff-box (told by Diderot in a letter, with its maxim about sparing the poor the shame of holding out a hand) and the starving satirist whom Diderot helps to profit from his own libel by writing a dedication to a prince who hated him. Marmontel and Morellet are cited as witnesses to his open-handed conversation and his habit of improving, in his head, the works authors brought him. Morley's emphasis is that Diderot "was as good as his sentiment".

**Abuse of that generosity, and tolerance of social castaways.** Diderot's family reckoned three-fourths of his life given to others; the Formica leo story is the client's parting jibe. Morley hedges whether tolerance for "social castaways and reprobates" makes the virtue greater or smaller ("I know not whether to say..."). He then gives Diderot's reasoned case against "public prosecutors for the universe" and for fixing the eye on what is good: a position Morley praises as the noblest of traits and as a rebuke to "better men".

**Knowledge as consolation, but life before books.** Morley says we do not know how Diderot trained his understanding; the Shaftesbury annotations and early pieces show Montaigne, Pascal, Augustine and the classics, English literature as stimulant and, above all, Bacon, whose ideas Morley calls "the direct source of the great undertaking of Diderot's life" (an anticipatory pointer to the Encyclopædia, not elaborated here). Hack work (sermons for Portuguese colonies) is endured because a line of Homer or a problem in geometry restores serenity. Yet Morley insists Diderot was not "essentially a man of books": closer to [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] than to [[voltaire]] or Fontenelle, with the formula "Faire le bien, connaître le vrai" and truth sought as a means of doing good. This is a small early statement of the practical, social orientation Morley later makes central to the Encyclopædia, though nothing here yet speaks of the project as infrastructure.

**Marriage, mismatch and Morley's reticence.** Diderot, penniless, presses for marriage with a seamstress, is treated as a madman by his father at Langres, falls ill, and is nursed into marriage by the two women (1743). Morley's verdict is that "destiny was hostile": a dutiful, pious wife whose goodness, in his conjecture ("Perhaps it was so here"), took forms that exasperated a restless man, then the Puisieux and Voland connections. He closes with a Victorian-liberal move: little profit in assigning blame, such irregularities were "in the manners of the time", and it would be as impertinent to blame the new opinions as to blame the old ones for the vices of Dubois, Louis XV or Rohan. The assessment of the wife and of Mme Puisieux is Morley's, drawn from limited evidence which he admits.

## Notable passages

**Pity and the snuff-box (c1).** Morley's thesis for the whole portrait: Diderot's feeling was real, unsentimental and acted on.

**The Formica leo (c2).** The cost of that generosity, in Diderot's own telling as Morley relays it.

**Against public prosecutors (c3).** Diderot's case for leniency and for attending to the good in a work or character; the nearest thing here to a moral philosophy.

**Man of books or of life (c4).** Morley's balance between knowledge and practice, with the Faire le bien formula.

**Courtship and marriage (c5).** The 1743 marriage narrative, with Morley's fatalistic epigram on choice in marriage.

**Domestic disorder and Morley's refusal to judge (c6).** The mismatch, the liaisons, and the argument that religious opinion is not to blame.
