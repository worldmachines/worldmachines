# Diderot as contributor: confusion, insinuation and veiled theology

Having described Diderot's labours as editor, Morley turns to his labours as contributor, which fill more than four large volumes of the collected works. His verdict is that the whole is vast, uneven and disproportionate: the articles shift between history and controversy, serious treatise and caprice, and some are plainly written to suit Diderot's passing interests or are not what he thinks. Morley then illustrates with miniature moral essays, practical suggestions, trifles on anagrams and names, and an anecdote or two. He argues that Diderot repeatedly digresses to insinuate tolerance, suspended judgment and liberty, and that he treats antiquarian erudition lightly while still compiling a full article on Libraries. He sets Diderot beside Montaigne and Bayle (and Hume), the sceptical trinity, but says Diderot could not rest in doubt. The unit closes on the theological articles, where Morley finds no coherent creed in print, quotes a private letter as Diderot's true mind, and judges the coarse sarcasm of the age partly the Church's own fault.

## Themes

**Disproportion and confusion as the Encyclopædia's texture.** Morley's own judgment: "The confusion is immense." He attributes oddities of scale (the article on the power of the imagination in pregnant women, the long article on Theosophs) to Diderot's momentary enthusiasms, and notes that several entries (Genius, Laborious, Frivolous, Grandeur, Naitre) are essays rather than dictionary entries. This is an editor-contributor working without a rigorous ordered proportion, a counterpoint to any tidy picture of the project as a classification machine (compare [[composable-knowledge]], with the caveat that Morley describes the opposite of composability here).

**Insinuation under censorship.** Morley's chief example is the article on Aius-Locutius, where a Roman minor god becomes the occasion for a proposal that writing against government and religion be forbidden in the vulgar tongue but allowed in a learned one. Morley adds his own dry modern aside. The theological articles, likewise, keep "formal respect" while carrying "veiled and secret sarcasms" for readers who read between the lines. This is the incumbent-power-versus-philosophers dynamic at the level of the individual entry, though Morley describes it as dissimulation, not as a theory of elite illegibility.

**Suspended judgment over erudition.** Following D'Alembert's contempt for antiquarianism, Morley praises the Germans article (etymologies "so forced", the origins a "tissue of guesses") and the Celibacy article (which reproduces the Abbé Saint Pierre's demographic argument). He quotes Diderot blaming historians of opinion for generalising from two rabbis or two Fathers, and calls the doctrine "wholesome". Yet Diderot's Libraries article is long and painstaking, so he is not anti-book.

**Montaigne, Bayle, and the limit of Diderot's scepticism.** Morley quotes Diderot's praise of Montaigne ([[michel-de-montaigne]]) as the touchstone of a good intelligence and of Bayle as a master of ordered doubt, naming them with Hume ([[david-hume]]) as scepticism's "great trinity". His own claim is that Diderot's "vehement and positive demand for emotional certainties" made pure doubt difficult for him.

**Moralising, practical sense, and aphorism.** Diderot abandons an actuarial table in the article Man for an exhortation against saying "To-morrow, to-morrow"; he proposes cab-fare stations and a tax on liveries (too sensible ever to be imposed, in his own phrase as reported). Morley collects aphorisms such as the Stoics as the Jansenists of Paganism.

**Theology: public form versus private mind.** Morley says the articles Jesus Christ and Christianisme are common form or partly insincere, and that Providence yields no coherent creed. He therefore appeals to a private letter, which he reproduces at the risk of shocking the devout, as Diderot's true mind: Christianity as the most absurd and mischievous religion, with a graded preference for Deism with temples as the least harmful fetish. Morley's closing judgment is that the coarse sarcasm was unjust and shallow but that the French Church, lacking English Latitudinarians or later Anglican breadth, shared the blame.

## Notable passages

The opening verdict on confusion and disproportion (c1) states Morley's view of the contributor Diderot. The Aius-Locutius passage (c2) is the clearest worked example of insinuated doctrine, including the learned-tongue proposal. The Germans and Celibacy passage (c3) shows the positive, scientific preference over barren erudition. The Montaigne and Bayle passage (c4) gives Diderot's praise of the sceptics and Morley's limit on his scepticism. The private letter on Christianity (c5) is Diderot's own words as Morley reproduces them, set against the printed articles. The closing paragraph (c6) gives Morley's moral assessment of the sarcasm and shifts blame to the Church, with a pointed comparison to nineteenth-century English churchmanship; this is Morley's verdict and a dated one.
