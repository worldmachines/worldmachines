# Diderot's Letter on the Blind and the doctrine of Relativity

Opening Chapter IV, Morley first rehabilitates [[voltaire]] as a serious popularizer of Newton ([[isaac-newton]]), whose exposition of Berkeley's theory of vision and of Cheselden's 1728 operation on a boy born blind set the problem that Condillac took up in 1746. Réaumur's 1748 operation on a blind girl, which Diderot was refused leave to attend, prompts the Letter on the Blind (1749). Morley summarises its method (questioning a blind man at Puisaux), its anecdotes and its ethical aside, then turns to its treatment of God and introduces Nicholas Saunderson, the blind Cambridge mathematician, whose invented deathbed dialogue with a minister is where the unit stops. Throughout, Morley alternates paraphrase of Diderot with his own corrections, written from a Victorian vantage that finds the Letter "crude and loose" yet historically important.

## Themes

**Vision as inference, and the unreliable experiment.** Morley reports (as Voltaire's reading of Berkeley) that figures, magnitudes and distances are "not sensations but inferences", so that a man born blind and suddenly given sight would not at once discern them; Cheselden's patient was slow to distinguish objects, which was taken to confirm Locke and Berkeley. Diderot, in Morley's account, doubts that the experiment can be made conclusive, since the patient would need philosophical preparation worthy of Newton, Descartes, Locke and [[gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz]] together. Morley endorses this: "In this he was undoubtedly right."

**Relativity as the weapon.** Morley's own central claim: the Letter was the first effective introduction into France of the principles that knowledge is relative to intelligence, that thought is not the measure of existence and that experience does not limit what is possible. He frames this as a social fact: the doctrine was used "as a weapon of combat against the standing organisation", which rested on anthropomorphism and the absolute. This fits Morley's wider thesis that the philosophers' speculative moves served a practical attack on the old order. It loosely rhymes with WMP's interest in intellectual preparation of an interregnum ([[intellectual-vs-revolutionary]]), though Morley is describing a doctrine, not a machine.

**Diderot and Condillac as co-developers.** Morley recounts that Rousseau ([[jean-jacques-rousseau]]) brought Diderot and Condillac together as penniless Paris wanderers, that Diderot found a bookseller for Condillac's first manuscript, and infers from their weekly dinners that Diderot's talk helped move Condillac from Locke's sensation-plus-reflection to the claim of 1754 that reflection is "differently transformed" sensation. The influence, Morley says, ran both ways, since Condillac supplied a text for Diderot's first major work. This is Morley's inference from circumstance, not documented fact.

**The blind man's world.** The Puisaux blind man defines eyes by analogy to his stick and a mirror as a machine that sets things in relief away from themselves; Diderot asks how many philosophers have reasoned with less subtlety. Further observations: the blind have acute memory for voices, the cooperation of the senses is "an obstacle to their perfection", and animals may regard themselves as our equals. Morley interjects corrections, for example that no perfection of touch could disclose phenomena of light.

**Senses, shame and morality: Morley's rebuke.** Diderot says the state of our organs and senses influences our metaphysics and morality, and that a being with an extra sense would find our morality imperfect. Morley judges this a "crude and erroneous" illustration of ethical relativity, because moral ideas are relative to mental constitution and social conditions, not to the number of the senses, and because the artificiality of a convention such as delicacy is no test of its worth.

**From blindness to God; Saunderson.** Morley notes that the God question drew keener attention than the sphere-and-cube question, and that Diderot's treatment of the narrower question is the more coherent, since the born-blind cannot supply decisive evidence on the "insoluble enigma". He sketches Saunderson (professor 1711 to 1739, sceptic "or something more", inventor of a computing machine) as the exemplar Diderot chose, and notes that Diderot professes to reproduce his discussion of final causes.

## Notable passages

The passage on Relativity (c1) is Morley's thesis in miniature and carries his hedged claim that Diderot did not discover or establish the doctrine but introduced it "at the moment when circumstances were ripe for it". The Condillac passage (c2) gives the intellectual genealogy and the Rousseau anecdote. The passage on the impossible experiment (c3) shows Diderot's methodological caution as Morley reads it. The Puisaux blind man's mirror (c4) is the Letter's most quoted illustration of how a sense limits concepts. Morley's rebuke on morality (c5) is a good example of his habit of correcting Diderot from a later, social-scientific standpoint. The Saunderson introduction (c6) sets up the Letter's atheistic argument, which falls in the next unit.
