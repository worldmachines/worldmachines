# Diderot's theory of the drama and the failure of his plays

The opening of Morley's Chapter VII takes up Diderot as dramatist and theorist. Morley first frames the theory as part of the larger movement of the age: just as the Encyclopædia glorified pacific industry and civil justice, Diderot's theory of the drama glorified private virtues and domestic life, and the torch of dramatic art then passed to Lessing and Goethe. He then reports Lessing's high estimate of Diderot as critic, and delivers his own harsh verdict on the two plays (The Natural Son, borrowed from Goldoni, and The Father of the Family, somewhat better). Alongside the verdict he separates what he thinks sound in the theory (the use of pantomime and gesture) from what he thinks wrong (the stage as a pulpit for virtuous maxims). The unit closes with a generous portrait of Diderot's modesty toward better dramatists such as Sedaine and Collé.

## Themes

**Drama as the domestic counterpart of the Encyclopædia's positive spirit.** This is Morley's own interpretive frame, offered with a hedge: he says Diderot need not have consciously surveyed the whole movement of his time, only thought sincerely and "in the spirit of reality", and so found the strong currents. The rise of civil justice and industry over feudal privilege, and of domestic virtue over patriotic devotion, are for Morley "two great sides of a single movement". Morley says the French had only a glimpse of the promised land in art; moral energy then went into social action. This fits his book-wide thesis that the Encyclopædia's era turned from speculation to social and practical attack; it is Morley's reading, not Diderot's own statement.

**Diderot as critic, not creator (Lessing's verdict).** Morley reports Lessing's remark that since Aristotle no more philosophical mind than Diderot's has treated of the theatre, and that Lessing translated Diderot's plays and essay and owed him an impulse. Morley's own judgement is that Diderot lacked Lessing's dramatic fire but had "the eye of the philosophic critic", and that Diderot "invented German criticism". This is a small instance of the book's recurring claim that Diderot's influence ran through others, and it bears on how ideas travel between national traditions (the [[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]] connection is only gestured at in the unit's reference to Lessing and Goethe).

**The plays judged as failures.** Morley calls The Natural Son "one of the most vapid performances in dramatic history", supporting this with Lessing's reservations, a lone sympathy with the hostile Palissot, Rosenkranz's partial defence, the comparison with Goldoni's The True Friend (from which Diderot borrowed the structure), and the stage history (written 1757, staged once in 1771). He sets the failure on stage against fourteen years of success in print, which he attributes to "unflinching partisanship". The Father of the Family (written 1758, acted 1761) is better, popular in Germany and Italy, and revived in France in 1769, but "never struck root". Madame de Staël's line is quoted: Diderot put "the affectation of nature in the place of the affectation of convention". Morley's verdicts are his own and are those of a Victorian critic comparing with French comedy of his day.

**Pantomime and the inadequacy of language.** Diderot, according to Morley, argued that since pantomime does so much in life it should do much on the stage, and that the dramatist should write out the stage business. Morley thinks writing the action into the text makes the plays read like bald romances, yet endorses the principle about pantomime in the highest drama and agrees with Diderot's laments over the poverty of language. The stopped-ears playgoing experiment (told in the Letter on the Deaf and Dumb) shows a deep conviction, which Morley states, that language is a "poor, misleading, and utterly inadequate instrument"; he parallels a remark of [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] on definitions. Diderot's letter to [[voltaire]] praising Clairon's silent pathos in Tancred is the supporting illustration.

**Against the stage as pulpit.** Morley's central objection: Diderot held that artists should always have virtue in view and that the arts should unite with the laws in making us love virtue and hate vice. Morley answers that virtue wins us through sympathy kindled by interesting character and action, not by homilies; the Father of the Family's speeches on marriage are his example, and exhortation is called the feeblest bulwark against passion. He also faults the five acts of serious comedy without humour: laughter rightly stirred is itself a means to the moral end Diderot sought. The argument is Morley's own aesthetic liberalism with a side-swipe at pulpit moralising.

**Character and plagiarism.** Morley treats the unacknowledged borrowing from Goldoni as non-deliberate, explained by Diderot's absorption in an idea, and quotes Madame Geoffrin's remark that he "is always like a man who is dreaming". He ends with Diderot's generosity: unaffected delight in Sedaine's The Philosopher without knowing it and appreciation of Collé's play despite Collé's hostility.

## Notable passages

- **c1** states Morley's frame: Diderot as a seeker who "caught the rising illumination", and the theory of the drama as a glorification of private virtue and domestic life in a single movement with the rise of civil justice and industry.
- **c2** is the Lessing passage and Morley's claim that Diderot invented German criticism.
- **c3** carries Diderot's argument for writing the pantomime and Morley's qualified approval.
- **c4** is the stopped-ears experiment and Diderot's distrust of language.
- **c5** is Morley's core objection to the moralising stage, contrasting sympathy with homily.
- **c6** is his critique of serious comedy without laughter, and a further statement of his judgement of Diderot's method.

World Machines resonance is slight in this unit; the nearest rhyme is with the book's wider picture of the Enlightenment's social turn, and there is no genuine engagement here with legibility or machine concepts.
