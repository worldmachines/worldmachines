# The Encyclopædia's ancestry, Diderot's transformation of it, and D'Alembert

This opening section of Chapter V is Morley's own historical narrative with a few embedded quotations (Diderot's Prospectus, Chambers, Michelet, Condorcet, D'Alembert). He first surveys earlier encyclopædic attempts, judging them by whether they were governed by a real idea of order. He then names Francis Bacon as the true parent of Diderot's scheme and Ephraim Chambers's Cyclopædia as its occasion, recounts how the French translation project passed from Mills and Sellius through Le Breton and the Abbé Gua de Malves to Diderot, who in Morley's telling turned it into something far larger. A long portrait of D'Alembert follows, as the mathematical editor and the temperamental opposite of Diderot. Morley closes by claiming that the rise of an independent literary profession displaced the clergy as moral teachers, and that the Encyclopædia worked as a collective engine of that change.

## Themes

**Encyclopædism as an idea of order, not a list.** Morley's own criterion is that a real encyclopædia presents knowledge as a totality of organically connected parts. By this test he rates Aristotle as first glimpsing the idea, Pliny as not truly encyclopædic ("presided over by no definite idea of informing order"), Roger Bacon's Opus Majus as the best medieval attempt, and Alsted as merely arranging separate manuals under a verbal and logical rather than an objective division. Chambers had the right aim (things considered "relatively, or as they respect each other") but, in Morley's verdict, lacked the grasp to realise it. This resonates with the project's interest in classification and [[composable-knowledge]], though Morley is framing a history of an idea and does not talk about infrastructure.

**English parentage.** Morley, writing as an Englishman, stresses that both the efficient origin (Francis Bacon, acknowledged by Diderot in the Prospectus and by D'Alembert in the Preliminary Discourse) and the occasion (Chambers's 1727 Cyclopædia) were English. He cites Leibnitz ([[gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz]]) as endorsing the improvement of Alsted's book a century after it appeared.

**Diderot's transforming intuition.** On Morley's account Diderot was needy, took over a stalled translation project, and saw that the encyclopædic form could gather "all that new thought and modern knowledge" then existing "in unsystematic and uninterpreted fragments" into a whole. Michelet's anecdote about D'Aguesseau (quoted by Morley) is the testimony of a third party to Diderot's persuasive power; Morley presents it without endorsing its romantic colouring. Division of labour followed: Le Breton took on three further booksellers for capital, and Diderot, admitting his weakness in mathematics and physics, recruited D'Alembert.

**D'Alembert.** Morley portrays him as a foundling of Madame de Tencin, a Jansenist-educated geometer, an independent man who refused offers from Frederick and Catherine, a stylist and correspondent of Voltaire ([[voltaire]]), sickly and irritable, a "precisian" where Diderot was "exuberant, prodigal, and disordered". The details are narrative; the evaluative adjectives are Morley's.

**The new teaching order.** Morley's main argument in this unit: Diderot and D'Alembert made the profession of letters "definite and independent", rejecting suitorship and patronage, and thereby "the highest moral ideas now belonged no longer to the clergy, but to the writers". He then claims, in his own liberal-positivist voice, that the church eventually absorbed the philosophers' ideas, so that Voltaire, Diderot and Rousseau ([[jean-jacques-rousseau]]) were "the true reformers" of the Catholic creed. This is a Victorian and partisan reading, aimed at his own day's churches. It sits near the project's concern with the intellectual's relation to institutional power ([[intellectual-vs-revolutionary]]), though Morley frames it as moral and religious succession, not strategy.

**The encyclopædic band as a unit.** Morley concedes that the "single body with a common doctrine" was a logical fiction but holds that the popular impression was practically sound, and credits Comte with seeing the value of the "encyclopædic workshop" that united destructive schools in a constructive task. Diderot and D'Alembert cultivated the sentiment with contributor lists, éloges for the dead, and the metaphor of an Academy's transactions.

## Notable passages

**Bacon as true parent (c1).** Morley dismisses the long list of predecessors and names Bacon's idea of systematic classification as what "inspired Diderot, and guided his hand throughout", quoting Diderot's Prospectus acknowledging a debt to the chancellor who sketched a universal dictionary of sciences and arts when there were "not, so to say, either arts or sciences".

**Alsted as a failed encyclopædia (c2).** A compact example of Morley's test: Alsted announces a single syntagma but only assembles manuals and dictionaries, and organises them by verbal and logical division, in line with the age's preference for the verbal sciences.

**Diderot takes over (c3).** The passage where the bookseller's project is transformed by Diderot's "admirable intuition" and Chambers is demoted to "a mere starting-point".

**Letters as a new order (c4).** The D'Alembert and Diderot alliance, their break with patronage, and D'Alembert's own words about men of letters living "united and almost shut up among themselves" and giving "the law to the rest of the nation in all affairs of taste and philosophy".

**Philosophy reforms the church (c5).** Morley's central claim about the supersession of the clergy by writers and the transformation of Catholicism, ending with the line that such leavening of one doctrine by a foreign one is "the most interesting process in the history of truth". Treat as Morley's thesis, dated and openly partisan.

**The band and its fraternity (c6).** Comte's praise of the encyclopædic workshop and the editors' devices for creating a sense of collective authorship.
