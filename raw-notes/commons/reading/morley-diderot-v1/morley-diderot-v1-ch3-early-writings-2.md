# Diderot's first sceptical writings: passions, doubt, and natural religion

Morley continues his account of Diderot's early work by sampling the *Philosophical Thoughts* to show "the scope" of the book and Diderot's state of mind. He first quotes Diderot's praise of strong passions (and a later letter to Madame Voland) and reads in it a hatred of Christian asceticism; then a run of numbered extracts on scepticism, Pascal, the cruelty of the received God, the human origin of the biblical canon, miracles and testimony, and the deists' Cicero-style argument. Morley then offers his own diagnosis of the method of eighteenth-century controversy, adds Diderot's startling concession to the atheist's dice argument, summarises the follow-up *On the Sufficiency of Natural Religion*, and closes with a long account of Shaftesbury's permanent influence on Diderot's ethics and aesthetics.

## Themes

**Passion as the key-note.** Morley takes Diderot's opening apology for strong passions as "the key-note to Diderot's whole character" (Morley's verdict). Diderot's own words: only strong passions raise the soul to great things; "Sober passions produce only the commonplace." Morley sets this against the Church's ideal of heroism in effacement and judges that Diderot, "borne to the other extreme, left out even discipline" -- a hedged moral judgement that is Morley's, not Diderot's.

**Scepticism as touchstone, with a demand for rigour.** In Diderot's quoted sections scepticism is the touchstone; demi-scepticism is a mark of a feeble understanding; yet true scepticism requires counting and weighing reasons, and Diderot admits that "Every mind has its own telescope." He also argues that the pious ought to welcome universal examination. Morley adds that Diderot, like Voltaire and Condorcet (see [[voltaire]], [[nicolas-de-condorcet]]), always had Pascal in mind, and that the work is formally an answer to Pascal's *Thoughts*.

**Attack on revelation by evidential and moral argument.** Diderot (as quoted): the God of the preachers is a tyrant one would wish did not exist; the biblical canon rests on human rules of criticism so faith can only be proportioned to those; testimony weakens as improbability grows; children are taught religion without regard to capacity. Morley's own analysis: the eighteenth-century assailant demanded that the defender answer every objection of one who had never been a Christian, ignoring "the mental conditions by which a creed is engendered"; the modern apologist "escapes by explaining religion," whereas the older one was required to prove it. This is Morley's positivist/historical criticism of the method, and the 1878 contrast is his period view.

**Theism with a dangerous concession.** Morley stresses the position is "distinctly theistic," yet Diderot quotes a professor's dice-and-Iliad argument against atheists and replies that, given eternal matter in motion and infinite throws, the improbability of an ordered world is compensated by the number of throws. Morley records this as showing how forcibly the other side impressed him; he does not draw further consequences here. (It foreshadows the materialism of later work, but the unit itself does not say so.)

**Natural religion as halting-place; Shaftesbury.** In *On the Sufficiency of Natural Religion* Diderot says revelation adds no moral precept and no intelligible new truth. Morley judges there is no sign Diderot had examined the positive grounds of natural religion or answered Butler, and that he was "content with Shaftesbury's position." Morley then argues that Shaftesbury's optimistic harmony (coming after Leibniz, see [[gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz]]), the moral sense in the human breast, and the tie between beauty and morality gave Diderot's criticism of art its ethical colouring. Appeals to a law of nature against customary ordinances are presented as the analogue in religion of the legal appeal to the law natural.

A loose rhyme with World Machines: the demand that a creed be reduced to clear, intelligible, testable propositions echoes the modernity-machine drive toward legibility ([[modernity-machine]]), and Morley's critique (that it ignores how creeds are historically produced) is a complaint about what legibility-style reasoning leaves out. This is only a half-fit: nothing in the unit speaks of infrastructure or institutions directly.

## Notable passages

**The apology for passion.** Diderot's opening of the Thoughts and the Voland letter, including the Racine dilemma, which Morley declines to resolve as "casuistry."

**Demi-scepticism.** The sharpest statement of Diderot's early epistemic stance: refusing to examine privileged notions is itself a sign of unbelief.

**Morley on the method of controversy.** The most analytical passage in the unit: why demanding proof from natural premisses ends in negation and leaves the sceptic weighing one revealed system against another.

**The dice argument.** Diderot gives the atheist better arguments than the apologist did, using the analysis of chances.

**Sufficiency of natural religion.** Revelation adds nothing to morality and only propositions as unintelligible as "ancient Carthaginian."

**Shaftesbury.** Morley's account of why naturalism felt like dawn to men who knew religion through the Bull Unigenitus and confessional certificates.
