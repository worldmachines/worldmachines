# The Encyclopædia's positive spirit: labour, science, religion, discussion, toleration

This second half of Morley's survey of the Encyclopædia's general contents takes the work as a social and intellectual document. He begins with its "earnest enthusiasm" for productive industry, shown in the plates and in Diderot's visits to workshops, and reads it as honour transferred from knights and friars to labouring men and as a move from words to things. He then corrects the "common accounts" of its religion (in his view rationalistic scepticism aimed at sacerdotalism and miracle, not dogmatic atheism), praises the effect of its massed secular knowledge and the Preliminary Discourse's story of ordered progress, and closes on its ideal of many-sided discussion and its commitment to toleration. Throughout, the verdicts are Morley's own, a Victorian liberal-positivist's, and he writes with an eye on his own century's religious controversies.

## Themes

**Labour and the arts as a new basis of honour.** Morley compares the Encyclopædia to Virgil's Georgics as a "glorification of labour" and credits Diderot, "the son of a cutler", with inherited sympathy for skill. He reads the plates as "an animated scene of human life", and describes Diderot working at looms and having machines taken apart in order to instruct others. This is the infrastructure side of the project: a storehouse of the arts detailed enough to be reconstructed "after a deluge". It resonates with [[Legibility]] and [[composable-knowledge]] (making the realm's productive workings readable and reusable), though Morley frames it as moral and social honour for labour, not as administrative legibility.

**A social and an intellectual revolution.** Morley says the emphasis on science and the practical arts marks two things. The first is the association of honour with "pacific labour", the "recognition of the basis of a new society". He quotes the articles Journalier and Homme on the fate of the labouring majority, and says the nobles' and churchmen's failure to see this "was the fatality of the Revolution" (his judgement). The second is "the substitution of interest in things for interest in words", completing the movement of Bacon and Descartes. Diderot's preference for the essay over the treatise, and the Bibliomanie article's contempt for bookishness, are offered as evidence; Morley adds that studying the pathology of the past mind is not useless, a mild dissent from D'Alembert.

**Religion: sceptical and secularising, not atheistic.** Morley holds that accounts of the Encyclopædia as preaching dogmatic atheism are "almost universally misrepresented". In his reading the attack was social, directed at sacerdotalism, which required first toleration and then the destruction of the idea of miracle. The destruction works indirectly: through scientific exposition readers "lose their interest, rather than their belief, in mysteries", the "normal process of theological dissolution". Diderot's separation of science and philosophy from theology was meant to save philosophy, not theology, which marks him as "a constructive thinker". Morley also concedes the language is not consistent and that the censorship limited what could be said.

**The mass of secular knowledge and its classification.** Despite Diderot's own long list of defects, Morley says the union of secular acquisitions in one work gave them a "massive and sumptuous front" and made theology look "a shrivelled thing". He finds the genealogical tree borrowed from Bacon faulty (it follows faculties of the understanding rather than objective relations, so printing lands under Logic and architecture is scattered), yet judges that the effect of vastness and a "guiding thread" survives the objections.

**The Preliminary Discourse and anti-system method.** Morley credits the Discourse, mostly D'Alembert's, with a survey of progress (Bacon, Descartes, Newton, Locke, Leibnitz; see [[isaac-newton]]) that conveys "ordered growth and measured progress". Its two conditions of success are disregard of authority and avoidance of premature system. Both told against theology, which is authoritative and systematic. Descartes is honoured as the opener of the way even as his disciples' vortices and innate ideas are dropped. The ideal is "the spirit of system, yet never to construct a system".

**Discussion and toleration.** The Fermes Générales article and its editors' note show a wish to reach the best opinion by liberal debate, although Morley notes that the promised Régie article was never properly written after "eight years of storm". On toleration, Morley says the Encyclopædists were the most ardent propagators, in a France of the wheel, banishments and persecution of Protestants. The Tolérance article reproduces Locke and, oddly, Rousseau's Social Contract on atheists (see [[jean-jacques-rousseau]]), while Diderot's Intolérance is a series of maxims delivered in anger. Morley judges that this plea, more than the attack on dogma, inflamed the churchmen, "for most ecclesiastics valued power more dearly than truth".

## Notable passages

- **Diderot in the workshops** (c1): Morley's account of Diderot learning crafts so as to instruct others, and the aim of a reconstructible storehouse of the arts. The workshop visits are told as received ("It has often been told"), and D'Alembert's defence of arts and trades against scholarly trivia follows.
- **The two elements of the transition** (c2): honour for labour and the Journalier and Homme quotations, then the turn from words to things. The Revolution-as-fatality remark is Morley's own.
- **Religion and miracle** (c3): the central corrective, with Rosenkranz cited and Morley's claim that the Encyclopædia worked by displacing interest, not by arguing disbelief. The comparison with Jupiter and Juno is Morley's.
- **Preliminary Discourse** (c4): progress, authority and system, the Descartes qualification, and Montesquieu's remark on French attachment to old opinions.
- **Open discussion** (c5): the Fermes Générales / Régie note, framed by Morley as the original intention, only partially realised.
- **Toleration** (c6): the historical urgency of toleration in France, Tolérance and Intolérance, and Morley's reason why the churchmen hated it most. Morley's dating of the 'common view' of atheism is a nineteenth-century claim, and his religious generalisations about priests are strongly his own.
