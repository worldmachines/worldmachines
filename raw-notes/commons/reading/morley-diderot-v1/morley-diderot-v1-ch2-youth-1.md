# Diderot's Youth and the Rise of the Man of Letters

Chapter II opens Morley's biography proper. He places Diderot's birth at Langres in 1713 against Rousseau, [[voltaire]], [[david-hume]] and Kant, describes the cutlery-making family and the town's climate-shaped temperament, and narrates Diderot's Jesuit schooling, his father's tender handling of his runaway wish, and his refusal of law and medicine. Morley then widens out: the Paris man of letters of c. 1750 was a poverty-stricken drudge, yet in Morley's account he represented a new order which inherited the spiritual power of the Jesuits. The unit closes with the ten years (1734-1744) of tutoring, garrets and hunger, and Morley's verdict on the temperament that came through them intact.

## Themes

**Temperament from place and kin.** Diderot himself, as Morley quotes him, explains the Langres character by its violent weather: the man of Langres has a head "like the weathercock", quick in desire and idea but "cumbrous in speech". Morley endorses this ("This was thoroughly true") and reads Diderot's versatility, impetuosity and clumsy expression as inheritances. The father (grinder's apron, tears at the school prizes), the Diogenes-like sister and the devout abbe brother are drawn largely in Diderot's own words, with the brother serving as a foil: Diderot's account has religion as the "mischievous art of stifling nature".

**Jesuit pupil, Jesuit successor.** Morley says Diderot was, like many eighteenth-century reformers, a pupil of the Jesuits, who hoped to recruit him. His main argument here is his own judgement: the men of letters were not only pupils but the "immediate successors" of the Jesuits as teachers and directors of society. The transfer of the spiritual power from ecclesiastical hands, not the prices paid for books, is for Morley the significance of the change. He gives the Order credit its rationalist critics withhold (a practical, social habit of mind), while blaming it for laxity in moral rigour. This is the book's thesis-in-miniature about authority passing to a lay intellectual class; it loosely rhymes with WMP's interest in the intellectual preparing a revolution ([[intellectual-vs-revolutionary]]) and in legitimacy transferring between institutions, though Morley is describing a succession of functions, not machines.

**Definition of the man of letters.** Morley distinguishes the man of letters from the scholar, the systematic thinker and the imaginative creator. His is a dispersive, empirical role: popularising detached ideas that society can assimilate, dressing them in literary forms, and judging each case as it arises. Unlike the Jesuit he has no organic doctrine, tradition or discipline. Morley hedges ("We have no wish to exalt the office") and cites an unnamed "deep observer" on discipline, but argues that critical ages cannot be organised. Fontenelle (1657-1757) is the first great type. Note this is Morley's Comtean-tinged framing, and the Encyclopaedia is not yet named in the unit except as a terminus for Fontenelle's career.

**Poverty as the common lot of letters.** Morley lists Fielding, Johnson, Goldoni, Rousseau, Lessing and others as men who were doubtful of dinner; only Voltaire, Gray and Richardson escaped, since none lived by the pen. Diderot's own ten years of pupils paying in shirts and chairs, a tutorship quit because "What I want is not to live better, but to avoid dying", and the Shrove Tuesday swoon are told as anecdote.

**Character verdict.** Morley's own evaluation: Diderot was uncramped by formal mannerisms, uncorrupted by the culture-affectation that Goethe ([[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]]) "infected part of the world" with, never made his own life the centre of the world, and judged people "wholly to what they were". This is Morley's moralising portrait, and sets Diderot's expansiveness against self-cultivation as an end in itself.

## Notable passages

**The weathercock (c1).** Diderot's own explanation of Langres character, in the climatic-determinist taste Morley attributes to his generation. It gives the book's first picture of Diderot as mobile, swift and awkward in speech.

**The prize wreaths (c2).** The father melting to tears is the fullest piece of evidence for Diderot's affection for him; it is Diderot's recollection, retold by Morley.

**The new order (c3).** Morley's central claim of the chapter: the men of letters as a new order succeeding the Jesuits and taking over spiritual power.

**The man of letters defined (c4).** The most explicit definition of the type: dispersive, popularising and empirical, without doctrine.

**Shrove Tuesday (c5).** The vow never to refuse help to a living creature; Morley's gloss is that no oath was more faithfully kept and that adversity which does not make a man a miser or misanthrope proves a rich nature.

**Judging by what people are (c6).** Morley's closing summary of Diderot's disposition, with its explicit contrast to Goethean self-development.

Dated note: the Jesuit and Goethe judgements are Morley's Victorian opinions, not neutral history.
