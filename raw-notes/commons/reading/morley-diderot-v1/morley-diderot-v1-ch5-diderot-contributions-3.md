# Diderot's Liberty article, the political articles, and Morley's closing verdict on the Encyclopædia

This unit finishes Morley's chapter on the Encyclopædia. It opens with the article on Liberty, which Morley treats as shallow and probably insincere, and which he sets against a 1756 letter to Landois in which Diderot flatly denies liberty; Morley's explanation is that Diderot wrote what authority required. He then turns to the political and social articles (Luxury, Pouvoir/Souverain/Autorité, Multitude, Misery, Représentants, Legislator), assessing Diderot's democratic credentials, his views on property and representation, and his optimism about commerce and the decline of narrow patriotism, which Morley calls an anticipation of Cobden. The unit ends with a general reflection on what the Encyclopædia failed to be and a valedictory image of it as a ruin of a stronghold from which a band went out to strike a blow for humanity and truth. Throughout, the judgements are Morley's, a Victorian liberal-positivist reading, not Diderot's.

## Themes

**Social test versus truth (Morley's diagnosis).** Morley argues that Diderot's habit of measuring every conclusion by its presumed effect on society has its own danger, the same one ecclesiastics fall into when they resist free discussion lest it damage useful beliefs. In the Liberty article Diderot, on Morley's reading, forgets that Truth is the best of the things good for society, and writes that denial of liberty "ought to be punished by the magistrates", which Morley notes is exactly what the Jesuits said of attacks on God and the church.

**Confused free-will argument (Morley's philosophical criticism).** Morley says Diderot treats Determinists as if they denied that volitions exist, when the real question is whether they are caused; that Diderot equates liberty with the existence of will or the healthy state of the soul; and that the wager against Bayle ignores the antecedent supplied by the desire to save a thousand pistoles. He adds that murder remains an evil even if the murderer's will had antecedents.

**Self-contradiction and mystification.** Morley sets the article beside Diderot's 1756 letter to Landois, which says liberty is a word void of sense and only physical causes exist. His only explanation is that Diderot wrote what persons in authority required, as in the articles on Jesus Christ and the Bible. He calls such mystifications odious and shameful, a degrading price for the work, while also seeing in the performance evidence of Diderot's dramatic power as dialectician, casuist and sophist. This is a case study in censorship shaping the content of a knowledge project; it fits [[illegibility-as-elite-defense]] only loosely, since here it is the incumbent's pressure producing a distorted text rather than concealment by the elite.

**Political articles: sense, sympathy for the poor, distrust of the crowd.** Morley praises Luxury as sensible and free of commonplace and calls Pouvoir, Souverain, Autorité a retelling of the old unhistoric trust story. He stresses that Diderot and his school were not democratic in a Jacobin or anarchic sense: Multitude and Misery set a fastidious contempt for the crowd's judgement beside a fervent wish for its dignity and a blame of sovereigns for misery. Morley thinks this combination is rare among men of letters.

**Representation, property, and a standing democratic difficulty.** Under Représentants Diderot, in Morley's account, argues like Montesquieu for an assembly that assists a royal government, with citizenship founded on property and no thought of universal suffrage. Morley points out a logical tension: if each class must be represented by those who feel its needs, the poorest and most numerous need direct representation, yet Diderot describes them as ignorant. Morley adds that the Legislator article mixes outdated enthusiasm for Lycurgus, China and Peru (the influence of Rousseau's political sentimentalism, see [[jean-jacques-rousseau]]) and the belief that government should make its people virtuous, with sound points about taxing the rich. He contrasts the later reception of Turgot's reasoned edict prefaces, which provoked more than the laws themselves.

**Commerce, internationalism, and their costs.** Morley finds the most interesting part of the Legislator article in its anticipation of what England associates with Cobden: interdependence through commerce, the ruin of Leipsic, Lisbon and Lima felt on every exchange, and the weakening of narrow patriotism as nations compare themselves. Diderot also discerns the cost: commerce, like enlightenment, may extinguish magnanimous disinterestedness in favour of hard justice. Morley dismisses as falsified the claim that conquest must become a moral impossibility, noting Napoleon was then a child in arms. This resonates with the progress-and-industry thread ([[adam-smith]], [[the-relentless-revolution]]), though Morley is speaking in Cobdenite free-trade terms, not offering machine theory.

**What the Encyclopædia failed to be.** In his concluding remark Morley says that Diderot, the one Philosopher able to grasp it, failed to rise from mechanical arrangement of knowledge to a conception of the unity of the intellectual history of man, seeing religious and other systems as aspects of intellectual effort rather than superstition. Such an idea would have made an Encyclopædia indeed, a survey of the questions and answers of the world. This is Morley's own positivist-historical desideratum, and it touches [[composable-knowledge]] and the aim of a [[history-machine]]-style synthesis only as a loose rhyme. He still holds that an urgent social task excused the lack of completeness, and he closes with the image of a monumental ruin.

## Notable passages

**Liberty as insincere (c1).** The opening paragraph sets out Morley's charge and quotes the article's demand that denial of liberty be punished by magistrates, then compares it to Jesuit reasoning.

**The contradiction with the Landois letter (c2).** The 1756 letter denying liberty entirely, Morley's explanation of forced pretence, and his remark that the fervour of the false case shows Diderot's dramatic power.

**Multitude (c3).** Diderot's article telling the reader to distrust the multitude in reasoning, morality and sentiment, but that it is right in the end because it echoes the few sensible men who shape posterity's judgement.

**Représentants (c4).** Morley's presentation of property-based citizenship and the tension with representing each class by those who feel its needs.

**Cobdenic commerce (c5).** The passage on commerce as a new bond among men, the decline of national enthusiasm, and the cost to magnanimity.

**The closing verdict (c6).** What the Encyclopædia lacked, why it was not postponed for philosophic completeness, and the elegy for the monumental ruin and its band going out to strike a blow for humanity and truth.
