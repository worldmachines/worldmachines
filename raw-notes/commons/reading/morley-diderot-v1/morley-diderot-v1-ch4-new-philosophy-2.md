# Saunderson's argument, relativity, and the breach in Catholic metaphysics

This stretch of Morley's chapter on the Letter on the Blind (1749) continues his account of the dying blind geometer Saunderson's conversation with a clergyman. He first notes Diderot's mystification (the invented "Life of Saunderson" by Dr. Inchlif) and the Royal Society's resentment. He then summarises and quotes Saunderson's speeches: the sceptical retort that the wonders of nature are evidence only for the sighted, the elephant-and-tortoise regress against "God" as an explanation, and the conjecture that early nature produced many malformed creatures, of which only self-sustaining combinations survived. Morley glosses this as a rough anticipation of variability and adaptation (the 'modern hypothesis' of his own day), defends it against Voltaire's objection that Saunderson denied God merely because he was blind, and then widens out: the true significance, in his judgement, is the shift from an absolute, anthropocentric view of the universe to a relative one, the necessary first step in dissolving the Catholic intellectual system. The unit closes with Diderot's hedged disavowal of atheism, a suggestion for a tactile language for the deaf-blind, and some literary asides.

## Themes

**Saunderson's anti-design argument (Diderot's words as Morley quotes them).** The blind professor says the argument from the wonders of nature is weak for a man who cannot see them, that astonishment is the habit of treating whatever exceeds our strength as a prodigy, and that invoking a Being merely creates "a new knot" harder than the first. The elephant-and-tortoise image makes the point as a regress. He then asks "leave me free" to doubt that today's order always held, proposing instead that early matter in motion produced shapeless or faulty creatures and that "all the faulty (vicieuses) combinations of matter disappeared" while only self-supporting ones persisted. He extends the same logic to whole worlds and to the world's duration (the ephemeral insect who is more reasonable than the one who thinks the world eternal).

**Morley's reading as a forerunner of Darwinian reasoning (Morley's judgement, hedged by him).** Morley says Diderot has "a distinct enough conception, though in an exceedingly undigested shape" of incessant variability and of adaptation as the condition of survival, and that even as "a bald and unsustained guess" it was a blow at final causes. He adds, as his own view, that the inference of a deity from adaptation ignores the "marvels of mis-adaptation" and the "appalling law of merciless and incessant destruction." This is explicitly framed by Morley as a discussion that is "the familiar discussion of the day" for his 1878 readers; it reads now as a period gloss on a pre-Darwinian passage, and Morley himself calls the idea undigested. It rhymes with the later treatment of selection without a designer (see [[dennett-darwins-dangerous-idea]]), though Saunderson's version is a conjectural thought-experiment on chance combinations, not a mechanism of descent.

**Voltaire's objection and Morley's rebuttal.** Voltaire (see [[voltaire]]), in a playfully polite reply, dissented from Saunderson, "who denied God, because he happened to have been born blind." Morley concedes that the demand to "touch" God makes one sense the arbiter of all ideas, which is "absurd," but argues that this is not the centre of the argument: the case against final causes would have come as fitly from a sighted man.

**Relativity versus absolutism as the real philosophical battle (Morley's thesis).** For Morley, Catholicism was not only an institution and a faith but "a systematised theory of the universe" that made man the final cause of the cosmos and every judgement absolute. Mere rationalistic attacks on scripture or miracles could only partially destroy it; the attack "must begin in philosophy", by pressing "the relativity of our ideas" and freeing thought from anthropomorphism. He distinguishes the Church as a social organisation "made vital by a conviction" from the Empire as a military organisation, and says the greatest fact of eighteenth-century intellectual history is the overthrow of that sustaining conviction. He notes that the Catholic reaction's sharpest philosophical champion, Joseph de Maistre, first attacked the principle of relativity (compare [[counter-enlightenment]]; Morley's framing is that of a positivist partisan, and the universalism he defends sits in the tradition of [[enlightenment-monism]], though here he is arguing for relativity of faculties, not a single system).

**Diderot's hedged theism and practical hints.** Diderot's own reply to Voltaire says the 'order' is a metaphysical entity in our minds (the ruin-dwelling worm and ant who admire the gardener), and affirms belief in God in the morning light; Morley suspects this denial of atheism is "hypocritical", pointing to the care Diderot takes to argue Saunderson's side, and quotes the line that belief or disbelief in God is "not important at all." Morley also praises Diderot's suggestion of a touch-based written language for the deaf-blind, which he says Dr. Howe of Boston later realised.

## Notable passages

**The elephant and tortoise (c1).** The core of Saunderson's scepticism: wonder is a function of our limits, and "God" as a stopper just becomes a harder knot. The 'old' question of what sustains the sustainer is the engine of the passage.

**Chaos, monsters, survival (c2).** The speech that Morley singles out as foreshadowing the theory that was in his day "the great stimulus to speculation": malformed combinations vanish and only self-perpetuating mechanisms survive.

**Morley's gloss on variability and adaptation (c3).** A compact statement of what Morley takes the passage to anticipate and why it hurts the doctrine of final causes, hedged as a "guess".

**Voltaire's objection and the mis-adaptation argument (c4).** Morley's own critical argument: design-from-adaptation is partial and relative, and omits mis-adaptation and destruction.

**Absolute versus relative; the Church as a conviction (c5).** Morley's central historical claim for the chapter: the decisive revolution of the century was the passage from an absolute, man-centred cosmology to a relative one, which was the "all-important preliminary" to breaking up Catholic construction. A structural, institution-and-ideas reading (a Church as social organisation held up by a conviction) that echoes, only loosely, WMP's notion of a world machine resting on shared assumptions ([[world-machines]]), though Morley is making a narrower claim about one intellectual system.

**The worm, the ant and Micromegas (c6).** Diderot's own image of insects admiring the 'architecture' of the ruins they shelter in, which Morley links to Voltaire's Micromegas and calls the type of the philosophy on which Catholicism rests.
