# Diderot's Letter on the Deaf and Dumb, and his imprisonment at Vincennes

This unit has two parts. Section II briefly considers Diderot's 1751 Letter on the Deaf and Dumb, which Morley calls substantially a fragment on aesthetics (deferred to another chapter) but which touches the line of thought of the Letter on the Blind: Diderot proposes to study the natural order of ideas through the language of gesture, and to 'decompose' man into his senses. Morley ties this to Condillac's Statue, defends Diderot against a charge of borrowing, and judges the gesture section crude by the standards of later research. Section III contrasts English penalties for heresy with French arbitrary imprisonment, narrates Diderot's July 1749 arrest and the Vincennes interrogation, the informers' reports, Voltaire's and Madame du Chatelet's intervention, Rousseau's visits and the genesis of his essay against civilisation, the episode with Madame de Puisieux, and Diderot's release in order to resume the Encyclopaedia, which Morley previews as a heroic labour.

## Themes

**Senses as separate beings, and Condillac's Statue.** Diderot's wish, as Morley quotes it, is to 'decompose' a man and examine what each sense contributes (a 'metaphysical anatomy'). Morley says it was reputed at the time to be the source of Condillac's Statue, then argues Diderot is free of any charge of borrowing: Condillac's book came three years later (1754), Condillac attributed the idea to Mademoiselle Ferrand, and Morley also points to Ibn Tophail's Philosophus Autodidactus as a more scientific precedent. This is Morley's own adjudication of priority.

**Method over metaphysics (a dated judgement).** Morley says Diderot saw that language questions had to be solved 'experientially', not by the old metaphysical methods, but that the time was not ripe. He calls the gesture section astonishingly crude relative to later study of gesture-language; this is an 1878 verdict and is flagged as such by its own reference to progress since Diderot.

**Two systems of repression.** Morley sets English prosecutions (Woolston, Annet, Mandeville), which however harsh were conducted by law, against the French lettre de cachet, 'that vile substitute for prosecution', which he says prepared a revolution. The French state's machinery (police lieutenant Berryer, informers, the priest of Saint Medard) is shown defending itself against a writer of philosophy. This resonates with [[intellectual-vs-revolutionary]] only loosely: Morley frames it as arbitrary authority provoking revolution, not as a theory of intellectuals.

**Diderot's evasions and Morley's moral register.** The interrogation transcript, in which Diderot denies authorship of the Letter on the Blind and Philosophic Thoughts under oath, is called by Morley 'a sorrowful picture' of an apostle's plight. Morley's moralising tone is visible throughout, including his sardonic account of Berryer's later reward.

**Patronage networks.** The governor of Vincennes was a kinsman of Madame du Chatelet; [[voltaire]] set her to write, and Diderot was moved from the dungeon to the chateau with parole and visitors. Release followed pressure from the booksellers interested in the Encyclopaedia.

**Rousseau and the first Discourse.** Morley quotes Rousseau's own account of his visit and of conceiving the essay against civilisation on a walk to Vincennes, and weighs the story that Diderot suggested the paradox that arts and sciences corrupted manners. He rates it not improbable, cites Michelet's tribute, but says the evidence is 'not decisive'; he finds it odd that Diderot, promoting the arts and sciences, would have instigated the attack. See [[jean-jacques-rousseau]].

## Notable passages

Diderot's 'metaphysical anatomy' of the senses; Condillac's Statue; Morley's remark on the experiential method; the opening English-French contrast; the Berryer interrogation; and the Rousseau episode, which ends in Morley's weighing of Diderot's responsibility for Rousseau's paradox. The unit closes with a preview of the Encyclopaedia as an heroic engineering-like labour, deferred to the next chapter.
