# Diderot's private life: Sophie Voland, the dressing-gown, the divided house and Grandval

Morley opens Chapter VI by rebutting the assumption that eighteenth-century unbelievers were Grub Street vagabonds: once settled to his "huge task" Diderot became "a very orderly person", though his attachment to Mademoiselle Voland was, by Morley's lights, no better than his neighbours' (his verdict, with regret). He then uses Diderot's letters to her, their odd provenance, and the playful essay on his old dressing-gown as windows on the man's daily life: the cheerful poverty of his room, a wearing marriage to a wife Morley (following Rousseau) calls a shrew, ferocious editorial labour, and the sociable, talk-filled autumn weeks at Holbach's country house. The unit closes on Morley's own comment on the coarseness of the Grandval talk and on a long letter-passage in which Diderot argues that rich and poor differ little in happiness.

## Themes

**Morley's moral apology.** The chapter is as much Morley's judgement as Diderot's life. He excuses the liaison by the manners of the time (marriage "habitually regarded as merely an official relation"), insists that the Voland connection was decorous and respectable, and says openly that one may be "sorry" Diderot was no better than his neighbours. Later he treats the grossness of Grandval talk as a matter of fashion while insisting that calling such things "only points of manners" does not make them "unimportant". These are Morley's Victorian evaluations, not Diderot's.

**The letters as a documentary monument and a small mystery.** Morley stresses the authenticity of the Voland letters on stylistic grounds, then recounts a murky transmission (Jeudy-Dugour, who sold manuscripts in 1829 and published in 1830; the Hermitage library; Grimm) and concludes that the "spirit of mystification" of the eighteenth century still hovers over Diderot. The unit also shows the letters' physical infrastructure: Mademoiselle Voland's replies were routed through Damilaville's revenue office, which Morley reads as an instance of connivance between authority and its foes. This echoes the book's wider picture of the circle of [[voltaire]] and the philosophers using official machinery for their own correspondence, though Morley here offers only an anecdotal sketch.

**Elective poverty and luxury's tyranny.** In the Regrets on My Old Dressing-gown (quoted at length by Morley) a single piece of scarlet luxury forces the engravings, chairs, books and table of the philosopher's room into consistency with itself until "the edifying retreat of the philosopher became transformed into the scandalous cabinet of the farmer-general". The humour carries a real moral claim, that things and status cohere into a total environment that captures the owner, and that the old list carpet keeps pride in check. It is a small domestic version of material culture reshaping the person, a rhyme at most with the project's interest in infrastructure shaping assumptions, and entirely unconcerned with world-machine scale.

**The divided house.** Morley sets Diderot's happy cheerfulness abroad against a sour marriage: Madame Diderot is, on the authority of [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] as Morley cites him, a shrew and a scold; Morley says blame cannot be exactly divided, then moralises about the ignorance and narrowness imposed on women by the social ideal (citing Wesley to his wife) and about the exclusion of women from public interests. That social diagnosis is Morley's own, in his reforming-liberal register. Brighter scenes (the daughter's fête-day speech, the lost pinks) are Diderot's account in his letters.

**Industry as a way of life.** Diderot works ten hours a day for a month at proof-sheets, wears his eyes out over plates and diagrams, falls asleep over a candle and burns half his papers, and keeps the accident from his wife. Morley uses this to show the labour behind the Encyclopædia and the persecution Diderot expected as its fruit; it is a human-scale view of an editorial infrastructure project, not an analysis of it.

**Grandval and the culture of talk.** Diderot's autumns at Holbach's house (from 1759) are painted as a model day: morning work, large dinner, long walk with talk of history, politics, chemistry, literature, physics, morality, cards and early sleep. The guests are sketched in a line each (Grimm, Galiani, Marmontel, Helvétius), and Diderot plays Socrates, Panurge, Pantophile. Morley says that if one could be transported into that decade for a day, one might pass it at Grandval. This is a vivid portrait of the salon-as-knowledge-network in miniature, though Morley treats it as biography and not as a theory of how ideas spread.

**Equalising reflection.** The closing letter-passage argues that with sound mind and a pure conscience "the rest is nothing", that rich and poor differ little in happiness, and that trouble is universal. Morley presents it as sincere, spontaneous reflection of the kind interpolated amid gossip, and notes that Diderot's style travels well in translation.

## Notable passages

**Provenance of the letters (c1).** Morley's account of the authenticity and murky transmission of the Voland letters, tied to his remark that mystification haunts Diderot's writings even in the nineteenth century, and a foretaste of a still more interesting monument to come.

**The old dressing-gown (c2).** The core of the essay as quoted: the robe moulded to the man, the ink-streaked lappets that announced the writer, and the loss of mastery to the new scarlet. Diderot's own irony, quoted by Morley, with Morley's introduction framing it as a picture of the room.

**Household and marriage (c3).** Morley's picture of the dreary companionship, Diderot's night return from Holbach's, the torrent of injustice, and the cry "Ah, how hard life seems to me to bear!", capped by Morley's pathos.

**Labour and the burnt papers (c4).** The passage on indomitable industry, ten-hour proof-reading, and the fire.

**A day at Grandval (c5).** The extended sketch of life at Holbach's country house and its company, ending in Morley's wish to spend a day there.

**Rich and poor (c6).** Diderot's paradox that happiness is nearly equal across conditions, in the words Morley quotes from the Grandval letters.
