# Diderot's letters: sensibility, casuistry and the salon table

This stretch of Morley's sixth chapter is mostly a run of extracts from Diderot's letters, framed by short commentary of Morley's own. It opens with two dark meditations on the smallness and misery of human life, which Morley treats as relief from Diderot's habitual busy attention rather than his normal mood. Morley then presents Diderot as a 'sentimentalist in the rationalistic century' who takes feeling, not ethical textbooks, as his standard, and offers anecdotes of childish kindness and gracious old age, aphorisms on friendship, idleness and the burdens of social engagements, and a remark on rhetoric. The rest is reported table-talk: Holbach's disgusted account of England (with a story from Hume), two cases of conscience told by a curé, a note on Chinese ennoblement and on women's letters, a grim report of a prosecution over a prohibited book, and Galiani's fable of the Nightingale, the Cuckoo and the Ass, which dramatises the quarrel between creative genius and co-ordinating method.

## Themes

**Sensibility against duty (Morley's framing).** Morley's own verdict is that Diderot is 'never callous nor neutral' but judges 'not from the standards of the ethical text-books'. The 'casuistry of feeling' is said to interest him endlessly, with 'pliant feeling' invoked 'against the narrowness of duty'. Morley sets him against Balzac, Goethe ([[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]]) and the misanthropic sentimentalism of Rousseau ([[jean-jacques-rousseau]]). Morley also pushes back, in his own voice, on Diderot's remark that Nature heeds neither good nor evil: the moral distinction 'is so much wrung from the forces that Diderot here calls Nature'. That is a Victorian-moralist correction, not Diderot's view.

**Pessimism as reaction, not creed.** The two opening passages are Diderot's words as Morley quotes them: the world as a comedy seen from the gods' distance, and human life as imbecility, quacks and ignorance. Morley says they are not Diderot's habitual vein.

**Happiness as tenderness and consolation.** The eighty-year-old woman who prizes a tear and a tender word above 'ten glorious deeds', Diderot's 'delicious repose', and his advice to comfort the wretched all place the good in feeling and sympathy. Morley uses the old woman's scene to explain why a French society that valued such moments would find the English 'hard, surly, and cheerless'.

**Outside view of England (Holbach's, via Diderot).** Holbach returns discontented with English buildings, gardens, amusements, faces, colleges and missionary zeal. This is a third party's report relayed by Diderot, and Morley flags it in advance as an example of French impressions of English hardness. Hume ([[david-hume]]) supplies the anecdote of the Huron convert who prefers brandy to sacramental wine; Garrick's mot is that London is good for the English but Paris for all the world.

**Cases of conscience.** The curé's two stories pit rigid rule against pity: a father who would let a son marry the disgraced girl instead of dying, and a priest who dies reconciled while the curé protects his wife and children from illegitimacy. The company approves the curé's practical wisdom. Opinion splits over the first case, and the passage shows the 'casuistry of feeling' in action rather than described.

**Censorship as lived injustice.** Diderot's account of the apprentice and colporteur pilloried, whipped, branded and sent to the galleys over copies of a banned book is an eyewitness-style letter. It ties the severity to the authorities' resentment of Voltaire's ([[voltaire]]) article 'Tyrant'. His closing judgement is that ignominious penalties are worse than death. It shows the book trade as a target of the state, which fits the unit's wider setting, though Morley adds no commentary here.

**Method versus genius.** Grimm calls method 'the pedantry of letters'. Galiani answers with a fable in which the ass, a pompous judge, chooses the cuckoo's method over the nightingale's brilliance, and then applies it with a joke: Grimm is the nightingale, Le Roy the cuckoo, Galiani the ass who decides for method. The fable is satirical and does not settle the question; Diderot's own comment praises the telling and the pantomime.

## Notable passages

**The comedy of the world (c1).** Diderot imagines watching human affairs from the Epicurean gods' distance and notes that moral outrage scales with size and mass. It is a compact statement of his habit of turning observation into a question about morality.

**Morley's portrait of the sentimentalist (c2).** The key interpretive passage: Diderot as 'half Titan of the skies, half gnome of the lower earth', sympathetic, approving or disapproving but never neutral, and not bound by textbook morals. Morley's own hedge is that modern taste does not allow reproducing most of his cases.

**Holbach in England (c3).** A long catalogue of dislikes, relayed second-hand. It mainly records how a French philosophe saw English society; it is not a neutral report.

**Soulpse and the converted priest (c4).** Two cases where a good-hearted curé's pity beats rules.

**Prosecution for a banned book (c5).** A letter that ties provincial judicial cruelty to ruling-class anger at Voltaire's anti-tyrant satire.

**Nightingale, Cuckoo and Ass (c6).** The apologue of creative genius against co-ordinating method.

Nothing in this unit engages the project's machine vocabulary directly. The censorship letter loosely rhymes with the incumbent-versus-philosophers struggle that frames the Encyclopaedia, and the method-versus-genius fable touches the tension between ordered knowledge and spontaneous invention, but Morley does not draw either connection here.
