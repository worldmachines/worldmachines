# Illness, Responsibility and the Erewhonian Reformers

After the consumption trial of the previous chapter, the narrator admits he felt unhappy once alone, having been carried along by the court's unquestioning confidence. He then does something characteristic of him: instead of condemning the Erewhonian position, he argues that punishing people for misfortune is the ordinary condition of human life, with a string of analogies (the lamb, the millionaire's son, the yellow-fever ship, the asylum, the serpent) that cut both ways. He next describes how Erewhonian treatment of convicted invalids was softened, through a reformer's absurd three-part classification of diseases (laudanum, castor-oil, sulphuric acid), and finally reports the views of a minority of 'malcontents' who want the sick treated as unfortunate but dangerous, not as culpable, and who press their case mainly on grounds of cost.

## Themes

**The narrator's ironic apologia.** The narrator, not Butler's thesis-voice, writes 'with great diffidence' that society may punish misfortune and reward luck. The argument is built to be uncomfortably plausible: responsibility just means liability to answer, and society limits its own rights. The target is probably Victorian England (the narrator himself notes that in England a man on a plague ship is held responsible, and that lunatics are held 'irresponsible' yet imprisoned), and the passage reads as a satirical mirror in which the inversion (illness as crime) exposes how English law already treats misfortune, poverty and madness. This is a reading, hedged; Butler keeps the voice deadpan so the reader cannot easily locate the author's own view.

**Convention as invisible bondage.** The narrator observes that 'that which we observe to be taken as a matter of course by those around us, we take as a matter of course ourselves', and that the judge, jury and even the prisoner cannot 'emancipate' themselves from 'the bondage of the ideas in which he had been born and bred'. Justice, he concludes, 'is relative'. Here the satire turns on the narrator himself, who has just been absorbed by the court's consensus.

**Reform as farce.** The reformer's division of all illness into head, trunk and lower limbs, treated respectively with laudanum, castor-oil and sulphuric acid, is presented as a step to 'familiarise the public mind with the principle'. The target appears to be both ham-fisted penal and medical reform and the complacent claim that incomplete reform is nonetheless progress.

**The malcontents and the economy argument.** The Erewhonian minority liken the sick man to rotten fruit, to be removed for society's protection but not further punished, and argue that concealment of illness is produced by the scouting of the ill, not by physic. Their own main appeal is to 'men's pockets' rather than 'their heads', and they would still flog and hang 'but they would do so pitifully'. This is an Erewhonian reformist voice reported by the narrator; its self-interested, utilitarian tone and its residual cruelty keep it from being a clean Butlerian alternative. The narrator's closing remark that these opinions 'can have no possible bearing upon our own' is plainly ironic.

**Resonance, hedged.** There is a faint rhyme with WMP's interest in institutions that make a population legible and governable (see [[Legibility]]): sickness is rendered a reportable, punishable category and concealment is the system's predictable by-product. This is only a gloss; Butler is writing penal and medical satire, not a theory of administrative legibility, and no machines appear in the chapter.

## Notable passages

The narrator's defence of punishing misfortune (c1) is the chapter's core piece of ironic reasoning, ending with the image of convention as the banks of a river. The serpent passage (c2) distils the argument that a creature can be put down for being what it is while still being pitied. The reflection on the judge and prisoner (c3) names the unexamined inheritance of ideas. The reformer's three-class classification (c4) is the chapter's comic centre. The malcontents' account of their own position (c5) and their economy argument (c6) are the only extended Erewhonian dissenting voice so far in the book.
