# The Colleges of Unreason: Elopement Plan and the Case for Unreason

The chapter opens in the narrator's romantic plot. After Arowhena's quiet remark that she is sorry for everyone, he accepts that marriage in Erewhon would be an "unrighteous marriage" by local custom, and settles on running away with her to Europe. Two obstacles remain: his status as a prisoner on parole, and Arowhena's consent, which she gives. Withdrawn to a lodging, he is under growing social suspicion (a hostile newspaper article, the king dwelling on his watch and his "lie about the balloons"), and he feigns drinking to explain his wasting looks. A cashier of the Musical Banks, Thims, takes him for a change of air to the chief seat of the Colleges of Unreason. There the narrator reports Thims's account of the curriculum: "hypothetics" taught through a dead "hypothetical language", plus professorships of Inconsistency and Evasion, justified by an Erewhonian argument that reason needs unreason. The last paragraphs defend the "double currency" (the Musical Banks' two coinages) against reason's reach.

## Themes

**Education as ritual preparation for the impossible.** The Erewhonian defence of hypothetics is that teaching a boy only the things that exist would give "a narrow and shallow conception of the universe"; youths are instead drilled in answering questions about "utterly strange and impossible contingencies". The narrator, himself a target, hedges that his disapproval may stem from being "trained so very differently" and "out of sorts", then says he thinks the defence insufficient. A likely reading is a parody of the Victorian classical curriculum (a dead language prized above live problems; maintenance for proficiency), though the text itself names no England.

**The inversion of the argument for unreason.** The Erewhonians' own logic is inconsistent in the way they celebrate: hypothetics is justified as preparation for the extraordinary, while Unreason is justified as developing faculties "required for the daily conduct of affairs". The narrator notices this ("here they depart from the principles"). The professors then argue that reason cannot exist without unreason, so more unreason means more reason. This is a deliberate pseudo-syllogism, the mock-logic of the satire, which also lampoons reasoning from a premise of mutual dependence.

**Reason distrusted as extremist; the mean as illogical but better.** The Erewhonian voice says "Extremes are alone logical, but they are always absurd" and that reason "might even attack the personality of Hope and Justice" and "abolish the double currency". The target is plausibly an establishment that shields its institutions (the Musical Banks) from rational scrutiny; the argument's real function is to protect the double currency. Whether Butler is also half-serious about the limits of logic is left ambiguous; this is not a settled thesis and the narrator does not endorse it.

**The narrator's compromised position.** His beauty-blind praise of the college city ("I cannot imagine that there can be any fairer in the whole world"), his lies of illness, and his dependence on a Musical Bank cashier show a narrator adopting the local protective camouflage (taking "a leaf out of Mahaina's book") while planning an escape. The Erewhonian punishment of illness as crime frames his pretence at drinking.

**World Machines resonance (a gloss, not Butler's claim).** The chapter's theme of an institution that legitimises its own habits by defending inconsistency and evasion rhymes loosely with [[more-utopia]]'s habit of satirical inversion as a mirror on the writer's own society. It does not map onto the Dawn/Day/Dusk machine frame; there are no machines in this unit, and the college argument is about reason, not technology.

## Notable passages

**Arowhena's "I am sorry for every one."** The turn in the love plot: a single sentence that makes the narrator recognise the custom bar to their marriage (c1).

**Hypothetics.** The curriculum's rationale and the narrator's reluctant, hedged dissent (c2).

**Inconsistency and Evasion.** The tension between the two justifications and the professorships, with students learning to defend any inconsistency and find a pretext against any clear injunction (c3).

**Reason's extremes and the mean.** The core of the case against reason as sole guide (c4).

**Unreason as part of reason.** The closing argument and its application to the double currency (c5).
