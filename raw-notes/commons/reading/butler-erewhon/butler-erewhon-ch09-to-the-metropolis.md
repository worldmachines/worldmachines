# The journey to the metropolis and the house of Senoj Nosnibor

The chapter opens on a comic collision of moral codes. The narrator, horrified that he is asked to lodge with a man who has 'embezzled', refuses, until his interpreter explains that embezzlement is a mere misfortune of the moral sort, while the narrator's own former poverty was a punishable offence. Chastened, he accepts, parts tearfully from Yram, and travels for about a month, blindfolded by day, to the capital. Along the way he is questioned about his temper rather than his health, and educated visitors from the Colleges of Unreason explain the statues on the pass, the fate that would await Chowbok's tribe, and (briefly, as a promise of later extracts) the abolition of machines four hundred years earlier. He arrives at the palatial house of Senoj Nosnibor, meets Arowhena, and ends the chapter with the host weeping in the straightener's room while his family calmly eats dinner.

## Themes

**Inverted categories, narrated by a man who does not see the inversion.** The interpreter's outrage that the narrator would 'consort with the basest criminals, and yet deem simple embezzlement a bar to friendly intercourse' is the book's mirror in miniature: illness and poverty are crimes, while financial fraud is a condition to be treated. The likely Victorian target (a reading, not stated here) is the English habit of treating poverty and sickness as moral failure while excusing the respectable rogue. The narrator is himself a satirical target: his reasons for refusing are reputation and commercial calculation ('converting them if they are the lost tribes of Israel, or making money out of them if they are not'), and he caves because of his pension, not conviction.

**Wealth as measurable power.** Men are classed 'by the number of foot pounds which they have money enough to raise, or more roughly by their horse-power'. This is a mechanical metaphor applied to social rank, one of the few places in the chapter where the machine vocabulary is turned on persons. It sits oddly beside the later abolition of machines, and the narrator does not remark on it. Loosely, it rhymes with a [[Legibility]]-style move of reducing people to a single scalar measure, though Butler's joke is about money-worship, not state administration.

**Ill temper as the thing one is asked about.** Hosts ask about temper, not health, and treat a confessed bad mood with sympathy, sweets and smelling-salts. The narrator finds it more comfortable to hide his natural bad temper than to endure the condolences. The reversal (physical health presumed, moral state inspected) is the mirror image of the English norm.

**Machines abolished: first report.** The educated visitors say that four hundred years earlier mechanical knowledge was beyond the narrator's own, until 'one of the most learned professors of hypothetics' wrote a book proving that machines would supplant humans and become 'instinct with a vitality as different from, and superior to, that of animals, as animal to vegetable life'. The country made a 'clean sweep' of machinery not in use for 271 years and made further invention a crime classed as typhus. The narrator reports this at second hand and defers the book to later chapters, so the argument is the Erewhonian professor's, as relayed, not yet argued. The decision is described as persuasion by 'reasoning, or unreasoning', which is the narrator's own ambivalence. Resonates with [[useless-machine]] and the idea of deliberately halting a technological trajectory, though here the halt is legal, absolute and fear-driven, not a design choice. The old railway road and the ruined railway station surviving as scenery show the abolition as a ruin-making act.

**Fear and sacrifice at the borders.** The statues on the pass are explained as propitiation of the 'gods of deformity and disease', formerly served by capturing the ugliest people over the range. The 'Hospital for Incurable Bores', where foreigners might serve as 'professional borees', is a speculation from the Erewhonians, which the narrator relates to Chowbok's terror. It is satire of institutional absurdity, but it also hints that legal fictions can cover older sacrificial practice (informants insist the sacrifice of their own people was 'untrue').

**The narrator's complacency.** His resolve 'to behave just as though I were in England until I saw that I was making a blunder' is a policy of unexamined cultural assumption, and his protests of exact accuracy at the end are the standard unreliable-witness pose.

## Notable passages

The interpreter's 500,000 horse-power description of Nosnibor introduces the money-as-power measure and the first sign that wealth confers the highest social standing, even over the stigma of embezzlement. The quarrel over 'ill or poor' and the royal pardon granted for the narrator's 'meritorious complexion' sets out the logic of the inversion. The temper passage shows the everyday form of the same logic. The visitors' account of the statues and the borees shows the frightening side of Erewhonian legal fictions. The machine paragraph is the first statement of the abolition, with the 271-year compromise and the typhus fiction, and is explicitly a preview of the Book of the Machines. The closing scene of Nosnibor weeping while Arowhena 'helped herself composedly to the salt' prefigures the straightener chapter and shows how moral treatment is made routine and domestic.
