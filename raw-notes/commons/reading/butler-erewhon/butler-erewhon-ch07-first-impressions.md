# Arrival: a beautiful people, a medical examination, and the watch in the museum

The narrator walks four hours with his guides along an Alpine path, past wayside shrines to youth, beauty and dignified age, through villages of strikingly handsome people, to a considerable town. There the chief magistrate has him stripped and physically examined (pulse, tongue, chest, muscles, eyelids) and, finding him in robust health, goes through his swag. The pipe puzzles the officials; the watch provokes "hatred". The magistrate takes him to the town museum, which is mostly broken machinery that is several hundred years old, including steam-engine fragments and a carriage built for iron rails, and compares the watch with the old clocks and watches there. The narrator surrenders the watch, relations improve, and he is lodged in a comfortable room in the common prison, where he is plainly a prisoner for an unknown period. All of this is the narrator's first-person report. The Erewhonians cannot yet be understood, so what he reads into their behaviour is guesswork.

## Themes

**Machines as a taboo object, shown before it is explained.** The chapter never says why broken machines are kept. It shows the magistrate's reaction to the watch and a museum arranged "not for instruction, but curiosity". The explanation (the Erewhonian Book of the Machines) comes later and is outside this unit. The museum resembles a decayed, ornamental archive of a technology that once functioned, so it half-rhymes with [[useless-machine]] and with the project's decay-sequence idea (an infrastructure that survives as a museum piece). It does not fit neatly, though. These objects were deliberately broken or banned, and nothing in this chapter says they declined on their own.

**Paley's watch argument turned inside out, with the narrator as the butt.** The narrator expects the savage-sees-watch-infers-designer inference and admires Paley. He then wonders whether the magistrate takes the watch to be the "designer of himself and of the universe", and finally finds the look is neither reverence nor fear but hatred. Butler's joke targets the narrator's complacent reasoning from analogy. It touches the design-argument themes that [[dennett-darwins-dangerous-idea]] and [[skyhooks-and-cranes]] later address, but only as a hint. No evolutionary argument is made here.

**Health as the Erewhonian measure of a person.** The first people the narrator meets who are ill are held in the magistrate's anteroom, while the villagers are almost uniformly beautiful and "well kempt and tidy". The magistrate's examination is the first thing done to the narrator, and it is a medical one. The chapter does not state the rule, but this sets up the later inversion (illness treated as a crime). That is a reading from the later book, and this unit does not confirm it.

**The narrator's mercenary and missionary habits.** He bows to local customs only because "to be all things to all men" is Pauline expedience, he is shocked by reverence given to mere beauty, and he forgets "all thoughts of their conversion" at the sight of the women. The satire falls on him as much as on Erewhon. The observation that they are "the very best-bred people that I ever fell in with" despite "extraordinary obliquity of mental vision" is the narrator's retrospective opinion, not Butler's thesis.

**Familiar and strange.** Glass, shops, bottle signs, dogs and sweets are "generically the same as in Europe, the differences being of species only", with blue barley-sugar and terriers in place of cats. This is the book's mirror method: the same institutions with slight changes.

## Notable passages

**The watch and Paley (c1).** The narrator's confident prediction of what Erewhonians will think, and the magistrate's actual reaction, which the narrator misreads twice, are the chapter's best compression of the book's method. Observer and observed misread each other, and an argument from design is put to the test.

**The museum of broken machinery (c2).** The first concrete sign that machines were once present and are now kept only as broken curiosities. The narrator, whose frame is progress, is astonished at the sight of "our own most advanced inventions" labelled and ruined.

**The watch placed with the clocks (c4).** The magistrate treats the watch as the same kind of thing as the museum's exhibits, "The design was different, but the thing was clearly the same", and is calmed only when the narrator gives it up. The narrator's guess that it is a customs offence ("contraband", "ordinary tolls") is his own mistaken frame.

**The physical examination (c3).** The narrator is judged by the state of his body before anything else, which anticipates the inverted treatment of health and crime in the later chapters.

**The beauty of the villagers (c5).** The narrator's rapture is part of the satire, since he forgets his plan to convert them. The passage also sets the baseline of a healthy-and-handsome society against which illness will later be punished.
