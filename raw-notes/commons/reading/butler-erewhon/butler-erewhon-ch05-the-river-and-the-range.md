# The River, the Raft and the Singing Statues

The narrator, alone since Chowbok left him, descends to a muddy glacial river, builds a raft of iris-like flag stalks, and is swept down a rapid but lands on the far (left) bank. Over supper he muses on Chowbok, the guide he had tried to make "a real convert to the Christian religion", and the next morning climbs to the summit of the pass, where solitude and gloom threaten his sense of who he is. In the mist he meets a circle of huge seated figures, takes them for living fiends, discovers they are statues, and then flees when the wind makes their hollowed heads sound like organ-pipes. He closes by saying (from his later vantage in England) that the sound recalls a Handel chord, and names the country he is entering: Erewhon.

## Themes

**The narrator as satirical target.** The unit's longest reflection is not about the mountains but about the narrator's religion, and it is the narrator's voice throughout, not Butler's thesis. His motives for converting Chowbok are openly self-interested: he recalls "the promise of St. James" and reasons that the conversion might "compensate for irregularities and short-comings in my own previous life". The baptism from a pannikin, done hurriedly before noon, and the theological worry when Chowbok tries to steal the brandy the same evening, are comic. Read as a hedged satire, the target seems to be Victorian mission piety and its casual contempt for the convert ("impenetrably stupid nature"), though the text never states that critique; it lets the narrator condemn himself. Chowbok's only real hold on the prayer-book is the title of Adelaide the Queen Dowager, confused with Mary Magdalene.

**Self-reliance after losing a helper.** The narrator counts what Chowbok did for him and says he was "the loser by his absence", then has to improvise a raft, a fire and a meal alone. This is incidental survival narrative, not a theory of tools or machines, and nothing in it should be pushed toward the later Book of the Machines.

**Identity under solitude.** On the summit he feels "that dreadful doubt as to my own identity—as to the continuity of my past and present existence", which he calls the first sign of distraction in those lost in the bush. This is a narrator's anecdote, but it loosely touches the question of how continuity of self is sustained, which the project handles under [[habit-and-identity]]; Butler's fuller treatment of habit and memory is outside this unit, so the link is only a pointer.

**The threshold as sublime horror, then bathos.** The statues are ten, six or seven times life size, "neither Egyptian, nor Assyrian, nor Japanese", each "terrible after a different kind", one fallen and "exquisitely ludicrous". The narrator counts fifty to test whether they are alive, then discovers the hollowed heads that turn wind into sound. The swing from terror to explanation to renewed terror (the heads were conceived by beings "into whose hearts the Evil One had put it") shows his credulity.

**Music as an ominous motif.** The "music" had haunted him since Chowbok's reminiscences and the previous evening; he knows it was "my own fancy". The statues give it a physical source, and the closing aside about Handel on the organ adds the narrator's later, English frame. Whether Butler intends any link to the later Musical Banks is not decided by this unit.

## Notable passages

**Chowbok and the narrator's motives (c1).** The paragraph that sets out the narrator's ambition to convert Chowbok, his own pedigree (grandson of an archdeacon, son of a clergyman), and his hope that a convert would hide a multitude of his sins.

**The baptism (c2).** The mock-solemn account of baptizing Chowbok from a pannikin, after reasoning that the missionary had omitted "the second, and certainly more important, ceremony".

**The prayer-book (c3).** The only part of Christianity that took hold of Chowbok is a royal title confused with a saint's name; the satire here is gentle and absurd.

**Identity doubt (c4).** The narrator's fear of losing the continuity of his self in the silence of the summit.

**The statues (c5).** The description of the ten barbaric figures, a "sort of Stonehenge", terrible and ludicrous at once, with hollowed heads.

**The wind chorus (c6).** The moment the statues moan and sing; the narrator's panic and flight.

What does not fit WMP: the unit has no machines, institutions or long-range dynamics. It is a threshold scene, useful mainly for fixing the narrator's unreliable character before the Erewhonian chapters, and for the early motif of sound, belief and idolatry.
