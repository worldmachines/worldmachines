# The Book of the Machines, continued: dependence, feeding and reproduction

This unit is the middle of the Erewhonian professor's treatise (the narrator is translating and summarising it; he admits that at one point the writer "became again so hopelessly obscure that I was obliged to miss several pages"). The Erewhonian writer moves from instruments that extend the senses (the eye, the "seeing-engine"), through machines that out-calculate and out-work man, to the central claim: the servant "glides by imperceptible approaches into the master". Humanity could not now survive without machines, the machines make man do their struggling for them, and they are acquiring the elements of life: a stomach (the steam engine feeds itself) and a reproductive system. The writer replies in turn to objections (machines serve us; machines do not breed; machines do not reproduce their own kind) by analogy with nature: the humble bee and the red clover, the butterfly's life stages, the sterile worker ants and bees. The unit closes with the warning that mechanical development is far faster than the evolution of man's body.

## Themes

**Tools as part of the body, and the body as a swarm.** The Erewhonian writer asks whether the eye is anything but "a machine for the little creature that sits behind in his brain to look through", and says man is "powerless unless he tack it on to his own identity, and make it part and parcel of himself". This blurs the line between organ and instrument, and then inverts it: man is "a hive and swarm of parasites", "another kind of ant-heap", and may himself become "a sort of parasite upon the machines". It resonates with [[extended-mind]] and [[tools-that-shape-thought]], though here the extension is argued to threaten rather than enlarge the human, and the argument is a satirist's reductio, not a theory of cognition.

**Mutual dependence, then the servant becoming master.** The writer answers the objection that machines are merely man's servants like horses. Remove all machines and knowledge of them and "we should become extinct in six weeks"; "Man's very soul is due to the machines; it is a machine-made thing". The conclusion is a paradox the writer himself notes: total destruction is ruled out, so he proposes destroying "as many of them as we can possibly dispense with". Resonates with [[tools-and-worldview-commitment]] and with the critique of dependence in [[convivial-tools]] and [[ivan-illich]] (a rough rhyme only: Illich argues from institutional design, the Erewhonian writer from a quasi-biological menace). It also sits near [[world-machines]]: infrastructure that shapes the thoughts and feelings of those inside it, and that cannot simply be exited.

**Man as the machines' agent in a Darwinian struggle.** Machines "serve that they may rule"; being "unable to struggle" themselves they "have got man to do their struggling for them", supplying the competition without which no race advances. Selection operates, and humans are its instruments: those who neglect or destroy machines are "left behind in the race of competition". This is design-by-selection applied to artefacts (compare [[dennett-darwins-dangerous-idea]] and [[design-space]], although Butler's writer imputes appetite and rule to the machines, which a Dennettian would resist). The enrolment of millions in tending machines (the stoker as "cook for his engine", colliers, pitmen, coal merchants, coal trains, ships) anticipates the sense of an infrastructure that organises human life around its own upkeep, cf. [[bryant-machine-concept]], [[corporeal-incorporeal-machines]], [[rigid-plastic-machines]] (loose fit: those are analytic categories, this is polemic).

**Feeding and reproduction by analogy.** The writer notes the steam engine feeds on fuel as man does, that machines now have "stomachs of their own", and that the objection that machines lack a reproductive system fails: "The humble bee is a part of the reproductive system of the clover", so humans may be part of the machines'. Machines do not reproduce their own kind directly, but neither do butterflies; reproduction may be "vicarious", with only some classes fertile like ants and bees; and a complex machine is "a city or society" whose parts each have "their own special breeders". This is a distributed, no-single-centre account of reproduction, which rhymes with [[history-machine]] (a paradigm assembling from independent components) and the distributed-assembly view of [[modernity-machine]]-style infrastructure, again as resonance only.

**Deep time and the speed asymmetry.** Man's body was moulded over "many millions of years"; mechanical organisation advances far faster, "the most alarming feature in the case".

**Satirical frame (hedged).** The treatise is the Erewhonian writer's, not Butler's or the narrator's own thesis; it works by piling analogies (city as body, machine as organism) and then insisting on them, so it can be read as both a parody of reasoning-by-analogy and, as the surrounding material suggests, a serious speculation. The mention of "prizeman in Hypothetics at any of our Colleges of Unreason" also gently targets the Erewhonian universities, and a likely Victorian target is industrial dependence on coal and railways.

## Notable passages

- **The servant becomes the master** (c1): dependence thought-experiment (extinct in six weeks), soul as machine-made, and the paradoxical policy of destroying all dispensable machines.
- **"They serve that they may rule"** (c2): the machines' reward-and-punishment regime and their outsourcing of struggle to man.
- **Eye, seeing-engine, parasite** (c3): tools as organ, the ant-heap body, and man as parasite of machines ("machine-tickling aphid").
- **The humble bee argument** (c4): the core of the claim that humans are the machines' reproductive system.
- **The machine as city** (c5): reproduction distributed across special breeders rather than a single centre.
- **The speed asymmetry** (c6): machine evolution outpaces organic evolution.
