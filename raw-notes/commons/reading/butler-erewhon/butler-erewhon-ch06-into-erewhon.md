# Into Erewhon: arrival, hospitality, and the narrator's missionary fantasy

Having fled past the statues of the pass, the narrator follows a watercourse downhill, takes comfort from a log bridge ("savages do not make bridges"), and comes below the clouds into a sunset view of plains, towns with steeples and domes, and a winding river. He sleeps under a tree and wakes among goats; two girls see him and run, and an hour later return with six or seven armed men. The men are handsome, dark, courteous and benign, and baffled by his fair colouring and clothes. They laugh with him about the statues, lead him to a hamlet, feed him on milk, goat's flesh and oatcake, and put him up for the night. They are startled, with some disapproval, by his matches and smoking. Next morning they inspect his arms, legs and feet, and he observes that their household is "quasi-European" but some five or six hundred years behind in appliances. He then conceives that they may be the lost ten tribes of Israel and that he might be "the instrument of their conversion"; after good food he sets off with them down the valley, but his excitement cools into commercial disappointment and the suspicion that his hosts "meant to keep me".

## Themes

**The narrator as satirical target, not Butler's mouthpiece.** The chapter's longest argument is the narrator's own: a chain of vanity ("I should rank next to the Apostles"), piety and self-interest. He admits he had always "admired, and envied, and respected" missionaries "more than I had exactly liked them". Read as Butler's irony about Victorian evangelism and imperial self-regard, this is a hedged reading; the text itself only shows the narrator reasoning from ethnological guesses (noses, lips, irreligion) to a conclusion he wants. Note that he says the discovery is what he alluded to in the opening pages of the story and that he now "feel[s] no longer uncertain", so the delusion persists beyond this chapter.

**Arrival as a legible, half-familiar utopia.** Everything is "slightly different" yet European: Italian villages, Algerian dress, birds and flowers named after English ones, "not at all like going to China or Japan". This is the classic traveller's-tale device of the unknown country that is a distorted mirror, in the lineage that [[more-utopia]] belongs to, though here the mirror effect is only set up and nothing of Erewhon's institutions has yet appeared; the chapter does not show Erewhonian society at all beyond hospitality and a first hint of disapproval at matches.

**Technology as a marker of difference.** The only technological observations are the skewer for a fork, the butcher's knife, few glazed windows, and the narrator's judgment of 'primitive' appliances, plus the hosts' hubbub at a struck match. Nothing here yet states the machine prohibition, so any connection to the later Book of the Machines or to [[useless-machine]]-style arrested development is retrospective gloss, not content of this unit. The matches reaction is at most a faint, unexplained foreshadowing ("why, I could not guess").

**Reciprocal examination and the narrator's body.** The hosts scrutinise his hair, colour, arms, legs and feet to settle "whether I was in all respects human like themselves"; his approved physique wins their respect. In light of the book's later Erewhonian valuation of health and bodily fortune, this is suggestive, but within this chapter it is only the narrator's report, and he is himself vain of being over six feet.

**Captivity under hospitality.** Kindness and constraint coexist: guards sleep in the hut, hands are laid on his arms, and he concludes they "had got me, and meant to keep me, in spite of all their goodness". The tone is mild unease rather than threat.

## Notable passages

**The view from below the clouds** is the narrator's emotional high point, framed by a Moses-on-Sinai comparison (a promised land "which it was not to be his to enter"), which is ironic given his later fate in the country.

**First look at the men** records both the Erewhonians' dignity and the narrator's self-image, and shows his racialised, travel-guide comparisons (Italians, Spaniards, Arabs).

**The quasi-European household** is where the narrator explains why the place seems familiar and a few centuries behind.

**The ten-tribes scheme** is the chapter's centre: religious ambition, vanity and a wish to be famous in the next world or this.

**The cooling** shows the same narrator tallying the commercial value of the country and noticing that he is a captive guest.
