# Death, Statues and the Concealment of Birth in Erewhon

The narrator reports how the Erewhonians treat death and its surrounding customs. Death is held to be a lesser matter than disease, and most people who are "said to die" are said never to have been born into the unseen world that counts. He then describes cremation, the scattering of ashes over a place the dead person chose, and the practice of commissioning flattering statues of oneself while alive. A long digression follows on how such statues overran the capital, were destroyed in a fury, were revived, and were finally regulated by an Act that makes public statues lapse after fifty years unless a jury votes to keep them, which in turn produced the paid-for decision not to make the statue at all. The chapter closes with the artificial-tear boxes sent in place of condolence, and the treatment of childbirth as a shameful illness to be concealed, ending with the legal fiction that a dead infant was "over seventy-five years old".

## Themes

**Death as a bugbear, not a sting.** The Erewhonian argument, as the narrator relays it, is that foreknowledge of death in general does not make us unhappy; only knowledge of the exact moment would. He suggests that "some power somewhere" mercifully prevents that knowledge, and the condemned-man example (reprieve, fire, lightning, choking at breakfast, heart failure) shows that certainty is never available. The conclusion, that death like life is "an affair of being more frightened than hurt", is a piece of comic reasoning-by-example, and it is not clear how far Butler endorses it. It mirrors the Erewhonian inversion that illness is the real crime while death is not even an offence.

**The unborn and the "truly born".** The opening remark that most people never reach the unseen world is the first hint of the pre-natal and birth-formula material the narrator promises later ("of which hereafter"). It reads as satire on conventional ideas of life and afterlife, but the unit does not explain it further, so any target is only a guess.

**Monuments and the life-cycle of an art.** The statue digression is the chapter's centre. Commemoration is driven by coteries, jobbery and the wish to preserve a name; statues pile up as encumbrances because there is no "drainage" for works that have been absorbed into the country's impression of itself. The narrator generalises that a noble art peaks briefly and then declines, and that an aged art "must be born anew". Statutory expiry with jury review (twenty-four men, a majority of eighteen needed) is a comic institutional fix for decay, and its outcome, paying sculptors not to make statues, shows a regulation producing a perverse, self-sustaining arrangement. This loosely rhymes with the World Machines idea that infrastructures decline and must be rebuilt (see [[history-machine]]), but Butler's concern is aesthetic and civic clutter, not a millennial cycle, and the fit is partial at best. The detail about periodic review of what the dead leave behind also touches on what a society chooses to retain and what it forgets.

**Custom, inconsistency and concealment.** The artificial tears, graded by intimacy, parody the etiquette of Victorian mourning; the narrator notes that the omission of the gift is "keenly felt". The final section applies the same logic to childbirth: pregnancy is treated as a disreputable illness, concealed from husbands, with the child hidden until it can walk and talk, and the whole scheme is held together by "this blessed inconsistency". The narrator calls the conventions "arbitrary and cruel" yet credits them with stopping fancied ailments, so his own stance is mixed. The likely English target is respectability and its silences, which is a reading, and the chapter does not name it.

## Notable passages

The opening paragraph states the Erewhonian ranking of death below disease and the claim that most people are never truly born into the unseen world. The condemned-prisoner paragraph is the most developed argument in the chapter, a chain of small possibilities leading to the maxim about being more frightened than hurt. The aside on arts, "better dead than dying", is the one place where the narrator generalises beyond Erewhon. The Act limiting public statues and its sequel, subscribers paying sculptors not to make the statue, is the sharpest institutional joke. The passage on the "blessed inconsistency" explains how Erewhonian society tolerates the very things its rules condemn, and the dead child found to be over seventy-five years old is the unit's last and darkest legal fiction.
