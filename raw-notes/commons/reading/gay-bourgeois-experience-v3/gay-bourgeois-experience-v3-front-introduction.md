# Introduction: Aggression as the Victorian Bourgeoisie's Central Preoccupation

Gay opens *The Cultivation of Hatred* by asserting that aggression has left indelible scars across all of history and that nineteenth-century observers — from Büchner and the Goncourt brothers to Spencer, Darwin, William James, and Simmel — largely agreed the human is an aggressive animal by nature. He surveys aggression's range, from gossip and invidious comparison to duels and war, argues it is often reactive (triggered by frustration and perceived threat rather than pure appetite), and introduces his own key term, "alibis for aggression": the religious, political, and especially scientific rhetoric Victorians used to legitimate militancy while still seeing themselves as constructive. He closes by casting the whole inquiry inside Freud's drive theory, in which Eros and aggression are simultaneous allies and adversaries whose contest is civilization's motor — and situates this volume as the third in his larger study of the bourgeois experience.

## Themes

**Aggression is a universal, adaptive endowment, not an aberration.** Gay catalogues a broad nineteenth-century consensus — Büchner, the Goncourts, Spencer, Darwin, William James, Simmel — that aggression is innate, "necessary to the most self-effacing of human creatures," and expressed across a spectrum from gossip and invidious social comparison to duels and war.

**Post-Darwinian science supplied a naturalizing alibi, and the persecuted 'convenient Other' shows its cost.** James's claim that "ancestral evolution has made us all potential warriors" is illustrated with a list of historical victims — Jews, Albigensians and Waldensians, Quakers, Methodists, Mormons, Armenians — anticipating the volume's recurring theme of respectable-seeming hostility toward chosen out-groups.

**Aggression is often reactive, a defense against threat rather than pure appetite.** Gay adopts a frustration-aggression thesis, illustrated by the "surly, often intemperate" backlash against the Seneca Falls feminists and against Impressionist painting, both read as anxious defenses of "time-honored styles of thinking."

**Victorians built alibis — legitimating rhetoric — to license aggression they also feared.** The chapter's key coinage, "alibis for aggression," names the religious, political, and especially scientific justifications that let the bourgeoisie see its own "attacks" (on markets, nature, empire) as "purely constructive activities." This loosely resonates with [[world-machines]]'s idea that an era's institutions carry their own legitimacy infrastructure — though Gay's alibis are moral rhetoric internal to a class's self-image, not the encompassing technological and institutional architecture a world machine names.

**That aggression was also mastery: control over time, space, and scarcity.** Gay insists bourgeois aggression was not only exploitative but self-disciplining and world-ordering — the drive "to get a grip on time, space, scarcity—and themselves as never before." This is the passage that speaks most directly to [[modernity-machine]] and [[legibility-as-machine-core]]: the Victorian project of measuring, scheduling, and rationalizing life reads as the same legibility impulse WMP locates at the Modernity Machine's core, here recast by Gay as a species of channelled aggression rather than a neutral technical achievement.

**Aggression rehearses and enlarges the self — a partial, imperfect echo of conatus.** Children's "saying no, refusing help, pushing out elbows" and adults' sublimated aggressiveness in reform movements are framed as healthy self-expansion rather than mere harm to others, which half-rhymes with Spinoza's [[Conatus]] (striving to persist and expand) — though Gay's aggression is inherently relational and typically other-directed, where conatus is a purely self-directed striving.

**Eros and aggression are instinctual rivals and allies, and their contest is civilization's motor.** Gay closes by casting "civilization itself" as "an interminable contest, only intermittently peaceful, between Eros and its great adversary, aggression," with Freud's own alternating work on a jokes book and a sexuality book dramatizing the pairing — the Freudian lens the rest of the volume applies to nineteenth-century culture.

## Notable passages

The opening thesis ("Aggression as history's fuel") sets up the volume by cataloguing the "scars" aggression has left across war, class conflict, and private life, and previews that the book will show aggression held "singular salience" for the Victorian bourgeoisie specifically.

"Post-Darwinian license for enmity" collects the William James passage naming concrete victim groups — Jews, dissenting Christian sects, Mormons, Armenians — as evidence of a widely shared "pugnacity" the era treated as biologically given.

"Frustration as trigger" pairs two of Gay's own illustrations of aggression-as-defense: clerical fury at the Seneca Falls feminists and academic fury at Impressionist painters, both read as anxious guardians of convention rather than simple hostility.

"Coining 'alibis for aggression'" is the chapter's conceptual hinge, where Gay names the rhetorical move — dressing militancy in religious, political, or scientific respectability — that the rest of the volume traces.

"Mastery over time, space, and scarcity" reframes bourgeois exploitation as also a project of world-ordering control, the line doing the most work for a World Machines reading of this unit.

"Eros and aggression as civilizational rivals" closes the introduction on Gay's Freudian frame, with the anecdote about Freud alternating between his jokes book and his sexuality book standing in for the larger argument the volume develops.
