# The Ambiguous Ideal: Manliness as an Alibi for Bourgeois Aggression

Gay opens his chapter on manliness by tracing it to a repurposed aristocratic
inheritance: the chivalric ideal of prowess, democratized and defanged, gave
the nineteenth-century middle classes — denied battlefields — a socially
respectable outlet for aggression in the “bloodless tournaments” of commerce,
industry, and politics. But the unit's real argument is that manliness worked
badly as an alibi, because the word itself was chronically unstable: publicists,
novelists, philologists, and mothers all used “manly” to mean incompatible
things, and the century eventually settled the incoherence not by fixing a
definition but by redefining manliness as aggression yoked to self-control.

## Themes

**A democratized aristocratic ideal repurposed for bourgeois combat.** Edmund
Burke's mourned chivalry never actually died; the Victorians instead
“democratized the courtly ideal of prowess,” turning a trait once natural only
to a warrior elite into a virtue expected of ordinary middle-class men, who
found in commerce, industry, and politics a bloodless substitute battlefield
(Baudelaire's “heroes of modern life”).

**Effeminacy anxiety fueled an international campaign for virility.** From
Kingsley's fear that policemen and comfort were making cowards of the
“educated and comfortable classes,” to Goethe lamenting lost
“Männlichkeit,” William James pitting stoic Greeks against a “feminine”
modern age, Henry James's Basil Ransom indicting a “nervous, hysterical,
chattering” age, and on to Bourget, Bierbaum, G. Stanley Hall, Sorel, and
Ratzenhofer — a chorus across Britain, Germany, France, and the United States
diagnosed bourgeois softness and prescribed struggle, Social Darwinist
“struggle for survival,” and contempt for “pacifism propagated by women of
both sexes” as the cure.

**Manliness doubled as sexual and aesthetic attractiveness.** Trollope's
Francis Tregear, Hoffmann's demonic Don Giovanni, and Grace Greenwood's
description of Otto Goldschmidt all fuse physical beauty with manly character,
suggesting the word did romantic and erotic work as much as moral or martial
work — “that expression of manliness, which of all characteristics is the one
which women love the best.”

**The term's “incurable imprecision” undermined its own function as alibi.**
This is the chapter's hinge claim: one observer called Oscar Wilde “manly,”
another “unmanly”; the word served as eloquence (Mommsen on Caesar), literary
style (Schlegel on Schiller, Macaulay on Machiavelli), simple approval
(Dickens uses it roughly ten times in *Martin Chuzzlewit*), a compliment paid
to women (William James to his daughter, Samuel Smiles to Eliza Lynn Linton),
and, just as often, an ironic or derisive tag (Dickens's Fladdock, Disraeli's
*Sybil*). Heine's mockery of Germans working themselves into “manly wrath”
against Napoleon only because their princes commanded it is Gay's clinching
case that a word this elastic could not reliably license aggression.

**The resolution: manliness as self-assertion disciplined by self-control.**
Rather than collapsing, the ideal was renegotiated: Gurlitt, Foerster, and
even the pugnacious G. Stanley Hall (who disliked “overpugnacity”) redefined
true manliness as firmness married to kindness and restraint — Thackeray's
Pendennis is manly precisely for resisting temptation. “The authentic manly
man ... was at once self-assertive and self-controlled,” and the
pure-virility extremists never won the argument.

**Room for tenderness, and the underside of the ideal.** Manly tears,
husbands assisting at childbirth, men who read poetry or wept at concerts —
none of this cost a man his manliness, just as strenuous, unflinching women
could remain “womanly.” Gay closes by noting that Ruskin's picture of the
yielding domestic queen presiding over a hearth while her consort brought
home “bleeding trophies” from finance or politics “was a wish more than a
fact” — the ideology of separate spheres described an aspiration, not
observed bourgeois life.

This chapter's central move — a respectable, load-bearing fiction (manliness)
that licenses and channels aggression while papering over its own internal
contradictions — resonates with how a [[world-machines]] regime's legitimating
assumptions organize what a civilization treats as normal and permissible;
here the licensing apparatus operates at the level of a personal virtue-word
rather than an institution, and Gay's point is precisely that this
particular alibi was unusually leaky, so the resonance is with legitimating
infrastructure in general rather than a clean case of it.

## Notable passages

The bourgeois-heroism passage (c1) supplies the chapter's founding claim: a
watered-down chivalric ideal repurposed for commercial “tournaments.” The
Kingsley passage (c2) gives Gay's own thesis sentence — manliness as “an
uncertain mechanism for the liberation of aggressive impulses” — anchored in
Kingsley's worry that policed comfort was producing “lap-dog” cowardice. The
“incurable imprecision” passage (c3) is the chapter's interpretive crux,
capped by the Hamlet-cloud image (manliness as “sometimes a weasel, sometimes
a whale”). Heine's send-up of German “manly wrath” (c4) supplies Gay's
clinching evidence that the alibi's elasticity defeated its own purpose. The
Gurlitt/Foerster/Thackeray passage (c5) shows the century's actual settlement
of the ambiguity in favor of self-controlled manliness. The tenderness/Ruskin
passage (c6) closes the unit by showing the ideal's practical give and by
flagging separate-spheres ideology as aspirational rather than descriptive.
