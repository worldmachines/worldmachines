# The Alibis of Domesticity: Self-Abasement and the Rise of the Women's Magazine

This unit opens Gay's account of how respectable nineteenth-century culture manufactured consent to women's subordination: girls were raised "for home," absorbing self-sacrifice as a psychological given, while the handful of early feminist voices who protested were isolated, ridiculed, or forgotten; the second and larger half of the unit shows how the new mass institution of the women's magazine — above all America's Godey's Lady's Book under editor Sarah Josepha Hale — diffused and hardened this domestic ideology across a huge popular readership for decades, only to find the "Woman Question" impossible to suppress by the 1870s.

## Themes

**Alibis for subordination, not just for aggression.** The unit opens mid-argument ("This versatile pair of alibis") extending the volume's larger thesis — that respectable rationales license hostility — into the domestic sphere: women's supposed emotional "influence," their own professed contentment, and appeals to "Nature" all served as alibis that made structural exclusion look voluntary, even benevolent, and let male self-esteem go unchallenged.

**Self-abasement as internalized discipline.** Gay stresses that education "for home" exacted "a heavy psychological price of which most of them did not become aware—directly," and documents a whole literary genre of "conscious self-denigration—almost self-flagellation," from Lydia Maria Child's analogy between women and an enslaved "colored race" sharing "a tendency to submission" to Marianne Farningham's tracts and Mrs. Elizabeth Stone's pseudonymous anti-suffrage volume. The doctrine was not simply imposed from outside; women's presses and women writers were among its most effective propagators.

**Feminist resistance existed, but stayed marginal.** Wollstonecraft's radicalism was "virtually repressed" after her death; Condorcet's demand for equality was "drowned in the avalanche of revolutionary violence"; Bentham's suffrage arguments went unpublished; Mary Hays and the Parisian journal L'Athenee des dames were met with ridicule (even from a woman reader who warned the journal off its own argument). This near-total suppression of a live, self-asserting impulse resonates with the World Machines idea of [[Conatus]] — striving to persist and expand — being tamped down by a capture regime, though the mechanism here is cultural conditioning and reputational punishment rather than the statistical/administrative averaging the concept usually names; what little pushback survived (Wheeler, Hays, L'Athenee des dames) is at best a fragile, largely defeated instance of [[capture-resistance]].

**The women's magazine as a diffusion technology.** From The Ladies' Mercury (1693) and the Journal des dames through Marianne Ehrmann's 1790s German periodicals, women's magazines existed before the nineteenth century, but print and distribution innovations turned "this trickle" into "a torrent": ninety-two French titles launched in the 1830s–40s alone, and Godey's reaching 150,000 circulation by 1860. This is a machine of ideological reproduction operating at genuinely mass scale — a technology for standardizing a single domestic ideal across a huge, geographically dispersed readership, which is what let a fairly narrow doctrine of womanhood become nationally normative rather than merely local or clerical.

**Sarah Hale's paradox.** Hale is presented as "a force that philanthropists and politicians chose not to overlook" — an editor of real ambition and "living refutation of commonplaces about woman's natural incapacity for aggression" — who spent four decades using that force to preach wifely submission, curating didactic fiction ("The Parlour Serpent," "Sweethearts and Wives," "False Pride") in which the only women's aggression permitted was directed at other, more aggressive women. Her own satire on a women's-rights convention, populated by hysterical men named "Wumenheyter" and "Easyled," leaks a "rage against men" she otherwise kept tightly controlled, even as she quietly and consistently agitated for better female education without ever substantially delivering it in her own magazine's pages.

**The consensus starts to crack.** By 1870, Charles Astor Bristed could call the "Woman Question" "a thing of the day" rather than an eccentricity — the unit ends on the doctrine's erosion, not its triumph, setting up the more assertive feminism the rest of the volume will track.

## Notable passages

Anna Wheeler's 1830 admission ("the hate of most men... that of the greater portion of the very sex") captures both the psychological cost of the domestic ideal and its self-perpetuating character — women themselves internalizing a "propensity for slavery." The long paragraph on "conscious self-denigration" catalogs how widespread and cross-national this literature of complicity was, using Lydia Maria Child's stark racial comparison as its sharpest instance. The paragraph tracing the women's magazine from 1693 to the nineteenth-century "torrent" establishes the institutional scale Gay wants readers to register before turning to Godey's specifically. The description of Sarah Hale as a "living refutation" of the incapacity-for-aggression cliché is the unit's central irony, restated more comically in the Wumenheyter/Easyled satire, which ends bluntly: women "have no business with aggression."
