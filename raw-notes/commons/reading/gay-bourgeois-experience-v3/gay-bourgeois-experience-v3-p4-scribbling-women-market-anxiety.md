# Scribbling Women: Gender, Aggression, and the Victorian Literary Marketplace

Gay's chapter—titled, in Hawthorne's own contemptuous phrase, "A D—d Mob of Scribbling Women"—applies the volume's argument about nineteenth-century aggression to the rise of the professional woman writer in Britain, France, Germany, and the United States. Writing for money was one of the few respectable careers open to middle-class women, and male writers and critics, "feeling crowded," read literary ambition in a woman as an act of aggression, answering it with everything from Samuel Johnson's bemused "Amazons of the pen" to Hawthorne's furious complaint about a "d-d mob of scribbling women." Gay traces the psychological toll this took on women writers themselves—guilt, pseudonymy, "identification with the aggressor," fictional heroines who renounce ambition for marriage—while also using sales and circulation figures, especially the German bestseller machine *Die Gartenlaube*, to show that the fiction elite critics dismissed as trash commanded audiences that "serious" literature could not touch.

## Themes

**Writing as a licensed but resented ambition.** Women's entry into authorship reads, on Gay's account, as a genuine advance in the "great redefinition of roles that marked the age," but one immediately contested: literary men, "feeling crowded, damned these invaders as too self-assertive by far." The hostility long predates Victoria—Horace Walpole calling Mary Wollstonecraft a "hyena in petticoats," Samuel Johnson worrying over "scribbling Amazons" who "set masculine tyranny at defiance"—which lets Gay read the Victorian conflict as the acute phase of a chronic condition rather than something new.

**Identification with the aggressor and the pseudonym.** The chapter's psychoanalytic key term, borrowed explicitly from Anna Freud, names the way women writers pre-empted male hostility by denouncing their own female colleagues' "popular trash," conceding that "great literature remained the province of men," or hiding behind a masculine pseudonym—George Sand, George Eliot, Currer Bell, E. Marlitt among them. Gay traces this partly to internalized "low self-esteem." Read against WMP vocabulary, the pseudonymous woman writer is a workable, partial case of [[capture-resistance]]: not full submission (she keeps writing, keeps publishing) and not open defiance (she disguises the fact that a woman is doing it), but a [[Conatus]]-like persistence that survives an otherwise-suppressive norm by negotiating with its terms rather than confronting them—capture-resistance under duress, not capture-resistance triumphant.

**Backhanded champions: "appreciation as aggression."** Even sympathetic critics reinstated the hierarchy their praise seemed to contest. G. H. Lewes argued for women's special aptitude for fiction on grounds of "greater affectionateness"; Rudolf von Gottschall credited women's "felicitous comprehension of social life" while calling it "the passive and reproductive talent of women." Gay's own gloss on Gottschall—"this was appreciation as aggression"—names the pattern precisely: essentialist praise that forecloses the possibility of women producing anything but a lesser, feminine-coded kind of literature.

**George Eliot turns on her own sex.** Eliot's 1856 essay "Silly Novels by Lady Novelists" is Gay's central case of a woman writer identifying with the aggressors rather than the victims of her culture: she ridiculed "brainless ladies" writing from "vanity" without exploring her own "promising diagnosis"—women's "enforced unworldliness"—that produced the fiction she mocked.

**Guilt and fictional renunciation.** Real women writers (Mary Brunton, Catharine Maria Sedgwick, Mary Virginia Terhune, Letitia Elizabeth Landon) recorded diary-and-letter anxiety about fame as a betrayal of domestic duty. Their own heroines are made to dramatize the same lesson: Augusta Evans's Beulah and Elizabeth Stuart Phelps's Avis both choose marriage over literary ambition, acting out the belief, voiced by Beulah's guardian, that "aggressiveness and happiness are incompatible—for women."

**A market too big to sneer at.** Hawthorne's complaint is the emblem of elite male resentment meeting raw sales figures: *The Lamplighter* sold tens of thousands of copies against *The Scarlet Letter*'s few thousand. Gay uses Margaret Oliphant's rebuttal—that Hawthorne addressed "an intellectual audience" while "the novelist's true audience is the common people"—to reframe the complaint not as usurpation of one market but as the emergence of new, "discrete reading publics" segmented by class, taste, and access to cheap print and lending libraries. That proliferation of parallel, mutually illegible audiences—a market elite critics could resent but not rank or contain—faintly rhymes with the [[divergence-machine]]'s variety multiplying beyond a legibility regime's reach; the fit is partial, since this is a Victorian print market rather than a civilizational infrastructure, but the shape of the problem (proliferation outrunning a would-be arbiter's capacity to sort it) is the same.

**Marlitt and the bourgeois formula.** The case of Eugenie John ("E. Marlitt") and Ernst Keil's *Die Gartenlaube* extends the argument to Germany and ties female authorship directly to bourgeois class ideology, the volume's overarching subject: reforming heroines, a repeated Cinderella plot, and "chaste German burgher femininity" triumphing over aristocratic "arrogant sanctimoniousness" built Keil a circulation of hundreds of thousands and Marlitt a fortune—while Keil, before her pseudonym was exposed, addressed his correspondence to "Herr Marlitt."

## Notable passages

**Hawthorne's "scribbling women"** (c1) is the passage that gives the chapter its title: an eminent male author converting a woman's commercial success into a personal grievance, complaining that "America is now wholly given over to a d-d mob of scribbling women."

**Identification with the aggressor and the pseudonym** (c2) states the chapter's psychoanalytic thesis in Gay's own terms and lists the pseudonyms—George Sand, George Eliot, Currer Bell, E. Marlitt among them—that recur as evidence throughout.

**George Sand's sword and pen** (c3) is Gay's closest reading in the unit: Sand's 1835 remark that, "not being a lad," she would "dispense with the sword and retain the pen," becomes for Gay a metaphor for literary ambition smuggled in through a borrowed, substitute masculine instrument.

**George Eliot's "Silly Novels"** (c4) covers Eliot's essay and Gay's closing judgment on it—"the history of aggression is a history of the subtlest mixed feelings"—his thesis statement for the whole chapter.

**Oliphant and the discrete reading publics** (c5) is Gay's evidence that the "invasion" male critics complained of was really the literary market fracturing into new, class-segmented audiences that had not previously existed at all.

**Marlitt's bourgeois Cinderella** (c6) shows the same dynamics playing out in Germany, with female authorship, popular formula fiction, and bourgeois self-congratulation at the expense of the aristocracy fused into one publishing phenomenon.
