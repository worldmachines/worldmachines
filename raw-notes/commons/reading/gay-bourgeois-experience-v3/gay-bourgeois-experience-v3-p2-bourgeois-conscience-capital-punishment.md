# The Bourgeois Conscience at Work: Capital Punishment and the Retreat from Public Vengeance

This unit traces the nineteenth-century campaign against capital punishment as a case study in the bourgeoisie's uneasy relationship with its own vindictiveness: reformers built a transnational abolitionist infrastructure that steadily narrowed the scope and frequency of executions across Britain, the United States, France, Germany, and Switzerland, even as defenders of the gallows kept marshaling religious, philosophical, and statistical alibis for retribution, and as flashpoints like the Guiteau assassination and the pre-1914 French and German legislative debates showed how quickly the "civilized" veneer could give way to naked demand for a sacrificial death.

## Themes

**Organized conscience versus persistent retribution.** Gay frames the century as a tug-of-war between an increasingly well-organized abolitionist lobby (Allen's 1809 Society, the Howard Association, state-level societies in Massachusetts, New York, and Philadelphia, continental counterparts in Belgium and Italy) and a retributive instinct that never fully yielded: Michigan's 1847 abolition survived repeated repeal attempts, Maine abolished, reinstated, then re-abolished the penalty within a decade, and the newly unified German Empire under Bismarck reintroduced the death penalty into its 1872 criminal code, reversing earlier abolitionist gains in Baden and Saxony. The struggle was never a simple linear "progress" narrative; it was fought and re-fought in legislatures, plebiscites, and courtrooms.

**Respectable alibis for keeping the gallows.** Even sophisticated, humane thinkers supplied cover for continued killing. Kant treated execution as an unconditional duty ("If he has murdered, then he must die"), and Goethe warned that abolishing the penalty would only unleash private vendetta — "self-help will immediately emerge." Decades later Paul Nacke updated the argument in explicitly Social Darwinist terms, describing the criminals worth executing as "virtual animals, sexual monsters like Jack the Ripper" whom "society should put out of harm's way" — a punitive echo of the book's broader "convenient Other" logic, here applied to the criminal rather than to race, nation, or class.

**Diagnosing the ritual as pathology.** The abolitionists' most psychologically acute move — and the one closest to Gay's Freudian thesis — was to notice that the state's violence against criminals gave pleasure to its agents and audience, not just security to society. Charles Neate's 1857 observation that some judges found "the pain of passing sentence of death... not unmixed with pleasure" is the unit's clearest statement of aggression enjoyed under cover of duty; the Saturday Review's 1856 defense of public hangings because they let crowds witness "not only a death, but a shameful death" makes the same point from the spectators' side.

**The Guiteau case as sacrificial ritual.** The assassination of President Garfield by the clearly disturbed Charles Guiteau becomes Gay's set piece for how the "civilized society in search of a sacrificial victim" actually worked: psychiatrists (including George M. Beard) diagnosed Guiteau as insane and said so publicly, but editorialists warned against "sentimental twaddle" that would let "this monster" live, and a jury coached by the judge convicted him in just over an hour. The episode dramatizes how thin the veneer of dispassionate justice was once a genuinely inflaming crime, rather than an anonymous statistic, was in question.

**Two rationales, endlessly recycled.** Following Benjamin Rush's 1792 pamphlet, Gay shows the abolitionist case settling into two durable arguments — the penalty is inhuman and it is ineffective — recombined for a century without much originality (Paul Nacke himself admitted in 1902 that "not a single new argument has been offered"). The inhumanity argument played out largely as biblical proof-texting, with conservative Protestants (Presbyterians, Baptists, Lutherans, Anglicans) invoking Leviticus and Numbers for "an eye for an eye," and Unitarians and Quakers countering with the Sermon on the Mount — the same book cited by both camps to opposite ends. The ineffectiveness argument turned on deterrence statistics and on a psychological insight (via Edward Livingston) that criminals, unlike soldiers, always "proceed with the hope that they will avoid detection," making the deterrent threat far weaker than its defenders assumed.

**Public spectacle versus private evasion.** A late, telling subplot: proposals to move executions out of public view (to appease squeamish opinion while keeping the penalty intact) were attacked by abolitionists themselves as insincere theater rather than genuine reform. English campaigners Cobden, Bright, and Ewart denounced private hanging as a "new dodge" and an "evasion of the main principle," while conservative opinion (the Saturday Review) defended the public spectacle precisely because it satisfied "the public's need for vengeance." Both sides agreed the ritual was about more than deterrence; they disagreed about whether hiding it was progress or hypocrisy.

**Statute outrunning practice.** By the unit's close (through 1908–1913 French and German debates on the eve of the war), Gay's evidence shows the death penalty becoming symbolically retained far more than it is actually applied — Britain narrows its capital offenses to murder, treason, piracy with violence, and arsenal destruction, and carries out well under half of the death sentences it still hands down. This produces a legitimacy pattern worth flagging for [[world-machines]] purposes: a punitive institution persisting in law (formally "Functional") while its lived application hollows toward the symbolic and rare — a real-world instance of something like the [[decay-sequence]], though applied here to a penal-legal institution rather than a canonical text or paradigm, and not carried all the way to "Absent" by 1914. The unresolved 1908 French vote (334–210 to keep the guillotine) and the 1911 German jurists' reversal toward retention likewise read as a [[gramsci-gap]]-like standoff — the old retributive legitimacy discredited but not yet displaced by a humanitarian penology that could fully replace it — a standoff the volume's later Epilogue (August 1914) suggests gets swept away rather than resolved.

## Notable passages

The opening paragraph sets Gay's thesis in miniature: Alexander Shand's 1888 observation that "society is no longer satisfied to lock up its wild animals, or hang them out of hand" frames the whole unit as a story of redescribing vindictiveness as pathology, while Gay immediately complicates it — society was "still hanging or guillotining its 'wild animals,' but more rarely and more hesitantly."

The Kant/Goethe passage shows how even the era's moral exemplars supplied respectable cover for retribution, with Goethe's vendetta-prevention argument later echoed and contested by German jurists debating the same point decades on.

The Guiteau passage captures the machinery of public "sentimental twaddle" rhetoric overriding a psychiatric insanity finding, and the speed (an hour and five minutes) with which a coached jury delivered the verdict the public wanted.

Neate's remark about judges for whom "the pain of passing sentence of death has been not unmixed with pleasure" is the unit's most direct statement of the book's central claim: that the ritual of punishment satisfies aggression, not just social order.

Edward Livingston's soldiers-versus-criminals comparison ("Soldiers march gaily to battle with the certainty that many of them must fall... Those who commit a crime... always proceed with the hope that they will avoid detection") is the unit's sharpest piece of deterrence psychology, undercutting the conservative assumption of rational calculation.

The closing statistical passage on Britain's shrinking catalogue of capital offenses and its "current against killing killers... running ahead of the letter of the law" is the unit's clearest evidence that the death penalty was becoming a formality that had outlived its practice well before it was formally abolished anywhere near universally.
