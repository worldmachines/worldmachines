# Manliness Contested: Thomas Hughes, His Critics, and the Cult of Honor

The unit tracks two related arguments. First, it follows Thomas Hughes's wildly popular ideal of manliness — launched in *Tom Brown's Schooldays* (1857), continued in *Tom Brown at Oxford*, and refined in *The Manliness of Christ* (1879) — as it complicates itself under Hughes's own hand and under the skepticism of contemporaries who suspected manly bluster was a mask for weakness. Second, Gay pivots from this relatively benign bourgeois manliness to the harsher, more rigidly codified "cult of honor" that survived into the nineteenth century in the antebellum American South, the Mediterranean world, and the German dueling subculture — a system in which "society was the superego," and which shows, by contrast, how much more unsublimated aggression the bourgeois version had actually tamed.

## Themes

**Hughes's manly ideal complicates itself from within.** *Tom Brown's Schooldays* opens by celebrating the "fighting" Brown family and Tom's prowess at back-swording and football, but by *Tom Brown at Oxford* Hughes is already distancing himself from the mindless "musclemen" among the muscular Christians, and in *The Manliness of Christ* he explicitly subordinates courage (which "some animals share with humans") to a more inclusive manliness that "entails tenderness and thoughtfulness" — "a great athlete may be a brute or a coward, while a truly manly man can be neither."

**Kingsley embodies the ideal's internal contradiction.** Hughes's ally Charles Kingsley was, in W. R. Greg's word, "fearfully pugnacious," one of the two most combative writers of the age; Gay reads Kingsley's pugnacity toward High Church and Roman Catholic clerics as itself an alibi for aggression, and notes that Kingsley's insistence on manliness left him "open to the suspicion that his insistence on manliness was a mask for its opposite" — he worried, obscurely, about effeminacy in himself even as he denounced it in others. Elizabeth Barrett Browning, tellingly, liked him precisely for the tenderness his public pugnacity concealed.

**Skeptics diagnose manliness as compensation.** Thackeray (in *Vanity Fair*, asking whether "men are cowards in heart" and therefore "admire bravery so much"), the Austrian novelist Adalbert Stifter ("Since the present-day world situation is one of weakness, I flee to strength"), and the *Westminster Review* (tracing the domestic-goddess ideal to male "jealousy" and panic at female competition) all read the cult of manly display as a defense — against personal weakness or against female aggressiveness. Gay notes this anticipates a properly psychoanalytic reading; a footnote cites Freud's 1915 remark that "masculine" and "feminine" are "among the most confused" concepts "in the sciences."

**Bourgeois anti-heroism as a deliberate counter-culture.** Thackeray, Trollope, Flaubert, and Fontane wrote "unheroic heroes"; Offenbach and Gilbert and Sullivan mocked generals, officials, and gods in operetta. This is presented as a secularized continuation of Enlightenment anti-militarism (Voltaire), replacing aristocratic heroics with "the undramatic domestic lives of ordinary mortals."

**The cult of honor as the harsher rival system.** Gay's real pivot: societies "devoted singlemindedly to the cult of honor" — the antebellum South, the Mediterranean, dueling Germany — regulated "all significant aspects of life... meticulously" and required affronts to be avenged by "ostracisms, duels, lynchings, vendettas." Unlike bourgeois manliness, which remained a live internal debate, honor culture left no room for ambivalence: "autonomy was sacrificed to the good opinion of others."

**Honor's gendered machinery.** Southern women are shown reproducing the code that victimizes their own sons and husbands, "modern versions of the fabled Spartan mother." A double sexual standard let young men collect sexual "trophies" among social outsiders while a woman's unchastity stained her male relations' honor "until the stain was erased, by aggression."

**Manliness curdles into political aggression late in the century.** Kingsley denounces bourgeois "effeminacy" to Sir Henry Taylor; Theodore Roosevelt defends "virility in an effete age"; Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.'s 1884 Memorial Day oration mourns a lost "race of conquerors." Gay's verdict: manliness became "the servant of diplomatic bullying, imperialistic adventures, and the insouciant resort to arms" — though, he stresses, it remained "a debatable ideal" well past Victoria's death.

## Notable passages

The Lamar/Preston Brooks episode is the unit's sharpest concrete case: a secessionist politician retroactively blames the Civil War on Charles Sumner's failure to fight back when caned on the Senate floor, reasoning entirely inside the honor code's logic ("We are men, not women... the fight had to come"). It shows the cult of honor operating as a real causal force in political history, not just a private code of manners.

The Greg-on-Kingsley footnote is the unit's clearest statement of Gay's "alibi for aggression" thesis: religious conviction licenses a man "boiling over with animal eagerness and fierce aggressive instincts... not called upon to control these instincts, but only to direct them."

The honor-culture definition paragraph ("These fairly benign readings of manliness...") is the pivot of the whole unit and the passage most legible to World Machines: it describes honor culture as an intensely legible, rule-bound regime governing "rites of passage, social intercourse, the choice of a mate, orders of rank and precedence, even commercial transactions" — a social infrastructure for channelling and licensing violence that resembles [[legibility-as-machine-core]], though here the object made legible and controlled is personal reputation and rank rather than territory or population. Gay's closing line, "society was the superego," also reads as a vivid instance of a regime capturing individual striving and redirecting it entirely toward external judgment — the same capture dynamic [[Conatus]] names, though Spinoza's frame is metaphysical where Gay's is a historically specific social institution.

Hughes's Rugby-world passage (the fighting Browns, back-swording, the football melee) and his later *Manliness of Christ* passage (distinguishing manliness from mere courage) bracket Hughes's own trajectory — from unreflective celebration of manly combat to a self-consciously "spiritual" redefinition — and are the unit's best evidence that the bourgeois ideal, unlike the honor code, was never settled doctrine but "a debatable ideal" contested even by its own popularizer.
