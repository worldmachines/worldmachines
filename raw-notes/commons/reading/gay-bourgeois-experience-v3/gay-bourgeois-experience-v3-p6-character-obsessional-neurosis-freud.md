# Character, Obsessional Neurosis, and the Machinery of Bourgeois Self-Mastery

Nineteenth-century advice literature enshrined “character” as the master virtue—self-control, thrift, industry, and “aggression disciplined and sublimated”—and prized it over intellect; Gay then traces how this cult of self-mastery produced its own diagnosable pathology, as Freud isolated obsessional-compulsive neurosis as the bourgeoisie's characteristic neurotic style, Max Weber found the same syndrome in the “obsessive capitalist,” and Gay closes by arguing that Freud's sardonic clinical lens undervalued how the very sublimation that produced neurotics also built the material achievements of the age.

## Themes

**Character as the alibi for controlled aggression.** The advice-book industry (Smiles, Whipple, Novalis's “completely fashioned will,” French “characterology”) treated character as more important than intelligence—Fouillee's rhetorical question about the positive sciences, English public schools valuing moral over intellectual formation—because character was the socially sanctioned form in which raw aggression got channeled into realism, temperance, and purposeful energy. Self-control was not the opposite of aggression but its disciplined expression.

**Self-control as freedom won by submission.** Smiles's paradox—“character was freedom won by submission to rule”—captures the literature's central move: the “one indispensable element” in good character was the laborious, never-finished conquest of inborn urges, since the native and the learned elements of character were held to be perpetually at odds (MacCunn: “all development involves repression”). The genre's optimism about self-remaking was persistently undercut by its own gloomy diagnoses of how little room for maneuver human nature actually allowed.

**From neurasthenia's catalogue to Freud's taxonomy.** Beard's 1880 diagnosis of “neurasthenia” swept every complaint of modern nervousness (blushing, insomnia, noises in the ears) into one vague, comprehensive basket, blamed on urban speed and crowding. Freud, building on Charcot, discriminated named types—obsessional neurosis, anxiety hysteria, paranoia—and relocated the cause from the pace of city life to the bourgeois family's repression of childhood sexuality, a move he pressed as cultural criticism as much as clinical diagnosis in “‘Civilized’ Sexual Morality and Modern Nervous Illness” (1908).

**Obsessional-compulsive neurosis as the bourgeoisie's preferred style.** Gay's central claim: allowing for exceptions, the nineteenth-century bourgeoisie had its own “recognizable preferred neurotic style,” obsessional-compulsive neurosis, which parodied the very virtues it prized. Hysteria and obsession are mirror-image failures of self-control—hysteria defies it with histrionic rebellion, obsession mimics it with ritual and doubt. The Rat Man's list-making, indecision, and enslavement to duty are “farcical reenactments” of bourgeois carefulness and industry; even his rare successes bring no pleasure, since the obsessive is “hypnotized by technique at the expense of effective work.”

**Weber's obsessive capitalist and the wider antibourgeois chorus.** Gay extends Freud's diagnosis to economic man via Weber's portrait of the businessman “imprisoned in the iron cage of modem life”—profit pursued as a symptom detached from pleasure, routine for its own sake, ancestry traced to Benjamin Franklin. Carlyle, Marx and Engels, Charlotte Brontë, and Mill are marshaled as a chorus that had already indicted the same compulsive, mercantile type on moral rather than clinical grounds.

**Gay's qualification of Freud.** The chapter closes with Gay's own verdict: Freud's picture of humanity “forever beset by clashing desires and prohibitions” is “original and compelling” but “not altogether satisfactory as cultural criticism,” because treating his patients as typical rather than marginal made aggression's outcomes look uniformly victimizing. Gay insists the same sublimated aggression that produced neurotics also collaborated with Eros to build cities, speed travel, and lengthen life—mastery is the achievement this volume's Epilogue will show collapsing in 1914.

## World Machines resonance

Whipple's ambition to make character “like other forces, calculated, and the issues of its actions predicted,” and French characterology's project of sorting persons into predictable types, are a legibility project turned inward: the same impulse toward measuring, ordering, and rendering-predictable that [[legibility-as-machine-core]] names for territory and population, here applied to the interior self. It is a partial fit—the target is psyche rather than land or population—but the mechanism (render the unruly calculable in order to govern it) is the same, and it is one expression of the [[modernity-machine]]'s broader regime of self-discipline that this volume documents across advice literature, law, and clinical medicine. The instinctual “inborn urges” that character-formation exists to suppress are also a case study in what a capture regime does to something like [[Conatus]]—though here the suppression is (by Gay's own closing argument) not simply lossy: sublimation converts the same striving into cities, travel, and communications rather than merely extinguishing it.

## Notable passages

The opening definition of “character” as “aggression disciplined and sublimated” sets the volume's terms: self-control is not opposed to aggression but is its socially respectable channel. Whipple's claim that character “can, therefore, like other forces, be calculated, and the issues of its actions predicted” shows the advice literature reaching for the same scientistic, legibility-minded confidence that characterized nineteenth-century social science generally. The passage naming obsessional-compulsive neurosis as the bourgeoisie's “recognizable preferred neurotic style” is the chapter's thesis sentence. The hysteria/obsession contrast paragraph lays out how both neuroses are “savage comments” on bourgeois ideals—one defying self-control, the other parodying it. Weber's “obsessive capitalist,” imprisoned in the “iron cage of modem life,” extends the diagnosis from private character to economic rationality itself. Finally, Gay's closing paragraph pulls back from Freud's sardonic clinical lens to insist that sublimated aggression also built the material civilization of the age—a corrective that keeps the chapter from reading as pure pathology.
