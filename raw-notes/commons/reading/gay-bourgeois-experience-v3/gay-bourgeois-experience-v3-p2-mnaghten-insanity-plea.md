# From the Insanity Plea to the Born Criminal

Opening from the 1843 M’Naghten trial, this unit traces roughly sixty years of Anglo-American and European argument over how much of a lawbreaker’s aggression the law should attribute to a broken mind rather than a wicked will. It runs from the M’Naghten rules and the “irresistible impulse” supplement, through Isaac Ray’s mid-Victorian optimism and an era of “bold, untrustworthy theorizing” about race and heredity, to Cesare Lombroso’s theory of the atavistic “born criminal,” the rival camps that formed around it (Havelock Ellis and Hippolyte Taine favorable, Gabriel Tarde and Paul Nacke hostile), and Hans Gross’s journal as an emblem of the new, institutionally distributed apparatus of criminological reform. It closes with Durkheim and Tonnies treating crime as a normal social fact, and with Gay’s own verdict that bourgeois society was trading “the savage thrill of acting out their vengeful impulses” for “less intense but more refined pleasures.”

## Themes

**The M’Naghten rules turn madness into a legal-medical boundary dispute.** Following Daniel M’Naghten’s 1843 acquittal for killing Robert Peel’s secretary, a panel of judges defined insanity as the inability “to tell right from wrong.” The later “irresistible impulse” rule extended the defense to offenders who kept their reason but were, at critical moments, “helplessly delivered over to his aggressive needs.” Gay reads 1843 as the year modern jurisprudence domesticated a view of mind, traced back to Plato, as “a highly vulnerable system of peremptory passions subjected to rational controls that at times break down.”

**Law and the new psychiatric expertise cooperate as senior and junior partners, not equals.** By late century, courts routinely weighed physicians’ testimony, but “the law remained, as it had always been, the senior partner.” The live dispute was not law versus psychiatry but nature versus nurture — and Gay is blunt about the science surrounding it: forensic psychiatry, like the “bold, untrustworthy theorizing” of Social Darwinism and skull-measuring race science around it, was often “a guessing game dressed up as a science.”

**Lombroso’s born criminal makes deviance legible as biology.** Lombroso’s atavistic stigmata — asymmetrical face, apelike arms, insensate skin, tattoos — convert moral judgment into a checklist of measurable bodily signs, a criminology built to read criminality straight off the body. This half-fits [[legibility-as-machine-core]]: it is legibility’s ambition (rendering a population’s deviance countable, classifiable, governable) without legibility’s rigor, since Gay notes the categories were self-confirming — Lombroso found born criminals “to no one’s surprise, least of all his own.”

**The reform movement is an accretion of institutions, not one man’s insight.** Lombroso stages his own origin myth — a single “dazzling moment” dissecting a brigand’s skull — which Gay immediately deflates: “The rest would be commentary—and partial retreat.” The real shift, on Gay’s account, came from journals, international congresses, translation projects, and organizations like the “German Society for Social Reform” accumulating quietly until the treatment paradigm became common sense. That is a good match for [[history-machine]]’s picture of a paradigm assembling silently and distributedly from independent components rather than arriving via a single visionary’s epiphany.

**The century’s arc: from retribution to treatment, from vengeance to refined pleasure.** By the 1890s, Durkheim and Tonnies could treat crime as a normal social fact rather than a moral one. Gay’s closing line states the volume’s deeper argument directly: expertise did not abolish aggression, it cultivated it into a different shape — bourgeois society trading the sight of retribution for the more refined pleasure of scientific management.

## Notable passages

The M’Naghten and “irresistible impulse” rules (**c1**) are quoted for their exact legal substance, since the unit’s whole argument turns on what those rules did and did not excuse. Lombroso’s programmatic statement of born-criminal theory (**c2**) and the catalogue of physical stigmata culminating in his self-mythologized “dazzling moment” (**c3**) together carry the central case study. The reception passage (**c4**) shows the theory splitting reactions along recognizably ideological lines — enthusiastic dehumanization (Taine’s “orang-utans with human faces”) versus methodological revolt (Tarde, Nacke) — without either side abandoning the shared project of managing crime scientifically. The list of reform institutions clustered around Hans Gross’s journal (**c5**) is the clearest evidence for reading this as a distributed rather than heroic history. The closing passage (**c6**) states Gay’s thesis in miniature: expertise re-channelled aggression rather than ending it.
