# The Victim as Executioner: Political Caricature and the Threshold of Licensed Aggression

This unit tracks political caricature and satirical theater across three nineteenth-century regimes — Daumier and Charles Philipon under the French July Monarchy, W. S. Gilbert (with Sullivan) under Britain's stage censorship, and the Munich weekly *Simplicissimus* under the German Empire — to show how each society drew, tested, and enforced a shifting line between tolerated wit and punishable insult, and how satirists who were themselves victims of that repression could turn their own aggression on safer, weaker targets, or provoke ferocious retaliation when they aimed at the summit of power.

## Themes

**Caricature as an alibi for aggression, tolerated only provisionally.** Daumier's 1831 lithograph *Gargantua* — Louis Philippe as a swollen glutton excreting bribery into medals and honors — began as a "vitriolic" but broadly licensed form of political commentary; the July Monarchy's own Charter of 1830 had promised press freedom. But the state's tolerance was never fixed: escalating prosecutions (520 suits against the press in the regime's first years), the confiscation of *Gargantua*, and Daumier's six-month imprisonment show a regime discovering, case by case, how much satirical aggression it could absorb before it moved to close the aperture. The 1835 laws, passed after an assassination attempt, finally "drew the line" precisely — converting an ad hoc, anxiety-driven toleration into codified prohibition. Daumier's own courtroom lithograph, showing an axe-wielding executioner behind a judge who tells a gagged defendant "you are free to speak, you have the floor," makes the hypocrisy of nominal free speech under real repression its explicit subject.

**The victim turns executioner.** The chapter's organizing idea, and the source of its title, is that repression does not eliminate aggressive humor so much as displace it. Once the political target that most provoked Daumier's rage became legally untouchable, he turned some of that same aggressive energy on "victims far less capable of retaliating" — women seeking divorce rights or entry into public life, whom he drew as shrill, grasping harridans. Gay explicitly generalizes this displacement: "Humorous aggression... may explode in almost any direction, and choose easy, defenseless targets as well as those who can strike back," comparing it to antisemitic humor told in periods of Jewish suffering — an instance of the book's broader theme of the **convenient Other** as a safer outlet for hostility than the true, dangerous target. The same pattern recurs in reverse at the unit's close: the *Simplicissimus* circle's mockery of Kaiser Wilhelm II — aggression against "the father" — brought "quick, pitiless counteraggression against the sons," so that "victims of repression who had turned executioner discovered that the bitten were biting back." Aggression and repression here run in both directions along the same chain.

**National calibration of the tolerable dose of aggression.** Gay's comparative method is explicit: "a nation... will get the humorous aggression its rulers can tolerate." France's regime, insecure after 1830, criminalized caricature outright by 1835. Britain, whose elite felt comparatively secure in its social position, allowed Gilbert and Sullivan's *Iolanthe* to mock peers, chancellors, and Parliament itself (sparing only the Queen) while nonetheless empowering a lord chamberlain, under the Theatres Act of 1843, to suppress plays offending vaguely defined "good Manners, Decorum," or "the public Peace." Germany was harshest of all: the Karlsbad Decrees of 1819, the 1874 press law's explicit ban on *Majestätsbeleidigung* (insulting the emperor), and prison terms in a *Festung* met even oblique mockery, on the theory — voiced by a Prussian minister of the interior — that "a joke, whether trivial or not trivial, is irrefutable." The felt danger of a joke, not its actual content, set the threshold.

**A resonance for World Machines, held loosely.** The instruments each regime built to manage this threshold — the Theatres Act's licensing categories, France's 1835 press law, Germany's 1874 statute naming precise offenses — are attempts to render an inherently ambiguous thing (what counts as dangerous mockery) into a fixed, administrable category, which faintly echoes [[legibility-as-machine-core]]'s account of institutions built to make an unruly domain legible enough to control. The fit is partial: this is legibility applied to speech and affect rather than to persons, land, or transactions, and Gay's own evidence (vague "guidelines," a minister declaring jokes "irrefutable" regardless of content) shows how badly aggression resists that legibility — the categories keep collapsing back into official anxiety and improvisation rather than settling into stable rule.

- The opening description of *Gargantua* (chunk c1) establishes Daumier's method and the regime's initial, still-permissive tolerance of savage caricature.
- The 1835 courtroom lithograph (chunk c2) stages the unit's central irony — an executioner with his axe standing behind a judge who invites "free speech" from a bound and gagged man — condensing the gap between a regime's professed and actual tolerance of aggression.
- The paragraph on Daumier's misogynist series (chunk c3) is the unit's clearest statement of displaced aggression: a victim of state repression redirecting his wit onto targets who cannot retaliate, explicitly likened to antisemitic humor.
- The Theatres Act paragraph (chunk c4) shows Britain's gentler but still real machinery of control, licensing plays against imprecise moral categories capacious enough to "satisfy the most prudish of Podsnaps."
- The Simplicissimus/Karlsbad Decrees paragraph (chunk c5) documents the harshest end of the spectrum, where officials treat the mere existence of a joke as self-evidently dangerous.
- The closing Langen/Wedekind passage (chunk c6) delivers the chapter's title thesis directly: satirists who had themselves been "victims of repression" turned "executioner" against the emperor, and were bitten back for it.
