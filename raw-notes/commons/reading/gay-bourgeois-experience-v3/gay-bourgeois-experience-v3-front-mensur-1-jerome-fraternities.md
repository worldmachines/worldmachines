# Mensur: The Cherished Scar

Gay opens Volume III's argument about “the cultivation of hatred” through Jerome K. Jerome's Wilhelmine-era travelogue account of the German student duel, the Mensur, then widens into a full sociology of the practice: its place among Europe's and America's lethal dueling cultures, its transmission from an aristocratic honor code into an overwhelmingly bourgeois student population organized into Corps and Burschenschaften, and a century of contested legitimacy — fabricated pedigrees, failed abolition campaigns, and a fraternity system that fed Germany's professional and political elite across the ideological spectrum.

## Themes

- **The double meaning of “cultivation.”** The Mensur illustrates the volume's controlling pun: individuals cultivate (indulge) aggression for its pleasures, while societies cultivate (restrain) it through rule-bound institutions. Gay reads the duel's ritualized structure as “an exercise in aggression checked by accepted rules” — violence licensed exactly to the degree it is fenced in.

- **Jerome as reluctant informant on buried savagery.** Jerome's travel account moves from comic ethnography of law-abiding German pedestrians and dogs to a “clinical,” “confessional” report on watching students slash each other's faces. His own escalating fascination across three successive duels (“I wanted more”) becomes Gay's central evidence for the Freudian claim that respectable civilization keeps “the savage” only just beneath the “starched shirt.”

- **A tamed descendant of a lethal European practice.** Set against real dueling deaths (Hamilton–Burr, Pushkin, Lermontov, a near-miss for Heine) and its steady presence in the fiction of Austen, Dickens, Trollope, Fontane, Chekhov, and Schnitzler, the bloody-but-survivable Mensur reads as “the duel tamed” — defanged enough that even a disapproving Quaker tourist, William Howitt, could call it “civilizing.”

- **Aristocratic form, bourgeois membership and motive.** Duelling fraternities performed aristocratic bearing and honor codes, but nobles were a minority of the student body (roughly one in six to one in twenty). The deputy Hermann Wendel's 1914 Reichstag jibe that “the bourgeois class has been feudalized and militarized” and Max Weber's dismissal of fraternities as mere “Avancementsversicherungsanstalten” (advancement-insurance companies) frame the Mensur less as a surviving feudal custom than as bourgeois status machinery borrowing feudal costume.

- **Elite reproduction across ideology.** The Corps supplied Germany's generals, ministers, bankers, professors, and lawyers while remaining “scrupulously unpolitical”: Wilhelm Liebknecht, Otto von Bismarck, and even Karl Marx (scarred over the left eye) all belonged to duelling fraternities — evidence that the ritual's function was class and status reproduction independent of, and prior to, political conviction.

- **A pedigree assembled after the fact.** The Mensur's “impressive-looking feudal pedigree was highly suspect”: defenders traced it from medieval towns to nobility to students, while the critic Georg von Below traced it instead to the “corrupt and decadent” court of Henry III of France. Gay concludes the credentials were “of uncertain origin at best. Possibly they were forged” — an invented tradition papering over the Corps' actual, much more recent, self-organization out of regional Landsmannschaften around 1800.

- **Persistent, unsuccessful opposition.** From the eighteenth-century Schokoladisten and Jena polemicists through Ringseis, Georg von Below, and the reforming theologian Adolf von Harnack, principled critics of the duel never disappeared and never prevailed against defenders like Heinrich Geffcken, who argued for a defensible “outer honor,” the Ehre — and even a proposed anti-duelling league's own members voted down a binding pledge to refuse challenges.

## Notable passages

- The opening thesis passage states the volume's project in miniature: cultivation as both indulgence and restraint of aggression, with the Mensur as the exemplary case.
- Jerome's confession is the chapter's psychological centerpiece — a first-person admission of mounting bloodlust that Gay treats as clinical evidence rather than travel color.
- Wendel's Reichstag speech and Weber's “insurance companies” gloss are the chapter's clearest sociological verdicts on what the fraternities actually did beneath their aristocratic performance.
- The “possibly they were forged” passage on the Mensur's contested pedigree resonates, loosely, with [[history-machine]]'s account of paradigms assembling silently and distributedly from scattered components: here, regional Landsmannschaften consolidating into Corps around 1800, only to be retrofitted afterward with a unified, mythic aristocratic ancestry. The fit is partial — Gay's frame is a decades-long invented tradition contested in pamphlets and legislatures, not a centuries-long historiographic paradigm shift that “lurches into visibility.”
- The closing “Politics had nothing to do with it” passage, together with the chapter's observation that duelling had lost “the persuasiveness of a living tradition” while retaining “the compelling force of an obsessive habit” (visible also in Fontane's Effi Briest, where a duel is fought purely from “an inherited sense of obligation”), rhymes with the [[decay-sequence]]'s drift from Functional through Allegorical to Ornamental: an institution whose original rationale (honor, courage-training) has drained away while its form persists on inertia. The fit is again partial, since the Mensur's decay plays out generationally within one social order rather than across a machine's centuries-long arc.
- More broadly, the Mensur as an accepted-rules mechanism for licensing and limiting aggression is a small-scale instance of what [[world-machines]] names at civilizational scale as an era's control infrastructure for channelling violence — though here the “machine” is a class ritual inside one nation's university system, several orders of magnitude below the scale the concept usually names.
