# The Empire of Fact: Bacon's Legacy and the Victorian Cult of Statistics

This chapter opens Gay's case that industrialization and the nineteenth century's “revolution in knowledge” exemplify aggression's constructive channeling: the mastery of nature through empirical science, statistics, and medicine. It traces how Francis Bacon's seventeenth-century program of “Human Empire” over nature was retroactively canonized by the Victorians (critical editions, biographies, statues, Macaulay's tribute, Darwin's self-professed Baconianism) as the founding charter of their “empire of fact.” It then surveys the century's institutionalization of fact-gathering — the Statistical Society of London, national censuses, moral statistics, Quetelet's social physics — alongside its stubborn shadow: bad data, racial bias in census results, and pseudoscientific panics (masturbation, the rhythm method) that fact-worship did not dispel. A central case study, Ignaz Semmelweis's discovery of the cause of childbed fever, is offered as the paradigm of disciplined empirical reasoning triumphing through method, while its telling of Semmelweis's ruin also registers the human cost of that discipline.

## Themes

**Aggression sublimated into mastery of nature.** Gay frames industrialization and the “revolution in knowledge” explicitly as “the most concerted series of aggressions ever launched against ignorance” — the volume's thesis that a drive normally spent on other people can instead be spent on nature, disease, and disorder, and count as civilizational achievement rather than violence.

**Bacon revived as ideology, not just method.** The Victorians did not discover Bacon; they curated him — critical editions (Spedding, 1857–74), biographies, a statue at Trinity College, and Macaulay's famous catalogue of Baconian benefits to humanity. [[francis-bacon]]'s program of exposing “idols,” submitting to nature “to be commanded,” and enlarging “the bounds of Human Empire” gave the century's practical men (engineers, statisticians, inventors like Babbage) a philosophical genealogy for programs that were really about present-day control.

**The cult of fact as substitute religion.** Huxley's “Sit down before fact as a little child” — issued in the grip of his own son's death — recasts scientific empiricism in the vocabulary of the Christian submission Huxley otherwise rejected. Gay reads this not as incidental rhetoric but as evidence that fact-worship filled a psychological role religion once held, demanding the same renunciation of wish in favor of external authority.

**Statistics and social physics as instruments of legibility.** The Statistical Society of London (1834), decennial censuses, and above all Adolphe Quetelet's “social physics” and “average man” sought to reduce social life — crime, suicide, marriage, birthrate — to lawlike regularities a state or reformer could act on. Quetelet insisted this determinism “enlarges, rather than reducing, the human soul's sphere of liberty,” an optimistic claim that knowing the law is itself a form of freedom. This is a fairly direct instance of what World Machines calls [[legibility-as-machine-core]] — making a population countable and governable — running at the confident height of the nineteenth-century [[modernity-machine]], though Gay's interest is the psychological motive (aggression made productive) rather than the complexity-destroying costs legibility exacts elsewhere in that framework. National styles diverged even here: British surveys relied on voluntary reformers and testimony, German ones on government questionnaires to bureaucrats, showing how one legibility project still refracted through different institutional cultures.

**The empire of fact was chronically undersupplied with facts.** Gay balances his admiration with irony: the 1840 U.S. census's racially skewed “insanity” statistics, physicians' confidently wrong advice on the rhythm method, and moralists' invented numbers for prostitution show that the century's proudest epistemic virtue — respect for hard data — coexisted with vast, confidently held error. Social investigators (Le Play, Riehl) also let a “good society” agenda quietly shape the “facts” they found, a caution about the neutrality claimed for legibility projects.

**Method as mastery: the Semmelweis case.** Semmelweis's investigation of childbed fever — testing and discarding hypothesis after hypothesis (miasma, student roughness, a priest's bell, birthing position) before isolating “cadaveric particles” and instituting handwashing — is offered as “constructive aggression at its best”: disciplined, patient, self-correcting inquiry that saved lives, even as Semmelweis's paranoid decline and death in an asylum shadow the triumph.

## Notable passages

Bacon's own words for his program — “the enlarging of the bounds of Human Empire” — supply the chapter's title and its central metaphor of knowledge as territory to be conquered (c1). Macaulay's itemized ledger of what “Baconian” science had delivered turns scientific progress into a kind of accounting of an empire's spoils (c2). Huxley's demand to “sit down before fact as a little child” shows fact-worship doing religion's psychological work at the moment of a father's grief (c3). Semmelweis's breakthrough — tracing childbed fever to “cadaveric particles” and cutting mortality to 1.27 percent by ordering handwashing — is Gay's model of applied empirical mastery (c4). Quetelet's claim that statistical law “enlarges, rather than reducing, the human soul's sphere of liberty” states the legibility project's paradox in its own terms (c5), while the chapter's opening line names the whole enterprise as aggression turned constructive (c6).
