# The Apotheosis of Conflict: Turning Competition into Natural Law

Opening Part One of *The Cultivation of Hatred*, this unit lays out Gay's three-alibi scheme for nineteenth-century aggression (competition, the "convenient Other," manliness) in general terms, then narrows to the first and most scientifically armored of the three: the claim, retrospectively labeled "Social Darwinism," that struggle and the elimination of the weak are simply how nature—and therefore society—works. Gay traces this alibi's lineage from Hobbes, Adam Smith, and Malthus through Herbert Spencer's pre-Darwinian evolutionary system to Darwin's own writing and its selective use by disciples, then follows the doctrine's contested reception across British religious culture and French fiction (Zola, Daudet, Bourget) through the 1880s and 1890s.

## Themes

**Alibis are plural, mobile, and can turn against their own users.** Gay's introductory pages insist that nineteenth-century alibis for aggression were never a settled consensus: they competed with each other, provoked guilt in some of the very people they licensed (Beatrice Webb's "new consciousness of sin," Bagehot's "excessive... sensibility to human suffering"), and were argued over as fiercely as the aggressions they justified. The alibi of competition is introduced as one contested strand among several, not a monolith.

**"Social Darwinism" is a name that erases its own history.** The unit's sharpest move is showing that the doctrine later baptized "Social Darwinism" by the 1880s did not originate with Darwin at all: Hobbes's state of nature, Adam Smith's invisible hand, and above all Malthus's population mathematics preceded the *Origin of Species*, and Herbert Spencer had already published *Social Statics* (1850) and coined "the survival of the fittest" (1862) as an independent system. Crediting Darwin's name for a distributed, decades-old intellectual formation is itself a small case study in how a dominant figure can retroactively absorb credit for a "history machine"-like accretion of independent components—though here the components were never hidden or silently assembling ([[history-machine]] is normally about invisibility until the paradigm suddenly appears; this is closer to the opposite: an already-visible, plural formation folded backward into one "great man's" name once the label caught on).

**Spencer's own doctrine strains against itself.** Gay devotes the unit's longest stretch to Spencer precisely because he refuses to fit the caricature: the same thinker who called the "purifying process" that starves "the sickly, the malformed, and the least fleet or powerful" a "merciful provision," also defended private charity, women's rights, and denounced imperialism and jingoism ("Aggression of every kind is hateful to me"). His statistical, population-level justification for withholding relief from individuals—see also Malthus's geometric-vs-arithmetic mathematics of population—reads as an early, doctrinal version of what WMP calls suppressing [[Conatus]] through statistical averaging: the individual's striving to persist is overridden by an appeal to the lawlike good of the aggregate. Spencer's late-life humane writings ("Re-Barbarization," his 1902 disavowal of "increase of population, growth of wealth" as the aim of progress) show the doctrine's author trying, unsuccessfully, to keep the alibi from swallowing his own values.

**Darwin's texts were mined selectively; the master was more cautious than his users.** Gay is careful to separate Darwin's actual reluctance to draw social-policy conclusions from his followers' eagerness to do so on his behalf. The *Descent of Man* passage on "civilised men" checking "the process of elimination" was seized on for anti-welfare arguments, even though Gay notes Darwin's private 1881 letter to William Graham shows he did, at least once, extend the doctrine to a racial hierarchy of "civilised" versus "lower races" being "eliminated."

**Reception was its own battlefield, split between religious accommodation and moral panic in fiction.** British religious culture largely negotiated an uneasy truce with evolution (Bagehot, Henry Drummond, Asa Gray reading natural selection as divine method) rather than the outright war the 1859 reviewers feared, while French cultural reception ran hotter: novelists like Zola, Daudet, and Bourget turned "the struggle for life" (*la lutte pour l'existence*, the coined *struggle-for-lifeur*) into a stock villain-and-suicide plot, using fiction to relitigate whether Darwinism licensed amorality. The slow, generational quality of the religious accommodation—anxiety publicly acknowledged, then patiently talked down over decades rather than resolved in a single rupture—has a family resemblance to a [[gramsci-gap]], though it is a smoother, more successfully managed transition than that term usually implies; no interregnum of "morbid symptoms" opens up, mostly quiet absorption.

## Notable passages

The unit opens by defining its central pun: alibis "**cultivated hatred, in both senses of the term**," licensing aggression while channeling it (c1) — the frame that makes competition, the convenient Other, and manliness parallel cases rather than isolated topics.

Spencer's own words supply the doctrine's clearest statement: "a stern discipline, which is a little cruel that it may be very kind," dismissing critics as "spurious philanthropists" (c2). Gay immediately complicates this by showing the "Social Darwinist" label is a misnomer applied after the fact to an already fully formed, independent system (c3), and later lets Spencer disavow the doctrine's imperialist uses in his own voice: "Aggression of every kind is hateful to me" (c4).

Darwin's *Descent of Man* is quoted at the point disciples found most usable—civilization's "checking" of natural elimination as an "injurious" softness (c5)—with Gay noting how selectively this was read against Darwin's broader caution.

Zola's Octave Mouret, "a Darwinian fighter, enjoying every moment of commercial combat" (c6), closes the unit's French material: competition among department stores rendered, without irony, as the same universal struggle for existence being debated in the sciences and the pulpits.
