# An Age of Advice and Neuroses

Gay surveys the nineteenth century's enormous prescriptive literature — pamphlets, tracts, didactic novels, etiquette manuals, medical guides — and argues that beneath its bewildering diversity of audiences and topics lies a single unifying demand: self-control, the rational will's mastery over instinct. Ostensibly a genre of moral uplift for an anxious bourgeoisie worried about the "noisy lower orders," declining religion, and effete comfort, the advice industry in practice built a "cultural superego" that channeled both aggressive and sexual drives into sanctioned outlets — temperance, thrift, and industrious activity — while still celebrating will and effort as the engine of mastery and success.

## Themes

**The advice industry as a symptom of bourgeois anxiety.** Gay frames the entire genre — moralizing pamphlets, didactic novels, etiquette books — as evidence that the nineteenth-century middle classes were not merely confident conquerors of a rationalized world but "exceedingly anxious" people seeking to "gain, or keep, command" over a world they feared was spinning out of control. The literature answers both an internal fear (unruly instinct) and an external one (subversive counter-advice from agitators, atheists, and birth-control propagandists).

**A long pedigree, democratized.** The genre did not begin with Victoria: Gay traces it back through Erasmus, Machiavelli's *The Prince*, Addison and Steele's *Spectator*, Rousseau's pedagogy, and Hannah More's tracts. What changed in the nineteenth century was scale — "the Victorian sermonizers democratized this literature, swelling a rivulet into a very Mississippi of advice" — turning courtly counsel into a mass household industry.

**Self-control as the master concept.** Whatever their ostensible topic — manners, health, child-rearing, business — advice writers converged on self-control (German *Selbstbeherrschung*, French *empire sur soi-même*) as the one indispensable virtue: "the triumph of reason over passion, of one's higher over one's lower nature." Gay explicitly ties this to Freud's later formulation of the pleasure principle versus the reality principle, presenting the Victorian doctrine as a lay anticipation of psychoanalytic drive theory.

**Temperance and the management of rage.** Temperance meant far more than abstaining from drink: it covered food, sex, and — most tellingly for Gay's aggression thesis — anger. The literature's most exacting test of self-command was "the management of rage, that supreme inciter of impulsive aggression," with moralists urging that aggressive urges be tamed not by emergency restraint but by making "trained self-command into second nature."

**Thrift as displaced self-denial, financial and sexual.** Thrift is presented as "only self-command in another guise" — the postponement of pleasure applied to money. Gay draws out its Freudian double meaning explicitly: physicians of the day linked the prudent husbanding of financial resources to the prudent husbanding of semen, so that "the need to master the sexual and the aggressive drives made common cause on the battlefield of existence."

**Mastery, not passivity: the love of power.** Crucially, self-control did not mean meekness. The literature also glorified industry and the "love of action and power" (idleness was denounced as viciously as immorality). Gay singles out George Moore's 1848 *Man and His Motives*, which locates in the infant's earliest motor pleasure "the love of power" as the root motive of all human action — a formulation Gay reads as containing, "in embryo, Nietzsche's will to power and Freud's early hypothesis of a drive for mastery." Self-command and self-assertion are two faces of the same coin.

## Notable passages

The opening paragraph names the phenomenon at scale: an era that could as well be called "the Age of Advice," whose sheer prescriptive output turned self-improvement into "a sizable household industry" ("scratch a realist and you found a moralist in disguise").

The definition-setting passage on self-control ties the entire genre's vocabulary — *Selbstbeherrschung*, *empire sur soi-même* — directly to Freud's pleasure/reality-principle distinction, making explicit that Gay reads this Victorian moral culture as psychoanalytic material avant la lettre.

The rage-management passage is the clearest bridge to the volume's aggression thesis: temperance's hardest test was not diet or drink but the discipline of anger itself, disciplined into habitual "second nature" rather than emergency suppression.

The thrift passage is Gay at his most characteristic, reading a mundane bourgeois virtue (financial prudence) as a displaced expression of drive management, explicitly yoking the sexual and aggressive economies together.

The Moore passage is the chapter's intellectual payoff: a mid-century advice book is shown to anticipate, independently, both Nietzsche's will to power and Freud's drive for mastery — evidence for Gay's larger claim that the advice literature's psychology of self-command was already doing proto-psychoanalytic work.

The unit's account of an internalized, self-administered discipline — a private technology by which the bourgeois self is made accountable, measured, and managed (health "earned," "invested," made to "yield its interest"; thrift as bookkeeping applied to the passions) — resonates with [[legibility-as-machine-core]], though the register differs: that concept names a state's project of rendering populations and land measurable for external control, while this is a civilization teaching its own members to render themselves legible and governable from within. The chapter's account of "the love of power" as the primal, universal motive that advice literature seeks not to suppress but to channel into sanctioned industry, thrift, and self-mastery also half-echoes [[Conatus]] — but where WMP's use of conatus emphasizes capture regimes suppressing striving via statistical averaging, this material describes the opposite mechanism: a regime that captures striving by redirecting and sublimating it, never denying the drive to persist and expand so much as disciplining its expression.
