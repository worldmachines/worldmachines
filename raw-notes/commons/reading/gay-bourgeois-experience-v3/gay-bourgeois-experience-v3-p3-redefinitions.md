# Redefinitions: Turning Aggression into Legitimate Politics

This is the opening chapter of Part Three, "Demagogues and Democrats," and it sets up the section's whole argument: the Victorian age redefined aggression across the board (competition secularized, race dramatized, manliness democratized), and politics was the "most spectacular beneficiary" of that rethinking, as the aristocratic monopoly on power became first an aspiration, then a serious claim, of male middle-class burghers. Gay defines politics as one of the "supreme fictions governing modem public life": an institutionalized, largely nonviolent contest that channels aggressive drive into rational self-interest or public ideals, sharply distinguished from raw hostility. He traces the slow construction of this fiction — from antiquity's and absolutism's frankly violent contests for power, through the eighteenth century's gradual softening of vindictiveness toward the defeated, to the nineteenth century's bourgeois-driven, unevenly distributed arrival of legitimate opposition, free press, and elections — and closes by naming the chapter's dark twin: the demagogic "Caesarism" that could seize the same machinery.

## Themes

**Politics as sublimated aggression, not its absence.** Gay's founding claim is that politics is "an exemplar of aggression channeled in the service of rational self-interest or realistic public ideals," explicitly not synonymous with hostility: "A pogrom may serve politicians, but it is not politics." The aggressive drive is constant across history; what changes is the institutional form available to channel it, and this chapter is essentially a history of that form's construction.

**Ancient and absolutist politics were unsublimated.** Benjamin Constant's 1819 lecture on ancient versus modern liberty supplies Gay's contrast case: the Athenian citizen was publicly sovereign but privately "virtually a slave," and contests for power in antiquity — down to the condemnation of Socrates — were "far more an acting out than a sublimation of aggression," routinely ending in death. Absolutist Europe extended the same logic through the fiction of royal infallibility, which displaced blame onto "evil advisers" and licensed vindictive purges: Richelieu's exiles and executions of rivals in the 1620s, the beheading of Strafford and Laud in the 1640s, Baron Görtz's decapitation in 1719.

**Modern politics required specific infrastructure, not just goodwill.** Gay is explicit that legitimate, rule-governed political contest is an achievement, not a default: it "requires a fairly sophisticated technology of communication and transportation, a high level of literacy, and the rather genial expectation of an inborn capacity for responsible conduct," conditions supplied only by the French Revolution and industrialization. This resonates with [[legibility-as-machine-core]] — Gay's frame is about channeling aggression rather than rendering a population administratively readable, but both converge on the claim that durable institutional infrastructure, not spontaneous restraint, is what makes non-violent contest for power possible at scale. The century-long build-out this chapter narrates is, in effect, the Dawn-to-Day construction phase of a political order within what WMP would call the [[modernity-machine]], though Gay's units are psychoanalytic (drive, sublimation) rather than the machine's own vocabulary of legibility and control.

**A gradual, distributed redefinition, not one invention.** The chapter's evidentiary spine is a sequence of independent witnesses spread across decades and countries — Constant (1819), Royer-Collard, Tocqueville (1831), Gervinus (mid-century), Sybel (1869) — each separately registering that "the prevalence of the bourgeois classes" and an irresistible "democratic tendency" were arriving. No single revolution or figure installs modern politics; it accretes. This is a loose cousin of [[history-machine]]'s account of a paradigm assembling silently and distributedly from independent components until it lurches into visibility, rather than "great-man" installation — though Gay's assembling agents are named intellectuals and politicians, not the more anonymous distributed components the WMP concept usually picks out.

**Bourgeois ambivalence and uneven national uptake.** "The prospect of power was more enticing than the prospect of democracy" for the middle classes, and the redefinition proceeded unevenly across societies: France's bourgeoisie monopolized power outright after 1830; Britain's deferred to a resilient aristocracy; Germany's Burgertum was delayed into the twentieth century; the American masses moved beyond patrician control entirely. Gay uses this comparative unevenness (via Tocqueville and Engels) as a diagnostic of how differently each society metabolized the century's redefinition of legitimate aggression.

**The apparatus of legitimation is also capturable.** The chapter ends on Tocqueville's 1831 uncertainty — "whether we are going toward liberty or marching toward despotism, God alone knows precisely" — pivoting directly into the section's title theme: "the rise of the new Caesars—political leaders at times in league with bourgeois, more often with their adversaries—who would impose despotism in the name of liberty." The same infrastructure built to sublimate and legitimate aggression is shown, in the same breath, to be seizable by a demagogue who turns it back toward domination. This is a structural cousin of [[capture-resistance]], though the mechanism differs from WMP's usual sense (statistical averaging suppressing conatus): here capture means one actor annexing a legitimating apparatus that was built to distribute contest among many.

## Notable passages

The opening paragraph states the chapter's thesis directly: the Victorian era's general habit of "redefinition" (competition secularized, race dramatized, manliness democratized) finds its "most spectacular beneficiary" in politics, as power shifts from aristocratic factions toward the male middle class.

The second paragraph gives Gay's working definition of politics as sublimated aggression — "among the supreme fictions governing modem public life" — and its sharpest line distinguishing legitimate contest from hostility outright: "A pogrom may serve politicians, but it is not politics."

A short but load-bearing sentence makes the infrastructural argument explicit: modern politics "requires a fairly sophisticated technology of communication and transportation, a high level of literacy, and the rather genial expectation of an inborn capacity for responsible conduct" — conditions Gay treats as historically achieved, not natural.

The long Constant paragraph supplies the ancient contrast case and ends on a blunt summary of pre-modern political violence: "And so were later efforts, for centuries. Blood remained their argument."

The Richelieu passage shows the absolutist mechanism (the "evil advisers" fiction) at work and lands on a line that echoes the book's own title: "Richelieu cultivated his hatred with a vengeance, but in an accepted style."

The closing passage pivots from Tocqueville's genuine uncertainty about liberty versus despotism into the chapter's final warning about "the rise of the new Caesars... who would impose despotism in the name of liberty" — the hinge into the rest of Part Three.
