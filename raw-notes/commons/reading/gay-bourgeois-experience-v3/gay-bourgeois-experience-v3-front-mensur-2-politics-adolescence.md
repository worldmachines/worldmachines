# The Burschenschaft, the Mensur, and the Alibis of Bourgeois Aggression

Gay traces the German student fraternity movement, the Burschenschaften, from its 1815 founding in the exhilaration and despair of the anti-Napoleonic wars through a century of state repression, anti-Semitic exclusion, and erotic mythologizing, arguing that its central institution — the ritualized sabre duel known as the Mensur, with its trophy scar (Schmiss) — was at once a licensed outlet for real aggression, a badge of honor and manliness, a site where the "convenient Other" (Jews) was both excluded and, paradoxically, recruited into the same violent proof of courage, and, underneath it all, a psychoanalytically legible ritual through which adolescent bourgeois men managed the problem of asserting and submitting to paternal authority at the same time.

## Themes

**Born political, born aggressive.** Unlike gentler student traditions, the Burschenschaften entered the history of political aggression right from their birth, founded at Jena in 1815 out of the mixed exhilaration of the anti-Napoleonic campaign and despair at the reactionary Vienna settlement that tried to restore the Old Regime and stuff nationalism and democracy back into the bottle of prerevolutionary institutions. Friedrich Ludwig Jahn's fusion of calisthenics and patriotic nationalism supplied the ideological seed; the fraternity's slogan, "Freedom, Honor, Fatherland," announced a program equal parts civic, moral, and nationalist — and, per Gay, no clearer proof of a "rising bourgeoisie" asserting itself exists.

**Honor as alibi.** Of the slogan's three terms, Honor was the load-bearing one: fraternities kept the aristocratic duel but insisted on purifying it into a spiritualized test of character rather than a brawl. Gay names the underlying mechanism directly: one of the most seductive alibis for aggression was the professorially-imbibed conviction that a principled, honorable rebel is entitled to take the law into his own hands — a straight, short line from abstract political conviction to real violence, with Sand's 1819 assassination of Kotzebue as the extreme case.

**Repression breeds counteraggression, not resolution.** The Wartburg Festival, Sand's murder, and the Karlsbad Decrees form a compressed escalation cycle: press laws, an investigative commission, purged professors, dissolved fraternities — aggression, even the intimation of aggression, energetically repressed by counteraggression. Yet the Mensur survived underground for two decades and outlasted 1848 and the founding of the Reich in 1871; the old order's repressive apparatus suppressed the forms of national-democratic aspiration without dissolving the forces beneath them. This loosely resonates with [[gramsci-gap]] — an old legitimacy enforcing itself against a new order it cannot yet let be born — though Gay's frame is a single institution's century-long half-life, not a civilizational-scale transition.

**The convenient Other, and its imitators.** After 1871, anti-Semitism curdled into doctrine: bigoted fraternities declared Jews satisfaktionsunfähig — unworthy of dueling, and so formally excluded from the honor economy. Yet Jewish students (Theodor Herzl among them) sought out the Mensur precisely to disprove the stereotype of cowardice, and separate Jewish fraternities, bourgeois all of them, adopted the same ritual. Exclusion and imitation ran on the same rails: the group cast as Other absorbed the very violence used to mark it as other, in pursuit of the status it was denied. A loose echo of nationalism's more organic, purity-demanding register sits behind the fraternities' logic (see [[nationalism]]), though Gay's interest here is social-psychological rather than intellectual-historical.

**Manliness, love, and the scar.** Late-century mythology recast the Mensur as a mating ritual: women were said to be "militant by proxy," courtship fiction had fiancées request a good-looking Schmiss, and Gay's own thesis crystallizes the fusion — the scar, in all other contexts a sign of failure, became the passport to success, aggression stimulating love and love stimulating aggression. Fraternal life also generated intense homoerotic bonds between men (Bloem's fictional Achenbach and Klausner "became brothers" after an embrace), which Gay reads as a refuge from, not a substitute for, heterosexual risk.

**Adolescence and its compromise formation.** Gay closes with an explicitly psychoanalytic account: adolescence revives childhood's forbidden sexual and aggressive impulses just as rebellion against paternal authority becomes physically possible, producing anxieties the Mensur is brilliantly conceived to manage. The duel let a young bourgeois gratify and at the same time defy his father — rebelling in form while reproducing paternal values in substance, a "most pliant, conventional rebel." Gay's verdict is double-edged: the Mensur was a living paradox, devised to fence in the spirit of aggressiveness even as it fostered that very spirit — it controlled violence and canonized it.

## Notable passages

The founding slogan passage (c1) supplies Gay's own reading of the Burschenschaft as bourgeois self-assertion in political form. The alibi-for-aggression passage (c2) is the unit's most explicit statement of Gay's central analytic device — a respectable rationale licensing real violence — applied here to student radicalism. The Karlsbad Decrees passage (c3) shows the state's control apparatus (press law, investigative commission, professorial purges) responding to that violence with counteraggression rather than resolution. The satisfaktionsunfähig passage (c4) captures the fraternity's anti-Semitic turn after 1871 and the disorienting fact that some Jewish students and fraternities took up the same weapon used to exclude them. The scar-as-passport passage (c5) is Gay's clearest fusion of the volume's aggression theme with an erotic economy of manliness. The closing passage (c6) delivers the unit's psychoanalytic thesis: the Mensur as a compromise formation between filial defiance and filial conformity.
