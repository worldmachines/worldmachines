# The Convenient Other: Race as an Alibi for Aggression

Gay opens Chapter 2 of *The Cultivation of Hatred* with the Dreyfus Affair and works outward: hating a collective "Other" is an ancient, endlessly renewable alibi for aggression, and the nineteenth century's distinctive contribution was to dress this old drama in the borrowed authority of science. He uses Freud's theory of unconscious projection as his explanatory key, tracks the slide of Enlightenment racial taxonomy (Linné, Buffon, Blumenbach) into full nineteenth-century racism (Gobineau, Broca, Knox, Lapouge, Freeman), and closes by reading the century's turn from triumphalist to anxious race theory as a symptom of deepening unease about where "modern civilization" was headed.

## Themes

**The Other is a psychological alibi, not a discovery.** French anti-Semites kept believing Captain Dreyfus was a German spy against all evidence, because "the reassuring split between friend and enemy is bound to yield" psychological dividends "to all but the most sophisticated minds." Gay traces the same pattern back through the Hebrews' covenant, Egyptian and Greek self-flattery, and Pericles's funeral oration: "the more one loved one's own, the more one was entitled to hate the Other." Politicians exploited it deliberately — Pomponne de Bellièvre's proposed crusade against the Turks, Seward's 1861 suggestion to Lincoln that a foreign war could reunite the country.

**Freud's projection explains why aggression needs a target.** Gay leans on Freud's mid-1890s clinical theorizing: people "defend themselves against their unacceptable thoughts or wishes by expelling them from their own mind into the outside world, onto the convenient Other." Because the mechanism is largely unconscious, it is effective — declaring superiority over "quarrelsome Irishmen" or "shopkeeping Englishmen" both denies one's own belligerence and licenses aggression against whoever now carries it. Gay's formula for the sequence: "I am bad / I am not bad / You are bad."

**Race pseudoscience is a classification project that curdled into a ranking project.** Linné, Buffon, and Blumenbach were still doing Enlightenment taxonomy — cataloguing human variation without yet using it to rank; as Gay puts it, "Race theorizing had not yet become racism." Over the century that taxonomy hardened into Gobineau's, Broca's, and Knox's science of hierarchy: skull measurements, brain weights, cephalic indexes. Tellingly, Broca's craniometrists kept the very findings that embarrassed their thesis (some professors' brains were smaller than some convicted criminals') and simply complicated their criteria rather than abandon the ranking — a measurement regime that could not be falsified because its real purpose was never epistemic. This rhymes with [[legibility-as-machine-core]]: the same drive to make a population readable, measurable, and orderable that Scott's states and Landes's clocks pursue for control-at-scale, here redirected toward manufacturing a hierarchy that licenses aggression against whatever it measures as lesser. It is legibility in the service of an alibi rather than of administration.

**The same drama restages at the level of nation.** Anglo-Saxonist and Celticist mythmakers built rival ancestral pedigrees from Tacitus and Montesquieu; the French, after 1870, disowned their own "Celtic heritage" in envy of German and English "vigor." The content of the imagined national character is arbitrary — what matters structurally is having one to be proud of and an other's to despise. This loosely rhymes with [[nationalism]] as another register in which a constructed "character" does the alibi-work that race does here; the two frequently blur in the unit's own examples (Aryan vs. Semite, Anglo-Saxon vs. Celt).

**Confident racism gave way to anxious racism.** Gobineau-era race theory is triumphalist, crowning a superior race at history's summit; by the fin de siècle of Lapouge and the Anglo-Saxon-versus-Celt panics, the tone turns defensive, fed by falling French birthrates, American anxiety about immigration, and aristocratic fear of democratization, and it forecasts an apocalyptic "race war." Gay reads this shift in mood as itself diagnostic of a mounting, almost uncontrollable anxiety over the direction of modern civilization in the decades before 1914. That loosely rhymes with [[gramsci-gap]]'s "morbid symptoms" of a legitimacy that can no longer convince itself — though the gap there names the interregnum between two millennium-scale world machines, while what Gay describes is a single civilization losing confidence in its own trajectory; the resonance is in the psychological shape of the anxiety, not the timescale.

## Notable passages

The Dreyfus opening (c1) states the unit's thesis in miniature before any theory is introduced: hatred of an Other is "immensely serviceable" because it "solidifies the bracing sense of one's merits" while "assuaging the secret fear of one's imperfections." The Linné-to-Blumenbach passage (c2) is the hinge on which Gay's whole argument turns — proof that racial classification and racism are historically separable, so that what happens afterward is a specific and explicable degradation rather than an inevitability. The projection passage (c3) is Gay's actual analytic apparatus, borrowed wholesale from Freud, and the promiscuous list of interchangeable targets it ends on (Freemasons, immigrant laborers, capitalists, and a garbled reference to well-poisoning slander) shows that the mechanism cares less about who the Other is than that there be one. Chekhov's letter (c4) matters because it shows a contemporary — writing before Freud had published the theory Gay uses to explain him — independently diagnosing the exact mechanism in the exact affair that opens the chapter, which is Gay's evidence that projection is a real social pattern and not merely his own retrospective imposition of psychoanalytic theory. Broca's brains (c5) is the unit's sharpest demonstration of pseudoscience's self-immunizing structure: honestly reported data that contradicts the racial ranking is absorbed by adding complexity rather than by revising the conclusion.
