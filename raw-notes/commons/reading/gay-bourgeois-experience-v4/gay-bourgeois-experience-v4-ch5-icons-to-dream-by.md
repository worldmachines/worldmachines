# Icons to Dream By: How the Bourgeoisie Sanctified Millet and Böcklin

This section of Gay's chapter on the visual arts examines how nineteenth-century bourgeois viewers consumed paintings — not simply as décor or investment but as vehicles for religious longing, self-projection, and (its opposite) evasion of self-scrutiny. After surveying the class-stratified art market and its advice literature, Gay contrasts kitsch, which foreclosed introspection, with two case studies in bourgeois canon-making: Jean-François Millet, whose Angelus was mass-reproduced into a quasi-devotional icon despite a substantially fabricated biography of poverty and rejection, and Arnold Böcklin, whose serial self-portraits and ambiguous Isle of the Dead became screens onto which German-speaking bourgeois audiences projected their own inner needs.

## Themes

**Mass reproduction democratized access to art without leveling taste.** Advice manuals (Constanze von Franken's household guide, a French domestic-economy manual of 1885) and popular magazines (Die Gartenlaube) coached the aspiring bourgeois on how to choose "good taste" in decoration regardless of income, while celebrating new reproductive techniques — woodcut, steel engraving, chromolithograph, photography — for making "accessible to large masses the rarest and most expensive treasures of all times and all climes." Income stratified originals from copies, but the conviction that art "had something more meaningful to impart than visual attractiveness" cut across income levels within the bourgeoisie.

**Kitsch functioned as a defense against inwardness.** Gay draws a hard line between viewers who could "probe beneath surfaces" and the far larger public content with the sentimental pleasures of family-magazine staples (stags, kittens, a "highly colored Savior"): designed for "a chuckle or a sentimental tear rather than deep emotion, let alone introspection," kitsch is named explicitly as a defensive structure against the volume's central theme of self-scrutiny.

**Millet's cult reveals the bourgeoisie manufacturing its own antagonist.** Antibourgeois critics (Gautier, Thoré) prized Millet because they believed the bourgeoisie despised him, but Gay shows this to be substantially fiction: Millet exhibited regularly, prospered, and received the Légion d'honneur. It was his own first biographer, Alfred Sensier, who "made Millet palatable to rich and pious collectors by overdramatizing his early poverty" and suppressed the fact that Millet's domestic arrangements were unconventional — the myth of the tortured, rejected genius was built for, and sold to, precisely the bourgeois audience it was said to indict.

**The Angelus became a substitute devotional object for a self-consciously disenchanted age.** Its price rose from 1,000 to 800,000 francs, it was reproduced onto napkins and salt boxes, and it drew explicitly religious readings from writers with no evidence Millet intended them — Gay quotes the German art historian Albert Dresdner's 1904 call, prompted by an essay on Millet, that "the world is disenchanted" and "it is imperative to teach it to recognize and honor the divinity and the divine once again." This is a striking, text-internal statement of re-enchantment as compensation for a perceived disenchantment, resonant with WMP's account of re-enchantment answering the [[modernity-machine]]'s legibility-driven disenchantment — though here the mechanism is not institutional reconstruction but a purchasable, mass-produced icon whose sacred aura is manufactured and monetized by dealers, biographers, and critics, closer to [[enchantment-as-managed-fiction]]'s claim that enchantment survives only because someone keeps staging and paying for it.

**Böcklin's art was read as continuous self-construction rather than singular self-expression.** Contemporaries insisted "the true home of his art was in himself" and that "everything he was came from his own self"; his seven self-portraits across three decades (with a fiddling skeleton, with laurels and columns, with a wineglass) function less as static self-images than as serial installments of an ongoing self-presentation. This resonates with [[self-construction-through-writing]]'s claim that the self is iteratively constituted by an act repeated over time, extended here from the diary or letter to the painted self-portrait — though Böcklin's medium is visual and public exhibition rather than private written self-address.

**The Isle of the Dead functioned as a mirror, not a message.** Deliberately unidentifiable in subject and setting, the painting drew wildly divergent readings — guardian angel, forgotten priest, mourning widow, elegy for antiquity — that Gay treats as evidence for his central claim about this material: "viewers responded more to their own inner needs than to the artists' message." Even Freud, "much like other conventional bourgeois," dreamt about Böcklin's paintings.

**Commodification's endpoint: the sacred image as advertisement.** Once fully absorbed into the reproduction economy, Böcklin's imagery was licensed to sell Odol mouthwash and sparkling wine — the terminus of a cycle in which quasi-religious aura, first manufactured for devotional and psychological consumption, becomes available for any commercial use at all.

## Notable passages

The curated passages track this arc: kitsch as refusal of inwardness; the reveal that Millet's suffering was substantially a biographer's invention pitched at pious collectors; Dresdner's disenchantment/re-enchantment diagnosis pinned explicitly to Millet's reception; the claim that Böcklin's art had no home but himself; the catalog of incompatible readings projected onto the Isle of the Dead; and Gay's closing thesis that bourgeois viewers read their own needs into images regardless of artistic intention. Together they support Gay's broader argument that the visual arts, no less than diaries or autobiography, served the nineteenth-century bourgeoisie as instruments of the "naked heart" — media through which an ostensibly secular, self-controlled class pursued the religious and introspective needs it could no longer admit to pursuing directly.
