# Heroes and Antiheroes

This unit traces the nineteenth-century cult of the biographical hero from its classic theoretical statement in Thomas Carlyle's *On Heroes, Hero-Worship, and the Heroic in History* (1841) through its didactic uses in mass-market Lives (Weems's Washington, Smiles's engineers) to its slow erosion by antiheroic debunking and a more psychologically candid, "scientific" biography — traced concretely through three successive Lives of Nelson (Southey 1813, Mahan 1897, Hannay 1911) and through the ideologically fractured biographical tradition around Napoleon.

## Themes

**Carlyle's Great Man theory as a diagnosis of bourgeois unheroism.** Carlyle's thesis — "the History of the World is but the Biography of great men" — is presented less as timeless truth than as a period symptom: Carlyle believed his own "scandalous" generation had lost the capacity for greatness, and hero-worship became, in Gay's account, "a desperate wish masquerading as an established fact." The unheroic, safe, unadventurous character of bourgeois life itself generated the craving for surrogate giants.

**Hero-worship as substitute religion and vicarious possession.** As traditional faith loosened its hold, Gay argues, idolatry of military leaders, virtuosi, and literary titans filled the resulting void. Carlyle's own gloss on Shakespeare — the English "could at least claim to possess him" even without resembling him — makes the mechanism explicit: identification with genius compensated for a felt absence of heroic stature in one's own unremarkable, bourgeois life.

**Antiheroic debunking as counter-current.** Thackeray's *Vanity Fair* ("the Novel without a Hero"), Daumier's lithographs, and Offenbach's operetta send-ups supplied a sophisticated, minority counter-tradition of ridicule against both classical and modern pseudo-heroes, anticipating Lytton Strachey's later demolition of "those two fat volumes" of Victorian panegyric in *Eminent Victorians*.

**Biography as a didactic technology for character formation.** Alongside advice literature, sermons, and conduct manuals, biography supplied "advice proffered through examples": concrete, ostensibly true success stories built to mold *character* — hard work, self-denial, temperance, duty — on the conviction, shared across national traditions, that "character could be learned." Samuel Smiles's *Self-Help* and *Life of George Stephenson* fused the two genres outright, presenting an idealized self-made engineer as character incarnate.

**The historicization of biography: from panegyric to psychological candor.** Gay uses three Lives of Nelson to chart a real historical development. Southey (1813) all but erases Nelson's affair with Emma Hamilton and permits only a single "blot" on his record; Mahan (1897) deliberately investigates Nelson's "inner life" and "underlying native character," treating erotic entanglement as evidence about the man rather than scandal to suppress; Hannay (1911), writing for the *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, goes further still, portraying Nelson's vanity and susceptibility to flattery with a bluntness Gay compares to Strachey's. Biography, across the century, becomes progressively willing to treat the subject as a full psychological interior rather than a monument.

**Ideological capture of the "great man": Napoleon as Rorschach test.** Napoleon biographies — royalist, liberal, romantic, Bonapartist — "made many Napoleons," each shaped more by the biographer's politics than by evidence; Pierre Lanfrey's monumental, hostile *Histoire de Napoléon* is Gay's central case of character-analysis turned into an instrument of political judgment.

**The limits of Victorian psychology: childhood unexamined.** Even biographers alert to the romantic doctrine that early years shape adult character — Sainte-Beuve, Carlyle himself — rarely did more than assert the connection. Working with only a "homemade commonsensical psychology," Victorian Lives habitually rushed through childhood "with a virtually audible sigh of relief," a limitation the volume's larger Freud-informed argument implicitly measures itself against.

## Notable passages

Carlyle's own formulation anchors the unit: "Universal History, the history of what man has accomplished in this world, is at bottom the History of the Great Men who have worked here," restated as "The History of the World is but the Biography of great men." This is the purest possible statement of individual-agency historiography, and it sits in instructive tension with Venkatesh Rao's [[history-machine]] concept, which holds that grand historical paradigms assemble silently and distributedly out of uncoordinated components rather than through heroic individual authorship — Carlyle's thesis is not merely different from but the direct historiographical antithesis of that idea, even though both are attempts to explain how large-scale historical change actually happens.

Carlyle's own anxiety — that "Hero-worship... professes to have gone out, and finally ceased" and that "the genius of the world lies in wait for heroisms... to pervert them or extinguish them" — reveals hero-worship as compensatory rather than descriptive: a lament for a present felt to be diminished relative to a heroic past.

The didactic strand crystallizes in the claim that character, unlike intellect, "could be learned" through emulation of an exemplary life. This treats the printed biography as a device for rendering an admired character legible and reproducible — transmissible from subject to reader as a stable template for self-fashioning. That is a different operation from the state-facing sense of [[Legibility]] as making populations readable for control, but it shares the same underlying move of compressing a complex, particular interior into a standardized, portable form; it also runs the mechanism of [[self-construction-through-writing]] in reverse and at one remove — not the biographer's own self built through the act of writing, but the reader's character built through consuming someone else's curated life.

Mahan's psychologizing of Nelson — interested "in the spirit of Carlyle" in Nelson's "inner history" and "underlying native character," and willing to treat his affair with Lady Hamilton as material for understanding rather than scandal to bury — marks the pivot toward the volume's larger "naked heart" project: the bourgeois self, even when it is someone else's self under a biographer's pen, becoming an object worth reading rather than merely admiring.

One of the biographical subjects handled at length is [[friedrich-schiller]], quoted through two hagiographic biographers (an English Life and Heinrich Düntzer's 1881 German Life) who treat his early death as martyrdom and his character as "virtually celestial" — a vivid instance of the panegyric mode the unit otherwise traces being slowly displaced.
