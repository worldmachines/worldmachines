# The Biographic Appetite: Reading for a Usable Past

Opening Chapter Three of *The Naked Heart*, Gay argues that the Victorian middle classes read biography and history not disinterestedly but in search of a **usable past** — a past that would flatter, orient, and enlarge their self-image — and that this appetite grew, by the 1870s and 1880s, into what Carlyle called the "Biographic appetite," an industrial market of series, biographical dictionaries, and eventually a critical, debunking counter-current that turned the bourgeoisie's self-scrutiny back on itself.

## Themes

**The usable past as a contested marketplace, not a consensus.** Gay frames the chapter's problem at once: educated bourgeois "indulged in a good deal of idealization" and pressured biographers and historians toward "powerful religious or political agendas," yet "there was never unanimity about the definition of what was usable in the past." The past is not simply received; it is fought over — "the clashing claims of pride and truth were fought out in the marketplace of ideas—or, more mundanely put, the marketplace of publishing."

**Professional "discipline" as displaced bourgeois self-control.** Gay reads the historians' own vocabulary symptomatically: Ranke-style source criticism, condemning even Hume, Voltaire, Robertson, and Gibbon for judging the past by their own "limited, self-satisfied perspective," is described by its practitioners as a "discipline" — a word that "suggestively hints at a commitment to self-control, that prized nineteenth-century bourgeois trait." Gay pushes this further into his Freud-informed register: the disciplined historian, in "anticipating the stance of the cultural psychoanalyst digging into times gone by," aims to surface repressed truth — "the sins of the fathers together with their virtues" — rather than collude in a "conspiracy of silence."

**An industrial, legibility-seeking infrastructure of exemplary lives.** The bulk of the section catalogs how thoroughly biography was systematized: cheap series (Morley's English Men of Letters, Twelve English Statesmen, the American Statesmen series, Les Grands Écrivains Français) manufactured trust through named, credentialed authors, while national biographical dictionaries — the Michauds' fifty-two-volume *Biographie universelle*, the German *Allgemeine deutsche Biographie* (45 volumes, 1,418 contributors, 23,273 lives), and Leslie Stephen and Sidney Lee's *Dictionary of National Biography* (63 volumes, 653 contributors, 29,120 lives) — aimed at comprehensive, standardized coverage of "great men—and some great women." This is a bourgeois legibility project in miniature: rendering an era's worthy lives archivable, comparable, and retrievable at national scale, "making the past ever more present." It resonates with [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and, more broadly, with the [[modernity-machine]]'s drive to make selves and their pasts readable and orderable — though here the unit doing the rendering is the individual exemplary life rather than the population or the state.

**From panegyric to disenchanted biography.** Gay traces a late-century turn against "mindless celebration": Ibsen, Shaw, and Zola's "realistic" unmasking, Ludwig Quidde's 1894 satire likening Wilhelm II to Caligula (which "ruined his career"), and American muckrakers Ida Tarbell and Lincoln Steffens made biography "an assault weapon on the recent past," a genre "deeply appreciated by progressive bourgeois even though—or perhaps because—the bourgeois deadly virtues were the principal target." The appetite for a usable past becomes, at its edges, an appetite for an indicting one.

**Reticence and disclosure on the same clock.** The section closes by dating a peak of squeamishness — a "virtual conspiracy of silence" — to around 1848–1857 (the Flaubert and Baudelaire obscenity trials, Dickens quietly deleting his own description of Nancy as a prostitute between the 1841 and 1867 editions of *Oliver Twist*), which recedes without disappearing by the 1880s. This is Gay's characteristic tension between the need to reveal and the need to conceal playing out in the biographical marketplace itself, not just in private diaries or confessional writing.

## Notable passages

The opening statement of the chapter's problem (the usable past as a fought-over, non-consensual construction) anchors the whole unit; the "discipline" paragraph supplies Gay's psychoanalytic reading of professional historical method; the Coleridge/Whibley/Carlyle paragraph supplies the section's title-phrase and periodizes the appetite from "age of personality" to "modern madness for biography"; the Dictionary of National Biography paragraph is the clearest instance of biography as legibility infrastructure; the Ibsen-Zola-Quidde-muckrakers paragraph marks the turn to disenchanted biography; and the Flaubert/Baudelaire/Dickens paragraph dates and then relativizes mid-Victorian propriety's peak.
