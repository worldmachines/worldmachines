# The Strains of Complexity

Gay surveys the major novelists of the naked-heart era—Hawthorne, Tolstoy, George Eliot, Dostoevsky, and Henry James—to argue that what set them apart from popular favorites was not technique but depth: they "dug deeper and (almost without exception) earned less." The unit tracks this price across a literary market that rewarded surface sensation and punished psychological candor, then gives extended case studies of Dostoevsky and James as the two most divergent routes to the same destination: the exposure of the mind's hidden, often shameful interior.

## Themes

**The market punished depth.** Hawthorne's 1855 complaint that a "d——d mob of scribbling women" outsold his searching fiction "by the 100,000" frames the whole unit: Tolstoy, Eliot, and James all commanded respect among the cultivated few while trailing Sue, Stowe, and Dumas by orders of magnitude in sales. This is a mid-Victorian instance of a split that [[carey-intellectuals-and-masses]] documents more fully one generation later among the modernists proper—an elite literary culture defining itself against, and losing the numbers game to, a newly enlarged reading public. Gay's version is milder than Carey's (no eliminationist rhetoric here, just wounded pride and real financial anxiety), but the underlying mechanism—serious writers as a routed incumbent—is the same, and [[the-mass-as-fiction]]'s point about "the mass" as a rhetorical rather than sociological category is already visible in Hawthorne's contemptuous, undifferentiated "mob."

**Revelation versus the veil.** Eliot's program—"There is a great deal of unmapped country within us"; "invisible histories must be made visible"—is a explicit charter for making the self legible through fiction, exactly the tension Gay's volume is built around. The Saturday Review's demand that novelists "throw a veil" over Maggie Tulliver's desire in The Mill on the Floss is the opposing bourgeois reflex: respectability as a demand for illegibility, privacy as reserve rather than repression. This maps cleanly onto [[legibility-as-machine-core]]'s reveal/conceal axis, though here the object being rendered legible is a fictional character's interior rather than the author's own—an adjacent, not identical, instrument to the diary or letter.

**Dostoevsky's ambivalence as realism.** Gay reads Dostoevsky's psychological authority as resting on characters who are shaken by contradictory wills at once—Stavrogin's wish to do both good and evil "and also take pleasure in that," Raskolnikov's inability to see his own crime as a crime, Smerdyakov's exculpatory suicide note. Dostoevsky's own biography (his father's suspicious death, the mock execution, Siberian exile, epilepsy, gambling) is presented as the raw material of this vision, and Freud, via Gay, is invoked directly: neurotics "teach valuable lessons" about ordinary mental life, and Dostoevsky's characters are effectively controlled case studies in ambivalence.

**The double as clue to the method.** The unit singles out *The Double* and its bureaucrat-hero Golyadkin—split into a servile and a persecuting self—as Dostoevsky's own key to a career-long preoccupation, from Golyadkin's midnight double to Ivan Karamazov's devil. Gay treats this recurring split-self figure as the clearest evidence of what the "realist in a higher sense" was actually doing: dramatizing a divided interior rather than inventing outlandish monsters.

**James's difficulty as a market failure and a strategy.** James inverts Dostoevsky's method—psychology through "cultivated encounters" and analysis rather than catastrophe—but pays an even steeper commercial price. His editor's own verdict on *The Ambassadors*, "too subtly fine for general appreciation... fold within fold of a complex mental web," reads as a diagnosis of deliberately cultivated difficulty rather than accidental obscurity. This is a strong instance of [[illegibility-as-elite-defense]]: James is not failing to communicate so much as refusing to simplify, and the unit is explicit that his contemporaries "mistook subtlety for frigidity"—illegibility read as a class and temperamental marker as much as an aesthetic one. The same difficulty lets him smuggle sexuality (Kate Croy and Densher's consummation in *The Wings of the Dove*) past Anglo-Saxon reticence by never opening the bedroom door.

## Notable passages

Hawthorne's letter to Ticknor is the unit's opening provocation, setting popular sales against literary seriousness in the starkest possible terms. Eliot's paired maxims from *Daniel Deronda*—"unmapped country," "invisible history"—function as a mission statement for the entire naked-heart project, immediately qualified by the Saturday Review's veil, which shows the public actively resisting the very mapping Eliot proposes. The Stavrogin/Raskolnikov/Smerdyakov passage is Gay's clearest statement of what makes Dostoevsky's realism convincing: not extremity but ambivalence sustained to the end. The Double passage explains why Gay treats a novella Dostoevsky himself considered a failure as psychologically indispensable—it names the split he never stopped writing about. Alden's rejection letter for The Ambassadors is the unit's sharpest evidence that James's late difficulty was recognized, and penalized, as such by the market in real time.
