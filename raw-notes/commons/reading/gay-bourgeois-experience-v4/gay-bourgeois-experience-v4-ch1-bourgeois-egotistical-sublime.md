# The Bourgeois Egotistical Sublime

The romantics scorned the bourgeoisie and prized their own status as an embattled elite writing for what Stendhal called "the happy few," yet it was romantic love, of all their legacies, that carried their cult of the inner life outward into Victorian middle-class culture and taught the bourgeoisie to prize and perform the same intense self-scrutiny -- the "egotistical sublime" -- the romantics had first cultivated for themselves.

## Themes

**An unintended inheritance.** Gay frames the whole unit around what he calls "a very banquet of unintended consequences splendidly exemplifying Hegel's cunning of history": writers like Hoffmann and Stendhal who despised or ignored bourgeois taste, and poets like Shelley and Hugo who imagined themselves persecuted, unacknowledged "legislators to the world," ended up as the direct source of the Victorian middle classes' emotional vocabulary. The romantics' self-image as embattled outsiders warring on philistinism obscured how thoroughly they would come to shape the very class they claimed to despise.

**Theories of love as ideology.** The unit surveys competing romantic theories of love as forms of applied metaphysics. Stendhal's *De l'amour* treats love as "crystallization," a self-deceiving mental operation the lover performs on the beloved -- desire reshaping reality to serve its own needs. Shelley's theory is frankly anarchist: love "withers under constraint," and marriage is an "odious" "usurpation" that makes "hypocrites or open enemies" of everyone bound by it. This is a claim of self-authored value set directly against institutional constraint, which rhymes in miniature with the migration [[romanticism-and-political-danger]] traces from romantic self-creation into collective nationalist politics -- though here the claim never leaves the bedroom for the ballot box; it stays a private ethic of love, not a political program. [[Friedrich Schlegel]] and Schleiermacher instead propose a near-theological synthesis of affection and passion, worked out in Schlegel's scandalous roman à clef *Lucinde* -- a thinly disguised account of his own affair with Dorothea Veit that his mother denounced as proof he had lost "religion" and "good principles," and that nonetheless, Gay argues, helped define the romantic conception of love for the Victorians who came after. [[Novalis]] and [[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]] appear as further instances of romantics whose own erotic lives -- a dead fiancee mourned alongside a live one being courted, a string of infatuations lasting into Goethe's seventies -- were far less "bourgeois" than the ideology they helped bequeath to the bourgeoisie.

**Domestication.** The bourgeoisie could not accept romantic love exactly as its theorists intended it -- anti-institutional, often adulterous, contemptuous of the "despotism" of law. Middle-class culture absorbed the feeling while discarding the doctrine: marriage became, in dramatist Friedrich Hebbel's phrase, what "makes a human being wholly human," love's highest incarnation rather than (as Shelley had it) love's prison. Gay ties this domestication to the era's new communications infrastructure -- cheaper printing, lending libraries, family magazines like *Godey's Lady's Book* and the *Gartenlaube* -- which carried a "debased" but happy-ending romanticism from Stendhal's "happy few" out to a genuinely mass readership. Reception was uneven: French and German-Jewish families kept arranged marriage far longer than English or American families, where courtship, though still bounded by prudence about income and respectability, gave individual affection real weight.

**Byron as the exception that proves the domestication.** Byron is the unit's test case: rumored to have committed incest and sodomy and to have conducted "homosexual flings," publicly disgraced amid separation scandal, his memoirs burned by his own friends as unfit to survive him -- and yet his poetry sold better because of the gossip, and readers who had never met him "responded as though a beloved friend had died." Bourgeois readers officially condemned him while structurally needing him: his transgressions let them experience romantic extremity vicariously, at no cost to their own respectability.

**The transparency demand.** The unit's psychological payoff is its account of what romantic love actually asks of lovers: not just feeling, but total mutual legibility. "Lovers must know everything"; the question "Do you love me?" is repeated endlessly because, per Benjamin Constant's Adolphe, the most categorical affirmation soon loses its effectiveness -- the demand for candor is really a demand for reassurance, "a narcissism for two." This insistence that the beloved (and the self) be rendered fully readable to an intimate other is a private-sphere, emotionally charged cousin of [[legibility-as-machine-core]]'s institutional project of rendering the world measurable and orderable -- though the mechanism here is confession and mutual disclosure rather than ledger or census, and the aim is reassurance rather than control. It connects more directly to [[self-construction-through-writing]]: Constant's Adolphe wants his beloved "to read me" -- the self here is verified and in part constituted by the act of being read by another, not simply felt in private.

**Secularized religion.** Gay closes by noting that this probing self-disclosure "looks suspiciously like a worldly version of the anguished self-examinations that believers had for centuries undertaken," but insists romantic love was "not simply a secular religion" -- it borrowed sacred vocabulary ("divine," "angel," "enchantment") for erotic ends without simply replacing devotion with desire; most Victorian couples, he notes dryly, wanted to sleep together *and* attend church together. His closing description of "the romantic and post-romantic campaign to re-enchant the world" as "most active in the sphere of love" resonates with [[enchantment-as-managed-fiction]]'s account of enchantment as a fiction that someone manages and someone else pays for -- though here the management is diffuse and mutual (lovers enchanting each other) rather than staged by one party at another's expense.

## Notable passages

The opening passage (c1) names the chapter's irony directly, framing everything that follows as an instance of unintended consequences. Stendhal's crystallization passage (c2) is the clearest statement of love-as-ideology in the unit, a nominally "scientific" account of self-deception. Shelley's anti-marriage tirade (c3) is the most radical position surveyed -- a rejection of "positive institutions" as such -- and the one bourgeois culture had to reject hardest even as it kept the underlying sentiment. The Lucinde passage (c4) shows why Schlegel's scandalous novel mattered beyond gossip: it fused philosophy and eroticism into a template that would "reverberate so powerfully in the inner life of the Victorians." The "narcissism for two" passage (c5) is the unit's psychological center, showing the demand for total transparency between lovers restated compulsively because reassurance, unlike information, never stays proven. The re-enchantment passage (c6) is Gay's most explicit naming of romantic love as religion's secular successor -- borrowing its vocabulary without simply replacing it.
