# The Art of Listening: From Sociable Noise to Sacred Silence

Gay opens this unit with William Sidney Mount's 1847 painting *The Power of Music* — a fiddler entertaining two white listeners in a barn while a black laborer, barred by the half-closed door, drinks in the sound from outside — and reads its "intent silence" as an emblem for the unit's real subject: the slow, effortful nineteenth-century invention of listening as an inward, quasi-religious act. The chapter narrates how secular music travelled from an accompaniment to gossip, flirtation, and card-playing into the era's most privileged vehicle for self-absorption, and how that transformation had to be taught, over and over, against habits that never fully yielded.

## Themes

**Music's promotion was not automatic; it was manufactured by converging social forces.** Gay traces a long run-up — the refinement of manners since the late Renaissance, the eighteenth-century "storm of sensibility" touched off by Samuel Richardson's *Pamela* and then by [[jean-jacques-rousseau]]'s *La Nouvelle Héloïse* and Henry Mackenzie's *The Man of Feeling*, the German *Affektenlehre* doctrine that music's business is to stir feeling, and a genuine consumer boom in clavichords and fortepianos after 1815 — that together made music available as the century's designated organ of inward feeling, eclipsing even poetry in some estimates.

**For most of the eighteenth century, listening was a social, not a private, act.** Gay assembles a rich catalogue of counter-evidence: Prince Lichnowsky's private concerts aside, most performances — Mozart's suppers, La Morlière's sneering aristocrat who calls attentive listening "bourgeois," the noisy opera houses with their liveried wine-sellers and balcony prostitutes, Stendhal's Roman audiences using "the opera-house as a kind of club" — treated music as a backdrop to sociability, flirtation, and even seduction. Silence, when it happened, was noteworthy enough that Mozart wrote home about it.

**The nineteenth century's silent, reverent listener had to be pedagogically manufactured, and the campaign never fully succeeded.** [[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]], presiding over the Weimar court theater, issued blunt written edicts against "irregularities" in 1803; Frankfurt's Museum association wrote decorum into its founding statutes in 1808 ("Dogs are not tolerated"); American orchestras like the New York Philharmonic scolded their own audiences in annual reports well into the 1890s. The repetitiveness of these interventions — the same complaints recurring decade after decade, across Vienna, Amsterdam, Chicago, and New York — is itself Gay's evidence that the ideal of silent listening was aspirational rather than achieved, an unending project of self-civilizing rather than a settled custom.

**The romantic critics of bourgeois philistinism were, ironically, its most effective educators.** E. T. A. Hoffmann's savage satires of chattering, card-playing concertgoers, and his fictional alter ego Kapellmeister Kreisler (the "mad musician who is saner than all comfortable Bürger put together"), attacked the bourgeoisie even as they instructed it in the very listening manners it lacked. Gay notes the deeper irony that Kreisler became "a favorite icon for the Victorian avant-garde, who fancied themselves at war with the bourgeoisie" — the romantic revolt against bourgeois culture was recruited into shaping that culture's own norms.

**Gay's Freud-inflected close reads silent listening as sublimation of bodily impulse — the chapter's clearest statement of the volume's method.** The unit's final movement argues that undivided silent attention "does violence to basic human impulses": the urge to hum, sway, tap, or explain one's response aloud is a "primitive physical appetite" that music "virtually enforces" by reaching "the deepest taproots of human experience," evoking a mother's lullaby or a father's rocking. Cultivated, silent listening is thus not a natural response to music but a disciplined *renunciation* of the body's own mimetic urge — exactly the tension between revelation and concealment, expression and control, that structures Gay's account of the bourgeois self throughout *The Naked Heart*.

## Notable passages

The opening reading of *The Power of Music* sets the book's real subject — "the ascent of inwardness in the Victorian age" — behind the genre-painting's comic surface. The passage on the 1760s–70s "storm of sensibility" locates music's promotion to supreme emotional art within a broader cult of sentiment. The paragraph on eighteenth-century sociability (Lichnowsky's exception aside) documents how thoroughly pre-Victorian listening was public and inattentive. Hoffmann's polemic captures the romantic in the paradoxical role of civilizing preceptor. Goethe's 1803 edict is the unit's clearest instance of a named historical actor legislating the new listening decorum into institutional existence. The closing passage on music's assault on "basic human impulses" is the unit's thesis statement, reading the trained silent listener as an achievement of sublimation.

This unit's discipline of interiority — training publics to convert an expressive, participatory impulse into silent, private absorption — is a small-scale instance of the [[modernity-machine]]'s production of the individuated, self-narrating subject: the concert hall becomes an institution that manufactures inward selves through enforced comportment, much as Gay elsewhere describes diaries and letters doing so through writing. It resonates, more loosely, with [[self-construction-through-writing]]: here the self is not written into being but *listened* into being, disciplined into interiority through a sensory practice rather than a textual one — the same core claim (the self is constructed, not given) applied to the ear instead of the pen.
