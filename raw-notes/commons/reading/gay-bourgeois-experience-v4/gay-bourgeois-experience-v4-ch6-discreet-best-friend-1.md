# The Diary as a Discreet Best Friend

Gay surveys nineteenth-century, mostly bourgeois, diary-keeping as an intimate address to a personified confidant rather than a neutral record: he traces the practice's Romantic-era origins, its commercial infrastructure in firms like Letts & Co., and the wildly various purposes — confession, ambition, therapy, loneliness, self-discipline, shared intimacy between lovers — that diarists made it serve, closing on examples (Emily Shore, Beatrice Webb) that show a diary's purpose, and the self it registers, shifting as the writer matures.

## Themes

**The diary as a fictive interlocutor, not a mere record.** Gay opens by distinguishing the Victorian habit from earlier diarists — Pepys, Evelyn, Boswell — who gave their feelings free rein "without inventing an independent personage for the purpose." Nineteenth-century diarists, by contrast, "lent their diaries human qualities," apostrophizing them, apologizing to them, bidding them farewell at the end of a volume. Maurice de Guérin's cahier is Gay's most vivid instance: a notebook addressed as a being with "a soul, intelligence, love, goodness, compassion, patience, charity." Gay attributes the shift to the late-eighteenth-century cult of emotional expressiveness and the Romantics' "impassioned involvement with self," the same inheritance that had already remade the letter into a dialogue with an imagined other.

**Privacy as the enabling condition of confession — and a provisional one.** The diary could function as confession, Gay argues, only because the writer trusted it would stay secret, "locked in the drawer of a desk or ingeniously concealed." That assumed inviolability, though, sat in constant tension with a wish to be read eventually: politicians and generals wrote with one eye on biographers, and Friedrich Hebbel's journal opens with a flamboyant address to his "future biographer" that Gay reads, in properly Freud-informed fashion, as "protesting too much" — Hebbel disclaims the wish for fame in the same breath he indulges it. Marie Bashkirtseff and Jules Renard make the same wish explicit, hoping their diaries would be published and would make them "famous." The confessional mode has a Renaissance ancestor in [[michel-de-montaigne]]'s frank self-examination, though the resonance is only partial: Montaigne wrote for print from the outset, while Victorian diarists insisted — not always truthfully — that they wrote for no eye but their own. The tension Gay describes, between a self made legible only to itself and a self secretly rehearsing its future legibility to others, echoes the WMP distinction between privacy as an illegible reserve and [[legibility-as-machine-core]] — though what is at stake here is a single self's self-presentation to posterity, not a state's project of readability. The diary's physical hiding place is also the literal domestic architecture of secrecy that [[intimate-place-bachelard]] treats phenomenologically: the desk drawer as a container that acquires symbolic depth from what is entrusted to it.

**An industrialized institution of self-knowledge.** What set the nineteenth century apart, Gay insists, was quantity, not novel intent: the devout, social climbers, and travelers had long kept records of a similar kind. But John Letts's bound diary volumes, launched in 1812 and expanded by his son Thomas into some twenty-eight varieties by 1839, turned private self-examination into a standardized, mass-manufactured commodity reaching customers "in the hundreds of thousands." Thackeray's 1862 tribute to the firm shows the diary-keeping habit fully absorbed into commercial and moral life. This is the diary as manufactured infrastructure for self-knowledge at bourgeois scale, alongside the letter, the biography, and the historical profession that Gay treats elsewhere in the volume.

**Diaries fashion, not just record, a self still becoming.** Emily Shore's diary began, in its first entry, as a flat catalog — "Our family consists of papa, mamma, and five children" — with none of the religious and mortal preoccupation that would dominate later pages; only in July 1838, the year before her death, did it "alter its character" and become, in her words, "a valuable index of my mind," confessing feelings she "would on no account submit to any human eye." Gay uses her case explicitly to refute "the conventional wisdom that the opening statements of a diary set its tone." This is [[self-construction-through-writing]] in its clearest form in the unit: the diary is not a container the self fills but an instrument that reshapes the self writing into it as circumstance and maturity act on the writer.

**A catalog of motives that resists any single essence.** Gay refuses to reduce diary-keeping to one function. It could be therapy prescribed by a doctor (Dreiser), a discipline against boredom or a hedge against forgetting, ammunition gathered for future self-exculpation, relief from "hateful solitude" (Alice James), narcissistic pleasure taken in rereading one's own past (Waiblinger, Macaulay, Wilde's Gwendolen Fairfax, who "never travel[s] without my diary"), a zone of shared intimacy exchanged between lovers or spouses (Wagner and Wesendonck, the Tolstoys), or, in Amiel's extreme case across 16,840 pages, a near-total substitute for a life the diarist felt unable to live directly.

## Notable passages

The opening paragraph (c1) states Gay's central distinction: Victorian diarists, unlike Pepys or Boswell, invented an independent personage to receive their confidences. De Guérin's 1834 apostrophe to his "cahier" (c2) is the era's most extravagant literalization of that personification. The paragraph on privacy (c3) shows the confessional mode's dependence on assumed secrecy running up against Hebbel's undisguised hope for a biographer's eventual readership. The Letts & Co. material (c4) documents the commercial machinery that scaled a private habit into "a recognizable ingredient in the bourgeois experience." Emily Shore's own account of her diary changing character (c5) is Gay's sharpest evidence that the diary constructs rather than merely records its writer. Amiel's list of what his journal was to him (c6) — "my dialogue, my society, my companion, my confidant" — gives the chapter's title its most complete gloss.
