# The Historian's Double Bind: Objectivity, Partisanship, and the Nationalist Classroom

This unit is section 6, "Agendas High and Low," of Gay's chapter on nineteenth-century historical writing—his account of "biography and history-writing" as one of the bourgeoisie's instruments of self-knowledge, refracted here through the discipline of Clio rather than the private diary. Gay first surveys why Victorians and their continental counterparts read history at all (moral formation, national flattery, narrative pleasure), then documents the profession's persistent claim to scientific "identification" against the pervasive reality of political partisanship—French, Prussian, English, American—before turning to the school history curriculum as the arena where that partisanship hardened into explicit civic pedagogy, closing on the August 1914 mobilization as proof of what was at stake.

## Themes

**History as flattering mirror, not neutral record.** Gay opens by cataloguing the non-epistemic uses that made history-reading a bourgeois habit: school moralizing, "gratifying feelings of superiority over lesser nations," proof of national progress, and sheer literary pleasure (Motley's tear-jerking close to *The Rise of the Dutch Republic*, Macaulay's invidious comparisons). He generalizes this into a structural claim via Burckhardt and Frederick Jackson Turner: each age tends to find in the past what flatters its own self-image, so that historians risk becoming "flattering mirrors of their time rather than unclouded telescopes trained on bygone days."

**The unstable ideal of scientific objectivity.** Augustin Thierry, Guizot, Mignet, and Heinrich von Sybel all insisted their overt political engagement never compromised their "science"; Ranke worried privately that the "running current" of present concerns "tries to dominate the past and force it into its course." Lord Acton's image of the "cloven hoof of party preference" names the gap between this professed disinterestedness and its practice. The aspiration itself—documentary rigor, professional journals (the *Historische Zeitschrift*, the *Revue historique*), disciplinary congresses debating whether history should "systematically support a certain point of view"—reads as a small-scale instance of the [[modernity-machine]]'s drive toward [[Legibility]]: making the past orderly, verifiable, citable. But the unit's whole argument is that this same apparatus was repeatedly annexed to nation-building loyalty rather than neutral administration, a tension Gay surfaces without resolving.

**Partisanship as a productive lens, when disciplined.** Against simple debunking, Gay lets Acton argue that committed historians "broken through to new perceptions with their gladiatorial posture"—"no man feels the grandeur of the Revolution till he reads Michelet, or the horror of it without reading Taine." Eduard Bernstein's discovery of the Diggers is the concrete case: his Marxist commitments functioned as a search heuristic that made a genuinely overlooked seventeenth-century group visible where nonpartisan professionals like Firth and Gardiner had not found it. The danger, Gay insists, is real ("tunnel vision"), but the corrective is self-discipline, not the impossible elimination of standpoint.

**The classroom as the site where historiography becomes nation-building.** Gay contrasts Dr. Arnold's Rugby—classical, history-as-character-training, explicitly hostile to "exclusive patriotism" as a form of "selfishness"—with the German Gymnasium under pressure from Wilhelm II ("We ought to educate national young Germans and not young Greeks and Romans") and the French lycée after 1871, where Duruy and Lavisse turned history teaching into deliberate civic-military formation (a pupil who does not become "a citizen imbued with his duties and a soldier who loves his rifle" represents wasted teaching). Oskar Jäger's dissenting insistence on "complete objectivity" and "only what really happened" is the chapter's clearest statement of the humanist "high" agenda against the nationalist "low" one named in its title. This is a documentary case of what [[imagined-communities]] describes: [[benedict-anderson]] emphasizes print capitalism and simultaneity as the nation's binding mechanism, where Gay's evidence is specifically curricular—the schoolroom rather than the newspaper—but the underlying claim, that national belonging must be actively manufactured through shared historical narrative, is the same.

**The stakes made visible in 1914.** The unit closes by cashing out the argument: the simplified, patriotic history absorbed in school and in the press, not the "demanding Rankean ideal," is what actually primed Europeans for the enthusiasm of August 1914—professors returning honorary degrees, cosmopolitan men of letters rediscovering compatriot solidarity overnight. Gay reads this as evidence that in crisis, "regressive, primitive emotions" long prepared by popular historical literature overwhelmed the profession's stated commitment to dispassionate truth.

## Notable passages

The opening catalogue of reading motives (c1) sets up the chapter's skeptical frame before any historian is named. Acton's paradox (c2) and Bernstein's case (c3) together carry the chapter's most counterintuitive claim—that partisanship, properly disciplined, can be a means of discovery rather than only distortion. The Burckhardt/Turner passage (c4) states starkly the risk on the other side of that same coin: unexamined self-flattery rather than deliberate advocacy. The Jäger–Wilhelm II passage (c5) stages the title's "high" and "low" agendas as a live institutional fight inside German secondary education. The closing August 1914 passage (c6) is the chapter's payoff, converting historiographical argument into historical consequence.
