# Dickens: Sentimental Showman, Unconscious Psychologist

Gay devotes this chapter to Charles Dickens as the representative novelist of the bourgeois century: a writer whose death in 1870 produced a national outpouring of grief, whose readership spanned peers and clerks, and whose core, most loyal audience was nonetheless the "proper middle classes" (Mrs. Oliphant's 1855 verdict that he was "a class writer, the historian and representative of one circle"). The chapter's real argument is psychological rather than biographical: against critics from Henry James ("the greatest of superficial novelists") to Trollope, Bagehot, and Taine, who denied Dickens depth, taste, or credible interiority, Gay builds a case—anchored in a 1897 Freud letter on Oedipus Rex—that Dickens's fiction, above all David Copperfield, encodes universal unconscious material: oedipal triangles, the family romance, ambivalence, and children's guilt for their parents' failings. The chapter closes by placing the novel itself inside the volume's central drama of concealment and disclosure, the bourgeois negotiation between the need to reveal and the need to conceal.

## Themes

**Dickens as the bourgeois novelist.** Gay stresses Dickens's sociological centrality before his psychology: an enormous cast ranging from slum to mansion, sales in the hundreds of thousands, and a readership whose emotional identification with his characters (Pickwick, David Copperfield, Little Nell) was itself a fact of Victorian bourgeois experience. Mrs. Oliphant's assessment that his sympathies for the poor notwithstanding, Dickens was fundamentally the bourgeoisie's own novelist, frames everything that follows: his psychological insight matters to Gay because it is *bourgeois* self-understanding refracted through a mass medium.

**The turn against sentimentality.** The death of Little Nell in The Old Curiosity Shop is Gay's test case for a shift in middle-class sensibility across the century: contemporaries (Lord Jeffrey, Forster) wept and wrote to Dickens in anguish, but by the 1860s–70s G. H. Lewes, William Dean Howells, and finally Oscar Wilde's line about a "heart of stone" register the same scene as embarrassing kitsch. Trollope's posthumous *Autobiography* delivers the harshest verdict, denying that any of Dickens's characters are "human" even while conceding his unmatched popularity. Gay uses this generational reversal to isolate the specific charge against Dickens—"maudlin," "unnatural," prissy about sex, incapable of a convincing love story—that his Freudian counter-reading has to answer.

**Freud's warrant for reading Dickens seriously.** Gay's pivot is Freud's October 1897 letter on the durable "gripping power" of *Oedipus Rex*: great fiction endures not through wit or plot alone but by merging literary felicities with universal, half-repressed inner experience. On this test, Dickens qualifies despite—Gay argues, partly *because of*—his lack of systematic thought: an untutored but acute intuition let him dramatize the oedipal triangle, the family romance (orphans and foundlings hunting for the truth of their birth: Oliver Twist, Esther Summerson, Estella), unreliable first-person narrators whose self-justifications betray more than they intend ("The History of a Self-Tormentor," "George Silverman's Explanation"), and children's tendency to internalize a parent's guilt as their own.

**David Copperfield as autobiography transmuted into oedipal bildungsroman.** Gay reads David's plot—the erotic monopoly of early widowed-mother happiness, the sadistic interloper Murdstone, the regressive marriage to child-wife Dora before the "proper" marriage to sisterly-then-erotic Agnes, the conveniently pruned or oedipally "available" households of Betsey Trotwood and the Wickfields—as a sustained working-through of oedipal material. He roots this in biography: Dickens set aside an autobiographical memoir once David's story claimed him, "as though fiction could serve as a substitute for his confession," transplanting the blacking-warehouse shame of his own boyhood into the novel word for word while explicitly and repeatedly insisting David Copperfield was not Charles Dickens.

**Agnes Wickfield as a Freudian case within the novel.** Gay's most granular close reading targets a secondary figure: Agnes's excessive goodness is diagnosed as a defense against guilt planted by her father's "diseased love"—his grief-stricken conviction, absorbed by Agnes as a child, that her birth caused her mother's death. Her serene self-sacrifice, correctly read, is less angelic character than symptom, and her repressed passivity toward David (a love she must not admit while cast as his "sister") exemplifies Gay's larger claim that Dickens's insight survives underneath, not despite, his most saccharine surface writing.

**The novel as arena for privacy and disclosure.** The chapter ends by generalizing: fiction had a "strategic role" in Victorian "battles over the proper range of the inward domain," licensed to trespass on the passions but obliged not to trespass "too far, or too indelicately." Dickens himself modeled the same bourgeois compromise offstage—guarding his failing marriage and the Ellen Ternan affair from public view even as his fiction let readers "see itself—and escape from itself." This is a productive but partial rhyme with [[Legibility]]: Gay's dynamic is the self choosing how much of itself to render legible to a *readership* rather than to a state or algorithm, but the same structure—legibility purchased at a cost, privacy defended as a reserve—is doing the work. It also resonates with [[self-construction-through-writing]]: Dickens's own self is not merely expressed but actively reworked through the act of turning confession into fiction, though here the self-construction is deliberately routed through a *disavowed* double (David is declared not-Dickens) rather than through direct autobiographical writing.

## Notable passages

The chapter opens by establishing Dickens's bourgeois centrality ("As the perceptive Mrs. Oliphant... observed in 1855"), then tracks the collapse of Victorian sentimental taste through the reception history of Little Nell's death, ending in Wilde's quip and Trollope's dismissal ("That epithet, 'unnatural,' cuts to the heart of the matter"). The hinge of Gay's argument is the 1897 Freud letter on Oedipus Rex, invoked to license reading Dickens as more than a crowd-pleaser. The autobiographical substrate of David Copperfield is laid bare in the blacking-warehouse passage ("Nowhere in David Copperfield do life and art intersect more poignantly"), and the Freudian reading is pressed hardest on Agnes Wickfield, whose "exemplary serenity" is unmasked as defense ("Ever since she was a little girl, she had mothered her widowed father"). The chapter's closing paragraph on fiction's "strategic role" in negotiating disclosure and privacy ("In the battles over the proper range of the inward domain") is the clearest single statement of the volume's larger thesis applied to the novel as a form.
