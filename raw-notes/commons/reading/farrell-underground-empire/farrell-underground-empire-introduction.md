# All Roads Lead to Rome

Farrell and Newman open by staging their central metaphor: beneath the visible monuments of American power (the White House, the Pentagon, Foggy Bottom) runs a hidden infrastructure of fiber-optic cables, financial messaging systems, and supply chains that functions like Rome's roads and aqueducts once did — built for commerce, but usable for coercion. The introduction narrates how a genuinely decentralized, business-built system of global networks, assembled after 1989 to escape Cold War-style power politics, nevertheless inherited the geography of American Cold War dominance, and how the September 11 attacks led U.S. agencies to discover and progressively weaponize that inherited centrality — first against terrorists, then rogue states, then allies, and finally China — culminating in a warning that an empire sustained by concealment cannot survive exposure.

## Themes

**Infrastructure is never just plumbing — it is a lever of political control.** The authors' governing claim is that "the plumbing is political": fiber-optic backbones, the SWIFT bank-messaging system, dollar clearing, and semiconductor design and manufacturing are not neutral technical substrate but "subterranean machineries" that a state sitting at their center can turn into "tools of coercion." This is a close cousin of [[legibility-as-machine-core]] — the [[modernity-machine]]'s drive to render the world readable and controllable at scale — except here the legibility is achieved not by state design but by capturing infrastructure that businesses built for entirely different, efficiency-seeking reasons.

**Empire by sleepwalking, not by plot.** Against Vladimir Putin's claim that the Internet was designed from the start as a "CIA project" to undermine adversaries, and against Thomas Friedman's mirror-image faith that a networked, "flat" world had abolished geopolitics altogether ("an emporium, not an imperium"), Farrell and Newman insist the truth is more mundane and more interesting: no one planned the underground empire. Private companies built the networks pursuing "efficiency and profit," and U.S. officials "believed their job was to get out of the way." Power accrued as an unintended by-product of infrastructure that happened to route through American territory — a pattern the book repeatedly calls not conspiracy but drift: "governments, too, can follow paths without anticipating where they lead." This silent, distributed accretion — followed by a sudden, visible "lurch" once the Trump administration and the U.S.-China confrontation made it impossible to ignore — is a close match for the [[history-machine]] pattern: a paradigm that assembles invisibly out of independent components (a Treasury subpoena here, an NSA program there, a Huawei ban elsewhere) until observers realize, all at once, that the pieces were always part of one system.

**Path dependence locked the new global economy onto old imperial rails.** Even though the post-Cold War networks were meant to transcend geopolitics, they were built the way medieval roads were built on Roman ones — "it is easier to build on top of" what already exists. The result: by 2002 less than 1 percent of global Internet bandwidth moved between two non-U.S. regions without transiting the United States; SWIFT's board was U.S.-bank-dominated and its data center sat in Northern Virginia; international dollar transactions ran through U.S.-controlled "dollar clearing." A supposedly novel, network-based world order thus "froze a moment in time" — the peak of American Cold War victory — and preserved it far longer than the underlying power balance justified. This is the flip side of [[divergence-machine]] logic: networks proliferated in the name of variety and decentralization, yet the routing geography quietly re-legible-ized around a single center.

**9/11 was the hinge that converted latent centrality into active coercion.** Before September 11, 2001, U.S. agencies possessed the structural leverage but used it, if at all, opportunistically. After the attacks, Treasury moved within weeks to demand SWIFT data and to weaponize dollar clearing, using both to cut Iran out of the global financial system; the NSA built systems that could "record every single phone call in an entire country." Crucially, each emergency measure became a template: "once-radical proposals became commonplace tools of policy," because "whenever a new use was discovered, it created a possible precedent for others." The metaphor shifts from roads to a spider: the U.S. could "detect the subtle percussions of what enemies and friends were saying" and "tightly wrap an adversary's economy in smothering strands that were stronger than steel."

**Visibility, not restraint, ends an underground empire.** Snowden's leaks, Jack Lew's warning that overuse could push transactions "outside of the United States entirely," Trump's undisciplined but escalatory extension of these tools against allies and against China's Huawei, and Macron's warning about becoming "somebody's vassal" together trace an arc from concealment to exposure. The authors' closing argument is explicitly normative and forward-looking: they favor a shift from unilateral "network imperialism" toward a negotiated "commonwealth" — echoing Cold War-era superpower dialogue — while warning that the empire "was able to retain its power so long because it was hidden in the shadows," and that now that it is exposed, "it will crumble, or worse." Allied and rival attempts to build "strategic autonomy" or rival infrastructure read as a form of [[capture-resistance]] against a system whose costs have become legible to its own supposed beneficiaries.

## Notable passages

The introduction opens on the extended Rome/roads conceit, moving from literal DC infrastructure (I-66, the NSA at Fort Meade, Foggy Bottom) to the claim that empires have always run on "trade routes, grain ships, and aqueducts" as much as on monuments, and that "history is lazy" — new infrastructure is built on old foundations. This sets up the book's recurring move of treating dull, technical systems as the true site of imperial power.

The two-false-narratives passage stages the book's argument by negation: Putin's claim that the Internet is a "CIA project" and Friedman's declaration that a "Wall" had given way to a "Web," reinforced by Clinton's "Jell-O" line and Carville's wish to be "reincarnated as the bond market," both get rejected as too simple — one sees deliberate plot everywhere, the other sees the end of power politics. The authors' alternative, developed across the rest of the book, is unplanned, path-dependent capture.

The NSA/SWIFT/dollar-clearing paragraph supplies the empirical backbone for the path-dependence claim, and the account of Treasury's post-9/11 pivot to SWIFT subpoenas and dollar-clearing sanctions against Iran shows the mechanism by which "once-off emergency measures" hardened into permanent policy infrastructure — the concrete process the rest of the book will trace in each domain (finance, surveillance, export controls, supply chains). The closing paragraph's warning that exposure dooms concealment-dependent power supplies the book's normative stakes and previews its argument for restraint over escalation.
