# Europe's Awakening into Winter

The chapter tells how the European Union's founding creed — that free trade and economic interdependence make war between partners unthinkable — collapsed in stages between the Trump administration's destruction of the Iran nuclear deal and Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine, forcing Brussels to discover, reluctantly and incompletely, that the open economic order it had trusted for security was itself a set of American-controlled chokepoints that could be turned against Europe as easily as against any adversary.

## Themes

**The founding fairy tale of "peace through trade."** The EU was built, the chapter argues, on the twin premises that market integration between France and Germany would make war unthinkable and that trade with Russia and China would gradually liberalize them (*Wandel durch Handel*, "transformation through trade"). Security was outsourced entirely to NATO and the American nuclear umbrella, leaving the Commission to police only "dull-seeming" technocratic matters like phytosanitary regulations. This is presented as a willed blindness: "All that European officials could see was the flatland of peaceful commercial exchange, stretching to infinity in two dimensions," while beneath it grew "the growth of America's underground empire," Russia's territorial ambitions, and Chinese authoritarianism — a passage that names the book's own governing metaphor and makes explicit that Europe's entire strategic vocabulary was unequipped to perceive coercive economic power as a security threat.

**Trump, not Russia, delivers the first shock.** Long before tanks crossed into Ukraine, the destruction of the JCPOA in 2018 is framed as the real rupture: the United States forced European firms to abandon billion-dollar Iranian contracts through secondary sanctions, revealed that EU "blocking legislation" was toothless, and pushed Iran off SWIFT over European objections. INSTEX, the barter mechanism Germany, France, and the UK built to route around the dollar, is depicted as a face-saving failure — a "laboratory" for "weird things" that completed one transaction and then did essentially nothing, because the EU was "unwilling to take the transformative steps" needed for a real alternative. Trump's open contempt for the EU ("a foe") and threats to leave NATO converted an assumed permanent alliance into a source of existential anxiety, and the chapter insists this — not Putin — is what first taught Brussels that dependency on American-run financial infrastructure was a vulnerability rather than a convenience.

**A contested vocabulary as displaced politics.** Buzzwords like "geostrategic Europe," "strategic autonomy," and von der Leyen's "geopolitical Commission" are treated not as empty jargon but as proxy battles over whether Europe should remain a purely commercial project or acquire coercive tools of its own — Macron's push for military and economic independence against Merkel's instinctive Atlanticism, and Phil Hogan's deliberately watered-down "open strategic autonomy," which subordinated security back to trade. The quiet 2019 European Council on Foreign Relations report commissioned jointly by the French and German foreign ministries — designed so the ministries "didn't want to have a direct footprint" — proposed the genuinely radical step of building EU tools to freeze American assets and search for "reverse dependencies" in other powers' economies, ideas that were shelved rather than adopted but that seeded everything that followed.

**Germany's gas addiction as a case study in sleepwalking.** Nord Stream 2 is the chapter's central illustration of how commercial convenience overrode strategic warning for decades: cross-party consensus (Christian Democrats and Social Democrats uniting even against the Greens after Navalny's poisoning) protected a pipeline explicitly contracted to block Commission oversight, while Ukraine's warnings were ignored until Ted Cruz and U.S. gas-state senators, not European leaders, found the pipeline's actual chokepoint (Allseas' pipe-laying ships) and used extraterritorial sanctions to freeze construction. The passage argues climate transition itself, by making Russia's long-term gas revenues expendable, "made it cheaper and easier for Russia to deploy gas as a weapon" — undercutting the very "repeated game" logic that had underwritten decades of peaceful supply.

**The 2022 response reveals both new capacity and enduring dependency.** Von der Leyen's March 2022 speech — sweeping SWIFT bans, the freeze of roughly $600 billion in Russian central bank reserves negotiated in seventy-two hours — is presented as a genuine, historic break from EU precedent, achieved partly because the U.S. deliberately let Europe take the lead and partly because Germany's willingness to sacrifice Nord Stream 2 removed the usual veto logic. But the chapter immediately undercuts the triumphalism: implementing sanctions exposed that the EU lacks its own OFAC, its own intelligence on ownership structures, and uniform national capacity to enforce anything, meaning "the more that the EU sought to build its own sources of power and authority, the more it realized that it needed what the United States had." The chapter closes on unresolved exposure to a second Trump term, to Chinese leverage over green-technology supply chains, and to winter itself, with no promise that Europe's new self-understanding has actually closed the gap it revealed.

## Notable passages

The chapter opens by staging von der Leyen's speech as the moment sleep ends — "For decades, the EU had slumbered and dreamed of a world without power politics. Now, it had woken" — before immediately complicating the triumph: "Waking up was one thing. Making economic war was quite another." The flatland passage crystallizes the thesis about willed blindness to underground power, naming the book's central image directly in EU terms. The line describing Europe's realization that it had been "transformed while it was asleep from an ally to a mere outlying province of a greater empire" and "press-ganged into service by a United States that was breaking bad" marks the emotional pivot from complacency to fear, predating the Ukraine invasion by years. The ECFR report passage shows the mechanism by which radical policy ideas enter circulation quietly, through commissioned think-tank cover, before later becoming mainstream Commission thinking — a textbook instance of a paradigm assembling in the dark, akin to a [[history-machine]] lurching toward visibility, and a concrete illustration of the [[gramsci-gap]] between a dying "peace through trade" legitimacy and a not-yet-built geopolitical one. Vestager's blunt retrospective — "we have had a hard awakening into the era of weaponized interdependence" and "we were just greedy" — is the chapter's own gloss on its argument, admitting that Europe's exposure was self-inflicted rather than merely imposed. The closing fairy-tale inversion, in which Europe "had finally fully awoken, cold and alone, on a bare hillside in winter," ties the chapter's Sleeping-Beauty conceit to the book's larger claim that the postwar [[world-machines|world-machine]] premise of trade-as-security has entered its dusk, with capture resistance — [[capture-resistance]] — becoming, for the first time, a self-conscious European project rather than an American one.
