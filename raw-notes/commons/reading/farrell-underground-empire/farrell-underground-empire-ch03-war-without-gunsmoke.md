# War Without Gunsmoke: The Weaponization of Huawei

This chapter traces the multi-decade rise and sudden strangulation of Huawei—China's national telecommunications champion—as the paradigm case of the United States converting the "underground empire" of dollar clearing and intellectual-property control into an instrument for crippling a specific foreign company, and it closes by showing how badly outmatched China's own retaliatory tools are by comparison. The narrative runs from Ren Zhengfei's Mao-inflected strategy of rising from the countryside to "surround the cities," through Huawei's rise into a global 5G leader threatening American telecommunications dominance, through the manufactured-seeming but actually improvised campaign that used a Wall Street Journal reporter's tips, a leaked ZTE document, HSBC's compliance vulnerabilities, and finally an obscure export-control footnote to bring Huawei to its knees—arresting its CFO Meng Wanzhou along the way. It ends by cataloguing China's comparatively feeble efforts to build parallel infrastructure (CIPS, an Unreliable Entity List, semiconductor self-sufficiency drives) and its structural inability to inflict costs on the United States the way the United States inflicts them on China.

## Themes

**Huawei as guerrilla, then as prey.** The chapter frames Huawei's entire history through Maoist military metaphor—Ren's "encircling the cities" strategy, the book that likened Huawei to a "pack of wolves" devouring a "great lion" from the periphery inward, Huawei University's courses on "The Particularity of the Law of War" and "Re-Reading The Art of War." This is not incidental color: it is the chapter's organizing irony. The insurgent tactics that let Huawei out-flank Shanghai Bell, Lucent, Ericsson, and Nokia by starting in neglected rural and developing markets are the same tactics the United States eventually turned back on Huawei itself, driving it from the metropolis back toward the periphery it started in. "The lion had rounded on the wolves and was driving them back to the outskirts of its territory."

**Finance and information as the opening wedge, IP as the finishing blow.** Meng's arrest was legally anchored not in export-control violations (which the U.S. could not easily prove or safely disclose) but in bank fraud against HSBC—itself coerced into cooperation because it was already under a deferred-prosecution agreement for laundering cartel money and desperately needed to stay inside the dollar-clearing system. This is [[weaponized-interdependence]] in its purest form: the U.S. did not need to prove Huawei broke sanctions, only that Meng's PowerPoint to HSBC misrepresented Skycom's relationship to Huawei, which put HSBC's own dollar access at risk and thus HSBC's cooperation was guaranteed. Once the case against Meng stalled as a purely symbolic instrument, the real kill shot came from a different chokepoint entirely: the 2020 "foreign-produced direct product" rule, an obscure footnote in 9-point font at the bottom of a 320-page Entity List that extended U.S. jurisdiction to any foreign-made chip built with U.S.-origin design software or equipment—reeling in TSMC and cutting Huawei off from advanced semiconductors almost overnight. Global phone market share collapsed from 20 percent to 4 percent within a year.

**An improvised weapon, not a master plan.** Despite Chinese state media's insistence that the U.S. and HSBC had "conspired" since 2012 to "entrap" Huawei, the authors stress that U.S. officials were making it up as they went, hamstrung by an erratic Trump who saw Huawei restrictions as a bargaining chip for a trade deal ("a black hole twisting all other issues around itself") even as hawks like Bolton and Ross tried to lock the restrictions in place. The authors' own 2019 academic article coining "weaponized interdependence" turns out, per Chris Miller's *Chip War*, to have directly inspired a Trump administration official's use of the FPDP rule—a case of a critical, warning framework being read instead as "a beautiful thing" and "a good playbook to implement." This is a striking instance of a [[history-machine]]-style emergence: a weapon assembled from bureaucratic improvisation, an unrelated ZTE prosecution, a leaked laptop, and an academic paper, rather than designed top-down.

**Asymmetric capacity for retaliation.** The chapter's second half pivots to China's countermeasures and finds them structurally weaker at every turn. China has no analogue to the U.S. power over HSBC—when China sanctioned Gayle Manchin, she was merely "flattered"; when the U.S. sanctioned Carrie Lam, she had to be paid salary in literal cash because no Chinese bank would touch her account. CIPS processes a fraction of what SWIFT does. China's market-access weapons (banning Norwegian salmon, Australian coal, NBA broadcasts) are "self-limiting"—they hurt China nearly as much as the target, and China quietly reimports Australian coal the moment it needs it. The chapter's closing line ("Judged by the size of its economy, China was a global power. Judged by its influence over global economic networks, it was at best an also-ran") is the chapter's thesis in miniature: raw economic scale does not translate into control over the [[legibility-as-machine-core|choke points]] of the world economy—only decades of infrastructural centrality does. This maps directly onto the [[gramsci-gap]] dynamic of the [[modernity-machine]] in dusk: the incumbent hegemon still commands the plumbing even as its underlying economic weight relatively declines, and can use that plumbing to punish a rising challenger long before that challenger can build parallel infrastructure of its own.

**Xi's response as a mirrored, defensive Long March.** China's leadership explicitly read the Huawei campaign, alongside the Snowden revelations, as proof that "key and core technologies are controlled by others," prompting Xi to call for a new "Long March" of technological self-reliance—an eerie mirror of the Maoist countryside-to-city logic that built Huawei in the first place, and of Ren's own 2021 admission that "if we pursue only what's practical, we may forever lag behind." The chapter suggests this is less a matter of a single grand strategy than of both sides reaching for the same guerrilla logic against a materially stronger opponent, in China's case now including the state itself.

## Notable passages

The chapter opens on Meng Wanzhou's homecoming to Shenzhen and immediately punctures the celebratory framing: the reader learns Huawei is now "desperately trying to reinvent itself" while the U.S. campaign against it unfolded during her three years of house arrest. This sets up the chapter's central claim, stated directly: the Huawei fight "wasn't an economic spat over mobile phones or cell towers. It was a new phase in a ruthless struggle" between an empire built "half by accident" and a challenger determined to build a position of strength against it.

The wolves-and-lion extended metaphor, drawn from a hagiographic book about Ren, is doing real analytical work: Huawei ate into the territory of incumbents "from the very periphery to the core," a strategy later inverted when the U.S. drove Huawei itself back toward the margins.

The mechanics of the FPDP rule passage is the chapter's technical core, showing exactly how an obscure regulatory footnote became a global chokepoint over semiconductor manufacturing—arguably the clearest illustration in the book of how legibility over one narrow technical dependency (chip design IP) can be leveraged into control over "trillions of dollars" of unrelated global commerce.

The airport-arrest scene—devices bagged to block signals, passcodes surrendered, three hours of interrogation before formal arrest—renders the abstraction of "weaponized interdependence" as a single human body detained on the strength of a financial technicality, which the chapter immediately generalizes: without the underlying dollar-clearing empire, HSBC would never have cooperated and Meng would have "continue[d] onward, never dreaming."

The closing comparison of Chinese and American coercive capacity—Carrie Lam paid in cash piles versus Gayle Manchin's mild flattery, salmon bans versus dollar-clearing exclusion—crystallizes the chapter's asymmetry thesis and sets up the book's broader argument about which power actually controls the world economy's underground infrastructure.
