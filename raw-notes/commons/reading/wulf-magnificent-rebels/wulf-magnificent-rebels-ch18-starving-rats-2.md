# 1802: Schelling's ascent, the Schlegel divorce, and the scattering of the Jena Set

Wulf narrates the winter of 1801-02 through the summer of 1803. The Fichte-Schelling quarrel goes public, with onlookers cheering the spectacle, while Schelling, aged twenty-seven, reaches the height of his fame lecturing on the 'Philosophy of Art'. In Berlin August Wilhelm Schlegel translates the Jena Set's ideas for a mixed public audience. Meanwhile Caroline and August Wilhelm's letters sour over money, her Berlin visit (March to May 1802) ends the friendship, and the couple agree to divorce. The Weimar proceedings, brokered by Goethe, take until 17 May 1803. Caroline then leaves Jena with Schelling and marries him a month later. A short section on the peace of Amiens and Paris as a magnet for artists and scientists leads into Friedrich Schlegel's plan for a journal called Europe. Wulf frames the period as a time for endings.

## Themes

**The philosophers' feud as public spectacle.** Fichte, enraged, threatens 'to expose every last flaw' in Schelling's thinking, and Schelling answers that his system is a 'philosophy whole and entire', an alternative to the Wissenschaftslehre and not a part of it. Wulf reports that their old adversaries enjoyed the mauling, quoting one of them ('when philosophers start eating one another like starving rats'). That is gossip and jeering by opponents, not Wulf's own verdict. Her framing is that the group's unity was giving way to rivalry, with pupils devouring teachers. This fits the fracturing of [[The Jena Circle]], though Wulf here narrates it rather than theorising it.

**Schelling's art-centred Naturphilosophie.** In the 1802 lectures Schelling ties art to the Absolute. He says humans can only truly comprehend nature through the creative output of artists, not scientific treatises. This is reported through his own words to students, and students called it the 'poetry of the universe'. See [[Naturphilosophie]]. His jibe that the English value mathematics only for 'Spinning Jennies & Stocking-weaving machines' sets poetry against utility and manufacture. That loosely rhymes with the [[modernity-machine]] contrast between legibility and something unmeasurable, though Schelling's argument is aesthetic, not a critique of infrastructure.

**August Wilhelm as synthesiser of the Jena project.** In Berlin he gives public lectures open to women, presenting the Jena Set's ideas. Wulf's own comparison: unlike Enlightenment thinkers for whom 'progress' and 'future' were watchwords, he studied the past to make sense of the present, and she likens his cross-disciplinary connections to [[alexander-von-humboldt]]'s comparison of plants across continents. This is her interpretive analogy, not a claim by the actors.

**Marriage dissolves through money, grief and shifting loyalties.** Wulf builds the breakup from letters: Caroline's defence of her spending, August Wilhelm's sharp reply, hints that he preferred she not come, Sophie Bernhardi's pregnancy and lost baby, and the final exchange where he feels released from his obligations. Wulf explicitly hedges the cause: 'It's unclear precisely what happened,' offering several potential causes, and then relies on Caroline's own retrospective account. Caroline later says they 'should only ever have been friends' and that only children would have made the alliance 'indissoluble'. Caroline's unconventional marital path, a recurring Wulf theme, appears here as practical, bureaucratic work.

**Divorce as a court and gossip affair.** A Weimar consortium of councillors, with Herder ([[johann-gottfried-herder]]) as president and hostile to the Schlegels, has to decide the formalities. Caroline notes that the earlier Mereau divorce is unhelpful because the duke wants to ensure the exception does not turn into a rule. [[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]] advises, amends the petition and arranges for them to avoid appearing. The Allgemeine Literatur-Zeitung insinuates Schelling caused Auguste's death, and August Wilhelm, despite the separation, defends Schelling. Wulf calls the men's continuing friendship amazing.

**A time for endings; Europe reordered.** Friedrich Schlegel and Dorothea Veit leave for Paris to launch a journal, Europe, asking whether the continent can be seen as one whole, unity in diversity. Novalis's collected works appear posthumously, Schiller sells his Garden House, and Caroline settles in peripheral lodgings. The Treaty of Amiens gives Europe its first peace in nearly a decade and makes Paris, filled with Napoleon's looted art and science collections, a destination. This is background scene-setting; Wulf notes scientists in France freed from orthodox beliefs, but does not develop it as a thesis here. Friedrich's European-unity question touches [[nationalism]] only by anticipation.

## Notable passages

**The starving rats (c1).** The public break with Fichte, and the onlookers' glee. The voices are the adversaries', which Wulf quotes.

**Philosophy of Art (c2, c3).** Schelling's claim that art is the intersection of ideal and real, music is nature's original rhythm, and only artists let us comprehend nature; then the English jibe about Spinning Jennies and the Philosophy of Cooking.

**August Wilhelm's Berlin lectures (c4).** The most explicit statement in the unit of the Romantic method as synthesis over taxonomy, with Wulf's Humboldt analogy.

**Cause of the split (c5).** Wulf's hedged reconstruction of what ended the marriage.

**Caroline's verdict on the marriage (c6).** Caroline's retrospective self-assessment and Schelling's telling his parents about her.
