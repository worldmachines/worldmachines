# After Jena, continued: the Humboldts, Hegel, Novalis, Schiller and Goethe

This unit is Wulf's epilogue-style tying off of the Jena Set's later lives. She follows Wilhelm von Humboldt into education reform and language theory, and his marriage to Caroline to its end; Hegel out of Jena to the Phenomenology of Spirit and a Berlin chair; Novalis into a posthumous myth built by Friedrich Schlegel and Ludwig Tieck; Schiller as the overlooked enabler of the whole circle; and Goethe through Napoleon, Faust, bereavement and old age. The strongest interpretive moves are Wulf's own: that Schiller 'made possible' the group, that Novalis was frozen in youth while others turned conservative, and that Goethe's late self-description reframes genius as collective.

## Themes

**Jena's self-determined Ich carried into institutions.** Wulf presents Wilhelm von Humboldt's Berlin university as shaped by 'his time in Jena and by the concept of a self-determined Ich': education as self-emancipation, teaching joined to research, arts joined to sciences. This is Wulf's claim about lineage, and it links the Romantic self to a lasting institutional form (compare [[infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness]], though Wulf is telling institutional afterlife, not Gay's social history).

**Language, nation and world view.** In retirement Humboldt develops ideas from Herder and Fichte ([[johann-gottfried-herder]]) about language and nationhood; his quoted view is that 'each nation is a mental whole'. Wulf reports this as Humboldt's own words; it sits on the path that the book elsewhere traces towards [[nationalism]] (see also [[counter-enlightenment-and-nationalism]]), but Wulf does not draw that line in this unit.

**Freedom in marriage, and its costs at the end.** Wilhelm and Caroline von Humboldt keep an open marriage; friends say it 'gives and takes such complete freedom', and Caroline writes that 'Love is the purpose of our life'. Wulf shows the unconventional experiment lasting almost forty years and ending in grief.

**Hegel against Romantic enthusiasm.** Wulf frames the Phenomenology of Spirit as Hegel's reckoning with Schelling and his zealous disciples: rigour, logic and 'serious business' against ingenuity, imagination and the celebration of the artist-genius. The Romantic-vs-rationalist tension appears here inside the Jena inheritance itself (cf. [[counter-enlightenment]], though Hegel's move is not simply pro-Enlightenment). Schelling's grumble that without him there would be no Hegelians is reported as his complaint.

**Myth-making about Novalis.** Schlegel and Tieck edited Novalis by chopping fragments and suppressing political and scientific work, so the poet was cut off from his practical interests; Schlegel himself said including them would be 'misleading as to the character of the author'. Wulf argues that this created a legend (Sophie as 'otherworldly creature', Novalis as Christ-like figurehead), and that because Novalis died before Napoleon's coronation while Schlegel, Schelling and Fichte 'became religious, conservative and nationalistic', he alone stayed the 'First Romantic'. See [[Novalis]], [[Friedrich Schlegel]]; this is a case of curation constructing a canonical image, resonating loosely with [[Legibility]] in that the editing made the man simpler to read, though Wulf does not use that frame.

**Schiller as the hidden hub.** Wulf calls Schiller the 'unsung hero': through Horen and invitations he brought August Wilhelm Schlegel, Schelling and others to Jena and 'made possible' the 'Alliance of Minds' ([[friedrich-schiller]], [[The Jena Circle]]). His Letters on the Aesthetic Education of Man underlie the Schlegels' beliefs even as they disowned him. Schiller's own line, that posterity would turn them 'from contemporaries into neighbours', is his words and cuts against a tidy group-identity story.

**Goethe the realpolitiker.** Goethe dislikes the emotional nationalism after Jena, favours cooperation over resistance, accepts Napoleon, and meets him at Erfurt. Wulf shows Faust's reception (Schelling, Coleridge, Staël), his unprocessed grief at Christiane's death, his loneliness for the dispersed Jena friends, and Schlegel's shock at Schiller's hostility in the published letters. See [[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]], [[romanticism-and-political-danger]] for the contrasting political temper.

**Genius as collective.** The unit closes on Goethe's late statement that thousands contributed to his works and that his 'life's work is that of a collective'. This is Goethe's own self-description, but it resonates strongly with the [[Symphilosophie]] ideal and with WMP's [[history-machine]] contrast to great-man historiography, with the caveat that Goethe means borrowing and absorption of the world, not organised group authorship.

## Notable passages

**Humboldt's university (c1).** Education as 'turning children into people' and self-emancipation, grounded in Jena. Load-bearing for Wulf's argument that the Jena self had institutional consequences.

**Language and nation (c2).** Humboldt's quotations on worldview and the nation as a mental whole, with his later linguistic scholarship.

**Hegel frees himself (c3).** The Phenomenology as rejection of imagination and feeling in philosophy, the Romantic-to-rationalist turn.

**The Novalis myth (c4).** How editing and posthumous framing made a saint of the poet.

**Schiller's neighbours (c5).** Schiller's structural role and his own doubt that the group shared beliefs.

**A collective (c6).** Goethe's closing self-assessment, the unit's most quotable reframing of authorship.
