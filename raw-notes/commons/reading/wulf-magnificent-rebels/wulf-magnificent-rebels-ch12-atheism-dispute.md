# Fichte's atheism dispute, dismissal from Jena and exile in Berlin (1799)

Wulf tells how the elector of Saxony complained about Fichte's article on belief in a divine world-governance, which equated God with the moral order, and how Duke Carl August of Saxe-Weimar was pushed to punish him. Goethe and Voigt hoped for a mild reprimand, but Fichte answered with the combative Appeal to the Public, then bluffed with a threat of resignation that Voigt accepted. The scandal filled the newspapers and letters of the German lands. Fichte's dismissal alarmed the younger Romantics, and he found no post elsewhere. He ended up in Berlin, a city he disliked, where he was attacked by Kant and hit back. The unit ends with Wulf's summary of how Fichte and Schelling saw themselves as the new generation.

## Themes

**Religion as pretext, revolution as the real charge.** Wulf argues that the affair was never really about religion: not even the duke believed Fichte was an atheist. According to Wulf's account of the duke's letters, what troubled him was Fichte's revolutionary leanings and his influence over students. Fichte's own reading is similar: his enemies feared radical change more than atheism. This is a hedged claim, since it rests on the duke's and Fichte's own words. It fits [[romanticism-and-political-danger]] only loosely, because here the fear is of revolutionary politics, not yet of nationalism.

**Fichte's God is the moral order.** Fichte's article, as Wulf paraphrases it, treats belief in God as belief in a moral world. God is within us, in our capacity to choose altruism or selfishness. The 'living and active moral order' is God. Wulf presents this as the Ich-philosophy applied to theology. Fichte also compares Herder's essay on God and [[baruch-spinoza]] to his own case and calls the resemblance to atheism greater in Herder's essay.

**Censorship in a patchwork of states.** Wulf contrasts Pitt's repressive acts in Britain with the haphazard enforcement in the German territories. Prussia's 1788 Censorship Edict was lightly enforced, and Berlin officials refused to confiscate Fichte's pamphlet because doing so would give it more importance. Saxe-Weimar gave professors 'freedom from censorship', meaning self-censorship as representatives of the state. The scandal is therefore a case of fragmented political geography shaping intellectual freedom, with the French Revolution as the background fear.

**Temperament and self-destruction.** Fichte is shown as combative and proud, with acquaintances and his wife Johanne describing his fury and his pride. His resignation bluff backfired. Goethe, who had long defended him, would have voted against his own son for using such language against a government. Wulf's account is pieced together from several people's opinions, so these judgments belong to the speakers.

**The circle's stake in the affair.** August Wilhelm Schlegel, [[Friedrich Schlegel]], Caroline and Schelling all read the dismissal as an attack on their own freedom. Their responses were mixed: solidarity in letters, a defence that no journal dared to print, and a Friedrich Schlegel piece he never published so as to keep a return to Jena open. Fichte's colleagues did not resign in solidarity, and the students petitioned in vain. For [[The Jena Circle]] this shows both loyalty and self-interested caution.

**Kant, succession and the self-made Ich.** Kant publicly called the Wissenschaftslehre untenable. Fichte answered by publishing Kant's private admission of the weakness of his old age, and Schelling agreed that Kant should bow out. Wulf closes the unit with an interpretive passage: Fichte and Schelling saw their own lives, a ribbon weaver's son and a theologian who left his path, as proof that an empowered Ich could steer its destiny.

## Notable passages

**Fichte's God (c1).** Wulf reports Fichte's identification of God with the moral order, which was the reason for the Saxon complaint. It sits next to the Ich-philosophy's stress on choice between altruism and selfishness.

**Censorship map (c2).** A short comparative passage on why control was easier in centralised states, and how Prussian civil servants still refused to confiscate the pamphlet.

**The Appeal to the Public (c3).** Fichte's counterattack reframed the case as a defence of free speech against 'idol worshippers, atheists, liars', and shows his temperament and rhetoric.

**The real charge (c4).** Wulf's plain statement that the affair was about revolutionary leanings, with the duke's regret over the 1793 appointment. The duke also blames [[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]] elsewhere in the unit for charm toward troublemakers, and [[friedrich-schiller]] advises Fichte to be measured.

**The bluff that backfired (c5).** Fichte's threatening letter to Voigt, including the Herder comparison ([[johann-gottfried-herder]]), and Voigt's acceptance of it as a resignation, ending his Jena career.

**Kings of the new generation (c6).** Wulf's interpretive close: the self-made Ich as a model that could change the world, which reads more like Wulf's own framing than a report of a single letter.
