# Illness, Gossip and the Death of Auguste (Jena, March to July 1800)

Wulf narrates the first half of 1800 in Jena. Caroline Schlegel falls seriously ill with a 'nervous fever' and is treated with the heroic regimen of Dr Hufeland (baths, plasters, wine, quinine, laudanum) until a spa cure is prescribed. While the household nurses her, Dorothea Veit sends Schleiermacher a stream of spiteful letters about Caroline, Schelling, August Wilhelm and even fifteen-year-old Auguste, and Friedrich echoes the suspicion that Caroline is engineering a match to keep Schelling close. Caroline travels with Schelling and Auguste to Bamberg and then Bocklet while August Wilhelm stays behind. Meanwhile the wider circle is dissolving: Fichte, Schelling and the Tiecks leave, Goethe visits less often, and Schiller, himself close to death, wonders why Goethe keeps company with the Schlegels. The unit ends with Auguste's sudden illness, a mistreated dysentery, her death on 12 July, and the collapse of Caroline, Schelling and August Wilhelm into grief.

## Themes

**The group as a network turning on itself.** Dorothea's letters are the vehicle for the phrase 'republic of despots' (her words, reported by Wulf, not Wulf's own thesis). The unit shows the [[The Jena Circle]] at the moment when shared enterprise gives way to gossip: the only people 'still talking' are August Wilhelm, Caroline and Schelling. Wulf reports Dorothea's judgements as hers (Caroline 'selfish', Schelling 'a vain stubborn braggart') and does not adopt them. Friedrich's accusation is likewise reported as an accusation. Compare the collaborative ideal of [[Symphilosophie]]: here the intimacy of the group produces rivalry rather than joint writing.

**Private life as scandal-machine.** The carefully staged travel plan (Schelling leaving early to meet Caroline on the road) is an attempt to manage rumour, and Dorothea notes that it fails: 'everybody knew'. Wulf presents the unconventional arrangements of the circle (the divorced wife, the younger lover, the husband who stays behind) as lived experiments whose costs fall on real people, including Auguste. This touches the theme of the self and free choice in practice, though Wulf here stays with scene and gossip rather than argument.

**Goethe's use of the Schlegels.** Wulf gives an explicit interpretive line: Goethe 'genuinely liked' the Schlegels but 'also used them', for verse help, translation and antiquity queries, in exchange for counsel in their feuds. Schiller's incomprehension is his own reported view. Goethe's Jena productivity is credited (via Schiller to Körner) to the town itself.

**Medicine on the cusp of change.** Hufeland's regime and the Bocklet doctor's laxatives (which Wulf calls 'perhaps the worst medical intervention for her symptoms, but common at the time') contrast with the Brunonianism Schelling met in Bamberg, which treated the body as an organism to be kept in balance. This is a small echo of the organism-thinking of [[Naturphilosophie]] in Schelling's own life, though Wulf does not draw that link in this passage. The professional refusal to take advice from 'a philosophy professor' fits the same period tension between speculative and practising knowledge.

**Political backdrop.** Napoleon's 1800 campaigns (Rhine crossing, Alps, Italy, the French closing in on Bamberg) run alongside as background; Friedrich thinks only of 'domestic battles'. Wulf uses the juxtaposition ironically rather than causally.

## Notable passages

Dorothea's letters to Schleiermacher (c1, c2) are the unit's central document of the circle's decay, and Wulf is careful to mark them as one partisan's voice, admitting even Dorothea acknowledged Caroline's early kindness. The passage on the exodus and Goethe's relation to the Schlegels (c3) gives the structural picture: the group dissolving, with 'republic of despots' as Dorothea's own phrase. The Brunonian passage (c4) is the fullest window into medical thought. The account of Auguste's death (c5) is the emotional hinge; Wulf reconstructs the sickroom conditionally ('Caroline might have', 'Perhaps Caroline read') and so signals imagination rather than record, before returning to documented quotations from Caroline and August Wilhelm.
