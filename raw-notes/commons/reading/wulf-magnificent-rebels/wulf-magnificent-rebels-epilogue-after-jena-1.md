# After Jena: Fichte's national self, Caroline's death, and the scattering of the Jena Set

The opening of Wulf's Epilogue (Part I, 'After Jena') is a run of short biographical afterlives, framed by Caroline's 1807 lament that the Jena circle is 'scattered across the world' and no longer 'making poetry together'. It moves from Fichte's 1807 Berlin lectures, through Caroline and Schelling in Munich and her sudden death in 1809, to August Wilhelm and Friedrich Schlegel (and Dorothea) in Coppet, Cologne and Vienna, and ends on Alexander von Humboldt as the friend whose fame and ideas carried furthest. Mostly narrative, but Wulf inserts a few interpretive verdicts of her own, above all on Fichte.

## Themes

**From individual Ich to national Ich (Wulf's own argument).** Wulf reads Fichte's Addresses to the German Nation as an extension of his Ich-philosophy: the friends had used the Ich-experience as the lens on reality, and now Fichte 'paved the way for a bigger Ich – the Ich of a nation'. She flags this as 'a dangerous idea, and one that would be exploited in Germany in the future'. That is her retrospective judgement, not Fichte's. It bears on [[nationalism]], [[romanticism-and-political-danger]] and [[counter-enlightenment-and-nationalism]], though Wulf gives a single paragraph to the link rather than a developed causal account. Fichte's language argument (German as a living language, others grafted on a dead Latin root) is reported as his claim.

**The group as a scene that dissolves.** Caroline is cast by Wulf as 'the heart of the Jena Set' and 'the conductor of their great symphony'; her nostalgia for the nightly open door and familiar faces, and the Schlegels' later disowning of Jena, show how much the [[The Jena Circle]] depended on physical proximity and a hostess. The [[Symphilosophie]] ideal of collective making does not survive dispersal. Friedrich in particular discards 'his youthful ideas and anyone associated with the Jena Set'.

**Self, transience and freedom in a woman's life.** Wulf presents Caroline as having lived, 'unlike most women', in control of her own destiny, and choosing transience ('we are all just wanderers on earth'). This is the book's interpretive frame about the self, women's freedom and lived Romantic ideals; the gossip about her death (Hegel's 'the devil had come and taken her', Charlotte Schiller, Friedrich's 'dead to me') is reported gossip and should be attributed to those speakers.

**Reaction from the self to the divine.** Friedrich's 1808 conversion to Catholicism is presented as the culmination of an old wish for a 'religion filled with feeling and imagination' (the November 1799 Jena meeting) and of increasing conservatism. His later verdict that Fichte was 'idolising the Ich and the self' and had 'confused the self with the divine' is Friedrich's, not Wulf's. The turn from [[Friedrich Schlegel]]'s early Romanticism toward medievalism and Catholic authority is a Romantic path away from the Enlightenment (cf. [[counter-enlightenment]]), but Wulf tells it as personal biography, not as a theory of the counter-Enlightenment. Note also that Dorothea's translations were published under Friedrich's name.

**Nature as organism survives (Humboldt).** Alexander von Humboldt is the one figure whose Jena inheritance is shown flourishing: he called [[Naturphilosophie]] 'revolutionary', still kept rational method, but 'quietly opened the door to subjectivity' (Wulf's phrasing), and described nature as 'a living whole' and 'a wonderful web of organic life'. Wulf says this concept 'still shapes our thinking today'. See [[alexander-von-humboldt]] and [[wulf-invention-of-nature]]; here Wulf gives a short summary rather than an argument. Goethe appears only as Humboldt's friend and as the target of Friedrich's resentful 'old worn-out demigod' ([[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]]).

## Notable passages

**Fichte's Addresses (c1).** Wulf describes the December 1807 lecture series, Fichte's claim that the Germans form a nation by shared language and culture, and her own warning about a 'bigger Ich'. It is the unit's clearest link from the self-philosophy of Jena to political nationalism.

**Caroline's happiness and death (c2, c3).** Caroline in Munich embraces transience and misses Jena's open door; Wulf then assesses her life as lived 'determined, confident and in control of her own destiny' and as the conductor of the group's symphony.

**August Wilhelm and Madame de Staël (c4).** His letter 'I am proud to be your property' and Wulf's comment that de Staël 'wanted nothing less than total submission' show a Romantic intellectual as a servant of another's project. It is also a channel by which Jena ideas reached her book on Germany.

**Friedrich's conversion and repudiation of Jena (c5).** The 1808 conversion and the disparagement of Fichte, Schelling and Hegel. Friedrich's statements are his own.

**Humboldt's living nature (c6).** Naturphilosophie translated into popular science writing, with the Cosmos quotation 'not a dead aggregate'.
