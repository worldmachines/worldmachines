# The French come to Jena: Napoleon's approach and Hegel's world soul

Wulf opens Chapter 20 in autumn 1806 with a Prussian and Saxon army of over a hundred thousand encamped in the Paradise park at Jena, a spectacle of uniforms and confidence, while Napoleon's larger army marches towards Berlin through Saxe-Weimar. She sketches the political backdrop (the end of the Holy Roman Empire, the Confederation of the Rhine, Prussia's late entry into war), then follows Goethe's retreat into nature study and his departure on 6 October. On 11 October news of a Prussian defeat sets off panic; on 13 October French troops walk through the unmanned gates and loot the town, and a fire destroys twenty houses overnight. Threaded through the disorder is Hegel, who posts the last of the Phenomenology of Spirit to Bamberg under threat and watches Napoleon ride by. Wulf uses him to show how German intellectuals split over Napoleon.

## Themes

**The end of the old order as backdrop.** Wulf says the French victories made the Holy Roman Empire crumble, and that Napoleon replaced it in summer 1806 with the Confederation of the Rhine. Caroline and Schelling, in their own letters, read this as opportunity: Caroline's image is Napoleon 'grazing through one country after another', and Schelling hopes the despotic monarchies will be swept away. These are the actors' hopes, not Wulf's claim.

**Intellectual life against the war.** The Jena Set is by now dispersed and dead in part. Goethe ([[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]]), ill on and off since Schiller's death, buries himself in colour theory and rocks, is nudged by duty into provisioning the troops, and on his last day looks at the town as 'the crucible of so many inspiring ideas' facing the French army. The lonely elegy for [[The Jena Circle]] is Wulf's narration of Goethe's mood, and the omen-like glimmer in the sky is reported as Goethe's own note.

**Civilians and the collapse of confidence.** The chapter contrasts the ornamented Prussian army with its rapid disintegration: stuck cannons, regiments barring each other's way, campsites left as ghostly squares of yellowed grass. Then the French looting, told at the level of hats, boots, trousers, buried valuables and half-dressed refugees. It is narrative scene-setting rather than argument, but it shows the fragility of the small-town scene in which the Jena ideas were produced.

**Hegel's Phenomenology as the mind watching itself construct reality.** Wulf's exposition of Hegel is her own gloss: building on Schelling, the book traces mind from consciousness to self-consciousness to 'absolute knowing', which she reads as the mind realising that reality is its own creation. This continues the book's thread of the self placed at the centre of thought, and rhymes with the project's interest in the construction of interiority (cf. [[infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness]]), though Hegel's spirit is a developmental process of mind rather than the bourgeois private self.

**Napoleon as world soul, and Germans divided.** Hegel's letters call Napoleon 'this world soul' and say everyone hopes for a French victory; Wulf says Hegel saw him as the executor of the Revolution's ideals and a step towards absolute knowledge. She stresses that Germans were divided: genius or tyrant, betrayer of 1789 or restorer of order. Caroline's 'destiny personified' and Goethe's later admiration (as Hegel later explained it) show the range. It resonates with great-man readings of history, the opposite pole from a [[history-machine]] account, and with the tension in [[intellectual-vs-revolutionary]], though Wulf frames it as the actors' perceptions.

## Notable passages

The Confederation of the Rhine passage and the letters of Caroline and Schelling (c1) show how the political rupture was read as possibility. Goethe's last day in Jena (c2) is the elegy for the circle. The Hegel passages (c3, c4, c5) give both Wulf's account of the Phenomenology and the 'world soul' encounter with Napoleon, together with the range of German judgements. The looting of 13 October (c6) is the vivid scene of the town's fall.
