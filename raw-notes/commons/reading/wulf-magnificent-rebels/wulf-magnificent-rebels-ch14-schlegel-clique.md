# Autumn 1799 in Leutragasse: household, affairs and Schelling's philosophy of art

Wulf narrates September to October 1799 in Jena. Friedrich Schlegel returns to the Schlegel house, and Dorothea and her son follow on 6 October. Caroline presides over a crowded, intellectually lively household while her marriage to August Wilhelm frays and she falls in love with the much younger Schelling. Fichte, now in Berlin, hears the gossip through his wife. Goethe moves between Weimar and Jena and mediates between Schiller, who is preparing to leave for Weimar to get away from the Schlegels, and the brothers. The chapter closes on Schelling's lectures, published as the System of Transcendental Idealism, and on the group's view of philosophy as a changing activity rather than a fixed doctrine.

## Themes

**The group as a lived scene.** Wulf shows the [[The Jena Circle]] as a physical arrangement of rooms, meals, walks, Italian lessons and readings of Dante, held together by Caroline's personality (Wulf's own image: if they were an orchestra, she was the conductor). Work is collaborative and domestic: Caroline and August Wilhelm have translated ten Shakespeare plays in four years, while Friedrich, Dorothea and Schelling each pursue a project. This is the everyday texture of what the project calls [[Symphilosophie]], though Wulf does not use that word in this unit.

**Life turned into writing.** Wulf argues that Novalis, Friedrich, Caroline and Dorothea 'believed that their personal experiences were reflections of a wider world'. She illustrates this with Caroline's novel outline, Dorothea's Florentin, Lucinde and the Hymns to the Night. This is a claim about the self as a source of universal meaning, and it resonates with [[self-construction-through-writing-gay]] and [[infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness]]. The rhyme is only partial, because Wulf's actors are self-consciously experimental where Gay's bourgeois are describing an inward habit. Caroline's novel never got beyond a synopsis, so the case rests on the outline and on the others' works.

**Freedom and its costs.** The couple's agreed 'freedom existing between us' turns sour once August Wilhelm's affair becomes 'serious' and Caroline's love for Schelling becomes visible. Wulf reports the gossip (Johanne Fichte's letters, Fichte's alarm) and attributes the moral alarm to Fichte and the letter-writers. She shows the collateral damage: Auguste is sent away to Dessau after Caroline scolds her. Friedrich, normally 'happy to break conventions', worries about having to choose sides. This anticipates the fracturing of the group that Wulf traces elsewhere.

**Goethe as broker, Schiller as outsider.** Schiller withdraws to Weimar. Wulf reports Goethe's later recollection that Schiller 'hated' the Schlegels and Goethe's remark that the friends 'chew the cud of their Ich's'. Goethe keeps a fragile peace by staying neutral and by concealing what each side does. This shows the circle as a network with a hub and non-communicating clusters, and Goethe ([[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]]) as a tie between them, with [[friedrich-schiller]] on the far side of the rift.

**Nature, experiment and art.** Goethe and Schelling read the Naturphilosophie together. Wulf presents Schelling as an idealist who still holds that knowledge originates in experience, and she quotes his line that every experiment is a question posed to nature. She then gives Schelling's System of Transcendental Idealism as the philosophical underpinning of Romanticism: the objective world is the mind's 'original, still-unconscious poetry', and art reveals the union of self and nature. Here Wulf adds her own interpretive gloss that Schelling's holism anticipates today's interdisciplinary research. See [[Naturphilosophie]] and [[wulf-invention-of-nature]]. This is an anti-reductive, anti-[[Legibility]] stance in spirit: neither counting nor instruments hold the key. The link to the project's frame is a loose analogy, not something Wulf draws.

**Philosophy as activity.** The friends accept revision as a virtue, and Novalis ([[Novalis]]) says their works may be 'already withered' by the time they appear. Wulf presents this as a response to a time of upheaval. Everything was in flux, and that was a 'modern phenomenon'.

## Notable passages

- **Caroline as conductor.** The description of the crowded household, its table for a dozen guests and Caroline's steering of talk. Dorothea's diary remark and Tieck's comment are quoted, so the 'magic circle' is the view of participants as reported by Wulf.
- **The self refracted through a prism.** Wulf's clearest statement in this unit of why private life mattered to the group, with Novalis's 'We have to build a poetic world out of ourselves'.
- **Keeping Goethe out of the fights.** The catalogue of secrets that keep the peace, with Goethe's remark about the 'Ich's'.
- **Goethe, Schelling and experience.** Goethe's empirical temperament meets Schelling's revised idealism, closing on Faust's lines on Nature's veil.
- **Art as the union of self and nature.** Wulf's exposition of the System of Transcendental Idealism.
- **Changeable thought.** The friends' willingness to change their views, and Novalis's line about withered works.
