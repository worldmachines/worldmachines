# Fichte's Ich-philosophy takes Jena

This unit follows Fichte's first period as a lecturer in Jena. Wulf opens with him drilling students to separate the Ich from the non-Ich (the wall), then explains, in her own voice, how his self-legislating freedom departs from Kant's duty. She then reports the reception: student hero-worship, alarmed fathers and professors, Goethe's amused engagement (including a link to his colour theory), and satirical and hostile critiques (egotism, despotism, Herder's pun). The political strand follows: Fichte's sympathy for the French Revolution worries Duke Carl August, who is reassured by Goethe. The last third is domestic and scene-setting: Fichte's wife Johanne, the Jena house, gossip about her dress, his lecture timetable, and the claim that he worked out his system live before his audience.

## Themes

**The self as foundation, freedom as choice.** Wulf's own gloss is that in Fichte's thought there were no absolute truths or laws created by God; there was only the self, and freedom and self-determination were the underpinnings. She contrasts Kant, whose Ich carried the burden of compliance with the Categorical Imperative, with Fichte, whose Ich acted from the position of free will. This is her explanatory framing of the philosophy, in narrative-historian mode, and not a technical exposition. It is the book's thesis about the invention of the self in concrete form; compare [[infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness]] and [[self-construction-through-writing-gay]], though Wulf's self is philosophical and volitional rather than a domestic or epistolary interiority.

**Freedom is morally bounded, and politically dangerous.** Fichte (in his own reported words) held that morality requires freedom and that only those who try to make others free are free. Contemporaries nonetheless feared the effect: fathers, Wieland, and the Duke worried about democratic talk and revolution. Wulf reports that critics predicted egotistical self-absorption and despotism, then judges much of the criticism unfair, since Fichte was neither advocating ruthless selfishness nor painting a world ruled by egoism, but notes his precarious balancing act between the empowered self, narcissism and equality and his own volatile temper. This anticipates the book's later dark side; see [[romanticism-and-political-danger]], with the caveat that here the danger is only feared and satirised, not yet realised.

**Reception as scene and network.** Students (over half of Jena's eight hundred), Goethe, Schiller and rivals form the social setting in which the idea spreads. Fichte says he generated his ideas 'with the listeners, and before their eyes', which Wulf frames as thinking done in public with an audience. That is an early instance of the collective idea production associated with [[The Jena Circle]], though here it is lecture-hall co-creation and not yet [[Symphilosophie]].

**Enlightenment ruler versus radical philosopher.** Wulf sets Carl August, an enlightened ruler in the mould of Friedrich the Great, against Fichte's declaration that governments exist to make themselves unnecessary. The contrast between Kant's slow reform and Fichte's revolutionary rhetoric marks a split within post-Enlightenment thought, loosely echoing [[counter-enlightenment]] debates, though Fichte here is radical and pro-Revolution rather than reactionary. Herder appears as a hostile critic ([[johann-gottfried-herder]]).

**Goethe and the subjective eye.** Wulf says Goethe was captivated by free will and the creative self partly because they seemed to connect with his colour theory, which turned Newton's physics of light into a study of vision. Goethe also joked ('Dear non-Ich') and admitted he struggled to follow Fichte's books ([[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]], [[friedrich-schiller]]). The link between Fichte and Goethe's optics is Wulf's interpretation.

## Notable passages

The opening lecture scene (c1) dramatises the Ich/non-Ich distinction as an embodied exercise. The Kant-versus-Fichte passages (c2, c3) carry Wulf's clearest statement of what the Ich means. The critics' passage (c4) records the charges of egotism and despotism as the contemporary counter-voice. Fichte's own words on the French Revolution (c5) mark the political edge. The passage on developing his system before his listeners (c6) shows the collective, live character of the philosophy.
