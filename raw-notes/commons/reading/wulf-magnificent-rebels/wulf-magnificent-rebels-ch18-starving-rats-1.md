# Separations: the Jena circle comes apart, 1801–1802

Caroline returns to Jena on 23 April 1801 to a plundered, silent house and a feud with Friedrich Schlegel and Dorothea Veit over her piano, porcelain and the gossip about her and Schelling. August Wilhelm reluctantly sides with Caroline, then leaves for an affair with Sophie Bernhardi in Berlin, returns briefly, and leaves again; Friedrich follows him to Berlin, indebted and self-absorbed, while Dorothea waits in Jena and finally leaves in January 1802. Alongside the household drama runs a philosophical one: Schelling's quarrel with Fichte, triggered by Friedrich's gossip, hardens into a real doctrinal split (Fichte's Ich as founder of the external world versus Schelling's Spinoza-inspired Absolute preceding subject and object), with Hegel defending Schelling in print while privately outgrowing him. Wulf narrates through letters and gossip; the interpretive framing is light and mostly in her judgments of character.

## Themes

**Friendship as a casualty of gossip and egotism.** The group that had lived its ideas together ([[The Jena Circle]], [[Symphilosophie]]) now fractures through reported talk: Friedrich's gossip sets Schelling against Fichte, and Caroline complains ‘how everyone gossips about everything behind your back’. This is Wulf's narrative of the dark side of the self-centred ethos, though here she shows it through scenes rather than arguing it; the 'starving rats' of the chapter title is not explained in this span.

**Philosophy as a quarrel over what comes before the self.** Schelling's objection to Fichte is that the Ich cannot found the world; he asks whether there is light only so that rational beings can see each other, and charges an ‘annihilation of nature’. Drawing on [[baruch-spinoza]], he posits the Absolute, the unity before it divides into self-conscious self and external nature, which severs him from Fichte's ‘Idealism of the Ich’ in favour of an ‘Idealism of Nature’ (see [[Naturphilosophie]]). The Absolute is glossed by Wulf as expanding Goethe's Urform (cf. [[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]]). This is the Ich-philosophy being challenged from within the circle that celebrated it; it rhymes with the Romantic turn from self to nature, though these are Wulf's summaries of dense arguments, not close exposition.

**Hegel's slow emergence.** Regarded by Schelling as a disciple and by his own students as a hesitant lecturer, Hegel defends Schelling in a pamphlet but is already working in silence toward the Phenomenology of Spirit, which Wulf says in 1807 would be ‘a reckoning’. Wulf contrasts Schelling and Fichte's ideas 'gushing' onto the page with Hegel's unhurried carving.

**Selfishness in private life.** Friedrich is portrayed (partly through Caroline's, Schleiermacher's and Dorothea's reports, so partisan sources) as debt-ridden, spoiled and interested only in being loved; August Wilhelm as enjoying his freedom while Caroline pleads for him; Dorothea, who had ‘truly lived Fichte’s empowered Ich’, becomes subservient. Wulf sets the lived outcome of the philosophy of the free self against the philosophy itself. Caroline's biases are flagged by Wulf herself.

## Notable passages

The Fichte-Schelling exchange on light and nature (c1) shows the disagreement in the actors' own words. The Absolute passage (c2) is Wulf's clearest statement of how Spinoza, Goethe and Schelling combine to displace the Ich. The Hegel passage (c3) plants his later importance. Caroline's arrival (c4) is the emotional key to the chapter's sense of loss, and the portraits of Friedrich (c5) and Dorothea (c6) give the personal cost of the group's ideal of self-assertion; treat these as accounts filtered through hostile or exasperated correspondents rather than settled fact.

For World Machines, the chapter is thin on machine-level structure but is a case study of a knowledge scene decaying at the level of relationships, and of the pre-Hegelian move from the self to an all-encompassing whole that later feeds into the [[romanticism-and-political-danger]] debates. Wulf is a narrative historian, not a machine theorist, and does not draw that link here.
