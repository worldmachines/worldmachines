# The Jena Set's world, why Jena and why Germany, and the book's argument

The second half of Wulf's Prologue does three things. It sets the despotic ancien-regime backdrop the Jena Set was born into and the shock of the French Revolution, which Wulf presents as the spark for Fichte's Ich and free will. It then contrasts the Enlightenment's clockwork, useful, mechanised world with the Jena Set's fear that reason alone had stripped reality of poetry, so that "The friends began to look inwards." Finally it answers "Why Jena? Indeed, why Germany?" with structural conditions (political fragmentation, weak censorship, many universities, mass reading, a university under four dukes and so under no one), before stating her own claim for the group's importance, defining Romanticism as deliberately indefinable, tracing influence via Coleridge to the Transcendentalists, and naming the book's central tension: free will versus selfishness.

## Themes

**Despotism as the starting condition (Wulf's scene-setting).** Versailles's clipped topiary is offered as an image of a people "bound to their destiny by birth and the king"; serfdom, censorship and mercenary-selling of subjects are listed to show a world of "despotism, inequality and control." This is Wulf's framing, illustrative rather than analytical.

**The Revolution as proof that ideas beat kings.** Wulf reports actors' words: Novalis on things becoming reality that would once have gone "straight to the philosophical madhouse", Friedrich Schlegel on the "power of words", Caroline's belief that "writers ruled the world", Fichte's pamphlet. She frames the Ich as "lit by the fire of the French Revolution", the liberation of the individual as a rebellion against the despotism of the state. This is a claim of Wulf's, supported by their quotations.

**Enlightenment as machine, Romantic reaction.** Wulf sketches an eighteenth century of natural law, instruments, steam engines, roads, maps and pendulum clocks, in which humans "remained cogs in a seemingly divinely ordained machine". The Jena Set (via Novalis's "monotonous clatter of a gigantic millwheel" and Hegel's "Everything is useful") feared reason alone. Resonates with [[modernity-machine]] and [[Legibility]] (clocks, maps and roads making the world readable and orderable), and with [[counter-enlightenment]] and [[isaiah-berlin]]'s framing of Romanticism against Enlightenment, though Wulf does not use this vocabulary and stresses the Jena Set wanted imagination alongside reason, not against it. Her "machine" is a metaphor, not a machine-theory of eras.

**Why Jena, why Germany: structural conditions.** Wulf's answer is contingent and institutional: a patchwork of over fifteen hundred states, so censorship "much more difficult to enforce"; about fifty universities; very high literacy and an 'Age of Paper'; and a Jena university controlled by four Saxon dukes so that "no one was truly in charge", giving professors unusual freedom. Schiller's judgement supplies the university explanation. Kant's programme (Wulf's gloss: mind and experience shape understanding of nature) had found fertile ground. This reads like an account of a scene or network arising from infrastructure, cf. [[The Jena Circle]], though Wulf offers no systematic model and the "strangely magnified" moment is narrative.

**The group and its comparison class.** Wulf compares the Jena Set to the Transcendentalists, Bloomsbury and 1920s Paris modernists, then asserts in first person that it is "intellectually speaking, the most important of all these groups": her own judgement.

**Romanticism defined by refusal of definition.** She reports scholars failing to agree and says the Jena Set liked this; they wanted "process of understanding" rather than absolute truth, tore down disciplinary boundaries, and (in August Wilhelm Schlegel's 1809 retrospect) wove poetry with prose, nature with art, etc. to "poeticise the increasingly mechanical clanking of the world". Relates to [[Symphilosophie]] and [[Naturphilosophie]] only loosely; neither is named in this unit.

**Influence.** Coleridge learned German, translated Schiller and Goethe, and read Fichte and Schelling; Emerson and the Transcendentalists came to Schelling through him. Wulf claims the ideas "rippled out".

**The self as inherited frame; thesis of the book.** Self-reflection became the method for understanding the world and part of the group's lived reality; they broke conventions in marriage and career. Wulf argues the Ich remains centre stage and that "We are this Ich": the liberation of the self from a divinely organised universe is "the foundation of our thinking today". Cf. the historical-construction-of-interiority theme in [[infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness]] and [[self-construction-through-writing-gay]], but Wulf treats the self as a discovery/liberation and as a legacy we must use wisely, not as an infrastructural construct. The book's core tension is free will versus selfishness ("tunnel vision of individual perspective" versus belief in change for the greater good).

## Notable passages

The Fichte paragraph (c1) links revolution, free will and the Ich, with Fichte's line that a person "should be what he is" and Schelling's "revolution brought about by philosophy". The passage on reason's downside (c2) contains Novalis's millwheel image and the turn inwards. The fragmentation passage (c3) and the four-dukes passage (c4) are Wulf's causal answer to "why Jena". The claim of importance (c5) is Wulf's own assertion plus Novalis's mission statement. The closing paragraph (c6) states the book's thesis, its tension and its claim of present-day relevance; it is interpretation, not narrative, and should be attributed to Wulf.
