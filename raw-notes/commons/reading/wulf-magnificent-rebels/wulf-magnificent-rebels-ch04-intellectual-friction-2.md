# Moving toward Jena: Shakespeare, Xenien, fatherhood and the Schlegel marriage

This unit joins two strands in the winter of 1795 and spring of 1796. In Braunschweig, August Wilhelm Schlegel and Caroline Böhmer work daily on a verse translation of Shakespeare, and Caroline becomes his indispensable critical partner. In Jena, Schiller invites August Wilhelm to move there, turns from philosophy back to poetry, and works ever more closely with Goethe, whose domestic life (Christiane, their son August, a dead infant) Wulf sketches at length. The strands meet when August Wilhelm visits Jena in May 1796, wins Goethe's approval, marries Caroline on 1 July 1796, and arrives with her in Jena on 8 July, so that the circle Goethe called the 'new generation' is 'almost complete'. Wulf writes as a narrative historian here: scene, letter and anecdote dominate, and her interpretive claims are light and mostly implicit.

## Themes

**Collaboration with concealed authorship.** Two models of joint work appear. August Wilhelm and Caroline's translation is a division of labour in which Caroline scans the verses and shapes the text; Wulf states that dozens of the three hundred reviews and essays published under his name were in fact written by Caroline. Goethe and Schiller's Xenien go further: they mixed lines and words 'so much that no one could tell any more who had written what', and Wulf says this concealed authorship let them 'aim sharper and hit harder'. This is the pre-history of [[Symphilosophie]] and of the collective scene in [[The Jena Circle]], though Wulf does not use those terms in this unit, and the Xenien are a satirical weapon rather than the shared philosophical project the Schlegels later develop.

**A women's contribution made invisible.** Caroline's education 'was paying off'; she is 'a razor-sharp critic' and 'was making herself indispensable'. Wulf frames the marriage as 'rescuing Caroline from social oblivion' while August Wilhelm 'gained an indispensable collaborator' - a reciprocal arrangement, though the byline went to him. The quoted 'one of Caroline's old childhood friends' is a reported remark, not Wulf's verdict.

**Translation and national culture.** Wulf reports (and partly relays August Wilhelm's own later claim) that the verse Shakespeare changed German theatre and led Germans 'to adopt Shakespeare as a national poet'. The link to [[nationalism]] is only a hint here; Wulf makes no political argument in this unit.

**Schiller's return from theory to poetry.** Schiller tells Goethe it is time to 'close up the philosophical shop for a while'; he fears his mind has become too theoretical and calls himself 'a poetic oaf compared to Goethe'. Humboldt's reassurance that he uniquely unites poetic and philosophical genius is a character's opinion, relayed by Wulf. See [[friedrich-schiller]] and [[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]].

**Domestic life and Rousseauian childrearing.** Goethe and Schiller are portrayed as doting, non-punitive fathers who let their sons 'roam freely in mind and in body'. Wulf explicitly ties this to Rousseau's Emile and the view that children are good by nature ([[jean-jacques-rousseau]]). Wulf also shows the unmarried household: Christiane cannot stay in Goethe's rooms 'because they weren't married'. This is a small window on the self and private life as lived, loosely echoing [[infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness]], though Wulf does not argue that here.

**Institutional infrastructure of a scene.** Horen, the Allgemeine Literatur-Zeitung, university lecturing and the Musen-Almanach supply money and audience; Schiller promises 'everything will fall into place'. This is a concrete picture of how a network is funded and housed, not a machine-theoretic claim.

## Notable passages

**Verse Shakespeare.** Wulf gives the significance of the translation (prose lost Shakespeare's dramatic power; the versions remain the standard German edition) and records August Wilhelm's line 'The recurring rhythm ... is the heartbeat of life' in the following paragraph (not curated as an anchor).

**Caroline as co-author.** The paragraph on her critical mind and unattributed reviews is the clearest statement of her role and sets up her later prominence.

**Schiller's poetic anxiety.** A compact portrait of the analytical mind versus the lyric impulse, with Goethe as the measure.

**Fatherhood and Rousseau.** Wulf's only explicit theoretical framing in the unit: children as 'a part of their lives', education based on freedom.

**The Xenien.** Composed weeks after the death of Goethe's infant son; humour as distraction and satire as collective weapon, including a couplet mocking Fichte's Ich-philosophy.

**Marriage and arrival.** Sets the personal and institutional stage for the Jena years.
