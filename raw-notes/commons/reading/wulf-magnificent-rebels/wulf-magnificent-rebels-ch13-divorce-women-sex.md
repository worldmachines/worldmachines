# Divorce, Lucinde and the equality of the sexes (1799)

Wulf's chapter runs two linked scandals through the winter and spring of 1799. Dorothea Veit, at her own initiative, divorces her banker husband Simon Veit in a Jewish court in Berlin, gambling her money, her standing and (by law) her children's custody, and is met with an avalanche of outrage. Friedrich Schlegel, now living with her, publishes *Lucinde* in May, an explicitly erotic and thinly disguised autobiographical novel that is also, in Wulf's reading, an allegory of equality between men and women. Wulf sets his view against Fichte, Kant and Schiller, records the abusive reviews and Dorothea's suffering, then closes with a domestic interlude (Fichte's daily routine at Dorothea's, the failed plan for a Jena Colony in Berlin) and the decision to move to Jena, into Caroline's household.

## Themes

**Divorce as lived selfhood.** Wulf stresses how hard divorce was under the 1794 Prussian General State Laws (few acceptable grounds, ostracism afterwards) and how Dorothea nonetheless pursued it. Her own words ('belonged to herself', 'I am happy now') are used by Wulf to make an interpretive point: while Fichte and Schelling wrote about the self-determining Ich, Dorothea was experiencing it. This is Wulf's framing, built on Dorothea's letters. It connects to the book's wider thesis about the invention of the self (cf. [[infrastructure-of-bourgeois-inwardness]], [[self-construction-through-writing-gay]]), though here selfhood is won against legal and social infrastructure rather than built inside a bourgeois home.

**Hypocrisy and the price of independence.** Wulf notes the outrage was 'a little hypocritical', since other women in Dorothea's Berlin salon circles also filed for divorce, but she was Moses Mendelssohn's daughter and had taken an impoverished intellectual as an open partner. Simon Veit's settlement was more generous than the law required.

**Lucinde as equality and as formal experiment.** In Wulf's account Friedrich intended the novel to provoke and to embody the equality of men and women ('petals of a single flower'), including role reversal in the love scenes. She contrasts this with the prevailing ideal of the compliant wife. Its fragmentary, generically mixed structure is presented as deliberate ('chaotic structure') and as a novelistic extension of the fragment form; the novel is called the genre best suited to the modern age by Friedrich, and Novalis is reported to share the view.

**The gap between free-will philosophy and gender.** Wulf argues Fichte 'seemed to forget his celebration of free will when it came to women': his Ich was only male, quoted from his own writing on marriage, and reinforced by his exchange with Johanne. Kant and Schiller are set alongside him; August Wilhelm's parody of Schiller's 'The Dignity of Women' gives the counter-voice. This is a pointed example of how Enlightenment-era freedom talk was gendered, in a way that resonates with [[romanticism-and-political-danger]] only loosely, since here the danger is exclusion rather than tyranny.

**Scandal and reception.** Wulf reports the Allgemeine Literatur-Zeitung's abuse, Schiller's contempt, Goethe's diplomatic evasion, Novalis's 'you lose yourself in a dizzy whirl', and the anti-Semitic and misogynist slurs against Dorothea (quoted, not Wulf's view). Fichte, careful not to complicate his life, praises the book as genius.

**Group as scene.** Letters as 'symphilosophising' (cf. [[Symphilosophie]], [[The Jena Circle]]), the 'family of magnificent outlaws' and the 'philosophers' convent', the proposed 'Jena Colony' in Berlin, and Dorothea's nervousness about living in Caroline's household show the group as a network held together by letters and domestic arrangements, with Caroline as its 'unchallenged centre' in Wulf's words. Friedrich's line about 'a marriage à quatre' comes from his own Athenaeum fragments.

## Notable passages

The divorce-law paragraph gives the structural constraints Dorothea defied. The 'belonged to herself' passage is Wulf's clearest link between Ich-philosophy and lived experience. The *Lucinde* equality passage and the Fichte-on-women passage sit in deliberate contrast. The passage on *Lucinde*'s confessional form and Novalis's reaction shows how the friends read it, and the structure passage ties it to the fragment aesthetic.

Caution: much of this chapter is gossip and scene-setting drawn from letters; Wulf's own arguments are the framing of Dorothea's experience as embodied selfhood and of *Lucinde* as an allegory of equality.
