# The Schlegels arrive in Jena, summer to winter 1796

Wulf, writing as narrative historian, follows August Wilhelm and Caroline Schlegel from their arrival in Jena on 8 July 1796 as they meet "everybody important": the Schillers, Goethe (who visits Caroline and, unknown to her, had earlier brushed off a plea for help), and Fichte, whose relations with Schiller have cooled. Against this social scene runs the war: French armies advance through Germany, threaten Schiller's family in Württemberg and Goethe's mother in Frankfurt, and put Jena's admirers of the Revolution in an awkward position. Friedrich Schlegel arrives on 6 August and is portrayed as brilliant, broke, arrogant and self-analytic; the Schlegels then share a larger house in Leutragasse. The unit closes with the Humboldts' return in November 1796 and their consensual open marriage, including Caroline von Humboldt's lover Burgsdorff living in the household. The unit is mostly scene-setting, and most claims about inner states come from letters that Wulf reports.

## Themes

**A scene forming through sociability.** Within days the Schlegels have entered Jena's parlours, clubs, concerts and amateur theatricals. This is the material texture of the network later called [[The Jena Circle]]: a small town where news "travelled quickly", and where Fichte and Schiller can avoid each other only because Schiller rarely leaves home. Wulf does not yet claim collective idea-production here. She shows the proximity and gossip that make it possible.

**Caroline as a self-fashioning, independent woman.** Wulf presents Caroline through dress (French-inspired, classically draped, with associations Wulf says Caroline "would have liked" for their link to democracy), education, conversational range and a stated "firm, almost instinctive need for independence". Hair and dress are read as republican signals. That is Wulf's interpretation. It fits the book's wider theme of the self as something made and displayed, though this unit only touches it lightly.

**The Revolution turns into war at the door.** Admiring the French "from afar" ends when Saxe-Weimar joins the coalition and French troops occupy Stuttgart and reach Frankfurt. Wulf reports the dilemma of Revolution sympathisers as a set of questions (patriots or welcomers?), and Schiller's remark that political matters he avoids "have a way of coming back home to roost". The political backdrop is the French Revolution, not yet [[romanticism-and-political-danger]] or [[nationalism]], which this unit does not argue.

**Friedrich Schlegel, self-analysis and self-regard.** Friedrich is drawn as the opposite of August Wilhelm, wanting to "live free" and be famous, hurt by conventional careers, identifying with Hamlet's turmoil through "constant and merciless self-analysis". His own words, and the judgments of his mother and of Caroline von Humboldt, are reported. They belong to those people, not to Wulf's thesis, though they preview the book's interest in egotism and interiority.

**Unconventional marriage as lived experiment.** The Humboldts' open marriage, agreed in 1791, is documented through Wilhelm's and Caroline's letters. Wulf stresses that they were untroubled by gossip or jealousy, and that Caroline von Humboldt thought the gossip about Goethe and Christiane Vulpius preposterous. It illustrates the group's practice of living ideas of freedom, and Wulf keeps its emotional description tied to the participants' own words.

## Notable passages

**Caroline's character** (c1): Wulf's portrait combines Friedrich's own words about her ("bold mixture of such different qualities") with the report that her independence made her unpopular with other women. It is a portrait Wulf draws largely from admirers' testimony.

**The war reaches Jena** (c2, c3): a compact account of how the Revolution's admirers were put in an awkward position, followed by Schiller's family's ordeal in Württemberg, which is Wulf's narration of a letter from his sister.

**Goethe and the plea he refused** (c4): Goethe's warm visit to Caroline contrasts with what Wulf tells the reader, and Caroline never knew, about his refusal to help in 1793. Wulf's phrasing ("Possibly feeling a little guilty") is a hedge over Goethe's motives. See [[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]] and [[friedrich-schiller]] for the wider cast.

**Friedrich Schlegel** (c5): a dense biographical sketch. The "rare and dangerous beast" line is Friedrich's own self-description. See [[Friedrich Schlegel]].

**The Humboldts' open marriage** (c6): one of the clearest presentations in the unit of an experimental relationship, resting on letters.
