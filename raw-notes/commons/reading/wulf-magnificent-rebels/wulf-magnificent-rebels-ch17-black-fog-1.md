# Darkness falls: grief, blame and fracture in the Jena circle, summer 1800 to New Year 1801

Wulf follows the aftermath of Auguste's death from July 1800 to the turn of the year. August Wilhelm and Caroline withdraw into shared grief in Bamberg and then Braunschweig, while Schelling sinks into guilt and talk of suicide. The wider circle turns on itself: Dorothea and Novalis blame Caroline (and Dorothea blames Schelling), Friedrich flirts and lectures badly, and Goethe, Fichte and Tieck respond with varying degrees of distance or tenderness. The unit ends with Caroline's letters to Schelling, her appeal to Goethe, and the two Schlegel-Schelling households marking the New Year apart.

## Themes

**Grief as a solvent of the group.** Wulf presents Auguste's death as the moment when the tensions of previous months (the Caroline-Schelling affair, the quarrels with Friedrich and Dorothea) turn into open accusation. Novalis, whom Wulf calls more traditional than the others about marriage and women, condemns Caroline; Dorothea, in Wulf's telling, sounds 'deranged' and circulates her own account of the death. Wulf reports these as the characters' claims, not established fact. Some, like Tieck, are pushed by death to reassess wasted time. This is a small-scale case of the fracturing Wulf traces across the book and loosely fits the [[The Jena Circle]] as a network whose cohesion depended on personal bonds.

**Gap between professed ideals and conduct.** Wulf points out that Friedrich, who 'advocated the equality of women in his writings', pursues Sophie Mereau, and that Dorothea retaliates with Ritter. The narrative frames freedom-in-theory against its egotistical practice, an early instance of the dark side of the self-centred ethos, though Wulf here is describing behaviour rather than stating a thesis.

**Caroline as the lived Ich-philosophy.** Wulf's interpretive claim: Caroline, without writing treatises, 'lived and breathed the new philosophical ideas' and embodied 'the empowered free self'. This ties the book's theme of the self and free will (see the general Romantic invention-of-the-self frame, cf. [[self-construction-through-writing-gay]], though Caroline's self-making here is in conduct rather than writing) to a woman's resilience.

**Goethe as patron and outsider.** Goethe works on Faust, botany and anatomy, finds Friedrich Schlegel tiring, and is asked by Caroline to help Schelling. See [[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]] and [[friedrich-schiller]]; the New Year's Eve scene contrasts jovial Goethe, lecturing Schiller and silent Schelling. Novalis's failing health is also noted ([[Novalis]]).

## Notable passages

**Auguste's grave (c1).** Sets the tone: Caroline 'only half alive', August Wilhelm writing obsessively and kneeling at the grave.

**Novalis turns on Caroline (c2).** Wulf's explanation is his aristocratic roots and conventional views on marriage; his remark that Auguste escaped a 'dark fate' is reported as his opinion.

**Dorothea's accusations (c3).** Dorothea's claims about Auguste, Schelling and the doctor are her reported claims, relayed by friends; Wulf does not endorse them.

**Friedrich, Sophie and Ritter (c4).** Shows the hypocrisy between doctrine and conduct.

**Caroline's core of steel (c5).** Wulf's clearest interpretive statement in the unit.

**The masked ball (c6).** Goethe steps in at Caroline's request; Schelling stays silent amid Weimar festivity.
