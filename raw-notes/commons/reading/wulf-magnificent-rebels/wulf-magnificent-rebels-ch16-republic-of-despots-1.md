# Estrangements: the Jena circle turns on itself, winter 1799–1800

This unit opens Part IV, "Fragmentation", with Chapter 16. Wulf narrates how the Schlegel household, once the hub of [[The Jena Circle]], becomes isolated and then internally hostile over one cold winter. A public war with the Allgemeine Literatur-Zeitung (its editors Hufeland and Schütz, and the reviewer Huber) hardens the friends' sense of being besieged. Schiller leaves Jena for Weimar and a daily note-traffic with Goethe, still scornful of the Schlegels' self-absorption. Fichte, visiting from Berlin, feels excluded and disapproves of Caroline and Schelling's open affair. Friedrich Schlegel turns from Caroline to hatred, Tieck sours on the whole household, Dorothea's resentment of Caroline is released, and Novalis stays aloof. The chapter closes with two contrasting scenes far from Jena: Alexander von Humboldt in the Orinoco rainforest, and Wilhelm von Humboldt in Madrid praising Fichte and Schlegel.

## Themes

**Collective creativity curdling into mutual estrangement.** Wulf states the turn plainly: "Gone were the days of symphilosophising. Instead of fighting the literary establishment and a world devoid of imagination, they fought among themselves." This is the negative image of [[Symphilosophie]]: the same intensity of shared life that generated ideas now generates gossip and grievance. Wulf gives several causes (the Schiller quarrel, Fichte's dismissal, the Lucinde scandal, the journal fight), and also physical ones (the exceptionally cold winter, money shortages, Tieck's gout). She does not reduce it to one cause.

**Egotism and the self-determined Ich, seen from outside.** Schiller, as reported by Wulf, judges that the Schlegels "had sullied the concept of a self-determined Ich with their self-absorption and arrogance". This is Schiller's view (paraphrased by Wulf), not Wulf's thesis, though it fits her larger "dark side of the self" storyline. The unit shows self-assertion as a social practice: public letters, public renunciations, deliberate provocation (August Wilhelm's "People hate us – good!") in which being hated is treated as proof of effectiveness.

**Gossip as the medium of the scene.** The epigraph (Caroline: "a den of murderers") and the flurry of letters show the circle as a network whose ties are carried by correspondence, and whose members reinterpret one another through it. Tieck's letters to his sister on Caroline ("hermaphrodite") and Dorothea ("monster") are gossip Wulf reports as Tieck's opinion; they show how the woman's intellectual freedom drew gendered hostility even from allies. The Schlegels gossip about the Tiecks in return.

**Unconventional intimacy meets conventional limits.** Fichte's philosophy was revolutionary, but Wulf says his attitude to marriage was "resoundingly traditional"; his discomfort at the free-love arrangements becomes a hurt withdrawal. Friedrich's turn against Caroline is given several hedged motives ("Maybe...Or maybe...Whatever his reasons"), so it should be read as Wulf's speculation.

**Public reputation systems.** The Allgemeine Literatur-Zeitung dispute is a fight over legitimacy: anonymous reviews versus a named critic who publicly leaves. The editors defend anonymity as ensuring honest reviews. This is a small case of [[counter-enlightenment]]-era avant-garde versus the Enlightenment review establishment, though Wulf frames it personally rather than intellectually.

**Nature as network, offstage.** The Humboldt passages contrast frozen Jena with the tropics and end on "a network of 'active, organic powers' – one great organism in which everything was connected." That is the Humboldtian vision of [[Naturphilosophie]]-adjacent nature ([[alexander-von-humboldt]], [[wulf-invention-of-nature]]). The irony, which Wulf builds through juxtaposition, is that unity is found in nature while the human circle disintegrates. Wilhelm's yeast-in-sourdough remark on Fichte and Schlegel is his opinion, sent to Goethe.

## Notable passages

**Isolation of the household.** Wulf's framing paragraph: guests and a full table, but only intimate friends; talk of the 'Schlegel clique'. It gives her list of causes.

**Caroline's fury at Huber.** The brutal Athenaeum review, written by an old acquaintance, prompts Caroline's letters. She casts it as a "universal battle of good against evil" and links her mood to Napoleon's coup, a political backdrop that Wulf reports through Caroline's own words.

**"People hate us – good!"** August Wilhelm's defiant letter and Schleiermacher's quip about the "blaze of hellfire": hostility as identity.

**Schiller's verdict.** Relief in Weimar, but continued animosity; [[friedrich-schiller]] finds "all that individuality shimmering on every page so repulsive". Compare the warm, breathless note-traffic with [[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]].

**End of the Jena Colony.** Fichte's frustration and the collapse of shared work.

**Tieck's turn.** Physical pain and gendered gossip; a satire he says he would have written if they had not been friends.

**Humboldt in the Orinoco.** The organism image, set against the Jena winter.

A half-fitting resonance for the WMP frame: the unit is a micro-scale case of a group that works as a scene fracturing under its own intensity (cf. [[history-machine]] vs great-people narrative), but Wulf offers no machine-level claim, only the personal chronicle.
