# The Athenaeum launches: defining 'romantic', imagination and the fragment

The unit opens in May 1798 with August Wilhelm in Berlin (consulting on Hamlet, and rumoured to be having an affair with the actress Friederike Unzelmann, which Caroline treats with a joke) and then turns to the main event, the first two issues of the Athenaeum. Wulf presents the journal as the friends' manifesto: a 'revolution of words', half of it made of fragments (Novalis's Pollen, the Schlegels' Fragments). She argues that it is here that 'romantic' was first used in print in its new sense, glosses that sense as a drive to perceive the world as an interconnected whole, and traces how the Jena group elevated imagination beyond Hume, Kant and Fichte. She then reports the reception (Wieland's bemusement, Goethe's delight, Schiller's disgust) and closes with the afterlife: Coleridge's German sojourn, Wordsworth's preface, and the spread of the fragment form into poetry, painting and music.

## Themes

**Language as political weapon.** Wulf reports August Wilhelm's fragment that language should be 'republicanised through the power of the general will', and frames the friends as using words as weapons in the wake of the French Revolution's own neologisms (sans-culottes, terroriste). This is her framing of their self-understanding rather than a claim about the journal's actual political effect.

**'Romantic' as an open, unfinished project.** Friedrich Schlegel's long fragment on romantic poetry is quoted, and Wulf insists (her own gloss) that to romanticise is not to be sentimental but to connect art and life, individual and society, humankind and nature. Novalis's formula ('I romanticise') is given as the actor's own definition. Poetry is a 'living organism' that should 'forever be becoming, never perfected', which Wulf ties to the group's open-ended working method: Fichte thinking in front of students, Novalis excerpting, Friedrich speaking. This resonates with [[Naturphilosophie]] (nature and poetry as organism), though Wulf does not draw that link explicitly here.

**Imagination as the bridge faculty.** Wulf sketches a short history (Plato, Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz and Johnson suspicious; Hume granting it limited scope; Kant and Fichte making it essential) and argues that the Schlegels and Novalis thought Fichte did not go far enough because he left out art. Novalis contrasts cold mechanical logic with living imagination and 'rational chaos'. Wulf notes that [[alexander-von-humboldt]] would carry imagination into natural science. The contrast with mechanical thinking loosely rhymes with WMP's opposition between [[Legibility]] and liveness, though this is a Romantic, not a WMP, framing and the fit is partial.

**Youth, modernity and antiquity.** Wulf argues that [[sturm-und-drang]] and Werther had prepared the ground but that the Jena Set gave the cult of youth its philosophical foundation, and that these first Romantics saw themselves as moderns (the 'Phantasie' spelling). Friedrich Schlegel uses antiquity as a prism and prefers the Dionysian to Goethe and Schiller's measured order.

**Symphilosophy.** The invention of 'symphilosophise' names their collective mode of work: two minds as divided halves. See [[Symphilosophie]] and [[The Jena Circle]]; here the journal is the concrete vehicle of that collaboration.

**Reception and the Goethe-Schiller split.** Wieland calls the journal 'a strange phenomenon'; Friedrich reports that everyone is talking about how brazen they are. Goethe ([[johann-wolfgang-von-goethe]]) is praised in the journal and openly roots for the Schlegels; Schiller ([[friedrich-schiller]]) is punished with silence and reacts with private fury. These are letters and reported opinion, not Wulf's verdict.

**Influence.** Sales were only a few hundred per issue, but circulation and lending gave it, in Wulf's judgement, an enormous long-term impact. Coleridge returns from Germany 'with the mind of a philosopher'; Wulf sees parallels between Jena and English Romanticism (turn to the self, imagination, emotion over neoclassical rules) and traces the fragment through Byron, Pushkin, Delacroix, Chopin and Schumann. Her strong claim that these ideas 'came to shape Romanticism across the world' is interpretive. For the Romanticism-versus-Enlightenment axis see [[counter-enlightenment]], though Wulf here presents the group as extending rather than rejecting science and philosophy.

## Notable passages

The 'revolution of words' paragraph (c1) shows the political-linguistic self-image of the group. The definition passages (c2, c3) are the core of Wulf's account of what 'romantic' meant in Jena. The imagination passage (c4) places the group in a philosophical genealogy from Kant and Fichte. The symphilosophy passage (c5) names the collective method. The Goethe/Schiller/Schlegel reception passage (c6) shows the group's fault lines, which matter for later chapters on the fracturing of the circle.
