# Framing the Monkey King: Yang's Foreword and Lovell's Introduction

This unit consists of two pieces of front matter preceding the translated novel itself: Gene Luen Yang's personal foreword, recounting how he first heard Sun Wukong's stories from his immigrant mother and later rediscovered them as an adult graphic novelist, and Julia Lovell's scholarly introduction, which sketches the novel's plot, traces its disputed authorship and accretive textual history, surveys the sixteenth-century Ming context that produced it, lays out the long-running critical split between reading it as comic farce and reading it as religious/philosophical allegory, and follows the novel's reinvention across five centuries of Chinese political upheaval into the present.

## Themes

**Inherited stories as cultural transmission.** Yang's foreword is not literary criticism but testimony: his mother told him Sun Wukong stories at bedtime specifically "so that I wouldn't forget the culture that she had left," and Yang frames his entire career — including *American Born Chinese* — as an attempt to recapture that transmission now that his mother has Alzheimer's and can no longer tell the stories herself. The foreword's closing move ties this personal thread directly to the value of a new translation: it exists to "brush off the dust so that we can rediscover what is lasting."

**Contested, anonymous authorship.** Lovell stresses that the 1592 hundred-chapter edition emerged from centuries of oral storytelling, drama, and a shared "fictional Rolodex" of episodes before any single compiler fixed it in print, and that neither the original publisher nor editor would admit knowing who wrote it — fiction (*xiaoshuo*, "lesser discourses") was culturally disreputable. The attribution to Wu Cheng'en rests on circumstantial evidence assembled by Hu Shi centuries later. This slow, distributed, anonymous accretion of folk material into a single canonical text before it lurches into visibility as an authored masterwork resonates with [[history-machine]] historiography more than with a great-man account of authorship.

**The double reading: farce versus allegory.** Lovell organizes most of her essay around a durable critical split — the novel as "good-humored supernatural slapstick" versus the novel as a Buddhist/Neo-Confucian allegory of disciplining the volatile "monkey of the mind" (a reading literalized by the pun that Chinese uses one word, *xin*, for both heart and mind). She marshals evidence for both camps — the comic set-pieces and running jokes on one side, the two classical prefaces reading Monkey as "the spirit of the heart and mind" on the other — and concludes the text is "a gloriously open text" that resists any single interpretation, irreverent toward all three of the religions it draws on.

**Monkey as vessel for political reinvention.** The introduction's second half traces how each era of Chinese turmoil recast the same protean character: Qing-dynasty sequels moralizing Monkey's misbehavior after the Ming collapse, a 1909 sequel using time-traveling pilgrims to satirize westernization on the eve of dynastic collapse, Mao publicly identifying with Monkey's "havoc-wreaking instincts" and invoking him to incite the Cultural Revolution, and post-1989 youth embracing *A Chinese Odyssey*'s image of Monkey as a directionless drifter rather than a revolutionary. Lovell explicitly notes that "adaptations and rereadings... have proliferated in periods of exceptional political and social turmoil" — an interregnum pattern in which an old order's collapse and a new one's absence keep generating fresh symbolic uses of the same insurgent myth, echoing [[gramsci-gap]] dynamics even though Lovell's frame is literary reception rather than institutional legitimacy.

**Social and religious satire embedded in fantasy.** Beneath the supernatural spectacle, Lovell reads sustained mockery of hierarchy: no ruler in the book comes off well, the Buddha's own subordinates demand bribes, the disciples are "ugly but useful" while the handsome, educated Tripitaka is the weakest link, and the Land of Women episode inverts gender roles to make male pilgrims feel "the discomforts of being a woman in male-dominated China."

## Notable passages

Yang's paragraph cataloguing Monkey's powers ("call a cloud down from the sky and ride it like a surfboard... How cool was that?") captures the foreword's register — a child's-eye wonder that Yang explicitly wants to pass on to the reader — and sets up his later claim that Superman and Spider-Man were merely "Western expressions of everything I loved about the monkey king."

The foreword's closing passage ("And that's why new translations... Because I want you to remember, too") is the thesis statement of the whole piece: personal memory and the act of translation are made explicitly equivalent, both being ways of refusing to let something disappear.

Lovell's account of the Hu Shi attribution ("Although there was no more obvious candidate... into a single novel") is the clearest single statement of how thin the evidentiary basis for Wu Cheng'en's authorship actually is, despite his name now being permanently attached to the book — essential for any note that treats "the author" as a stable category here.

The second (late-1600s) preface's allegorical reading ("Although the book is exceedingly strange... its movement and transformation cannot reach") is the strongest primary-source evidence Lovell quotes for the spiritual/allegorical camp, framing the entire pilgrimage as being "only about the retrieving or releasing of one's mind."

The Mao passage ("While plotting the Cultural Revolution... an effective tool of education") is the pivot of the reception-history section: it shows a head of state consciously weaponizing a five-hundred-year-old comic novel's rebel-hero as literal incitement to political violence.

The Monkey/woodcutter exchange ("Deep in this mountain lies the Lotus-Flower Cave... magic power. Beware!") is Lovell's chosen sample of the novel's verbal comedy — Monkey's blithe running commentary on the many ways he might be cooked and eaten — offered as direct textual evidence for the farce reading rather than assertion alone.
