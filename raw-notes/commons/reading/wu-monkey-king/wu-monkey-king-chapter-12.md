# The Grand Mass and the Calling of Tripitaka

A soul mix-up resolves the aftermath of Taizong's trip to the underworld: Liu Quan and Li Cuilian are returned to life, her soul lodged in the body of the emperor's just-deceased sister, producing a comic scene of mistaken identity before Taizong sorts out the swap and rewards the couple. He then turns to fulfilling his vow to the hungry ghosts of Hell, convening a grand Buddhist mass in Chang'an under the monk Xuanzang—only for the court's underlying anxiety about Buddhism's legitimacy to surface first, and then for Guanyin, in disguise, to intervene: selling sacred relics to the court, heckling Xuanzang's shallow "lesser vehicle" teaching in public, and finally revealing her true form to declare that only the Mahayana scriptures of Thunderclap Monastery can truly redeem the dead. Xuanzang volunteers on the spot, is renamed Tripitaka and sworn into brotherhood with the emperor, and departs west with an oath that failure means eternal damnation.

## Themes

**Resurrection is played as bureaucratic comedy, not solemn miracle.** The underworld's afterlife logistics—Yama's envoy grabbing the wrong soul, Liu Quan losing his wife "somewhere between the regions of light and dark," the revived Cuilian complaining about the palace's "garish yellow tiles"—render the supernatural machinery of the story mundane and slightly absurd, a bureaucratic error rather than a sacred restoration. The comedy defuses what would otherwise be a moment of high pathos, characteristic of the novel's tonal register throughout.

**Religious legitimacy is contested and then administratively settled.** The court's debate over whether Buddhism is a "corrupting foreign faith" that undermines the hierarchies of "ruler and subject, father and son" stages a real ideological fault line—Confucian social order versus a doctrine whose founder "abandoned parents, family, and ruler"—before the ministers resolve it by fiat, declaring a need for "syncretic balance among the three teachings." The resolution is imposed by decree (dissent now punished by broken arms) rather than argued to consensus, a top-down settlement of what had been a live legitimacy crisis. In miniature, this is the shape WMP calls the coexistence-in-friction of distinct orders resolved not by synthesis but by state power picking a winner—compare [[world-machines]]'s claim that three machines always coexist in friction rather than merge.

**Guanyin stages a test before offering the solution.** Rather than simply announcing herself, she disguises as a beggar-monk to sell relics, then deliberately provokes Xuanzang in public—"what can you tell me about Mahayana, the great vehicle?"—exposing the inadequacy of the officially sanctioned religious authority before revealing that a superior teaching exists and only she can supply it. The staged unmasking converts an obscure foreign doctrine into a visible, court-endorsed imperial mission in a single dramatic beat, compressing what might otherwise be a gradual legitimation process into spectacle.

**The vow structures the pilgrimage before it begins.** Xuanzang's oath—"if not, I will never return home and instead pass straight to Hell"—and the emperor's parting gesture of dropping native earth into the farewell wine ("cherish a pinch of dirt from your motherland above myriad ounces of foreign gold") together frame the coming journey's stakes and its emotional center: damnation on failure, homeland as the thing the traveler carries and must not forget.

## Notable passages

The soul-swap confusion ("The 'princess' rolled over and opened her eyes... Stop manhandling me, ruffian!") sets the story's comic register for a nominally sacred resurrection. The court debate passage shows Buddhism's legitimacy being argued and then settled by decree. Guanyin's self-revelation and written message ("Guanyin and Hui'an now rose up, took their actual forms... will become a golden Buddha") is the actual hinge of the chapter, converting court religious theater into a sanctioned quest. Xuanzang's volunteering and oath establish the mission's damnation-stakes. The closing farewell, with its pinch of native earth dropped into the wine, closes the Chang'an frame and launches the pilgrimage proper.
