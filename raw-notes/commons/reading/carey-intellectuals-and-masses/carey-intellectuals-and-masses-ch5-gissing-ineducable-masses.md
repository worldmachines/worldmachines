# George Gissing and the Doctrine of the Ineducable Masses

Carey reads George Gissing (1857-1903) as the writer who first assembled, in full and durable form, the intellectual case against mass culture that later figures like F. R. and Q. D. Leavis would only repeat. The chapter traces Gissing's belief that "the masses" are an ineducable natural category through his fiction's recurring devices, his treatment of women's education as especially dangerous, his own scandalous biography of theft, prison, and two brutal marriages to working-class women, and a persistent self-undermining strain in which Gissing's fiction exposes the very elitism it seems to endorse.

## Themes

**Physiognomy and the bookcase as a taxonomy of fixed types.** Gissing's standard method for introducing a character is to read either the face or the bookshelf: facial features yield reliable diagnoses of "intellect and personality," and bookshelves sort neatly into two camps — poetry and literature signal sensitivity, while politics, social science, and "modern thought" brand an owner as coarse or dishonest. This is a legibility project applied to persons: a scheme for making human worth instantly readable and permanently classifiable, prior to and independent of any encounter with the person (faintly resonant with [[legibility-as-machine-core]], though Gissing's target is caste rather than statecraft). The contradiction Carey isolates is that physiognomy implies determinism — Gissing called himself one outright — while the very existence of books implies the possibility of education and change; Gissing resolves this by insisting books only ever confirm what a reader already is, never transform what he is not.

**Education as gatekeeping, not enlightenment.** Every attempt in Gissing's novels to bring culture to the poor — May Tomalin teaching Piers Plowman and Bach to a working family, Walter Egremont lecturing Lambeth workmen on Sidney's *Defence of Poetry* — is staged as absurd, and Gissing blames the audience's incapacity rather than the missionaries' misjudgment. When a lower-class child does absorb poetry, as with cockney Joey's mangled recitation or piano-dealer Samuel Barmby's garbled *Paradise Lost*, Gissing renders the result as grotesque travesty rather than progress. Carey's point is structural: Gissing needs education to remain the exclusive property of an already-refined caste, so any evidence of the masses acquiring it must be recast as pollution.

**Misogyny as an amplifier of the elitist system.** Educated or aspirant women receive Gissing's harshest treatment — pretentious Camberwell sisters in *In the Year of Jubilee*, the physically and mentally destroyed matriculation student Jessica Morgan, the child-losing intellectual Cecily Doran, the murderous blue-stocking Mrs Wade. Gissing buttresses this with contemporary pseudo-science (Romanes on smaller female brains, Spencer on study unfitting women for maternity), while privately claiming to friends that he recognized "no restraint whatever upon a woman's intellect" — a liberality he reserved for private correspondence and denied to his fiction's lower-class women almost entirely.

**The autobiography beneath the doctrine.** Gissing's expulsion from Owen's College for theft (undertaken, he claimed, to save a prostitute, Nell, whom he then married), his subsequent choice to live in squalor among people he despised, and his second marriage to Edith Underwood, both marked by allegations — credited by H. G. Wells — of cruelty, deceit, and violence including beatings with stair rods, form the psychological substrate of his class theory. Carey argues the gulf Gissing insisted on between himself and "the masses," and his contempt for his own wives' ignorance, were necessary props for a self-esteem built on estrangement.

**Self-sabotage through fiction.** Gissing repeatedly creates "natural aristocrat" mouthpieces — Osmond in *The Unclassed*, Godwin Peak in *Born in Exile*, Dyce Lashman in *Our Friend the Charlatan*, Clifford Marsh in *The Emancipated* — who voice Gissing's own convictions almost verbatim, yet are exposed within their novels as frauds, hypocrites, or fools. Carey reads this less as calculated irony than as involuntary self-criticism: Gissing cannot stop indicting the very positions he holds. The clearest instance of this hypocrisy is biographical rather than fictional: Gissing mocks the snobbish Mrs Denyer in *The Emancipated* for despising ordinary tourists in Italy, while writing to his friend Bertz, at the very same time, in terms indistinguishable from hers.

**Knowledge versus doctrine.** Carey's counter-argument is that Gissing's abstract loathing of "the masses" as a category sits uneasily beside his unmatched, granular knowledge of individual poverty — dead feet after a Saturday shift, a paralysed cab-driver's legs, dye rubbed into greying hair to pass as young, vinegar-cravings among underfed girls. This concrete, compassionate material, Carey argues, is what makes Gissing's novels valuable even as his generalizing theory of the ineducable mass is what makes his intellectual legacy pernicious.

## Notable passages

The opening 1892 letter to Eduard Bertz sets the whole chapter's terms: newspapers reveal "extending and deepening Vulgarity," democracy is levelling society down, and the coming era belongs to "an Aristocracy of mind and manners." The physiognomy/bookcase passage supplies the mechanical core of Gissing's caste theory. Joey's cockney recitation of mangled verse is Carey's sharpest illustration of how viscerally Gissing recoiled from the poor's actual attempts at culture. The long "Gissing knows" passage is the chapter's counterweight — proof that Gissing's material sympathy exceeded his stated doctrine. The Wells testimony about stair rods roots the intellectual position in domestic cruelty. And the Rome letter, set beside the fictional Mrs Denyer it unwittingly echoes, is Carey's clearest demonstration that Gissing's fiction indicts opinions he held himself.
