# H. G. Wells Against H. G. Wells: System, Freedom, and the Masses

Carey argues that Wells's greatness as a writer is inseparable from a chronic self-contradiction: the same man who drafted eugenic blueprints for exterminating the unfit in *Anticipations* wrote his most moving fiction from inside the consciousness of the very clerks, drapers' assistants, and "failures" his programs would discard. Moving across Wells's scientific romances, popular novels, and utopian tracts, Carey shows a mind that never resolves the conflict between wanting to eliminate the mass and wanting, imaginatively, to live as one of its members — and argues that this irresolution, not his doctrine, is what makes him worth reading.

## Themes

**Imaginative duplicity as the source of value.** Carey's opening claim is structural: Wells's utopias "seem to waver and change into dystopias as we watch," and his fiction refuses to adjudicate between opposed responses to the same material — Bedford's violence and Cavor's admiration toward the Selenites are both allowed to stand. This duplicity recurs at every scale of the chapter: suburbs are both blight and enchantment, advertising is both fraud and the truest mass education, the superior individual is both hero and monster (Griffin, Moreau), and failure is both a thing to be eradicated and Wells's one true subject.

**System versus freedom.** Carey identifies a hinge around 1910: before it, Wells's imagination runs on freedom — the open road, the bicycle, science fiction's transgression of natural law, all of which liberate his lower-middle-class heroes into a dream of the English countryside. After it, population anxiety pushes him toward system: world government, single currency, and — crucially — categorization. "Once system is accepted, categorization follows, and abstractions like 'the masses' swallow up individual Mr Pollys and Mr Lewishams." This is close kin to the [[world-machines]] framework's contrast between a *pull* machine organized around legible, classifying order and a *push* machine of proliferating, non-convergent variety: Wells is a single mind trying to run both machines at once, and Carey's chapter is essentially a case study in that friction, staged inside one author's oeuvre rather than across an era. The drive to classify so that an "unmanageable number of unique realities" can be governed reads as a personal, miniature version of [[legibility-as-machine-core]].

**Classification versus the uniqueness it erases.** Wells's own 1903 philosophical paper rejected classification itself as a "fallacy... in what is quite conceivably a universe of uniques" — a position that, if held, would invalidate the racial and eugenic hierarchies his later thought depended on. Carey uses this to show Wells sawing off the branch he sits on: forty years later his D.Sc. thesis argues the opposite, that individual identity is illusory and only the collective species is real. The unresolved argument between these two Wellses is, in effect, a suppressed [[Conatus]] — the individual's striving to persist as itself against a system (his own) that wants to average it into a type.

**Contempt voiced by exemplary and villainous characters.** Wells repeatedly puts his own harshest positions about the mass into the mouths of characters marked as sinister (Ostrog: "The world is no place for the bad, the stupid, the enervated... Their duty... is to die") or otherwise admirable (Professor Keppel's tirade against "oafish," "under-nourished riff-raff"). Carey reads this displacement as evasion — a way of airing views "but not to be held accountable for them" — evidence that some part of Wells, his "story-telling gift," distrusted the creeds his intellect produced.

**Sympathy for the excluded as Wells's real achievement.** The chapter's emotional center is Carey's claim that "despite his contempt for failures, failures are his only successes." Mr Polly, Kipps, Hoopdriver, and Lewisham — all victims of "lost educational opportunities" — carry Wells's warmth precisely because they lack the qualities he elsewhere insists should rule the world. Their exclusion from an ideal of culture repeatedly figured as a desirable, unattainable woman (Christabel astride the wall, Beatrice at Bladesover) gives Wells's fiction its recurring erotic-social geometry of a wall that only she can cross.

**Kindness that still condescends.** Carey does not let Wells's affection for the lower-middle class off the hook: it depends on infantilizing them. Polly, Kipps, and Hoopdriver are repeatedly described as children, and the narrator's declared love for "my Kippses" as "little pink strips of quivering, living stuff" is, Carey notes drily, "not the way we talk about equals" — sympathy that still reduces its object to a pet.

## Notable passages

The chapter opens on the Selenites passage to establish the method: Wells's fiction stages irreconcilable responses without adjudicating between them, and the image of the machine-processed Selenite's hand offering "a sort of limp appeal for lost possibilities" becomes, by the chapter's end, explicitly Wells's own "one tragic subject."

The derision passage — "Going to work is a misery and a tragedy for the great multitude of boys and girls" — is Carey's evidence that Wells's tenderness toward the young excluded from opportunity was neither incidental nor safely contained; Carey suggests it was precisely this tenderness that Wells had to suppress with "cold violence towards the weak and inferior" in order to write his eugenic tracts at all.

The Keppel outburst ("I hate common humanity... Clear the earth of them!") is placed to show how thin the membrane is between Wells's reasoned population politics and raw contempt — the speech starts from a defensible complaint (bombing, shooting) and slides, unremarked, into wishing the poor and "under-nourished" out of existence.

The Kipps passage ("I see through the darkness the souls of my Kippses... little pink strips of quivering, living stuff") is the chapter's clearest demonstration that Wells's professed love for ordinary people never escapes a vocabulary of pity that infantilizes and objectifies its object even while defending it against "the claw of this Beast" of Stupidity.

Trafford's vision of the Oxford Street crowd as "shoaling mackerel on a sunlit afternoon" closes the arc: unlike an intellectual's benign pastoral acceptance of the mass (Carey compares Yeats and Orwell elsewhere in the book), Trafford's mackerel-humanity is intolerable to him — he wants people to stop being mackerel — and his attempt to make the clerk Dowd "representative" of a class immediately erases the individuality it claims to honor, which Carey identifies as Wells's problem in miniature.
