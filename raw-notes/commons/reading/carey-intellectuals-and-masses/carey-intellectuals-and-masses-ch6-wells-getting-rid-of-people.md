# H. G. Wells and the Extermination of the Masses

Carey reads Wells's entire career—non-fiction polemic and science fiction alike—as an elaboration of one childhood wound: the destruction of the Kentish countryside around Bromley by suburban development. That grievance, Carey argues, hardens into a generalized loathing of "mass-mankind" and metastasizes into explicit proposals, across Wells's non-fiction, for sterilizing, imprisoning, or killing the "inferior" majority of the human race, while his fiction returns obsessively—and with mounting relish—to fantasies of wiping humanity out entirely.

## Themes

**Childhood trauma as origin myth.** Wells's hatred of the suburban masses is traced to a single biographical fact: Bromley's population nearly tripled in his boyhood as railway-driven "development" buried the fields and streams of his early memory. Carey treats this not as incidental biography but as the psychological engine of Wells's mature politics—the semi-autobiographical *New Machiavelli* passage in which the child realizes "building was the enemy" recurs, transposed, in novel after novel (*Tono-Bungay*'s suburbs as a "tumorous growth," *Ann Veronica*'s villas like "bright fungoid growth").

**Malthus and Darwin weaponized.** In *Anticipations* (1901) Wells fuses Malthusian scarcity with Darwinian selection into a program he presents as ethics rather than cruelty: the "nation that most resolutely picks over, educates, sterilizes, exports, or poisons its People of the Abyss will be in the ascendant." The argument explicitly denies human equality and frames extending it to "inferior" masses as being "swamped in their fecundity"—a vocabulary of biological threat, not political disagreement.

**Genocide proposed as policy, softened by euphemism.** Wells's New Republic kills the unfit "with an opiate" rather than brutally, which Carey presents as a horror precisely because of its bureaucratic gentleness. The logic extends explicitly to race: the "swarms of black, and brown, and dirty-white, and yellow people" who cannot meet the New Republic's efficiency standard must, in Wells's own words, simply "go." Carey does not let euphemism launder the content—he shows Wells's later, more moderate proposals (means-tested parenthood, forced labour for debt, sterilization on a second offence in *A Modern Utopia*) as softened versions of the same eliminationist premise, not a repudiation of it.

**Woman as scapegoat.** Overpopulation is persistently gendered female in Wells's fiction—unchecked fertility and consumerist vanity are woman's fault, and several Wells mouthpieces (Clissold's Clementina, Marjorie in *Marriage*, Karenin in *The World Set Free*) indict women's nature directly, up to fantasies of sex-selection to reduce women "to a minority." This sits inside Carey's larger book-length argument about intellectual contempt for the masses, with women doubly targeted as both breeders of the surplus population and emblems of vulgar mass consumption.

**Legibility as utopia.** *A Modern Utopia*'s world state administers 1,500 million people through thumbprints, photographs, and a central Paris index tracking births, marriages, deaths and convictions for the entire globe—a fantasy of total population legibility that Wells imagined decades before the computing power to realize it existed. This is a strikingly literal anticipation of the [[modernity-machine]]'s core drive toward [[legibility-as-machine-core]]: making a population fully countable, sortable, and administrable is not a side-effect of Wells's utopia but its precondition, and the register (who may breed, who is sterilized, who is exiled) is the mechanism by which "inferior" life is filtered out.

**Fiction outstrips policy.** Where Wells's essays stop short of a workable extermination plan, his stories supply one: killer ants, man-eating crabs, Martian invasion, world-ending air war, engineered plagues, atomic holocaust. Carey's closing claim is that this progression—crabs and ants giving way to the mass death of *The War of the Worlds*, *The War in the Air*, *The World Set Free*, and *The Shape of Things to Come*—reveals that "destruction lured him even more powerfully than progress," and that the settings chosen for annihilation (London's suburbs, by name) make the fiction a repeated, escalating revenge on Bromley.

## Notable passages

The childhood-origin passage from *The New Machiavelli* anchors the whole chapter's argument: Wells's world-historical population theory is shown to begin as a boy's grief over a vanished river and fields. The *Anticipations* passage coining "People of the Abyss" gives Carey's clearest documentary evidence that Wells's eugenics was not metaphorical—the vocabulary of "picking over," "exporting," and "poisoning" a population is offered as sober policy. The opiate-killing passage shows the New Republic's violence dressed in bureaucratic mercy, and the passage on "swarms of black, and brown, and dirty-white, and yellow people" who must simply disappear removes any doubt that the eugenics has an explicit racial target. Finally, the artilleryman's speech in *The War of the Worlds*—gloating over the extinction of "all those damn little clerks"—is Carey's proof that the fiction does not merely depict mass death but savors it, collapsing the distance between Wells the rationalist planner and Wells the fantasist of annihilation.
