# Conservation as Control (Grove)

**Kind**: synthesis across the notes on [[grove-green-imperialism]]. Grove states the double edge in a sentence of the Introduction: the conservation apparatus later became “a highly convenient form of social control” over “unruly marginal subjects” and a trigger for anti-imperialist resistance ([[grove-green-imperialism-introduction]]). The individual notes each record one case. Set side by side, they show *when* the edge cuts which way.

## The series

- **Mauritius: conservation against capital.** Poivre's reserves, felling caps and fixed timber price were aimed at land speculators and absentee grandees. His “new moral economy” paired the protection of nature with the protection of the slave population, and he called deforested ground “land in servitude” ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s5-poivre-programmes]]). Resistance came from European landowners only ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s1-caribbean-forests]]).
- **The Cape: one hedge, three jobs.** The almond and thorn hedges of 1659–60 conserved timber, fixed the land grid and built “a highly symbolic hedge between the white settlement and the Hottentots” ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch03-s4-het-paradijs]]).
- **Tobago: omission as policy.** Vattel's doctrine that uncultivated land could be treated as unowned fused environmental and territorial claims. Grove warns that “whoever controlled the terms of this powerful discourse, in science and in legislation, might also affect or even police the control of people and land” ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s1-caribbean-forests]]). The survey that laid out plantation, town and forest-reserve lots left the Caribs “out of the map-making process altogether,” and within twenty years they “had disappeared as a separate population” ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s2-soame-jenyns]]).
- **St Vincent: the forest as weapon.** A larger, organised black Carib population burned surveyors' houses and maps and forced a formal concession of “a common right of nature” to the woods in 1770 ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s3-carib-resistance]]). Here conservation and control pulled *apart*. Settlers wanted the forest cleared because it gave the Caribs cover, and the King's Hill reserve was opposed partly on those grounds ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s4-anderson-advocacy]]). Anderson, the reserve's advocate, linked the Caribs' expulsion to clearing for cane and wrote of it with “melancholy sensations” ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s5-anderson-critique]]).
- **Malabar: the burden of proof.** The 1805 proclamation vested all royalty rights in the Company. Wrede's memoir put “the onus probandi that any part of it is private property” on customary users who had never needed papers ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s1-company-forests-2]]). The later search for mountain timber also served to suppress or co-opt resisting tribal groups ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s1-company-forests-1]]).
- **Sind: the harsher template was inherited.** The amirs had created their shikargahs often by forcibly removing villages ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s1-company-forests-1]]). Napier first opened them to free grazing. Stocks's 1852 report then recommended reverting to the amirs' enclosure by “stakes or walls” and exclusion of “goats and... any living being,” and Dalhousie adopted it ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s6-dalhousie]]).
- **India after 1835: drought as the ratchet.** Each drought (1835–9, the early 1860s, 1877–8) brought new forest protection and extended state control over land and timber at the expense of customary rights, which the state also wanted for taxation and pacification. Grove presents this as an intentional double motive ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s7-wider-perspective]]).

## The connection

No single note puts these side by side, and together they say three things.

1. **The variable is who already stood on the land.** On uninhabited Mauritius the same desiccationist reservation could only collide with planters and speculators, so it read as critique of capital, and Poivre could yoke it to anti-slavery. On inhabited Tobago, St Vincent, Malabar and Sind it met forest peoples and became a sorting of people as well as trees. The Mauritius–Caribbean contrast is Grove's own ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s1-caribbean-forests]]). Carrying it forward to India is this page's reading.
2. **Classification did the work before enforcement did.** In every inhabited case the decisive act is a category: cultivated or idle land, reserve or plantation lot, Crown property or unproven claim. Tobago shows the category working by silence, a map that did not see the Caribs. St Vincent shows that a category needs force behind it and can be refused.
3. **The harsh version was not always colonial.** Sind shows colonial conservation reverting *toward* a stricter pre-colonial model, which cuts against reading exclusionary forestry as an imported European invention. It also fits Grove's attack on the “Merrie India” picture ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s1-company-forests-1]]).

## What this reading must hold

- **Grove does not make conservation a mask.** The Conclusion insists that the scientists were often sincere reformers and sometimes “actively anti-colonial” ([[grove-green-imperialism-conclusion]]). The same people who built reserves also indicted slavery and famine policy.
- **Resistance could win, for a while.** The St Vincent concession held until the 1791 rebellion and the later removal to Bequia ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s3-carib-resistance]]).
- **The frame is the wiki's.** The notes read these cases through [[Legibility]] and [[legibility-as-machine-core]] and say that Grove writes legal, administrative and environmental history, not a theory of state sight.
