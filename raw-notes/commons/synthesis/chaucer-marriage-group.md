# Chaucer's Marriage Group and the Striving to Self-Govern

A cross-cutting reading. Chaucer's tales about marriage do not stand alone; they answer one another across the pilgrimage, and the connective tissue is textual, not just thematic. Justinus, mid-argument in the Merchant's Tale, points the company to "the Wife of Bath... of marriage, which ye have now in hand"; the Merchant's own prologue invokes Griselda's patience against his shrewish wife; Chaucer's *Bukton* envoy cites the Wife again as an authority. What emerges is a distributed debate — traditionally grouped by scholars as "the marriage group" — over a single question no single tale resolves: whether desire and self-governance can be captured by a contract or a cage. That question is where the tales meet the World Machines vocabulary of [[Conatus]] and [[capture-resistance]].

## The debate

- **The Wife of Bath states the thesis: sovereignty.** Her prologue sets lived experience against "authoritative texts," re-reads Scripture to license serial marriage, and narrates her mastery over five husbands as an economy of leverage. Its climax is capture-resistance made literal: her husband Jenkin disciplines her nightly by reading from a book of "wicked wives," and she answers by physically destroying the book and winning "by mastery all the sovereignty." External instruments of legibility and control met by refusal — and her Tale's collected answer to "what women most desire" is exactly *mastery* ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-wife-of-baths-tale]]).
- **The Clerk's Griselda is the thesis inverted: near-total capture.** Griselda's advance, unconditional oath of obedience makes her a striving self almost perfectly captured by an arbitrary sovereign will, her endurance legible as virtue only inside Walter's frame. But Chaucer's own comic envoy refuses to let that frame stand — instructing wives to be "eager as a tiger" and never let humility "nail" their tongues — a surplus the authorized moral cannot govern, in the spirit of the [[untimely]] ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-clerks-tale]]).
- **The Merchant makes control architectural, and shows it fail.** January's walled garden and single silver key are an attempt to build a fully surveilled space around his wife's body; the counterfeited key and his blindness dramatize capture-resistance enacted by the ostensibly captured party ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-merchants-tale]]).
- **The Franklin proposes the synthesis: marriage without mastery.** Arviragus and Dorigen found their marriage on the principle that "love will not be constrain'd by mastery," he keeping only the empty "name of sovereignety" for appearances. The tale then tests that freedom against a rashly-binding word and resolves it not by a rule but by a cascade of ungoverned generosity, closing on an open question rather than a verdict ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-franklins-tale]]).

## Nature against the cage

Two further units generalize the marriage quarrel into a claim about striving as such. The Manciple's Tale offers the batch's most direct proof-text: through the caged bird that spurns a gilded cage for the wild wood, the pampered cat that abandons milk at the scent of a mouse, and the she-wolf, it argues that "kind hath her domination, and appetite flemeth discretion" — appetite cannot be domesticated by comfort or confinement, however well the cage is gilded. This is nearly a statement of [[Conatus]]: a being's essential striving overriding any regime built to contain it ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-manciples-tale]]). The apocryphal *Court of Love* stages the same collision from the institutional side — monks and nuns forced into vows "in tender youth" cursing an imposed chastity against a nature that gave them "appetite to love" ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-court-of-love]]).

## The connection

Read together, the group makes one claim none makes alone: Chaucer treats marriage as the proving ground for whether a self's drive to govern its own body, goods, and desire can be captured by an external order — oath, key, cage, or vow — and keeps concluding that it cannot be, without residue. The Wife wins her governance back; May defeats the key; the Manciple's bird flies; even Griselda's total capture is undercut by an envoy the official moral cannot absorb. The tales cross-reference each other precisely because none is meant to settle it: the debate is the point. The Wife's argument that God calls people to different offices "as vessels of gold and wood" — a defense of legitimately divergent ends against one totalizing template of virtue — gives the whole group a loose but real resonance with the [[divergence-machine]]. What the tales stage as comedy and casuistry, WMP tracks as [[capture-resistance]]: the persistent refusal of a living thing to be instrumentalized wholly to another's ends.
