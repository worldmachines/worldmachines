A reader of Montaigne's *Essays* ([[montaigne-complete-essays]]) meets what looks like a contradiction. The [[apology-for-raimond-sebond]] calls reason “an instrument of lead and of wax”. Yet the essays on education, conference, books and experience build everything on judgment ([[judgment-over-learning-montaigne]]). Each note treats its own essay. Set side by side, they show that the two attitudes have different objects, and that the Apology's sceptic and the education essays' pupil are trained in the same practice.

## What the Apology destroys

The target is reason as a foundation: a criterion, a first principle, a system that settles disputes.

- Every science rests on presupposed principles taken “by authority and upon trust” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-10]]).
- Errors and truths enter the soul by the same channel, so we hold truth, if at all, “by chance” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-12]]).
- Any instrument or reason that needs checking falls into a regress, “round again upon the wheel”; a fit judge would be “a judge that never was” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-15]]).

The Apology attacks the philosophers' claim to certainty and the claim of private reason to be “controller-general” of everything.

## What the other essays trust

What they trust is judgment as an exercised faculty that weighs particulars without claiming to settle them:

- Judgment is an oar that tests every subject and stays on the shore when a subject runs too deep ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch50-55-democritus-heraclitus]]).
- It is formed by digestion, conversation, travel and history read for the judgment of men ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch25-education-children-1]], [[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch25-education-children-2]]).
- It must “weigh, sort and distil” experience rather than reckon it up ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch08-art-conference-1]]).
- In law it is preferred to codification: few, simple laws and judgment on the spot, “For every foot its own shoe” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-1]]).
- Applied to one's past choices, it judges a decision by its occasion, not its outcome: “I do not consider what it is now, but what it was then” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch02-repentance]]).

## The connection: one practice under two names

The Apology describes the Pyrrhonian practice this way: argue both sides, assert nothing, keep consent “ambiguous”, and follow appearances in action ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-6]]). The education essay asks for the same thing of a boy: hear diverse opinions, and “remain in doubt” if he cannot choose ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch25-education-children-1]]). Of the Art of Conference says the same of Montaigne himself. Having deprived his judgment “of the right of determining”, he finds no belief offensive and welcomes contradiction, and holds that “To hunt after truth is properly our business” while possession “belongs to a greater power” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch08-art-conference-1]]). Of Books closes the loop by naming the confession of ignorance one of the surest testimonies of judgment ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch10-books]]). The “doctoral ignorance” that follows knowledge is a product of learning, not a lack of it ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch50-55-democritus-heraclitus]]). Self-study teaches the same lesson: its depth yields ignorance and modesty, and a hatred of “wrangling arrogance” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-2]]).

So the demolition and the trust are not in tension. Scepticism is what judgment produces when it is exercised well. The pedant who stores learning without judging it ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch24-pedantry]]) and the dogmatist who claims certainty are the same failure seen from two sides. Each takes something received as settled instead of weighing it.

## Where judgment acts: doubt as a civic remedy

Of Cripples shows what this practice does in public. Men ask “How is such a thing done?” before “Is such a thing done?”. Rumour grows as each teller patches the story. The world's abuses come from fear of confessing ignorance. Montaigne therefore wants modest phrases, children taught to inquire, and courts able to declare “The court understands nothing of the matter”. Having examined accused witches himself, he judged they needed hellebore, not hemlock: conjecture is priced too high when it burns a person ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch11-cripples]]). Here suspended judgment is a practical, humane verdict, not paralysis.

## Where the fit is imperfect

The notes keep several frictions visible:

- The Apology refuses a cult of stupor: removing the sense of evil would “annihilate man himself” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-5]]).
- It also says few souls can be trusted with their own judgment and most should stay “under pupilage”, yet Montaigne keeps inquiring ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-11]]).
- That We Taste Nothing Pure says a certain bluntness of mind suits business better than over-subtle inquiry ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch18-20-giving-lie]]).
- Of Presumption confesses an irresolution he calls a defect for public affairs, and calls reason “a two-edged and dangerous sword” even in Socrates' hands ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch17-presumption-3]]).
- In religion, judgment finally gives way to faith, which the Apology presents as grace received, not judgment exercised ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-15]]).

The unified reading accounts for the main line, not for every sentence.

## World Machines resonances, hedged

The notes link the confession of ignorance to [[socratic-thinking]], with the hedge that in Cripples Montaigne frames it through judges, witnesses and the Areopagites, not dialogue. Of Experience's refusal to sort people “into certain species and chapters” rhymes with the project's concern with [[Legibility]]. Montaigne is not hostile to classification as such; he is sceptical that it can be done ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-2]]).

## Contradiction as method, and the measure of the possible

Four further notes sharpen the distinction between reason as criterion and judgment as exercise. The Apology's middle slices do not condemn the philosophers for contradicting themselves. They read Plato's many voices and the inconsistencies of Seneca and Plutarch as a deliberate method, and compare the beauty of parliamentary judgments, which lies in the dispute of contrary arguments rather than in the conclusion. Montaigne's own response to the clash of doctrines is to be instructed and humbled by it ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-7]]). What the Apology removes is the claim of a theory to be what is: philosophy is “sophisticated poetry”, and Plato's own standard is only the most likely ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-9]]). Weighing the most likely is exactly the exercise the education and conference essays train.

The Defence of Seneca and Plutarch shows that exercise calibrating itself. We must not judge the possible by what seems credible to us. Montaigne gauges great souls by finding some seeds of their elevation in himself, and calls it folly to judge at one view things that have so many aspects ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch32-defence-seneca-plutarch]]). I.47 shows the limit of judgment in practice. For every commander's decision an equally plausible opposite case exists, and our deliberations themselves share in fortune ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch47-uncertainty-judgment]]). The trust in judgment survives because it never claims to settle the outcome; see [[outcomes-are-not-verdicts-montaigne]].
