Several short essays in Montaigne's *Essays* ([[montaigne-complete-essays]]) each refuse one kind of backward reading: from success to God's favour, from a fulfilled forecast to the forecaster's skill, from a victory to the general's wisdom, from promotion to merit. Each reading note treats its own essay. Read together, they show one refusal at work in four settings, and they show what Montaigne puts in its place: since the result belongs to fortune, the act must be judged by its intention and its occasion, and honour should be staked only on what lies in one's own hands.

## Four refusals

1. **Providence.** I.31 objects to propping faith on the prosperity of enterprises. Those who boasted of Rochelabeille as a sign of God's favour then explained Moncontour and Jarnac as fatherly scourges, and so “blow hot and cold”. For a Christian it is enough to believe that all things come from God. Confidence grows where no one can check: “nothing is so firmly believed, as what we least know” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch31-35-man-soberly-judge]]).
2. **Prophecy.** Almanac-makers hit a truth among countless misses, and nobody records the misses. In the Diogenes reply the drowned are missing from the votive paintings, and obscure prophecy lets posterity read it as it likes ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch11-15-prognostications]]).
3. **Counsel.** I.47 is one long demonstration that every verdict on a commander has a mirror. Pursue or spare the beaten enemy, stand or charge: a persuasive case exists either way, and had Caesar lost, Pompey's stillness at Pharsalia would have been praised. Montaigne concludes that our counsels and reasonings themselves share in fortune's uncertainty ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch47-uncertainty-judgment]]). I.23 sets two acts of clemency side by side, the Rouen prince's and Augustus's, which ended in opposite fortunes. Montaigne declines to credit the one or blame the other, since no one knows whether the prince, had he acted otherwise, would have escaped his end ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch23-various-events-from-counsel]]).
4. **Merit.** Offices are given more by fortune than by merit, since princes choose by conjecture, and events are a poor testimony of a man's worth ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch08-art-conference-2]]). Reputation too is fortune's work ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch16-glory]]). Of his own prudent actions, Montaigne gives fortune two-thirds ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch12-physiognomy-3]]).

I.33's catalogue of coincidences, in which Fortune seems to have more judgement than we, shows the danger of the backward reading: outcomes can look intended when they are not ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch31-35-man-soberly-judge]]).

## The connection: judgment moves from result to act

None of these essays states the consequence for conduct in general. Set side by side, they do. If the outcome certifies nothing, then what remains to be judged is the choice at its moment and the part of the action that was one's own:

- **Choose the honourable course.** Because the shortest road cannot be known, I.23 recommends the way of greatest honesty and justice; a man who fails while doing the noble thing keeps the glory of his intention ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch23-various-events-from-counsel]]).
- **Judge by occasion, not result.** Of Repentance judges a past decision by its circumstances: “I do not consider what it is now, but what it was then” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch02-repentance]]).
- **Let conscience judge.** Of Glory makes conscience the seat of virtue precisely because fame follows fortune ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch16-glory]]).
- **Stake honour on what you command.** A mounted man hazards his valour on his horse's fear or fury, so Montaigne prefers weapons “of which we are able to give the best account” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch48-49-war-horses-destriers]]).
- **Govern the self.** In his own voice: “Not being able to govern events, I govern myself” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch17-presumption-2]]).

The same reasoning carries into his politics of trust. Pre-emptive punishment of conspiracy has profited few, so I.23 recommends open confidence over suspicion ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch23-various-events-from-counsel]]). In Of Physiognomy, his open face and frankness disarm two bands of armed men, though he refuses to credit his own prudence for it ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch12-physiognomy-3]]).

## Hedges

- **Not plain rationalism.** Montaigne reports his own Socrates-like inklings that served him well ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch11-15-prognostications]]), and allows that divine goodness may have used him as a vain instrument ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch12-physiognomy-3]]).
- **Prudence survives.** He still reasons about army size and Vercingetorix's error from examples ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch34-observation-means-carry-war]]). The I.23 ethic is one of acting well under ignorance of outcomes, not a rejection of prudence, and trust works only if the face is clear of fear ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch23-various-events-from-counsel]]).
- **Both ways.** The I.33 note observes that the scepticism cuts both ways: events look like justice, yet he refuses to read them as divine verdicts ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch31-35-man-soberly-judge]]).

## World Machines resonances, hedged

The notes on I.11-15 and I.47 read these essays against the project's wariness of confident forecasting ([[world-machines]]). Prediction appears as a social force, since the Marquis of Saluzzo is reported to have changed sides out of fear of prophecies. Montaigne, however, is sceptical of the whole enterprise where the project is not, and he offers no machinery behind fortune ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch11-15-prognostications]], [[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch47-uncertainty-judgment]]).
