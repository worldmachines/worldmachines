The usual summary of death in Montaigne's *Essays* ([[montaigne-complete-essays]]) is a reversal: the Stoic rehearsal of death in Book I gives way to acceptance of the body in Of Experience. The stations are set out in [[learning-to-die-montaigne]]. Read together, the notes support a more exact claim. Book I already holds three separate strands, and Book III keeps two of them. What it drops is premeditation, and the work premeditation was meant to do does not disappear but passes to the body.

## Three strands in I.19

1. **Premeditation.** Make death familiar by constant imagination, and so disarm its novelty: “The premeditation of death is the premeditation of liberty” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch19-study-philosophy-learn-die-1]]).
2. **Nature's order.** In a ventriloquized speech, Nature argues that death belongs to the order of the universe, that dying is spread through the whole of life, and that a short life and a long one are equal ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch19-study-philosophy-learn-die-2]]).
3. **Ceremony as the mask.** The terror comes from the deathbed's “terrible ceremonies”, the weeping, tapers and physicians. Beneath the visor is “the very same death” a servant died without fear, and peasants are more assured than people of quality ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch19-study-philosophy-learn-die-2]]).

The same slice also carries a seed of what follows. Montaigne already reports, hedged with “I hope”, that his maladies frightened him more at a distance than when he had them, and that Nature loosens our attachment as illness takes hold.

## Strand one fails a test of experience

- **Death has no apprenticeship.** Use Makes Perfect concedes that we “are all apprentices when we come to it”. Only death's approaches can be practised, and imagination “enhances near one-half” of what it fears ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch06-use-makes-perfect]]).
- **Pain is not opinion.** The senses judge pain, so it is not mere opinion ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch40-relish-good-evil-depends-1]]).
- **The sage is frail.** “The most regular and most perfect soul in the world has but too much to do to keep itself upright”, and heroic Stoic defiance of pain reads as a courage “broken loose from its place” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch02-drunkenness]]).
- **Composure is ceremony.** The rule of a composed face under pain is itself “ceremonial”, since philosophy should form us “to be, not to seem” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch37-resemblance-children-fathers-1]]). Here strand three is turned against the Stoicism of strand one.
- **Death is faced sideways.** Almost no one looks death in the face; only Socrates does ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch04-diversion]]).
- **The verdict.** Of Physiognomy states it outright: preparation torments more than the thing; “we trouble life by the care of death”; death is the end of life, not its aim ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch12-physiognomy-2]]).

## Strands two and three survive and grow

Nature's order returns as Montaigne's own view, no longer ventriloquized. Nature “will, at the time, fully and sufficiently instruct you” in how to die ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch12-physiognomy-2]]). Diseases have their own lives and limits, and one should let nature “take her own way” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-3]]). Death “mixes and confounds itself throughout with life”, and a natural, gradual death is a mercy ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-4]]). The ceremony strand returns in the plague: death there is short and “without ceremony” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch12-physiognomy-2]]). It returns again in his wish for a private death without theatre, untroubled by friends' mourning ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch09-vanity-4]]). The peasants whom Book I offered as evidence against ceremony become, in Book III, the main witnesses against philosophy.

## The connection: habituation changes hands

Premeditation in I.19 was a technique of habituation: “often turning and returning” the thought until its sting wore off ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch19-study-philosophy-learn-die-1]]). Book III does not give up habituation. It moves it from the imagining mind to the body, to nature and to custom:

- **The stone.** It does what the meditations were for. It is a gentle memento mori with which “thou shakest hands with it once a month”, and a school of fortitude. Montaigne admits that the consolation is a self-administered rocking to sleep, not proof ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-3]]).
- **Decay.** Ageing habituates without any exercise: a tooth falls without pain, and losses come so gently that “I shall be stark blind before I shall be sensible of the age and decay of my sight” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-4]]).
- **Custom.** Among the plague-stricken peasants, a whole province reached by usage a courage equal to “the most studied and premeditated resolution” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch12-physiognomy-2]]).

In Book I the mind rehearses death in imagination so that the body will not fear it. In Book III the body and ordinary usage do the rehearsing, and the mind's job is to manage imagination so that it does not add fear. That is why the last pages can turn from death to the full, attentive enjoyment of being. Body and soul are a marriage, not a divorce. Those who try to rise above the human “transform themselves into beasts” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-5]]).

## Hedges

- **No neat progression.** Book I already doubts itself, and Book III does not settle into a doctrine. Of Physiognomy leaves open whether the peasants' steadiness is mere stolidity, in which case we should “teach nothing but ignorance” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch12-physiognomy-2]]).
- **Irony.** The consolation to the imagination is offered with irony about whether it persuades ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-3]]).
- **Diversion is palliative.** Montaigne calls diversion palliation, not cure ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch04-diversion]]).
- **Self-report.** All of this is Montaigne's self-report about one body. The notes stress that he does not claim universal validity for his case.
- **Piety.** The closing enjoyment of life is framed as gratitude to God, not libertinism ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-5]]).

The claim here is about how the strands are arranged, not a story of conversion.

## Age as a rehearsal the body performs

Several notes on age, not used above, bear on the hand-over. In Upon Some Verses of Virgil Montaigne reports that “'tis now my body's turn to guide my mind”. His body now preaches to him, and when it has the stone the mind seems to have it too; he faults the philosophers for leaving health out of the causes of the soul's states ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch05-some-verses-virgil-1]]). The essay's last slice adds that nothing in us is purely corporeal or purely spiritual ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch05-some-verses-virgil-5]]). All Things Have Their Season shows age doing the work of detachment. His longest design reaches a year, he takes leave of every place, and he counts it “the only comfort I find in my old age” that age mortifies his cares for the world, for knowledge, for health and for himself. A man “may always study, but he must not always go to school”, and late study should be whatever helps him go out better ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch27-28-cowardice-mother-cruelty]]). Two qualifications follow. First, that leave-taking is still a deliberate disposition of the mind, so the mind has not simply handed its task to the body. Second, attention to decline is not only late. The Book I essay Of Age already argues that death by old age is rare, that past forty one has outlived the ordinary term, and that his understanding and constitution have declined since thirty, a decay dangerous because it is painless and obscure ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch56-57-prayers]]). Read with the rest of this page, the change is less in noticing decay than in what is done with it: Book I uses it to argue for putting a life to use early, while Book III lets the body's decline teach the detachment that premeditation was meant to supply.
