# Horse Power as a Pre-Fossil Energy Regime (Chaffetz)

## Horse Power: The Pre-Fossil Strategic Energy Regime (Chaffetz)

David Chaffetz's *Raiders, Rulers, and Traders* (2024), ingested as [[chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders]], supplies something the Medieval section of [[machines-as-energy-regimes]] only gestures at. The horse appears there once, as a farm-power augmentation alongside the heavy plow and crop rotation. Chaffetz's case is that across Eurasia — as opposed to western Europe, where he argues the horse mattered comparatively little — the horse was not an augmentation of the agricultural energy base but a **separate strategic energy regime with its own geopolitics**, running from roughly 1000 BCE to 1919 CE, and that it was displaced by petroleum in a transition the book narrates in detail.

**The conversion chain, and its ceiling.** The regime converts grass into muscle at a fixed ratio and cannot be scaled by decree — which makes pasture, not treasure, the binding constraint on imperial reach. [[chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch08-genghis-khan-empire]] is the clearest case: "Pasture control lay at the heart of Mongol statecraft," with envoys clearing pasture ahead of armies and generals splitting forces along separate routes to avoid overgrazing. The empire's outer boundary is set by carrying capacity — Hülegü stopped at the gates of Syria because Syria "lacked sufficient grass and water for the Mongols' one hundred thousand horses." An imperial frontier drawn by forage arithmetic rather than by an opposing army is a rare and clean instance of that page's thesis.

**The forage-return argument.** The steppe's centuries-long military advantage is given a straightforwardly energetic explanation in [[chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch11-the-great-game]]. Moorcroft's observation that Kabul's winter forage — clover, alfalfa, threshed chaff — was effectively free is glossed by Chaffetz as "the secret to the superiority of the Central Asian cavalry over the Indian cavalry." Cheap input energy, not martial culture. The mirror case is [[chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch04-heavenly-horses]]: China's chronic horse deficit is traced to mineral-poor soil that leached the selenium and calcium needed for height, the difficulty of procuring alfalfa at scale, and stall-feeding that produced animals without endurance. A polity's cavalry is a function of the free energy its ground yields.

**Maximum power, visible.** The Tang establishment in [[chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch06-equine-mania]] is the regime at full throughput: seven hundred thousand horses requiring ninety thousand remounts annually, fifty-eight stud farms, Turkish grooms recruited on a massive scale (more than a million Turks are thought to have immigrated into China as grooms or cavalrymen) — a scale the book compares to WWII naval shipbuilding and the Space Race. And [[chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch09-riding-the-whirlwind]] shows the Odum logic biting: by Timur's era cavalry had "lost its low-cost status," and the throughput had to be *fed continuously or lost*, so the army had to keep conquering to keep eating. Timur's troops were paid in *ulufat*, named from the word for alfalfa; Aurangzeb's Deccan campaigns are described as a "perpetual-motion machine," and Nader Shah sacked Delhi for the Peacock Throne and the Koh-i-Nur and still could not cover his own remount bill, going bankrupt and then being assassinated. An energy regime whose maintenance cost outran any polity's ordinary revenue and therefore compelled permanent extraction. Tipu Sultan's Mysore in [[chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch11-the-great-game]] is the same structure in miniature: subsidizing warhorse breeding on land with no natural grassland cost more per horse per year than most families earned, which structurally required Mysore to fight wars for indemnities simply to break even.

**The handover, dated.** [[chaffetz-raiders-rulers-traders-ch12-last-horse-powers]] narrates the transition as a genuine regime change compressed into two decades, and it is unusually well-marked. Churchill's 1912 switch of the navy to oil is named as the quiet prior cause. In 1914 the logistics of moving Russia's mobilized million horses by rail take so long that the "Russian Steam Roller" arrives too late to matter — a rail network built for the new regime failing to serve the old one. The 1919 Third Anglo-Afghan War is decided not by Skinner's Horse riding through the Khyber but by RAF bombing of Jalalabad and the palace at Kabul; T. E. Lawrence, having won the war's one great cavalry action at Megiddo in 1918, immediately recommends the RAF replace ground troops entirely. In 1941 the same regiment trades horses for tanks in Burma.

The book's closing image is the one worth keeping here, because it is literally that page's thesis: "geologists found the new black gold underneath the old steppe lands." The fossil regime's fuel was extracted from beneath the pasture that powered the regime it replaced. Genghis Khan, Timur and Nader Shah become, in Chaffetz's line, "as remote as the Paleozoic forests."

**What this adds to the argument.** Two things. First, a pre-fossil energy regime with a *hard, mappable ceiling* — useful against that page's Tainter section, since the horse regime shows a complexity-and-throughput spiral (bigger armies, longer campaigns, fodder trains, artillery) hitting a carrying-capacity wall that no administrative innovation could move, which is a cleaner test case than the fossil transition where the ceiling keeps receding. Second, a reminder that energy regimes are geographic before they are technological: this one belonged to a specific 3.1-million-square-mile grassland ([[the-eurasian-steppe]]), and its politics were the politics of who could reach that grass.
