# Deviant entrepreneurs and the hollowed-out state

The editors of [[gilman-deviant-globalization]] make one claim about the state. Deviant entrepreneurs become political actors through money, violence and private provision of services. Their success turns state weakness into a positive feedback loop. Most of them want autonomous zones, not the state, because ruling would oblige them to serve “ungrateful and low-profit clients”. So [[deviant-globalization]] thrives in weak, well-connected states rather than failed ones ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-conclusion-1]], [[gilman-deviant-globalization-conclusion-2]]).

Several chapter authors, writing independently and mostly earlier, describe this relationship. They do not agree. This page lays their positions along a spectrum, keeps each with its author, and draws one connection none of them makes. The arrangement is a reader's.

## A spectrum of relationships

**1. The state as cover (Ellis).** For Ellis, illegal business locates where rule of law is weakest; institutions and governability explain the West African trade better than prices. “A pliable sovereign state is the ideal cover for a drug trafficker.” He roots this in 1980s “shadow states”, in which rulers draw authority from controlling markets, layered on older indirect-rule practices that let unofficial networks operate out of officials' sight ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-ch07-ellis-west-africa-drugs-2]]). He ties the 1980s expansion partly to structural adjustment, with his own hedge that Nigerian marijuana exports came first ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-ch07-ellis-west-africa-drugs-1]]). The trafficker here needs the state to go on existing, as a sovereign shell.

**2. The state as joint-venture partner (Junger; also Flynn, Guevara and co-authors, Black).** Junger describes Niger Delta bunkering as running on rented soldiers. It is a “brutal functionality” in which officials and militants all earn more than in a transparent economy, and in which illegality lets the government recast environmental grievances as crime ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-ch12-junger-blood-oil-2]]). Flynn's Philippine bars run on a legal euphemism the state has no financial incentive to end ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-ch02-flynn-sex-trade-1]]). Guevara and co-authors report politicians and soccer-club barons in Paraguay's cigarette business ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-ch06-guevara-paraguay-tobacco]]). In Black's Somalia, the forces fighting the insurgency are among its armourers ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-ch15-black-arms-gulf-of-aden]]).

**3. Autonomous zones (the editors; Sullivan's working definition).** The editors' middle position is in the Conclusion. Sullivan's definition of [[criminal-insurgency-sullivan]] is close to it: the criminal insurgent's “sole political motive is to gain autonomous economic control over territory” by hollowing out the state ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-ch16-sullivan-criminal-insurgencies]]).

**4. Replacement (Sullivan's third phase; Robb's proto-states; MEND as Junger reports it).** Sullivan's “criminal state successor”, which he says has “yet to emerge” but may be forming in Mexico, would rule enclaves like warlords. Robb's guerrilla entrepreneurs are pushed by survival either into transnational crime or into proto-states such as Hamas, which runs schools, food distribution and health care ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-ch17-robb-brave-new-war]]). Junger reports that MEND broke the bunkering equilibrium, and that its stated aim is control of the delta's oil rather than ransom ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-ch12-junger-blood-oil-2]]).

## The connection

The editors' claim that most deviant entrepreneurs avoid taking over the state is in tension with Sullivan's forecast of a criminal successor. Neither side supplies evidence that settles it: Sullivan's third phase is a projection, and the editors' claim is an argument about incentives. Robb's chapter offers a mechanism that bears on the dispute, though he does not apply it to criminal actors. In his account, Hamas's 2006 electoral win was likely to fail because it inherited the very weaknesses that had made the state vulnerable.

Put beside the editors' “ungrateful and low-profit clients”, this gives a reason why positions 1 to 3 may be stable and position 4 unstable. Whoever captures a hollowed state inherits the hollowing. Ellis's pliable sovereign and Junger's functional equilibrium are arrangements for avoiding that inheritance: the state stays in place to absorb the costs while others take the rents. On this reading Junger's MEND, which disrupted the equilibrium by renewing a political claim, is the exception that shows what the equilibrium was for.

This remains a hedged juxtaposition. Robb is writing about an insurgent movement, not a cartel. The editors themselves say no stable equilibrium is likely, since legitimacy erodes and the state may implode, as they say happened in Colombia and Zaire/Congo ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-conclusion-1]]). Sullivan's warning that ungoverned zones attract extremists also points toward instability. The contradiction between the editors and Sullivan is left standing.

## Where the authors disagree on valuation

The editors read this as development that is real but ultra-libertarian and corrosive of public goods. Sullivan and Robb read it as a security threat. Ellis, an area historian, says the drug trade is already perverting politics and leaves open how new the turn to predation is. Junger shows that the arrangement works for those inside it while villagers outside the lit Shell compound stay in the dark ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-ch12-junger-blood-oil-1]]).

## World Machines resonances (hedged)

The Conclusion notes read the collapse of the postcolonial state-development consensus as rhyming with a late [[modernity-machine]], and the proliferation of non-state actors as close to the [[divergence-machine]]. The note on Sullivan raises a [[gramsci-gap]]-style interregnum. All of these authors are journalists, area specialists or security analysts writing about decades, not machine cycles, and none uses that vocabulary. The resonance is the reader's.
