# Converging World Crises (Bayly)

**Kind**: synthesis across the notes on [[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world]]. Each note describes one crisis, or the interval between two. This page lays them side by side, names the loop they share, and draws the consequence for the many notes that reach for [[gramsci-gap]].

## The series

- **1720–1820, the world crisis.** Rising war costs collided with revenue that could not be raised without revolt. This trap caught the agrarian Asian empires and the European monarchies from opposite directions: Aurangzeb's Deccan wars on one side, France's debt-financed American war on the other. The Seven Years War fused regional crises into one, and the feedback ran both ways, down to Napoleon's remark that Waterloo was lost in India ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch03-world-crisis-anatomy]]). Behind the trap lay industrious revolutions whose localized conflicts spun out of control, forcing states into a defensive “elephantiasis” ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-intro-thematic-problems-of-history]]). A post-1720 millenarian convergence had primed it ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch01-fringes-and-archaic-globalization]]). A “culture of opposition” decided how far each state's legitimacy collapsed ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch03-climacteric-sapping-legitimacy]]).
- **1815–48, the interval.** This was a period of “flux and hiatus” and “unstable pluralism” ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch04-wreck-of-nations-and-trade]]). Restoration regimes stitched together hybrid legitimacy. Their armies, lawyers and secret police grew, but not enough ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch04-losers-legitimacy-and-state]]).
- **1848–65, the wars of legitimacy.** Russian pressure pushed the Ottomans and Qajar Iran into “new bureaucracies, more transparent taxation systems, modern armies”, and these reforms themselves “raised issues of legitimate rule”. Direct chains ran from a dispute over the holy places of Palestine through the Crimean War to the 1857 Mutiny ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch04-asian-and-european-revolutions]]). Bayly treats 1848–65 as an extension of the 1780–1820 “wreck of nations”: the same ingredients, uneven growth and crises of legitimacy, with a different outcome ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch04-civil-war-and-convergence]]).
- **1890–1914, the great acceleration.** The Qing and Ottoman monarchies fell within a few years of each other ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch13-predicting-crisis-and-new-imperialism]]). A “second military revolution” pushed the cost of arms and manpower ahead of what states could fund, producing elephantiasis “both after 1780 and again before 1914” ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch13-liberalism-crisis-and-motors-of-change]]).

## The connection

1. **One loop, run three times.** No single note lays the three crises out as one mechanism. Put the chapter 3 trap, the chapter 4 finding that defensive modernization unsettles sacral legitimacy, and the chapter 13 “second military revolution” together, and the same loop appears. War costs outrun revenue. The state reforms in order to extract and count. The reforms unsettle the religious or dynastic ground the state stood on. Finally, locally available discourses of opposition decide whether the result is rupture, containment or managed reform.
2. **Each turn ratchets the state.** The revolutionary age “armed the state more than it armed the people” ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch03-ideological-origins-third-revolution]]). The American Civil War left a more powerful, centralized government. After 1848–65 Western states were consolidated at home and the West/non-West differential was at its widest between 1860 and 1900 ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch04-civil-war-and-convergence]]). War and plunder built capacity that was “historically unprecedented” ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch07c-state-resources-and-obligations]]), and “war was the origin of nationalisms” ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch06c-new-imperialism-and-internationalism]]). By 1914 the state could mobilize, count, equip and feed some fifty million men ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch13-uniformity-complexity-and-the-road-to-1914]]).
3. **A shared correction to the Gramsci-gap readings.** At least eight notes flag [[gramsci-gap]], each with its own hedge. One says the timescale is years or decades, not millennia. One says the “morbid symptoms” are racial exclusion rather than hegemonic crisis. One says restoration regimes were stitching together a working hybrid rather than sitting in limbo. One says the gap is closed by war. Taken together, the hedges point one way: Bayly's interregna are short, they repeat, and the first two close by *reinforcing state capacity*. The chapter 4 note says it outright: these crises “resolve toward stronger states rather than toward genuine non-legitimacy” ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch04-civil-war-and-convergence]]). Where an old order is not replaced, as with the Russian and Chinese monarchies, it is external war rather than a new legitimacy that ends it ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch11-european-elites-and-conclusion]]). This suggests reading Bayly's crises as ratchet steps *within* a machine's construction rather than as gaps *between* machines. That is a reading of the notes, offered as a question for the frame, not Bayly's claim.

## What this reading must hold

- **No law.** Bayly insists on contingency. The Ottomans, Qajars and Romanovs might have bought off enough subjects to survive, and 1914 came from the rapidity of change and “a series of massive miscalculations” ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch13-liberalism-crisis-and-motors-of-change]]).
- **Not Europe first.** Sikh, Wahhabi and Chinese sectarian crises of legitimacy arose from the 1710s to the 1760s, before or alongside 1776 and 1789 ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch03-world-crisis-anatomy]]).
- **The third crisis breaks the pattern.** The war that closed it did not resolve the dynamic but radicalized it into harder ideologies of class, race and nation-state ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch13-uniformity-complexity-and-the-road-to-1914]]).
- **The notes disagree about the interval.** One note reads post-1815 Europe as an interregnum ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch04-wreck-of-nations-and-trade]]). Another reads it as active, working hybridization ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch04-losers-legitimacy-and-state]]). Both readings are kept.

## 1848 read close up (Clark, Revolutionary Spring)

Christopher Clark's [[clark-revolutionary-spring]] is a Europe-centred, close-grained account of the opening of Bayly's second crisis. It bears on the loop above in three ways.

**The closure matches.** Clark's 'hiatus, not transition' lasts days and weeks ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch05-establish-a-government]]). It ends not in a vacuum but in a post-revolutionary state that one historian he quotes calls 'not restorative at all, but innovative, on a broad front', with bigger and better-organized police, growth policy, state-financed railways and telegraphs, and new income taxes that drew legitimacy from new suffrage ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch09-constellations-circulation]]). The Conclusion's synthesis digested 'just enough of the revolution' to proof states against further upheaval ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-conclusion]]). This independently supports the reading that these crises close by *reinforcing state capacity*, and the notes on Clark carry the same hedge: the gap is short and state-strengthening, not an interregnum between machines. See [[post-revolutionary-synthesis-clark]].

**The trigger differs.** Bayly's loop starts from war costs outrunning revenue. Clark's 1848 has fiscal pressure in it: Prussia's 1820 debt law meant the king needed an assembly to finance railways, 'no assembly, no railway' ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch03-rock-of-order-cracks-avalanche]]), and the Vienna settlement fused domestic constitutions with the international order ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch08-geopolitics]]). But the immediate shock was the compound subsistence, industrial and credit crisis of 1845–7 ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch01-precarity-and-crisis]]). Clark contrasts 1848 explicitly with the war-driven revolutions of the 1770s–1815: no revolutionary power projected ideology by arms, and the wars of 1848–49 were counter-revolutionary police actions ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch09-global-1848]]). Here war closes the crisis rather than opening it.

**The synchrony is looser.** Clark's cascade is cognate rather than causal, synchronized by news across nodes with their own accumulated instability ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch04-nouvelles-diverses-february]]). Beyond Europe it is reception rather than diffusion, with 'no central nervous system' and no demonstrable link to the Taiping Rebellion ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch09-global-1848]]). Britain kept calm at home partly by 'franchising out' contention to the imperial periphery ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch04-dogs-that-didnt-bark-end-of-beginning]]), a mechanism that fits Bayly's global frame. Clark does trace the geopolitics of 1848–49 toward the Crimean War, through Anglo-Russian friction and Russia's turn to a distinct path ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch08-geopolitics]], [[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch09-censorship-to-public-relations]]). The pairing with Bayly's global synchrony is the note-taker's; Clark 'stays with narrative and analogy rather than mechanism' ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-introduction]]).
