# Spinoza, Leibniz, and the Paradigm Transition Problem: Stewart's Record

Extends [[spinoza-leibniz-and-the-paradigm-transition-problem]] with material from Matthew Stewart's *The Courtier and the Heretic* ([[stewart-courtier-and-heretic]]). The parent page sets Rao's reading of Leibniz as the first great modernist error against Lohse's reading of the monadology as a pragmatic shitty castle; the sections below test both against Stewart's documentary record.

## Stewart's Documentary Record: What the Paper Actually Shows

Stewart's double biography supplies the documentary record beneath the higher-level dispute in [[spinoza-leibniz-and-the-paradigm-transition-problem]] — and the record is more specific than either Rao's or Lohse's reading needs it to be.

**The lapse is on paper, in Leibniz's own hand.** Through the winter, spring and autumn of 1676, Leibniz's private Paris notebooks fill with fragments of a projected masterwork whose title — *The Elements of a Secret Philosophy of the Whole of Things, Geometrically Demonstrated* — is nearly the title he would have given the *Ethics* he had not yet read. He writes "the reason for God is God" and "an infinite whole is one," restating *causa sui* and infinite-attribute Substance almost verbatim, then recoils within paragraphs: "God is not something metaphysical, imaginary, incapable of thought, will, or action… Rather, God is a certain substance, a person, a mind." The cycle repeats in April. Becalmed off Sheerness and drifting by canal toward The Hague, he drafts *That a Most Perfect Being Exists*, starts from his own principle of sufficient reason, calls the intermediate steps "easily demonstrated" and "manifest," and arrives at one substance of which all things are modes — the opening propositions of the *Ethics* in miniature, misattributed to Plato's *Parmenides*. Then the manuscript stops mid-word: *Essentia est pr…* ([[stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch11-approaching-spinoza]]).

**The reversal is dated, and it is nearly instantaneous.** A note of December 12, 1676 — written on arrival in Hanover or in the carriage away from The Hague — already concludes that "a God of the kind in which the pious believe" cannot coexist with Spinoza's doctrine. Contemporary marginalia on Spinoza's letters to Oldenburg go further: "Therefore moral philosophy is destroyed." Everything after 1676 is elaboration of a verdict reached within days of the meeting, not the slow ripening of a position ([[stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch13-surviving-spinoza]]).

**And the fortification does not hold, on its own terms.** Run Leibniz's system against Spinoza's on God, Mind and Salvation and each defence fails inward: whoever built the pyramid of possible worlds decides whether there is one world (Spinozism) or a God subject to a prior nature (atheism); a God who is not a monad makes monads modes of one substance, a God who is a monad is just another determined thing; pre-established harmony was already being called a plagiarism of Spinoza's parallelism by 1712; and Leibniz's own ethics gives each monad "a conatus of sorts" ([[stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch16-return-of-the-repressed]]).

This matters for the tension the parent page holds. Lohse's charitable reading — the monadology as a moat its builder knew would not hold — gets direct documentary support here: not merely Russell's inference that Leibniz "took care to be illogical," but Leibniz's own 1714 concession that Spinoza "would be right, if there were no Monads." Rao's reading gets support too: the December 12 note shows a mind closing the question fast rather than sitting in the dread. What neither reading anticipates is the *speed* — the castle was not designed after long deliberation about what the patrons would tolerate. It was thrown up almost the moment the alternative became visible.

## A Third Reading: Multiplicity Rather Than Error or Calculation

Rao reads Leibniz as making an error; Lohse reads him as making a calculation. Both readings assume there is a stable Leibniz underneath — a real belief either mistaken or concealed. Stewart's biography argues there was not, and this is a genuinely third position that the parent page's two-column table cannot hold.

The documented behaviour is not one mask over one face. In 1671–72 Leibniz denounces the *Tractatus* as "intolerably impudent" to Thomasius, as "the terrifying work on the liberty of philosophizing" to Arnauld, urges Spitzel to savage it — and simultaneously writes to "Mr. Spinoza, celebrated doctor and profound philosopher" under an optics pretext routed through an intermediary lawyer so no direct trail exists. Each correspondent receives a different, mutually incompatible account of how well he knows "the Jew Spinoza." Later he tips Spinoza off that his own patron Huet is preparing a public refutation ([[stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch07-many-faces-of-leibniz]], [[stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch11-approaching-spinoza]]).

Stewart explicitly rejects both the prosecutorial reading (Russell's self-serving careerist) and the apologetic one (the misunderstood public-spirited visionary), because motives cannot be sorted into a stable hierarchy of real versus cover when each layer of unmasking reveals only another layer. His formulation is that the problem was "not that he did not always tell the truth, but that he was... constitutionally... incapable of telling the truth" — multiplicity, not duplicity. Lewis White Beck's line does the work: Leibniz was "all things to all men" and therefore "no one thing to everybody."

The irony Stewart draws from this is exactly the one the parent page needs. The man who founded metaphysics on the monad — indivisible, permanent, windowless, immune to outside influence — was himself "incomparably fragmented, multiplicitous, exposed to the influence of others, and impossible to pin down." Read this way the monadology is neither error nor moat but *compensation*: a wholeness secured at the level of metaphysics because it could not be located in the self. The later biography supports the reading — Leibniz's vanity, greed and compulsive status-seeking look less like character flaws than like the labour of a self that did not actually trust its own permanence, and had to fight continuously to keep an identity intact ([[stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch17-leibniz-end]]).

If that is right, the parent page's structural question (what intellectual honesty requires during paradigm transitions) gets harder rather than easier. A shitty castle can in principle be confessed. A constitutional multiplicity cannot, because there is no single occupant to do the confessing.

## Pricing Spinoza's Path: What the Alternative Actually Cost

The parent page asks whether Spinoza's path is always available, or whether the shitty castle is sometimes the only option. Stewart's record prices the alternative, and the price is not metaphorical.

Adriaen Koerbagh, whose Spinoza-influenced tracts against clerical deception appeared in 1668–69, received what amounted to a death sentence by imprisonment. Frans van den Enden — the bookseller, Latinist and radical democrat who first opened Descartes and the republic of letters to the young Spinoza — was tried in 1674, in an investigation Louis XIV himself oversaw, and hanged, denied even a nobleman's ax as a foreigner and commoner. In 1672 the de Witt brothers were lynched and dismembered by a mob; Spinoza's impulse to post a placard reading *ultimi barbarorum* near the site was, by Leibniz's own secondhand account, restrained only by his landlord locking the door ([[stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch06-hero-of-the-people]], [[stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch08-friends-of-friends]]).

Nor was Amsterdam's tolerance a blanket permission. The parent page correctly notes Spinoza would have been killed most other places — but Stewart's account adds that Dutch toleration rested on "an enlightened understanding of commercial self-interest" rather than principle, and that it had firm limits once crossed: a *cherem* "among the harshest ever issued by his community," a knife attack on the young Spinoza, and a 1665 Voorburg petition that denounced him by name as "a man who mocks all religions" ([[stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch02-bento]]).

Spinoza's own accommodations are therefore instructive, because he made them. The *Tractatus* appeared anonymously under a false Hamburg imprint. He withdrew the Dutch translation, and in 1675 he postponed publishing the *Ethics*, which never appeared in his lifetime. His signet ring bore a thorny rose and the motto *Caute*. He refused to let Tschirnhaus show his manuscripts to Leibniz ("I think it imprudent to entrust my writings to him so hastily"), asking in effect whether Leibniz was a spy ([[stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch09-leibniz-in-love]]). The *Ethics* reached print only through a clandestine operation — Rieuwertsz, Jelles, Schuller and unnamed collaborators transcribing in back rooms along the canals while a Vatican-organised dragnet of priests, ministers and a rabbi hunted the presses, the publishers surviving by lying convincingly to investigators ([[stewart-courtier-and-heretic-ch13-surviving-spinoza]]).

So the contrast the parent page draws is not castle-builder versus open dissenter. It is between two ways of managing the same danger: Leibniz concealed the content and kept the position, while Spinoza kept the content and concealed the channel — anonymity, delay, posthumous publication, and a defended circle of transmission of the kind the framework calls a [[The Thicket]]. Both are [[capture-resistance]] under pressure. The difference is what each was unwilling to give up.
