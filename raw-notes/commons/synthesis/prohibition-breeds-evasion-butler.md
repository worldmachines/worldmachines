# Prohibition breeds evasion: how Erewhon lives with its absolute rules

Erewhon makes three absolute prohibitions: on illness, on killing animals for food, and on machinery. Each reading note describes one of them. This page connects them, because they share one dynamic. **An absolute rule does not produce the behaviour it demands. It produces concealment, legal fictions and compromise**, and Erewhon has institutions whose purpose is to teach people to live that way. The machine ban, the part of the book that matters most to World Machines, is not an exception to this pattern but an instance of it.

## Three prohibitions, one pattern

**Illness.** Because ill-health is punished, Erewhonians become skilled at hiding it. Polite fictions ("to have the socks") cover it ([[butler-erewhon-ch10-current-opinions]]). Faces are painted and families keep secret medicine-chests, until it is "impossible to say whether any one was well or ill" without long acquaintance ([[butler-erewhon-ch14-mahaina]]). Mahaina claims dipsomania, a treatable vice, because that is safer than admitting a weak body. The malcontents diagnose the cause: concealment is produced by scouting the ill, not by physic ([[butler-erewhon-ch12-malcontents]]). Childbirth is hidden as a shameful illness, and the legal fiction declares a dead infant "over seventy-five years old", all held together by "this blessed inconsistency" ([[butler-erewhon-ch13-views-concerning-death]]).

**Meat.** A prophet's ban on killing animals, backed by an "unseen power", is tightened by his disciples (eggs, milk) and answered by the populace with fictions: animals that "commit suicide", die "in self-defence" or are "warranted still-born", and inspectors' certificates that document fraud rather than change behaviour ([[butler-erewhon-ch26-rights-of-animals]]). The narrator's verdict is that the real damage was "the jar which undue prohibition gave to the consciences" of the many. Students keep up a pretence of abstinence for one another, and one young man, ordered beefsteak by his doctor, kills himself. The ban falls only when a botany professor, suspected of being a secret meat-eater, pushes it to an absurdity (plants are cousins too, so only windfalls may be eaten) and an oracle's "Be killed or kill" lets the country repeal it ([[butler-erewhon-ch27-rights-of-vegetables]]).

**Machines.** The professor of the [[book-of-the-machines]] wanted three hundred years of invention destroyed and wished for five hundred. The number was haggled over and settled at 271 years after "a wrangle over a mangle" ([[butler-erewhon-ch25-the-machines-concluded]]). Total eradication proved impossible, so the subject survived as antiquarian study. The narrator learns that the Professors of Inconsistency and Evasion are why the purge was never "carried to its legitimate conclusions" ([[butler-erewhon-ch22-colleges-of-unreason-2]]). Broken engines are kept and labelled in a museum ([[butler-erewhon-ch07-first-impressions]]). In the end a balloon, which needs "no complicated machinery" and is built by a foreman who "threw himself heart and soul into the matter", carries the narrator out of the country that banned machines ([[butler-erewhon-ch28-escape]]).

## The institutions that make evasion a skill

What turns these three stories into one is that Erewhon teaches evasion. The Colleges of Unreason hold professorships of Inconsistency and Evasion, where students learn to defend any inconsistency and find a pretext against any clear injunction. They are taught that "extremes are alone logical, but they are always absurd", and the argument's working function is to protect the double currency from reason ([[butler-erewhon-ch21-colleges-of-unreason-1]]). The Professor of Unreason defends the coerced birth formula as an education in compromise, "but they never tell this to the boy" ([[butler-erewhon-ch18-birth-formulae]]). The narrator observes that Erewhonians "very often do not believe or mean things which they profess to regard as indisputable" ([[butler-erewhon-ch20-what-they-mean-by-it]]), and that where they build a system of practice on a certainty, "they seldom quite believe in it" ([[butler-erewhon-ch18-birth-formulae]]). The Musical Banks survive the same way. People tolerate jokes about them and anonymous attacks on them, but punish plain, unambiguous criticism by calling the speaker unwell ([[butler-erewhon-ch15-the-musical-banks]]). Ydgrun, the real goddess of respectability, tolerates denial as long as she is "obeyed and feared", and day-to-day practice runs on "unwritten compromises (for the most part in Ydgrun's favour)" ([[butler-erewhon-ch17-ydgrun-and-the-ydgrunites]]).

Even the cure for clutter becomes a fiction. An Act makes public statues lapse after fifty years unless a jury votes to keep them, and the outcome is that subscribers pay sculptors *not* to make the statue ([[butler-erewhon-ch13-views-concerning-death]]).

## The connection

No single note puts it this way. The habit of non-literal belief that keeps the Musical Banks standing is the same habit that makes the absolute prohibitions bearable, and it is what turns each of them into a hollow form. The illness law yields a population of skilled dissemblers. The meat law yields certificates and suicidal sheep. The machine law yields a negotiated cut-off date, a museum and a loophole. In each case the rule is kept by not being meant. Butler does not say whether that is a vice or a mercy. The narrator calls Ydgrun "beneficent and useful", and his maxim at the close of the meat story, that "reason uncorrected by instinct is as bad as instinct uncorrected by reason", is his, not settled authorial doctrine ([[butler-erewhon-ch27-rights-of-vegetables]]).

## World Machines resonances (hedged)

- **Halting a technology.** For a project interested in whether a society can deliberately stop a technological line, Erewhon's lesson is double. The halt happened, through civil war and decree, but it was never clean: it was negotiated down, preserved as antiquarianism and leaked at the edges. Butler offers this as satire, and nothing in the text argues whether the ban was wise.
- **Forms that outlive their function.** Several notes compare the Musical Banks and the straighteners' theatre to the performative and ornamental stages of a [[decay-sequence]] (the ritual kept after the purpose has gone). The fit is loose. Butler's target is moral and religious hypocrisy, not infrastructure, and the machine museum is a ban's residue, not a decline.
- **Legibility and its failure.** The meat inspectors' labels and the punishment of illness are attempts to make conduct and bodies readable, and both produce opacity and fraudulent paperwork instead. This loosely echoes [[Legibility]], hedged as the notes hedge it: this is satire of rules-lawyering and respectability, not of state mapping.
- **Beliefs that persist without being believed** sit near [[zombie-ideas]], again only as a gloss.
