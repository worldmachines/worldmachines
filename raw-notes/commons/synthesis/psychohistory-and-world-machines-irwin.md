# Psychohistory and World Machines: Irwin's Ibn Khaldun

Extends [[psychohistory-and-world-machines]] with material from [[irwin-ibn-khaldun]].

The parent page's intellectual genealogy names Toynbee, Spengler, Sorokin, Ibn Khaldun and Turchin, and it rejects the deterministic Asimov version in favour of something like long-range climate forecasting. Irwin's biography of [[ibn-khaldun]] is the book in this wiki that looks most closely at two of those ancestors, and at the line connecting them to Asimov. Read across its chapters, it gives the genealogy a more cautious shape.

## The line to Foundation is real but ghostly

Irwin says psychohistory “shows the influence of the Muqaddima, as transmitted and popularized in Arnold Toynbee”: challenge and renewal, and the Mule modeled on Timur. He immediately adds that Gibbon's influence is “more obvious and more influential”, and that Gibbon was retrospective where Seldon was prospective. The influences are “ghostly”, and Ibn Khaldun himself offered no prospective mathematical science ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-future-last-days]]).

The intermediary is a weak link. Irwin sketches Toynbee's civilizations as automata run through challenge and response toward a “fixed and doomed rhythm”, a caricature he calls unkind, and records E. H. Carr's dismissal of such pessimism as “the characteristic ideology of a society in decline”. Toynbee worked from Quatremère's French, leaned on the discredited Banu Hilal narrative, and his ideas are “almost entirely discredited now” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-toynbee-rosenthal]]).

## Laws of the past, no forecast of the future

The Muqaddima's laws “provided no exact guidance to what would happen next”. Ibn Khaldun thought the future resembles the past “more than one drop of water resembles another”, saw no progress and proposed no reform, and left prediction to divination: astrology, malahim prophecy, the za’iraja ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-future-last-days]]). Irwin's last chapter qualifies this: Ibn Khaldun believed his laws would explain what would happen as well as what had happened ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch11-ending-up]]). In this reading the ancestor held a laws-of-history view without a forecasting method, and he believed history was near its end.

Two further points bear on forecasting. Irwin treats fourteenth-century futurology as political: rulers listened to diviners, and promises of foreordained victory ignited revolts, so forecasts changed what they forecast ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-future-last-days]]). And the theology Ibn Khaldun mostly endorsed treats causal regularity as God's habit, which is a poor foundation for any science of prediction ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch04-aristotle-theology-maliki-law]]).

## A family of pessimistic cycles

Irwin places the Khaldunian cycle among Polybius, Vico, Spengler, Mann's four-generation *Buddenbrooks* and others, and notes that cyclical theories “embody prophecies” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch04-philosophy-cyclical-history]]). The pessimism common to them is the thing Carr diagnosed ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-toynbee-rosenthal]]). The Ottoman readers show the alternative. They took the Khaldunian life cycle of regimes as a diagnostic while hoping decline could be arrested ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-afterlife-french-germans]]), which is closer to the parent page's posture of estimating ranges in order to influence them than to fatalism.

## Range of validity

Irwin's strongest contribution to the parent page's methodology is about scope. The dynastic cycle fits the early Arab conquests, the Almoravids and the Almohads but not the Merinids, Hafsids, ‘Abd al-Wadids or Mamluks ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch03-nomads-asabiyya]]). It must have been induced from a handful of cases ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-arab-american-novels-orientalism]]), and Ibn Khaldun applied it inconsistently. The models, built from medieval data, bear comparison with Darwin, Marx and Durkheim but “cannot apply to modern societies” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch11-ending-up]]).

This supports the parent page's claims that theory is “a compression of patterns” and that “loose ends are fine”. It adds a warning the parent page does not state: an ancestral cycle model comes with a range of validity that its admirers tend to drop. The parent page's “still cyclical-ish” method inherits both the decline bias Irwin and Carr identify and the scope limits Irwin documents. The hedges travel: Irwin is a historian of Ibn Khaldun, not a theorist of forecasting, and the WMP reading here is an extrapolation from his evidence.
