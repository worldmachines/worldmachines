# The Silk-for-Silver Trade (Mann)

Mann's account in [[mann-1493]] of the link between American silver and Chinese goods through Manila. He treats it as the economic counterpart of the [[columbian-exchange-mann]] and dates to it 'a new, distinctly modern era' ([[mann-1493-ch01-shiploads-of-silver]]).

## Supply: Potosí

The Cerro Rico strike of 1545, mercury amalgamation using Huancavelica mercury (a process known in China for centuries), and Viceroy Toledo's mita labor quota turned Potosí into an extraction system. Once amalgamation replaced Andean smelters, 'Spaniards could treat natives wholly as a source of labor'. Mann rejects the 'three to eight million' death figure as an exaggeration but calls conditions appalling ([[mann-1493-ch04-treasure-of-the-world]]). Stamped bars travelled by llama train to Arica, Lima and the convoys ([[mann-1493-ch01-reversals-of-fortune]]). The road across Panama was a fragile chokepoint for much of the crown's silver, worked by enslaved Africans who became maroons ([[mann-1493-ch09-in-the-isthmus]]).

## Demand: a China without working money

Following Richard von Glahn, Mann traces Chinese money from bronze coin through the Song huizi and Yuan inflation to the Ming. Repeated coin bans and each emperor's voiding of the last one's coins left the empire with 'no functioning currency'. Merchants used privately certified silver ingots, and the state then took over 90 percent of its revenue in silver by the 1570s. China had almost no silver mines, so it needed foreign metal. Buying Japanese silver from wokou to pay taxes that funded campaigns against the wokou meant that 'the Ming government was at war with its own money supply' ([[mann-1493-ch04-out-of-money]]). The ban on private sea trade, meant to monopolize trade's profits, bred smuggling and piracy on the Fujian coast and was rescinded in 1567 ([[mann-1493-ch04-extra-effort-merchants-pirates]]). As for why Fujian, Mann's appendix answers that its trading families descended from Zaytun, the medieval port of a 'first pass at globalization' ([[mann-1493-appendix-b-globalization-in-beta]]).

## The link

Legazpi and Urdaneta's expedition of 1564 found a return route across the Pacific. After a botched encounter at Mindoro in 1570, junks arrived in 1572 willing to sell silk and porcelain for silver ([[mann-1493-ch01-shiploads-of-silver]]). Twenty-plus junks a year carried petty, debt-financed merchants to Manila's Parián. Spain's cartels, quotas and taxes were evaded by smuggling on both sides, and between a third and a half of American silver went to China, with Mann's caveat that exact figures cannot be calculated ([[mann-1493-ch04-wooden-noses]]). Manila's Spaniards, dependent on the Chinese and afraid of them, ran a loop of restriction, massacre and repopulation ([[mann-1493-ch04-magic-mountain]]). The galleons also carried fifty to a hundred thousand Asians to New Spain, on Edward Slack's estimate ([[mann-1493-ch08-shook-up-city]]).

## Effects

- **In Europe.** Flynn and Giráldez's more than 150,000 tons of silver produced a monetary explosion and a falling silver price, while Spain's unindexed silver taxes lost real value. Mann's chain runs through Spanish defaults to revolts and the seventeenth-century 'general crisis', with his hedge that silver was 'not the sole cause' but 'an essential part' ([[mann-1493-ch01-reversals-of-fortune]], [[mann-1493-ch04-treasure-of-the-world]]). Flynn and Giráldez call the Europe-centred story 'a kind of sideshow', since 'most of the silver actually went to Asia' ([[mann-1493-ch04-treasure-of-the-world]]).
- **In China.** Silver funded a commercial boom but also inflation and a tax system fixed in silver weight. When silver's price fell to the world level around 1640, real revenue collapsed. Atwell's claim that this helped end the Ming is, in Mann's words, 'vigorously debated'. Flynn stresses that China gave up a significant share of its GDP to obtain the metal ([[mann-1493-ch04-magic-mountain]]). The Qing coastal evacuation later froze the money supply ([[mann-1493-ch05-hidden-passengers]]). American crops arrived along the same routes, with ecological fallout that Mann traces through the Qing highlands to the Loess Plateau, whose erosion he calls 'the accidental legacy of the global silver trade' ([[mann-1493-ch05-malthusian-interlude-mountains]], [[mann-1493-ch05-unlearning-from-dazhai]]).

## Two disputes Mann leaves open

- **Who drove it.** Official statistics put China's share of silver at no more than a quarter; a newer group of historians argues for as much as half. The stakes are whether European expansion is the prime mover or the earth is 'a single economic unit largely driven by Chinese demand' ([[mann-1493-ch01-reversals-of-fortune]]). Mann quotes Andre Gunder Frank on Europeans buying 'a seat' on the Asian train ([[mann-1493-ch01-shiploads-of-silver]]).
- **What trade is for.** Following Findlay and O'Rourke, trade is both a win-win market mechanism and a tool of statecraft in which 'both sides usually do not win'. Manila and the Ming were caught between the two roles ([[mann-1493-ch04-magic-mountain]]).

## World Machines resonances, with their hedges

- **[[braudel-world-economy]].** The notes place the silver network near Braudel's world-economy, but note that Mann stresses causal linkage between distant places rather than core-periphery structure, and narrates a hinge event rather than theorizing world-economies ([[mann-1493-ch01-reversals-of-fortune]], [[mann-1493-ch01-shiploads-of-silver]]).
- **[[legibility-as-machine-core]].** Stamped, serial-numbered bars and assayers' certification form a small legibility apparatus whose limits show in systematic Basque evasion ([[mann-1493-ch04-treasure-of-the-world]]). Spain's failed Manila cartel is the state's counting failing ([[mann-1493-ch04-wooden-noses]]). Mann does not use this vocabulary.
- **[[weaponized-interdependence]].** China's money supply depending on foreigners rhymes faintly, though 'nobody here is deliberately weaponizing anything' ([[mann-1493-ch01-shiploads-of-silver]]).
- **[[modernity-machine]].** Mann's 1571-72 date is a journalist's marker, not a claim about the machine's 1200-1600 construction window ([[mann-1493-ch01-shiploads-of-silver]]).
