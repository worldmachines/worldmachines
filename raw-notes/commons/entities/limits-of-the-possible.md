# The Limits of the Possible (Braudel)

**Source**: [[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1]], whose subtitle is this phrase.

## Definition

Braudel's organizing device for *The Structures of Everyday Life* is a "border which in every age ... separates the possible from the impossible, what can be done with a little effort from what cannot be done at all." Between the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries it was set by food supply, population pressure, low labour productivity and slow control over nature, and it "hardly changed at all." Braudel gives overland transport as his example: it did not become "commonplace, regular, well-developed" until about 1830, just before the railway ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-preface]]). The Introduction ties the phrase to the trilogy's plan: Volume I's job is to weigh material life and the ceiling it lived under ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-introduction]]).

The concept is a **ceiling on material life**, not a thesis about ideas or institutions. Almost every chapter measures it in a different unit.

## The ceiling, measured

- **Births and deaths.** Before the modern era population moved in "ebb and flow," crossing "critical thresholds" and then being corrected by the "extremely crude" mechanism of epidemic and famine ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch01-guessing-world-population]]). Under the **biological ancien régime** birth and death rates both sat near 40 per 1000, so every catastrophe was answered by a wave of marriages and births. The note calls this the limits of the possible "made mechanical: not a metaphor but an arithmetic of compensation" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch01-eighteenth-century-biological-watershed]]).
- **Grain.** Wheat yielded about 5:1 (4:1 net of seed) for three centuries, and population, prices and even the possibility of towns were bounded by that ratio ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-wheat-cultivation-and-yields]]). Converted into the hours of labour needed to buy a quintal, grain crossed Braudel's "always serious" 100-hour line around 1540–50 and did not fall back below it until 1880–90 ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-wheat-trade-price-and-bread]]).
- **Land.** Population pressing on fixed land forced a choice between arable and pasture, so meat retreated from European tables for three centuries after 1550 ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-luxury-and-meat]]). The Far East's vegetarianism "certainly does not spring from idealism," because a rice paddy gives about five times wheat's calories per hectare ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-rice]]).
- **Energy.** Braudel ranks Europe's power sources: animal traction about 10 million horse-power, wood 4–5 million, water-wheels 1.5–3 million, then human muscle, then sail. He concludes that "lack of energy was the major handicap of ancien regime economies," and specifically energy that could be transported, since machinery "existed and was constantly being perfected" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch05-wind-water-wood-coal]]). The gap between a furnace producing 100–150 tons a year and a modern blast furnace "divides two civilizations" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch05-iron-a-poor-relation]]). Brandy's spread was capped by primitive stills until particular inventions raised the ceiling ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-drinks-wine-beer-alcohol]]).
- **Distance and logistics.** Pre-industrial scale was capped less by population than by what could be moved, fed and organized. An army of 10,000 was "a far greater force than 50,000 or 100,000 men would do today" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch01-scale-of-reference]]). Transport changed so little that "Napoleon moved no faster than Julius Caesar," and travellers were bound to fixed itineraries of inns, caravanserai and anchorages ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch06-transport-and-history-of-technology]]).
- **Imagination.** Mercier, picturing the year 2440, "made the mistake of assuming that this world of the future would not change its scale" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch08-big-cities-peking-and-london]]).

## Not purely natural

The border is technical and cultural as well as physical. The New World's fisheries went largely unfished by the societies living beside them while European fleets crossed an ocean to Newfoundland. Technology and custom, not raw abundance, set the ceiling ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-table-manners-and-everyday-foods]]). Following Boserup and Gourou, tool change is "the result and no longer the cause" of shrinking fallows, and the binding constraints are "technical parameters," meaning inherited habits and available land ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-the-rest-of-the-world]]). Geography sets "long-lasting limitations" on housing but "does not explain everything" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch04-houses-throughout-the-world]]). The notes point out that in these two units Braudel does not use the phrase itself. The reading is the note-writer's.

## How the ceiling moves

Braudel explicitly refuses determinism. "I am not suggesting that the path of history ... has been entirely dominated by numbers" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch01-many-against-the-few]]), and "technology is only an instrument and man does not always know how to use it" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch05-wind-water-wood-coal]]). His model has two kinds of limit. Sometimes technology is an *achievable possible* that people are not yet ready to use, like the scythe, confined to hay for centuries. Sometimes it is a hard ceiling. Breakthroughs come only when economic, social and psychological pressure cracks it, and rising labour costs, not invention, trigger the substitution of machines for men ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch05-human-and-animal-energy]]). Technology then "gradually reaches new forms of equilibrium on higher levels than in the past, only to remain there for a long time": "first the accelerator, then the brake." Braudel read the 1970s oil shock as another encounter with the "ceiling of the possible" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch06-transport-and-history-of-technology]]).

The volume dates the loosening. Births begin to outpace deaths durably in the eighteenth century, unevenly and reversibly, with French crises still in 1772–3 and 1779–83 ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch01-eighteenth-century-biological-watershed]]). European fashion becomes perpetual around 1700, a hinge the note reads as the ceiling "visibly" loosening ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch04-costume-and-fashion]]). Transport and communications changed only with the railway, the steamship and the intercontinental cable of 1875 ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch06-transport-and-history-of-technology]]).

## World Machines resonances

- The ceiling is the kind of deep infrastructure that [[world-machines]] points at, but the notes keep a distinction. Braudel's material life is defined "by resisting change, not by construction and decline," so it is a ceiling that holds, not a machine running a Dawn–Day–Dusk cycle ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-preface]]). The 100-hour grain line rhymes with the frame's logic of a hard structural ceiling, "not the scale of the claim" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-wheat-trade-price-and-bread]]).
- The biological ancien régime's compensation arithmetic is the substrate that [[secular-cycles]] models. The note on it marks that page as supported ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch01-eighteenth-century-biological-watershed]]).
- The energy budget belongs next to [[machines-as-energy-regimes]] and [[vaclav-smil]]: "civilizations of wood and charcoal, as those of the nineteenth were civilizations of coal" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch05-wind-water-wood-coal]]).
- The "accelerator, then brake" rhythm rhymes with the Dawn-to-Dusk cycle only loosely. Braudel's unit is a technology or infrastructure regime, and his trigger is scarcity, not exhausted legitimacy ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch06-transport-and-history-of-technology]]).

## Volume II: the ceiling carried upstairs

[[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2]] mostly works on the market economy and capitalism, not on material life. But many of its reading notes find ceilings of the same shape in economic, social and institutional registers. Braudel rarely uses the phrase there, so each application below is the note-writer's reading. The notes mark most of them as partial.

- **Savings.** Kuznets finds that no society seems to save above roughly 20% of production, and that pre-modern capital formation was mostly consumed by maintenance and replacement until "the industrial revolution was above all a transformation of fixed capital." The note calls this "a genuine, if economic rather than material, instance" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch03-capital-capitalist-capitalism]]).
- **Technique follows factor cost.** Skilled Chinese and Indian workers used "only their hands and two or three tools" where Europeans "would use over a hundred." Braudel reads this as a rational response to cheap labour, not backwardness, and cites the *Encyclopédie*'s converse: "wherever labour is dear, it must be supplemented by machines." The note calls this a restatement of the limits of the possible "applied to manufacturing" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch03-fourfold-classification-pre-industry]]).
- **The life of an industry.** Industries rise and fall in a "parabola." Old-regime cycles ran short because production kept meeting a ceiling in "raw materials, labour, credit, technology, energy, or the home or foreign market" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch03-industrial-profits-overland-transport]]).
- **Energy, again.** Deforestation priced wood and coal out of Europe's blast furnaces, and merchant capital withdrew from mining at home. This is a ceiling of "the pre-industrial energy regime rather than a failure of capitalist organization" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch03-mining-manufactories-finance]]).
- **Organization without technology.** At Brie, capitalist tenant-farming coexisted with technology in which "nothing changed ... until the nineteenth century" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch03-heart-of-europe-capitalist-agriculture]]).
- **Capital that society will not use.** Capital regularly exceeded productive demand, because "every society has its own ways of saving and spending, its own prejudices and motivations and its own restraints on investment." The note calls this "close kin" to the concept, with a ceiling that is "social and institutional" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch04-credit-banking-and-money]]). Limited liability and negotiable shares existed for centuries before they could displace the small patriarchal firm ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch04-individual-firms-and-joint-stock]]).
- **The state's reach.** In taxing a kingdom's currency reserves "it was simply not possible to go beyond certain limits" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch05-all-pervasive-state-borrowing-castile]]). France had some 12,000 royal officials for 15–20 million people, a quantifiable ceiling on state capacity. The note adds that the concept "targets demographic and physical ceilings on material life rather than bureaucratic reach specifically" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch05-state-culture-incomplete-entity-islamic-model]]).
- **Employment.** The economy never generated full employment and left a permanent sub-proletariat. The note says this rhymes "loosely," since here the constraint is a shortfall of opportunity rather than a biological or demographic limit ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch05-below-subsistence-climbing-out-of-hell]]).
- **How thinly power spreads.** Power and wealth concentrate in an elite often under 5% of the population in nearly every documented society. Braudel offers this as an observed constant, not a mechanism ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch05-opening-hierarchies-vertical-elevation]]).
- **Slow frames and slow minds.** The seigniorial regime survived a millennium as "a slow-changing frame that channels what any given actor ... could actually attempt" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch03-land-money-seigniorial-regime]]). On usury, "mentalities had not changed as quickly as economic practice" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch05-christendom-usury-puritanism]]). Pedlars' survival through every ban and crisis shows an informal structure outlasting formal orders ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch01-shops-and-pedlars]]). The self-sufficient rural economy "beneath the market" gestures back down to the original ground-floor ceiling ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-ch01-market-statistics-labour-market-beneath-market]]).
- **A threshold that opens rather than caps.** In the Conclusion the market economy arises "of its own accord" once "a critical threshold had been reached." The note observes that the direction is inverted: "there the limits cap growth from above, here a threshold unlocks it from below" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-conclusion]]).

## A tension this extension creates

This page defines the concept as "a ceiling on material life, not a thesis about ideas or institutions." The Volume II notes stretch it into savings, credit habits, legal forms, state capacity and elite size. Both readings are kept here. The extension is useful because the same shape recurs: a long plateau under a limit that holds regardless of will. But it is the note-writers' move, not Braudel's. When Braudel himself talks about limits in Volume II, he promises to "reveal the limits of capitalism" by situating it sector by sector. The foreword note calls that "a narrower, sectoral sense of limit" than the Volume I concept, "though both are Braudel hunting for a system's boundary" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v2-foreword]]).
