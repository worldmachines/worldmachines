# Johann Wolfgang von Goethe (1749–1832)

## Biographical Context

- Poet, novelist, scientist, statesman
- Dabbled in gardening (vegetable and botanical gardens at his Weimar residence)
- Key texts for [[Kulturkritik]]: *Elective Affinities* (1809), *Faust I & II*, "On Dilettantism" (*SW18*), the poem "Der Spaziergang" (echoing Schiller's similar poem)
- Correspondence with his sister Cornelia (1767): "Don't laugh about this seemingly foolish philosophy, the seemingly paradoxical pronouncements, they are the most splendid truths"

## Goethe's Relationship to Rousseau

Goethe engages Rousseau's culture-critical paradox through **Wiederholte Spiegelungen** ("repeated mirrorings") — a *Steigerung* (heightening, intensification) rather than a dialectical synthesis. His rhetorical mode creates "a pulsating rather than a synthetic process of sublation."

Unlike Rousseau and Schiller, Goethe does not believe in the utopian potential of the English garden. For him it is *dystopia*, not utopia.

## Rejection of the English Garden

Goethe's objections to the English garden are twofold and fundamental:

1. **Subjective/aesthetic**: It "foregoes objectivity for the sake of subjectivity" — it "perpetuates the current bad habit of wanting to be unrestrained and lawless, and of fantasizing capriciously in aesthetic matters for it can, unlike the other arts, neither be corrected nor disciplined" (*SW18*, pp. 760–62)

2. **Ontological**: It "destroys both art and nature. It makes do with semblance because it turns reality into fantasia," and "diminishes the sublime of nature and nullifies it by simulating it" (*SW18*, p. 760)

Goethe calls its mischievous design "naturspäßig" — as if nature were playing a practical joke. For Hegel (sharing Goethe's view), its picturesque variety precludes "eine in sich seiende Seele" — peace of mind.

## *Elective Affinities* as Laboratory

*Elective Affinities* (1809) is Goethe's test of the English garden ideal. Its main English garden project culminates in the deaths of all parties:
- Nature dies: Ottilie, Eduard, their child Otto
- Culture dies: Charlotte and the Captain (their "Entsagung"/renunciation)

"Sentimentalizing the garden meets with scorn; an attempt at turning three fish-breeding ponds back into a mountain lake is doomed" (*EA*, p. 135). The English garden, whether as cultivation (Schiller's remedy) or naturalization (Rousseau's remedy), results in death.

Goethe's diagnosis: "You cannot walk beneath palm trees with impunity" (*EA*, p. 212). "Free" becomes associated with "libidinal abandon and renunciation"; "wholesome" with "pathological insatiability and anorexia."

## Faust and the *Cura* Myth

Faust's English garden vision (the famous "Tarry a while" moment of damnation, lines 1699–1701) is spoken "in anticipation" and is the product of magic — an illusion. But Goethe introduces a deeper figure: **Sorge** (Care/Cura), drawn from Hyginus's *Fabulae*, where Cura shapes homo from humus — the original gardener and cultivator. When Faust rejects Care, she blinds him, forcing reliance on his "radiant light ... in my inner being" (line 11,500). The blindness reveals that genuine care — a life of concern for something other than oneself — is what a gardener requires. Faust becomes an English gardener only in blindness.

## *Kunstwahrheit* vs. *Naturwirklichkeit*

Goethe's aesthetic model is Claude Lorrain, whose landscape paintings have "the highest truth, but no trace of actuality." This distinction — *Kunstwahrheit* (artistic truth) vs. *Naturwirklichkeit* (natural verisimilitude) — is central:

Lorrain's landscapes "appear very natural to us, but we vainly seek in the actual world" their entirety — "it is the fruit of his own mind; ... of the aspiration of a fructifying divine breath." The ideal is available only in art, not in the English garden's simulation of nature.

Goethe: in Lorrain "nature proclaims herself to be eternal" (*SW22*, p. 534). "Semblance" becomes "symbol," and *wahrhaft* (authentic) is analogous to *Wahrheit* (truth).

## The *Steigerung* Principle

For Goethe, nature and culture are "interlocked in a dynamic upward movement of irreducible polar opposites" — they never blend. They are not synthesized but *intensified* through dynamic relationship. This is why Faust ends not with resolution but with "hinan" (upward/onward) — the play is "quite incommensurable" (Goethe to Eckermann). The final word is directional, not conclusive.

The English garden's error is precisely that it pretends to be a synthesis. Goethe sees this as aesthetically and metaphysically dishonest.

## Dichtung und Wahrheit: canny indiscretion (Gay)

In [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v4]], Peter Gay makes Goethe's autobiography the counter-model to [[jean-jacques-rousseau]]'s *Confessions*. Goethe made "discretion—or, perhaps better, a canny indiscretion—his governing principle," consulting eyewitnesses and documents to correct his memory. The title's ambiguity ("Dichtung" as fiction as much as poetry) announces that selection and embellishment will govern the book. Gay judges neither candid nor evasive an adequate description ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-ch2-between-probes-and-poses]]). Goethe called his works "fragments of a great confession," yet Freud, looking at a Goethe edition with Hanns Sachs, judged that they were "used by him as a means of self-concealment," the pairing that opens Gay's revelation/concealment method ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-ch2-in-rousseaus-shadow]]). The model had costs downstream. Goethe "smoothed down the harsher edges of his early years," and Friedrich Hebbel, instructing himself to imitate him and "drop the rest," falsified his own childhood far more ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-ch2-second-thoughts]]).

## A romantic who distrusted self-focus

Gay's Goethe stands on both sides of the century's argument about introspection. *Werther* is a station in the long genealogy of inwardness, yet Goethe is among those who warned that self-dissection curdles into "morbid subjectivity" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-introduction]]). He defined "the romantic as sick and the classical as healthy" and nonetheless "wrote like a romantic" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-ch1-imagination-unleashed]]). His string of infatuations into his seventies was far less "bourgeois" than the ideology of love he helped bequeath to the bourgeoisie ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-ch1-bourgeois-egotistical-sublime]]). He described his heated, broken-off letters as a continuation of the letter-writing that produced *Werther* ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-ch6-spirit-of-the-letter-1]]).

## Spinoza, Nature, and the refusal of the instrument

Gay's chapter on romantic heart-religion has Goethe, after Lessing, rereading [[baruch-spinoza]] as "the thinker drunk with God" ("Existence is God"), a line running on to Schelling's [[Naturphilosophie]]. As a working scientist, Goethe amassed specimens and ran optics experiments against Newton yet "detested the contrivances of modern science: the telescope, the microscope, the mathematical formula," as instruments that interfere with "the unmediated, pious experience of God-Nature." The note reads this as a rejection of the instrument that [[legibility-as-machine-core]] would extend. Where the modernity machine's tools are built to extend legibility, Goethe rejects them for breaking the communion they claim to serve ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-ch1-heart-religion]]).

## Legislating silence, and other appearances

As director of the Weimar court theater, Goethe issued written edicts in 1803 against audience "irregularities," Gay's clearest instance of a named actor legislating the new decorum of silent listening into existence ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-art-of-listening-1]]). He also appears as a marker of the novel's rehabilitation, a figure dignified enough to write novels without embarrassment ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-ch4-toward-the-terrible-core]]), and as a faithful reader of the liberal Parisian daily *Le Globe* ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v4-ch1-self-in-politics]]).

## Wulf's Goethe: centre of the Jena Set (Magnificent Rebels)

Andrea Wulf's [[wulf-magnificent-rebels]] puts Goethe at the centre of the Jena Set as its 'stern and benevolent godfather' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-1]]). The notes give the Jena-years material the sections above lack.

**Stalled and wary, 1794.** Wulf's Goethe is the 'Zeus of Germany's literary circles', a wealthy privy councillor in tiny Saxe-Weimar whose output had stalled and who had turned his back on his *Werther* phase ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch01-happy-event-1]]). He had avoided Schiller for years; Wieland's 'greatest egoist I've ever met' is Wieland's opinion ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch01-happy-event-1]]). The Terror, the war and the bombardment of Mainz drove him into science as a 'plank in a shipwreck'. Wulf argues his politics mirrored his geology: a Neptunist favouring slow sedimentation, he preferred slow reform to uprisings ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch01-happy-event-2]]). His unmarried household with Christiane Vulpius scandalised Weimar, in Wulf's reading chiefly because of her low standing ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch01-happy-event-2]]).

**The Schiller partnership.** The friendship began in July 1794 when Schiller told him his primordial plant was 'an idea', not an observation; Goethe later said the competition between object and subject sealed their bond ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch01-happy-event-2]]). They composed the *Xenien* together, mixing lines so no one could tell who wrote what ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch04-intellectual-friction-2]]).

**Patron of Fichte, partner of Humboldt.** Goethe recommended Fichte for the Jena chair ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch01-happy-event-1]]), quipped that Kantian theory was like fashionable factory products ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-1]]), joked 'Dear non-Ich', and linked free will to his colour theory, a connection that is Wulf's interpretation ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-2]]). He helped reverse the ban on Fichte's Sunday lectures ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch03-finest-minds-2]]) but, in the atheism dispute, said he would have voted against his own son for using such language to a government ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch12-atheism-dispute]]). In spring 1797 he and Alexander von Humboldt electrocuted frogs and compared anatomy; Goethe credited Humboldt with having 'forced us' into the natural sciences, and Wulf argues the influence ran both ways ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch07-little-academy-1]], [[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch07-little-academy-2]]). He moved from 'rigid realist' toward a middle position between Fichte's idealism and observation, and invited Schelling as a possible bridge ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch10-symphilosophy]]).

**Neutral hub.** Through the *Horen* quarrel he was the one figure who stayed neutral, shuttling between factions until exhausted ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch07-little-academy-2]]). He openly rooted for the Schlegels' *Athenaeum* ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch09-sublime-impertinence-2]]) and kept a fragile peace by concealing what each side did, remarking that the friends 'chew the cud of their Ich's' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch14-schlegel-clique]]). Wulf's explicit line is that he 'genuinely liked' the Schlegels but 'also used them' for verse help and translation ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-2]]). Asked to referee Novalis's *Europe* after the Fichte affair, he advised against publication, distinguishing what may be thought privately from what may be published ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch15-new-confederation]]). He had refused Caroline help in 1793, which she never knew ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch06-splendid-circle-1]]); later, at her request, he drew the grieving Schelling out ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-1]]) and steered the Schlegel divorce through Weimar's consortium ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-2]]).

**Faust and the fever.** In 1801 his delirium was dominated by Schelling's Naturphilosophie; Wulf reads the pact scene and 'In the beginning was the Act' as Idealist allusions ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-2]]).

**The end of Jena.** He mourned the 'current exodus' and failed to finish Schiller's *Demetrius* after his friend's death ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch19-current-exodus]]). In October 1806 he looked at Jena as 'the crucible of so many inspiring ideas' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch20-french-in-town-1]]); after soldiers burst into his bedroom and Christiane drove them out, he married her five days after the battle, saying such times require honouring conventions ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch20-french-in-town-2]]). Wulf presents him as a realpolitiker who disliked the emotional nationalism after Jena, accepted Napoleon, and closed his life saying thousands had contributed to his works: his 'life's work is that of a collective' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-2]]; see [[symphilosophie-wulf]], [[jena-set-wulf]]).
