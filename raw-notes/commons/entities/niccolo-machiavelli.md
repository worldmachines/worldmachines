# Niccolò Machiavelli

## Biographical

Florentine statesman and political thinker (1469–1527). Known primarily for *The Prince* (1513) and *Discourses on Livy*. After his exile from Florentine politics, he lived in rural semi-exile, where he developed both his major works and the studiolo practice documented in his letters.

## The Letter to Vettori (1513)

The passage cited in [[Jan 10, 2025 - AI Agents, Machiavelli's Study]] comes from Machiavelli's famous letter to Francesco Vettori (December 10, 1513), describing his daily routine in exile:

> "put on the garments of court and palace. Fitted out appropriately, I step inside the venerable courts of the ancients, where, solicitously received by them, I nourish myself on that food that alone is mine and for which I was born, where I am unashamed to converse with them and to question them about the motives for their actions, and they, in their humanity, answer me. And for four hours at a time I feel no boredom, I forget all my troubles, I do not dread poverty, and I am not terrified by death. I absorb myself into them completely."

This is the origin of *The Prince* — written precisely during this period of studiolo practice.

## Significance in this Wiki

Machiavelli appears not as political theorist but as the **exemplary practitioner of the [[Studiolo and Self-Cultivation]]** tradition. The letter shows:
- The physical ritual of dressing as preparation for intellectual work
- Reading as *conversation* with authors across time
- The study as escape from material anxiety (poverty, death)
- Self-cultivation through engagement with other selves

## Isaiah Berlin's Reading of Machiavelli

In "The Originality of Machiavelli" (collected in *Against the Current*, 1979), [[Isaiah Berlin]] offers a counter-intuitive interpretation: Machiavelli's real contribution was not amoral cynicism but the *discovery of two incommensurable moralities* — the civic/pagan morality of courage, greatness, and political excellence, and the Christian morality of humility, mercy, and otherworldly salvation. Neither can be reduced to the other.

This makes Machiavelli, for Berlin, the first thinker to crack open the monist assumption that there is one true morality. The implication is radical: if two genuinely valid moralities conflict irresolvably, then the entire Enlightenment project of a rational, universal moral framework is impossible. Berlin sees this as the seed of his own [[Value Pluralism]].

## Irwin: the Machiavelli–Ibn Khaldun comparison tested

Machiavelli is one of the most frequent partners in modern comparisons with [[ibn-khaldun]], and Robert Irwin's biography ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun]]) examines that pairing directly. The studiolo scene this page centres on has a counterpart in Irwin's material. Muhsin Mahdi and Patricia Crone suggested that Ibn Khaldun wrote the Muqaddima to understand his own political failure, and Irwin, who allows “there may be something in this”, compares Machiavelli and Clarendon, who wrote their histories after their careers were eclipsed ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch02-ibn-al-khatib-spain-return]]).

The comparison is common. Enan and Cheddadi draw it ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-arab-american-novels-orientalism]]), Gellner set Ibn Khaldun beside Machiavelli, Keynes and Weber ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-colonialist-mahdi-gellner]]), and even the anti-colonial translator Monteil made him Machiavelli's precursor ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-afterlife-french-germans]]). Irwin lists it among the Westernizing precursor claims he means to resist ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-preface]]).

Irwin finds the differences “more striking” than the likenesses. Machiavelli stresses fortuna, virtù, the psychology of rulers, war and rhetoric, and would keep religion out of politics. Ibn Khaldun seeks underlying laws, writes no guide to statecraft, is moralistic and religious, and has a concept, ‘asabiyya, that Machiavelli has nothing to match ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-arab-american-novels-orientalism]]). The shared trajectory of political operator turned gloomy generalizer holds up, while the shared theory does not. Berlin's claim, above, that Machiavelli separated civic from Christian morality is the sharpest point of contrast: in Irwin's reading Ibn Khaldun made no such split.

## Montaigne's Essays: a writer easy to contradict, and necessity without virtue

This section draws on the ingest of Montaigne's *Essays* in Cotton's translation as edited by Hazlitt ([[montaigne-complete-essays]]). The page lists [[michel-de-montaigne]] as a parallel figure in the studiolo tradition; the *Essays* also bear on Machiavelli's politics, once by name and once, on the reading notes' interpretation, by implication. In Of Presumption Montaigne says political debates are endless because their arguments rest only on experience and the variety of events. He cites Machiavelli as an example of a writer whose arguments are easy to contradict, and gives his own preference, “in my conceit”, for any ancient and constant government over change ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch17-presumption-3]]). In Of Profit and Honesty he grants that governments need vicious offices, and that a prince may be forced to break his word. He calls that breach a misfortune and “a lash of the divine rod”, not a virtue, and says that a prince who does it without regret has a conscience in a sorry condition. He leaves such commissions to men “more obedient and more supple” and ends by denying that utility proves an action honourable. The reading note treats this as a counterposition to Machiavelli, granting necessity and refusing to call it virtue, and records that this essay never names Machiavelli ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch01-profit-honesty]]).

## Montaigne's Essays: trust against suspicion, and the captains' books

This section draws on further notes from the ingest of Montaigne's *Essays* in Cotton's translation as edited by Hazlitt ([[montaigne-complete-essays]]). Three more chapters bear on Machiavelli, two of them only by implication, as the reading notes stress. In Various Events from the Same Counsel, Montaigne asks what a ruler threatened by conspiracy should do. He answers that anticipating plots by punishment has profited few, that advisers who preach distrust preach ruin and dishonour, and that open, fearless confidence is the better course. The note treats this as a contrast with the prudential tradition of guards, suspicion and pre-emptive punishment, and records that Machiavelli is not named ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch23-various-events-from-counsel]]). In the middle of Of Presumption, Montaigne hates the new virtue of feigning and dissimulation. He argues that a prince who breaks faith for one gain loses all future negotiation, cites Soliman's refusal to condone a breach of faith, and still allows that “A man must not always tell all, for that were folly”. Again the note reads the passage as standing in tension with a Machiavellian position that it does not name ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch17-presumption-2]]). The one direct mention is hearsay. Observing that great captains have favoured particular books, Montaigne reports that Machiavelli is said to be favoured in his own day, and himself prefers Caesar ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch34-observation-means-carry-war]]).
