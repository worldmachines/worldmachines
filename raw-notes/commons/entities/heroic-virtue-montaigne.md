Montaigne's *Essays* ([[montaigne-complete-essays]]) admire heroic virtue and doubt it in the same breath. A group of essays on Cato, virtue, constancy, and the most excellent men asks what separates real virtue from a fit of passion, a custom or plain obstinacy. They also ask what an ordinary man should do with examples he cannot follow. The answers do not settle into a doctrine.

## Admiring what one cannot do

Of Cato the Younger begins from tolerance. Montaigne says he can “more easily admit of difference than uniformity amongst us”, and approves the Capuchins without being continent himself. He sets a severe test: actions done for profit, glory, fear or custom are not virtue in the doer, since “virtue owns nothing to be hers, but what is done by herself and for herself alone”. The Spartans' refusal of the prize to Aristodemus, who fought to redeem his reputation, is his borrowed illustration. Against contemporary debunkers who find base motives behind every great deed, he calls it “the duty of good men to portray virtue as beautiful as they can”. The note marks the tension: he asks for charity toward motives while holding that motive decides virtue ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch36-37-cato-younger]]).

## A fit, not a habit

Of Virtue doubts that the ancient heroes' elevation ever became ordinary. Souls stirred by example are launched beyond their stretch by a kind of passion, then slacken until a lost bird or a broken glass moves them like anyone else. A very imperfect man may do anything, “order, moderation, and constancy excepted”. Even Pyrrho, by report, found it very hard to put off man entirely ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch29-30-virtue]]). The second essay of I.37 gives the psychology behind this. The soul passes between several passions too fast to notice, so “if we would make one continued thing of all this succession of passions, we deceive ourselves” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch36-37-cato-younger]]).

## What counterfeits virtue

Custom and obstinacy can produce what looks like heroism. Of Virtue sets the Indian widows and gymnosophists, whose fire is custom, honour and lifelong premeditation, beside local cases of rage and resolve ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch29-30-virtue]]). The Defence of Seneca and Plutarch makes the Spartan boy's endurance unremarkable once one knows that being caught stealing was the supreme disgrace. Men burn for opinions they do not understand, and “obstinacy is the sister of constancy, at least in vigour and stability” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch32-defence-seneca-plutarch]]). Valour spread by custom through the civil wars is only one little ray of virtue ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch07-recompenses-honour]]). Habit can also build real capacity. Roman soldiers bore arms like their own limbs, and Montaigne holds that only want of use makes armour intolerable ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch09-arms-parthians]]).

## Constancy as judgment

Of Constancy does not ask for insensibility. It allows flight and dodging, and Montaigne admits he starts at a sudden harquebuse. He reports the Stoic view that the first shock of fear is involuntary and that what counts is withholding consent ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch11-15-prognostications]]).

## The measure of the possible

The Defence of Seneca and Plutarch warns against making oneself the stamp of human nature: “We are not to judge what is possible and what is not, according to what is credible and incredible to our apprehension.” Montaigne sees great souls as far beyond him, yet finds some seeds of their elevation in himself, so that his judgment can follow where his wings cannot ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch32-defence-seneca-plutarch]]).

## Epaminondas

Among the three most excellent men, Montaigne puts Epaminondas above Homer and Alexander. His goodness is constant, uniform and incorruptible, while Alexander's virtue depends on fortune; conscience and manners alone show what we are. Even here Montaigne declines to imitate his hero's obstinate poverty, and names Alcibiades's as the richest life for an ordinary gentleman ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch36-most-excellent-men]]). Read beside Of Virtue, the choice is pointed. Epaminondas wins for the very constancy that Of Virtue finds almost incredible in the heroes.

## For commoner souls

Montaigne's practical advice is modest. Of Managing the Will says of the Stoic examples: “Let us not attempt these examples; we shall never come up to them.” It prefers flight from temptation and a popular stolidity of temperament ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch10-managing-will-2]]). Of Physiognomy says he has not corrected his nature by reason, like Socrates, but let himself go as he came, and he distrusts a probity that is a slave to precepts ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch12-physiognomy-3]]).

## World Machines resonances, hedged

The notes repeatedly compare the role of custom here with [[habit-and-identity]]. They add the hedges that Of Virtue asks whether virtue can become habit at all ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch29-30-virtue]]), and that in the Cato essay custom is a moral counterfeit, not an anthropological force ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch36-37-cato-younger]]).
