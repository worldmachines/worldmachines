The *Apology for Raimond Sebond* is Book II, chapter 12 of Montaigne's *Essays* ([[montaigne-complete-essays]]). It is long enough to be read here in fifteen slices. It is the book's fullest statement of scepticism, and it puts that scepticism to work for faith.

## Occasion and frame

Montaigne's unlearned father revered scholars “with so much the greater reverence and religion as he was the less able to judge of them”. He was given Sebond's *Theologia naturalis* and had his son translate it. Montaigne takes up two objections to Sebond. To the first, that faith should not rest on human reason, he grants that faith is a divine gift while defending reason as its servant. He then turns the objection into an indictment of how Christians actually believe: by habit, passion and party, “by the same title that we are Perigordians or Germans”, with each side in the civil wars drawing religion “like wax into so many contrary figures”. To the second objection, that Sebond's arguments are weak, he replies by setting out to “wrest the wretched arms of their reason out of their hands” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-1]]).

## Levelling man

“Presumption is our natural and original disease.” Man, lodged in “the lowest story of the house”, imagines the cosmos made for him. Montaigne argues from like effects to like faculties that beasts speak, reason and build at least as well as we do, and concludes in his own voice that “We are neither above nor below the rest”. The cat passage quoted on [[michel-de-montaigne]] belongs here ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-2]]). He then draws up a ledger: by our own confession we leave the beasts the real goods (peace, health, innocence) and keep the imaginary ones for ourselves. Learning did nothing for Varro or Aristotle against gout or death, and knowledge gives us illnesses in advance. He stops short of praising stupor, however. Removing the sense of evil would remove the sense of pleasure and “annihilate man himself” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-5]]).

## The map of philosophy and the Pyrrhonian way

Those who think they have found the truth (Peripatetics, Epicureans, Stoics), those who say it cannot be found (Academics) and those still seeking (Pyrrhonians) make up his map of philosophy. Montaigne reports, with sympathy, the Pyrrhonian criticism that the Academics' denial claims too much. He gives the Pyrrhonian practice at length: argue both sides, assert nothing, keep consent “ambiguous”, and in the actions of life follow nature, laws and customs. The emptied sceptic is “a blank paper prepared to receive such forms from the finger of God”. How far Montaigne himself holds this is, the notes stress, a matter of reading ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-6]]). Elsewhere he calls the Pyrrhonian position “more bold, and also somewhat more likely” than the Academics' probabilism, while still treating it as a position he weighs ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-12]]). The Pyrrhonists lack a language for their doubt, and here he ties the question “What do I know?” and its emblem of a balance to his own use ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-8]]).

## Reason turned on itself

- **Authority and presupposition.** Opinions are received “by authority and upon trust, as if it were religion and law”. Aristotle is the “god of scholastic knowledge”, every science rests on borrowed principles, and reason is “a supple utensil” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-10]]).
- **Soul and body unknown.** The philosophers' contradictory doctrines of the soul and of the seed show that man knows neither his mind nor his body: “who understands not himself in himself, in what can he?” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-11]]).
- **The unstable judge.** Reason is “an instrument of lead and of wax”, moved by sickness, weather, mood and interest. Montaigne offers himself as evidence: his judgment “floats and roams”, and the present opinion always feels certain however often the last was overthrown ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-12]]).
- **Opinion as seasonal.** Opinions “have their revolution, their season, their birth and death, like cabbage plants”. Climate shapes “the very faculties of the soul”, and every candidate natural law is disowned by some nation ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-13]]).
- **Texts and the senses.** Any text can be made to say anything. All knowledge comes through the senses, no sense can audit another, and we may lack senses we cannot know we lack, like the blind gentleman who uses the words of sight ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-14]]).
- **Regress and flux.** A fit judge would be “a judge that never was”. Instruments need demonstration and demonstration needs instruments, “and here we are round again upon the wheel”. Judge and judged are both in continual flux, and a quoted pagan concludes that only God truly is ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-15]]).

## Fideism, and its limits

The Apology's close is fideist: man can be raised above humanity only if God lends him “an extraordinary hand”, and “It belongs to our Christian faith, and not to the stoical virtue” to pretend to that change ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-15]]). Montaigne also limits his own weapon. To his addressee he calls it a “desperate thrust”, in which one quits one's own arms to make the adversary abandon his, to be used rarely. He argues that wit is “a wandering, dangerous, and temerarious utensil” that needs the bridle of laws and custom. Yet in the same slice he refuses to stop inquiring, saying that each attempt leaves the matter more supple for the next person, as bears lick their cubs into form ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-11]]). The notes leave that tension unresolved.

## Attribution

Much of the Apology is reported voice: the Stoics, Epicureans, Academics, Pyrrhonians, Plato, Plutarch and a long run of borrowed animal exempla. The notes separate these from Montaigne's own verdicts. Where his own scepticism appears, they read it as deployed on behalf of faith and caution against flattening it into secular anti-metaphysics ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-11]]).

## World Machines resonances, hedged

The closing exempla of the twelfth slice (Copernicus against the old heavens, Paracelsus against physic, the discovery of a new half of the world that overturned Ptolemy's “all is found”) loosely rhyme with the idea that an era's infrastructure of knowledge feels permanent until it is replaced. Montaigne's point, though, is epistemic humility, not a structural theory of eras ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-12]]). The picture of the cosmos as a divinely made machine bearing its architect's marks is read as a theological precursor of world-as-legible-artifact thinking ([[Legibility]]), with the hedge that Sebond's legibility is revelation, not a tool of control. The attack on pride rhymes with the later secular lineage of [[david-hume]] while differing from it in purpose ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-1]]). The suspicion of reason recalls the [[counter-enlightenment]], though in a sceptical-devotional register rather than as a political programme ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-5]]). For how this scepticism sits with the trust in judgment shown elsewhere in the book, see [[reason-demolished-judgment-trusted-montaigne]].

## The animal catalogue

Between the opening levelling of man and the ledger of real and imaginary goods, two slices pile up animal evidence. Nearly all of it is borrowed from Plutarch, Aristotle, Arrian, Apion and others, and the notes stress that Montaigne signals these sources rather than vouching for the stories. To say that beasts act only by natural instinct is, he argues, “not to take from them the dignity of knowledge and reason, but with greater force to attribute it to them than to us”. Chrysippus is made to grant that the dog at the three-way crossing reasons by elimination. The blind man's dog, which keeps its master from the ditch, is a case Montaigne says he has watched himself. Animals teach and learn, and some have faculties we lack and cannot fathom. The rule he draws applies to foreign peoples too: “All that seems strange to us, and that we do not understand, we condemn.” Most human desires, by an Epicurean sorting, are artificial, crowding out the natural ones like strangers taking over a city. The slice ends by asking whether war, our proudest action, is a prerogative or a testimony of weakness ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-3]]).

The next slice makes armies “an ant-hill disturbed and provoked” and levels princes with cobblers. It pairs every human virtue with a credulous animal exemplum, opening with the line “there is no animal in the world so treacherous as man”. The halcyon's nest is an art we can neither imitate nor understand, and dreaming dogs show that beasts also imagine. Beauty follows each people's appetite, not a natural rule, and man's upright stature is merely poetical ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-4]]).

## Doubting dogmatists and gods made in our image

A further slice argues that even the dogmatists mostly wrote doubtfully. Aristotle's obscurity is “a Pyrrhonism under a resolutive form”, and difficulty is a coin the learned issue to hide the vanity of their art. Plato's dialogues and the self-contradictions of Seneca and Plutarch are read as a deliberate display of varied fancies. Some doctrines were written for public utility, and even the boldest sects submit to the civil law in the end. Montaigne judges the least inexcusable ancient religion to be the one that acknowledges an incomprehensible power. After a long catalogue of contradictory opinions on the gods, he says that contrary opinions do not so much displease as instruct him ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-7]]).

A later slice generalises the charge. We make God in our own image: “'Tis far from honouring him who made us, to honour him that we have made.” Xenophanes' goose thinks the universe exists for geese. Astronomers fit the heavens with the axles and wheels of coachmakers, philosophy is “sophisticated poetry”, and epicycles are inventions handed over as current pay. The anatomists do no better with the body, and with Socrates Montaigne concludes that we know as little of what is at our feet as of the stars. The note reads this as an early claim that models are made, not found, which rhymes with the project's modelling modesty, though Montaigne's conclusion is sceptical and fideist, not constructive ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-9]]).
