# Voltaire

## Biographical Sketch

**Born**: François-Marie Arouet, 1694, Paris  
**Died**: 1778, Paris  

French writer, philosopher, playwright, and entrepreneur. Known for *Candide*, *Zadig*, *Letters Concerning the English Nation*, and polemical essays on religious tolerance and legal reform. Self-exiled to Ferney (French-Swiss border) from 1758.

## Role in the Modernity Machine

Per [[Ivo Velitchkov]], Voltaire was a key engineer of the [[Modernity Machine]], transforming two class relationships:

### Monarch ↔ Technocracy: Pet → Celebrity
Previous model: the intellectual as a subservient court pet. Voltaire's transformation: the intellectual as a **negotiated celebrity** — financially independent, operating outside patronage while exploiting it strategically.

Method: Lottery arbitrage (1729, ~500,000 livres with Charles Marie de la Condamine), share speculation, business investments, money lending. Financial independence meant he couldn't be silenced or starved.

He "became a creditor to the ruling class" — structurally parallel to Fugger's transformation of oligarchs.

### Epistemic Legibility (State → Public)
While the state was making subjects legible to itself, Voltaire inverted this:
- **Calas affair**: campaigned to expose secret French legal proceedings through direct requests and fictionalized factual narratives
- **Newton book**: made cutting-edge science legible to a wider public — starting a tradition that dominates nonfiction today
- **Celebrity as legibility**: If celebrity = "someone whose private life, personal opinions, and physical likeness are consumed as commodity by a broad public," Voltaire was the first European celebrity (after Erasmus and Luther in weaker versions of the concept)

## The Ferney Watchmaking Enterprise

At age 76, Voltaire founded a watchmaking social enterprise/business incubator at Ferney, serving Genevan *natifs* (artisans with limited rights in Geneva). See [[Ferney Watchmaking Enterprise]] for full detail.

**Quick facts**:
- Craftsmen: 40 (1770) → 600 (1773)
- Revenue: 450K → 600K livres/year at peak (1776)
- Markets: Spain, France, Russia, Turkey, Holland, Italy, Algeria, Tunisia
- Roles Voltaire played: manager, financier, virtual bank manager, sponsor, builder, buyer, international sales manager

## Contribution to the Divergence Machine

- ***Candide*** (1759): Ruthless parody of Leibniz's "best of all possible worlds" optimism — ridiculing prefigured moral ideology, shifting progress from theological certainty to evolving argument grounded in messy reality
- Tolerance campaigns: early steps in creating civilizational space for plurality
- "Attacked dogma with absurdity" rather than counter-dogma

## Moral Complexity (Pattern of Position Changes)

Voltaire's principled stances were often instrumentalized:
- **Turkey**: Previously supported Catherine the Great's military operations against Turkey; reversed when Turkey became a profitable market
- **Taxation**: Obtained tax exemptions for watchmakers; later proposed progressive taxation for political advantage
- **Religion**: Performed public Easter communion for social access while privately mocking religion; wrote letters to churchmen calling himself "true Catholic" to secure Académie Française membership
- **Prussia**: Congratulated Frederick the Great on a treaty that abandoned France; when scandal erupted, offered himself as a diplomatic spy; after falling from favor, forged letters to his niece to dramatize his side

## Voltaire in Douglass's Rousseau and Hobbes

Robin Douglass's study ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes]]) gives Voltaire three small but telling roles in the eighteenth-century quarrel over [[thomas-hobbes]]:

- **A dissenting voice on Hobbes.** While Malebranche, Clarke and Leibniz charged Hobbes with confusing mind and matter and so destroying freedom, Voltaire praised Hobbes for anticipating Locke on thinking matter ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-malebranche]]).
- **Luxury.** Douglass takes Melon as representative of the doux commerce theorists and cites Voltaire's praise of him as evidence of Melon's standing. This places Voltaire on the side of the luxury debate that Rousseau attacked ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-natural-goodness-golden-age]]).
- **The 1755 letter.** In his letter to Voltaire, Rousseau criticized Hobbes's religious intolerance and called him "the Sophist Hobbes". By 1762 Rousseau praised Hobbes as the only Christian author to see that political and religious authority must be united, a shift Douglass reads as a re-evaluation during the 1750s ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-methodological-problems]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-unity-civil-religion]]).

These are passing appearances. Douglass does not treat Voltaire's politics at length.

## Morley's Diderot and the Encyclopaedists (Vol. I)

John Morley, who had already written a volume on Voltaire, treats him in [[morley-diderot-v1]] as the Encyclopædia's candid ally and as Diderot's foil.

**Popularizer of Newton.** Morley rehabilitates Voltaire as a serious popularizer of Newton, whose exposition of Berkeley's theory of vision and of Cheselden's operation set the problem that Condillac and then Diderot's Letter on the Blind took up ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-1]]). Voltaire's polite dissent from that Letter, that Saunderson “denied God, because he happened to have been born blind”, is answered by Morley: the case against final causes would have come as fitly from a sighted man ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-2]]).

**Patron and ally.** When Diderot was imprisoned at Vincennes in 1749, Voltaire set Madame du Châtelet, a kinswoman of the governor, to write, and Diderot was moved from the dungeon to the château ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-3]]). He greeted the first volumes of the Encyclopædia from Potsdam as the glory of France and the shame of its persecutors, sent small contributions, and pressed the editors for method: etymologies, definitions, examples, clearness, brevity. Morley praises his good faith in complaining to the editors rather than of them ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-2]]). After D'Alembert's retirement he first urged resistance, then urged Diderot to finish the work abroad, which Diderot refused; his anecdote of the book at the king's supper, which Morley corrects in detail but accepts in substance, presents it as a compendium of the arts from the pin to the gun ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-4]]).

**Money and position.** Morley sets Diderot's encyclopædic pay against Voltaire's army-contract profits, and Voltaire's own pensions from Frederick against Diderot's dependence on Catherine ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-4]], [[morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-4]]). His article Tyrant, in Diderot's account, provoked the anger behind a provincial prosecution in which a colporteur was branded and sent to the galleys ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-2]]).

**Morley's ranking.** In the Preface Morley ranks Diderot above Voltaire and Rousseau as a thinker, with scientific method and constructive conceptions, while conceding Voltaire's superiority in literary expression ([[morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary]]). On the drama he notes that Voltaire's tragedies restored the conventional stage Diderot attacked, that Voltaire found the Greeks too colloquial, and that his “drunken savage” was a worse verdict on Shakespeare than Diderot's ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch7-the-stage-2]]).

## Volume II: Voltaire in Morley's second volume

This section draws on Volume II of Morley's study ([[morley-diderot-v2]]), where Voltaire appears as Catherine's flatterer, the System of Nature's alarmed critic and Diderot's late acquaintance.

**Catherine's celebrity-maker.** Catherine said Voltaire “made me the fashion”, and Morley shows him as a centre of European intelligence fed by her war bulletins. Asked about her husband's death, Voltaire called it one of the “family affairs with which I cannot possibly think of meddling”; Morley's reproach is that toleration on her banners silenced the philosophers on her crimes ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-1]]). In 1792 she banished his busts to the cellars ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-2]]).

**Theism for the police.** Alarmed by Holbach's System of Nature as a peril to the whole Encyclopædic brotherhood, Voltaire wrote a refutation and made sure Richelieu and the King heard of it, on the ground that society needs a God who punishes and rewards. Morley's judgment is that Voltaire never grasped the philosophic question and prized theism for its “supposed utility as an assistant to the police”, illustrated by the anecdote of locking the door against the servants ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-1]]). The political side of the book's Nature-versus-Religion antithesis “appalled men like Voltaire” ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-3]]).

**The meeting of 1778.** Voltaire and Diderot met only on Voltaire's last visit to Paris, after twenty-seven years. Voltaire said Diderot lacked “the talent of dialogue”; Diderot likened Voltaire to a ruined haunted castle inhabited by “some ancient sorcerer”; they quarrelled over Shakespeare ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch08-closing-years-2]]).

**Foil and measure.** His poem on Natural Religion was burnt with Helvétius's L'Esprit ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1]]). Morley says Helvétius's view that cruel customs rest on real or seeming utility was a doctrine Voltaire ignored ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-2]]). Voltaire found the nine volumes of Clarissa empty, which Morley reads as the incomprehension of a rebel against the Church who kept monarchic habits ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch02-romance-1]]). Candide is the foil for Jacques le Fataliste's lack of wit, though Morley also finds malice in it ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch02-romance-2]]). Diderot never shared Voltaire's fancy that China is a land of philosophers ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch01-other-dialogues-2]]), and Voltaire's light verse filled the gap between Racine and Chénier as Boucher filled the gap between Le Brun and David ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch03-art-1]]). In Rameau's Nephew, the philosopher-narrator defends Voltaire alongside Racine: cooling his vanity would extinguish his talent ([[morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-1]]).
