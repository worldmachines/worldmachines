# Thomas Hobbes

This page presents Hobbes as he appears in [[robin-douglass]]'s study [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes]]. It is Hobbes as a foil for [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] and as received in eighteenth-century France and Geneva, not a general account of his philosophy. Douglass keeps Hobbes's own views separate from "Hobbism", the French caricature (see [[hobbism-douglass]]). This page does the same.

## Texts and transmission

- Rousseau most probably knew *De cive*, likely in Sorbière's 1649 French translation, which Douglass reports was the most widely held Hobbes text in French libraries. The *De corpore politico* translation is less likely, and Rousseau "probably never read *Leviathan*" ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-methodological-problems]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-mirabeau-letter]]).
- Sorbière rendered Hobbes's self-love as *amour-propre*. This made the attribution of society's founding to amour-propre "little more than a literal reading" of the French *De cive* ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-nicole-bayle]]). Rousseau's amour de soi-même / amour-propre distinction answers that translation directly ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-natural-goodness-golden-age]]).
- The earlier French reception prized Hobbes's mechanistic and geometrical science. From the mid-1650s his reputation became political, and his name, paired with Spinoza's, became a byword for subversion ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-french-reception-opening]]).

## Hobbes's own positions, as Douglass reports them

- **Materialism.** Hobbes was a materialist who denied free will; *Leviathan* calls talk of it absurd. Against Bramhall he held that moral determination is "a mere word". Man is matter in motion, with self-preservation as the single principle of natural right ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-nature-free-will-passions]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-free-will-moral-nature]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-rousseaus-critique-reappraised]]).
- **Natural law and justice.** Justice has no place in the state of nature, but the laws of nature do operate there as dictates of right reason. Without security they bind only *in foro interno*. His French critics wrongly took this for a denial of natural law ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-barbeyrac-burlamaqui-natural-law]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-state-of-nature-to-political-society]]).
- **Not naturally evil.** Hobbes explicitly denied that man is naturally evil and held that the passions are "in themselves no Sin". Douglass notes Rousseau apparently read the very page of *De cive* where he said so ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-natural-goodness-golden-age]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-of-love-and-fear]]).
- **Against the rule of laws.** In *Leviathan* Hobbes ridiculed Aristotle's claim that laws, not men, should govern. He also advised the sovereign to govern by fixed, promulgated laws, and held him bound by the law of nature, owed to God alone ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-mirabeau-letter]]).
- **Sovereignty.** Covenants made from fear are valid; protection implies consent. Rights against the sovereign are self-defeating, because private judgements of good and evil (conscience included) breed war. The body-politic metaphor and the citizen/subject double relation are fully developed in *De cive*. Authorization is a doctrine Douglass calls unique to *Leviathan* ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-free-will-slavery-obligation]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-sovereignty-inverted]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-montesquieu-against-hobbes]]).
- **Reason and the passions.** Reason reckons consequences and cannot supply its own ends. The passions cannot be eradicated, only directed. Fear of death is the passion to be reckoned on. The last books of *Leviathan* redirect fear away from "Spirits Invisible" toward the state of nature. The law of natural equality answers pride ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-hobbes-and-fear]]).
- **Religion.** Hobbes proposed to reunite "the two heads of the eagle", civil and religious authority. Douglass reads the aim as primarily negative: removing ecclesiastical challenges that excite sedition ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-unity-civil-religion]]).
- **"Man is a wolf to man"** concerned relations between commonwealths, but the French took it to describe individuals ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-nicole-bayle]]).

## Rousseau's changing view of Hobbes

In the 1755 letter to [[voltaire]] and an early draft of the civil-religion chapter, Rousseau criticized Hobbes's intolerance and called him "the Sophist Hobbes". By 1762 he called Hobbes the only Christian author who saw both the evil and the remedy. That was an audacious endorsement at a time when the label Hobbism could condemn an author, as it did Helvétius's *De l'esprit*. Douglass dates the re-evaluation to between the *Manuscrit de Genève* and *Du contrat social* ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-methodological-problems]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-unity-civil-religion]]).

## Hobbes and Rousseau in Douglass's balance

Douglass's summary is shared problems, opposed solutions ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-oppositions-and-affinities]]):

- **Affinities.** Disordered passions as the source of politics. Reason alone cannot bind citizens. A unified political will. Absolute sovereignty. Civil religion. Douglass says Rousseau "followed and even extended" Hobbes on the unified will ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-sovereignty-and-law-opening]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-ordering-the-passions-opening]]).
- **Oppositions.** The state of nature as a state of war. Force as a ground of obligation. Alienable, representable sovereignty. Fear as the ordering passion. Rousseau answers with pity, free will, inverted sovereignty and love of fatherland ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-state-of-nature-state-of-war]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-of-love-and-fear]]). See [[sovereignty-inverted-douglass]] and [[love-and-fear-douglass]].
- **Hobbes as "lowering the bar".** Hobbes's war/peace dichotomy makes the state of nature a negative standard that can make even despotism look legitimate. Rousseau's golden age is a positive standard ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-rousseaus-critique-reappraised]]).

Douglass also notes that his Rousseau-centred reading parallels recent Hobbes scholarship that ties Hobbes's politics to his materialism ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-oppositions-and-affinities]]).
