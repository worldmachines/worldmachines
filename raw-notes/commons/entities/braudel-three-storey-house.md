# Braudel's Three-Storey House: Material Life, Market Economy, Capitalism

**Source**: [[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1]] (Introduction and Conclusion), the scheme that organizes all three volumes of [[fernand-braudel]]'s *Civilization and Capitalism*.

## The floors

Braudel's central move is to split "the economy" into three strata that coexist and partly oppose each other within the same social formation ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-introduction]]):

1. **Material life**, the ground floor. It is an "infra-economy" of self-sufficiency and barter within a very small radius, "the unconscious daily round," run by routine ("sowing, planting, terracing, sailing as they always have done"). It covers 80–90% of the world's population and is too diffuse and undocumented for ordinary economic history to see ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-preface]], [[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion]]).
2. **The market economy**, the middle floor. It consists of production, exchange, shops, fairs and banks: the "transparent," visible realities economic science was built to observe. The Conclusion calls it ordinary economic life, with "small profits, its micro-capitalism ... barely distinguishable from ordinary work" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-introduction]], [[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion]]).
3. **Capitalism**, the top floor. It is a "shadowy zone" of privileged actors, monopoly and action at a distance: the Fuggers (see [[jakob-fugger]]), the Welsers, the great Indies companies, "real capitalism, with its mighty networks." Braudel insists it is not synonymous with the market economy, and expects this to be the book's most controversial claim ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-introduction]], [[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion]]).

## Properties of the model

- **No oil-and-water line.** There is "no clear demarcation line as between oil and water" between material life and the market. The two run in a "double register" whose relative weight shifts by era. Material life swelled in the seventeenth century and again in the recessions of the 1970s ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-preface]]).
- **The ground floor is permanent.** After the 1973–4 crisis, barter, moonlighting and home work reappeared at a scale some economists put at 30–40% of GNP, entirely outside official accounts. For Braudel this is evidence that the infra-economy is a permanent substrate, not a pre-industrial relic ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-introduction]]). Barter survives inside monetized Europe as "internal colonies": Smith's Scottish village paid in nails, Corsica stayed unmonetized until after the First World War, and the truck system paid workers in kind ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch07-imperfect-currencies]]). "The peasants who form three-quarters of our people do not settle a tenth of their consumption in hard cash" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch07-rules-of-the-currency-game]]).
- **Capitalism's power is mobility.** Only the top floor has "comparative freedom of movement." It can swing between trade, manufacture, real estate, state loans or usury, because the floors beneath it are "inflexible." Inequality is named as the engine: "the inequalities, the injustices, the contradictions large or small ... made the world go round" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion]]).
- **Not a sequence.** The floors are simultaneous strata, not stages. Braudel walks "some of the way" with Marx but rejects the deterministic succession of modes of production ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion]]).

## The house seen through single commodities

The notes repeatedly find the whole model inside one commodity's career:

- **Wheat and maize.** "The peasant ate maize and sold his wheat, for which he could get about double the price." Subsistence and market are simultaneous uses of the same harvest, not successive stages ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch02-maize-and-dietary-revolutions]]).
- **Brandy.** It climbed over three centuries from an apothecary's medicine, through guild fights over the fiscal "privilege" to distil, to a national industry graded by the "Dutch test" and quoted on the Amsterdam exchange ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-drinks-wine-beer-alcohol]]).
- **Coffee.** It went from Ottoman social ritual to a Parisian commodity sold by pedlar women, and at the same time became a plantation crop with "an active capitalist sector" behind it ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-stimulants-chocolate-tea-coffee-tobacco]]).
- **Sugar.** "What used to be a medicine is nowadays eaten as a food." Sugar became a plantation monoculture that required capital, credit, refineries in a few ports and "a large labour force (in America the black slaves)" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch03-table-manners-and-everyday-foods]]).
- **Iron versus copper.** "Proletarian iron" stayed dispersed in material life, while copper, silver and gold drew speculative capital, with copper "favoured by early capitalism" (the Fuggers, Mansfeld, the Amsterdam exchange) ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch05-iron-a-poor-relation]]).
- **Printing.** Heavy capital costs put presses in the hands of financier-publishers whom Braudel calls "the Fuggers on a small scale" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch06-printing-and-ocean-navigation]]).
- **Arms.** Artillery "quite transformed inter-state warfare, economic life and the capitalist organization of arms production," and only rich states could bear the cost ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch06-gunpowder-and-artillery]]).
- **Fashion.** Lyon's silk merchants paid illustrators to change patterns every year so that Italian copyists always sold last season's design ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch04-costume-and-fashion]]).

## Where the top floor comes from

Credit is the ground on which capitalism "first flexed its muscles, found its instruments and ... became 'conscious of its own existence'" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch07-paper-money-and-credit]]). Its institutional home was the free Western town. Public loans, bills of exchange, accountancy and trading companies were town inventions. Following Mumford, capitalism was a "cuckoo's egg" in the medieval town's nest that outgrew it, and the territorial state inherited its instruments ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch08-originality-of-western-towns]]). Monetization is also the force that pulls ancient structures upward into the market. Any society that "opens its doors to money sooner or later loses its acquired equilibria," and societies outside the market are each "a condemned man on temporary reprieve" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch07-imperfect-currencies]]).

## World Machines resonances

- **[[world-machines]].** Three strata in permanent friction loosely echo three machines always coexisting. The notes insist the fit is partial: Braudel's floors are "synchronic strata within one social formation, not sequential thousand-year eras" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-introduction]], [[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-conclusion]]).
- **[[legibility-as-machine-core]], inverted.** Here the middle floor is the legible one, "transparent" and ready for statistics. Capitalism thrives in a zone that stays illegible to ordinary participants and to economic science, and material life lies below the threshold of documentation: "legibility and its evasion as two sides of the same boundary" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-introduction]]). See [[legibility-as-precondition-not-cause]].
- **[[capture-resistance]], only loosely.** Material life "refuses to enter fully into the movement of exchange," but it is protected by "distance and isolation" and backwardness, "a residue, not a strategy" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-preface]]). Where the poor escaped money, it was "a structural absence of capture rather than an active refusal of it" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v1-ch07-rules-of-the-currency-game]]).

## Volume III: locating the top floor

The chronological volume ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3]]) repeatedly tests where the top floor actually sits. The sharpest case is the V.O.C. Its corporate accounts show only modest profits, declining from about 1696 and near zero by about 1724. Braudel admits this half-undermines his own view of long-distance trade as capitalism's "super-trade" ("Can I have been wrong?"). He resolves it by placing the capitalist logic in a syndicate of top Amsterdam merchants and company administrators who controlled purchasing and warehousing. His verdict: "the V.O.C. was a machine which stopped where the profits of trade monopolies began" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-ch03-voc-asia-and-america]]). The Hansa, by contrast, "clung to an elementary kind of capitalism," thin on credit and never fully rising to the top floor ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-ch02-first-world-economy-bruges-hansa]]). At the Champagne fairs the real engine was Italian credit sitting on top of the cloth trade ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-ch02-italian-cities-champagne-fairs]]). In Spanish America capitalism existed only as "one commercial echelon" at the top of a mosaic of subsistence and partly open economies ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-ch05-spanish-portuguese-america]]).

Braudel also refuses two stronger claims. The late-eighteenth-century specialist industrialist is "the beginning of industrial capitalism," but not the moment "'true' capitalism begins" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-ch06-growth-and-division-of-labour]]). And finance capitalism is not a twentieth-century invention. It recurs wherever commercial capital outgrows its usual outlets, as in Genoa and Amsterdam ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-ch06-finance-conjuncture-living-standards]]). The top floor also needs the floors and zones beneath it. Following Wallerstein and Rosa Luxemburg, capitalism cannot exist without other modes of production surrounding and subsidizing it ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-ch01-an-order-among-other-orders]]).

## The Conclusion's restatement

The last pages of the trilogy carry the model into the twentieth century and call the distinction between capitalism and the market economy "the essential message of this long quest" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-conclusion-past-and-present]]). Capitalism is a long-term structure, not a sequence of stages. The "whole panoply" of commercial, industrial and banking forms was already deployed in thirteenth-century Florence and seventeenth-century Amsterdam. Its defining privilege is "the ability to choose," which rests on scale, credit, networks and a minority's unwritten rules. That is what lets it "change horses in mid-stream." Henry Hope's remark about trade applies to it: "It is often ill but it never dies." The top floor lives in symbiosis with the state, which takes on the costliest burdens while capital captures subsidies and contracts. Crises concentrate capitalism rather than end it.

The three floors appear again in the present: monopoly capitalism at the top, a competitive market economy of small and medium firms (Prato's workshops) below it, and an informal "lower floor" of fraud, barter, moonlighting and housework that some economists put at 30–40% of activity. Braudel calls the relation between the top two floors "a dialectic," not simple domination, because big firms need small ones for sub-contracting, flexibility and invention ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-conclusion-past-and-present]]). The note offers a counter-rhyme to [[world-machines]]. Braudel's capitalism "explicitly resists any fixed decay curve," where a world machine is expected to decline and cede ground ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-conclusion-past-and-present]]).

## World time as a further storey

Volume III adds a spatial counterpart to the vertical house. The Foreword casts "world time" as a superstructure, a "crowning achievement" built on and fed by forces beneath it, with two-way traffic between base and top. Even in the most advanced societies it "never accounts for the whole of human existence" ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-foreword]]). The dominant city of a [[braudel-world-economy]] stands in the same relation to the zones it organizes as the top floor does to the floors below. It moves an inert mass through a few well-placed levers, which is Braudel's marble-block image ([[braudel-civilization-capitalism-v3-ch01-world-economies-ground-rules]]).
