# Henri de Saint-Simon

**Claude Henri de Rouvroy, comte de Saint-Simon (1760–1825)** — aristocrat, American War veteran, revolutionary land speculator, penniless pamphleteer, and finally the master of a small circle of disciples that included Augustin Thierry and Auguste Comte. The third of Frank Manuel's five Parisian prophets ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris]]), and the one whose doctrine had the most direct institutional afterlife.

## A life built and rebuilt on speculation

Manuel's biographical sketch finds no throughline in Saint-Simon's career except restless reinvention ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-saint-simon-01]]): speculation in confiscated church and émigré property during the Revolution under the assumed name "Bonhomme"; a narrow escape from the guillotine through mistaken identity; a fortune burned underwriting his own "projects for the betterment of mankind"; poverty, madness, an attempted suicide; and only at the very end, sustained by young disciples like Olinde Rodrigues who transcribed his oral teaching, the doctrine he is remembered for. Manuel treats this not as eccentricity but as the psychological substrate of prophecy — a man possessed by "the spirit of foretelling" regardless of his fitness for the role.

## Science dethroned: three theories of the scientist

Saint-Simon's view of the scientist moves through three positions, and the movement is the intellectual spine of his early work.

1. **The scientist as natural aristocracy.** The *Lettres d'un habitant de Genève*, fully in Condorcet's spirit, casts scientists as a "floating elite" holding the balance between propertied and propertyless classes, destined to found a Religion of Newton with a scientific priesthood replacing the discredited clergy.
2. **The scientist as function of circumstance.** Bitter personal rejection by the Napoleonic scientific establishment (Laplace, Bouvard) curdles this into impersonal determinism — the individual genius as a mere expression of "a law of circumstances," an "absolute and anonymous rhythm of scientific evolution" that eclipses the scientist entirely. Manuel presents the oscillation itself as revealing: a man swinging between great-man and structural accounts of scientific progress, in a small-scale rehearsal of what [[history-machine]] describes at civilizational scale.
3. **The scientist as commodity.** Under the influence of Restoration liberal economists, scientists become timid sinecure-holders whose work is a product to be judged by industrialists. By the end of his life the scientist is the least-favored member of his "great trinity" of capacities, subordinated first to the industrialist and then to the religious moralist — the trajectory Comte would complete by banishing technical science altogether.

## From egalitarian atomism to organicist hierarchy

Saint-Simon keeps the Enlightenment premise that the good society must mirror what is natural in man, but his reading of Cabanis and Bichat convinces him the natural fact is *inequality*. He adapts Bichat's tripartite physiology (motor, rational, sensory) into three mutually exclusive social classes — industrialists, scientists, artist-moralists — each in its rightful station by natural endowment, with careers open to talent only *within* class. This is set explicitly against "atomist egalitarian society," which he likens to inert mechanical clockwork, in favor of an organic body social bound by a "vitalist" element: the love of humanity as a literal animating fluid. Manuel notes the anti-egalitarian premise is borrowed straight from the counter-revolutionary theocrats de Maistre and de Bonald and then translated into ostensibly scientific, physiological terms.

## The twilight of power

The late doctrine's boldest claim is not that men will stop wanting to dominate but that the *object* of domination will shift ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-saint-simon-02]]): "the desire to command men has slowly transformed itself into the desire to make and remake nature in accordance with our will." Aggression is treated as a fixed quantum of energy that can be rerouted rather than a social relation that can be abolished — the hinge on which Saint-Simon's entire optimism about a conflict-free industrial future turns, and, Manuel notes, a sleight of hand Marx later reproduces in his own faith that a class which only works can never become an exploiting class.

From this follows a de facto withering away of government (a phrase he never uses). Since disorder springs from poverty, idleness, and ignorance, and industry eliminates all three, even the police function dissolves into a duty "common to all citizens." What remains is pure administration on the model of a bank, a canal company, or an insurance society: governmental action "reduced to nothing, or almost nothing," the state as national workshop. This is [[legibility-as-machine-core]] pushed to its self-abolishing extreme — a fully rationalized, capacity-sorted order that needs no command function at all, only technical direction.

## The New Christianity

Saint-Simon judged the Catholic clergy to have forfeited their claim to intellectual and moral leadership by failing to keep pace with science; they must "vanish as classes," their remnants absorbed into new Academies of Reasoning and Sentiment. Manuel traces their decline from genuine intellectual authority (the medieval clergy as science's sole repository) through defensive superstition to a "mere shell" awaiting dissolution — a clean instance of the [[decay-sequence]] running an institution down toward the ornamental while its successor quietly absorbs its prestige.

The *Nouveau Christianisme* (1825) is the replacement: Christianity reduced to the Golden Rule, defended by the monist claim that it would be "blasphemy" to found a religion on several principles, with its nineteenth-century "appropriate application" being organized labor for "the poorest and most numerous classes" — the phrase that became the watchword of the whole movement. His dying insistence that "religion must bring itself into harmony with the progress of the sciences" rather than simply disappear, and his forecast that the Catholic system's fall would be "the signal for a new belief which is going to fill with its enthusiasm the void which criticism has left in the souls of men," describe a [[gramsci-gap]] and refuse to let it stand empty. His last words to Olinde Rodrigues: "A passing sleep followed by a perpetual awakening."

He also periodizes Christian history into an age of generalization (medieval doctrinal unity) and an age of particularization since Luther (specialization, private interest, corrosive egotism), prescribing not reversion but the *coexistence* of both tendencies — a rough analogue of the [[world-machines]] picture of contending organizing logics running simultaneously.

## The children of Saint-Simon

After his death the sect hardened under Enfantin into a schism-ridden church ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-children-01]], [[manuel-prophets-of-paris-children-02]]).

- **The malady of the age.** Their cry "Progress is in danger!" framed their generation as living in "a twilight world when that-which-had-been no longer held men's allegiance and that-which-would-be was not yet believed" — the [[gramsci-gap]] rendered as affect, with industrial anarchy, atomized science, and an art capable only of satire or elegy as the symptoms.
- **The social trinity.** Against the Enlightenment's one-dimensional rational-utilitarian man, they insisted man is irreducibly tripartite — scientist, industrialist, artist, answering to reason, action, and feeling — and, against Bichat, that these capacities are infinitely and simultaneously expansive rather than traded off. Feeling, atrophied in the present crisis, outranks the "glacial" scientific faculty, which yields the romantic cult of the artist-poet-priest as the ideal personality of the coming order.
- **Organic and critical epochs.** Kant's 1784 essay on Antagonism, transmitted through Cousin's lectures, is stripped of its Kantian caution and turned into an optimistic quasi-arithmetic law: antagonism contracts and love expands as association widens from family to tribe to nation to a coming religion of humanity, alternating between integrated "organic" epochs and destructive but necessary "critical" ones. Manuel notes this heartbeat model fed most directly into Marx.
- **Rehabilitation of the flesh.** Enfantin's typology of love — intimate, convenient, religious — administered by a "hierarchic priesthood of love," was less libertinism than an assault on Christian mind/body dualism: "as long as one vital drive in man's nature was curbed there could be no true happiness," an argument close to [[Conatus]], and drawing on a romanticized Spinozism transmitted through Eugène Rodrigues's Lessing translation.
- **Capacity as an administrative problem.** "Each according to his capacity! Each capacity according to its works!" presupposes that aptitude is clearly definable by a hierarchy of experts who slot each person into an "optimum spot," a designed "cubbyhole in the perfect system" — with inherited property abolished not out of egalitarianism but to clear obstacles to the sorting. The economic counterpart is a single directing bank weighing "the various credit needs of all branches of industry" against a central budget of the totality of annual products: GNP before the concept existed.
- **Trial and routinization.** The 1832 obscenity and subversion prosecution — which could not decide whether the sect was a conspiracy, a religion, or a swindle — became Enfantin's stage for distinguishing the "rehabilitation of the flesh" from bourgeois "disorder of the flesh." Afterward the movement dissolved into biography: Chevalier a Second Empire senator who called himself one of the "hierarchical men" (Manuel: "we would now say organization men"), the Pereires pillars of finance, Enfantin thirty more years a marginal mystic pursuing the Suez Canal, until the disciple Delaporte's cool awakening that the Father was "only Enfantin."

Manuel takes pains to distinguish this hierarchy from later totalitarianism — their power rested on persuasion and love, and "they never spilled a drop of blood in their lives" — while conceding that the unbounded categories "crimes against science" and "moral crimes" prefigure a vocabulary of denunciation.

## Legacy and quarrels

Comte, his secretary, broke with him in 1824 over whose name would appear on the *Système de politique positive*, and was still calling him a "depraved juggler" decades later; Comte's own retrospective diagnosis was that Saint-Simon suffered the "physiological" fault of expecting a permanent pupil even after his protégé's "beard had begun to grow" ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-comte-01]]). Manuel's Prolegomenon frames the rupture as a Judas-like betrayal within a lineage otherwise held together by discipleship ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-preface-prolegomenon]]). And the epilogue traces the abolition of inheritance and the doctrines of property and labor forward into the phrases Marx recombines in the *Critique of the Gotha Programme* ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-epilogue]]).
