Death is the subject Montaigne returns to most often in the *Essays* ([[montaigne-complete-essays]]), and the one on which his own experience most visibly tests his borrowed doctrine. This page lays out the stations. The argument about how they connect is in [[from-rehearsing-death-to-trusting-the-body-montaigne]].

## The Book I programme

*That to Study Philosophy Is to Learn to Die* starts from Cicero and recasts the matter. All sects aim at pleasure, virtue is the finest pleasure, and fear of death spoils every other pleasure. Not thinking of death is a brutish remedy. Instead, death should be disarmed of “his novelty and strangeness” by constant imagination. “The premeditation of death is the premeditation of liberty; he who has learned to die has unlearned to serve”, with the hedge that nature must help art. Montaigne, then thirty-nine, reports that he keeps himself ready ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch19-study-philosophy-learn-die-1]]). The second half adds three strands. Death should find him “planting my cabbages”. A long speech, given by Nature and not in Montaigne's own voice, argues that death belongs to the order of things and that a short life and a long one are equal. And the terror of dying belongs to the deathbed's “terrible ceremonies”, a visor to be lifted ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch19-study-philosophy-learn-die-2]]).

## Opinion and pain

Of the Relish of Good and Evil tests the Greek saying that men are tormented by opinions rather than things. The many deaths met with jokes or zeal support it, but pain is the limit: the senses judge it, and it is “the worst incident of our being”. The soul can still lessen it by patience ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch40-relish-good-evil-depends-1]]).

## Death cannot be rehearsed, only approached

Use Makes Perfect concedes that we “are all apprentices when we come to it”. Sleep and swoons show its approaches. In a fall from his horse during the civil wars he lay senseless, and from inside the state felt sweet, languid and painless. From this he infers that the apparently agonised dying are not much to be pitied. He adds that imagination “enhances near one-half of the essence and reality of the thing” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch06-use-makes-perfect]]).

## Faced sideways

Of Diversion observes that almost everyone meets death looking elsewhere: at devotion, vengeance, glory, children or the heat of a fight. Only Socrates faces it head on. During his stone attacks Montaigne finds that small particulars (a footman's tears, a friendly touch) are what make up the regret of life ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch04-diversion]]).

## The stone

The stone arrives in the years of writing. He rejects the precept of a composed face under pain as “ceremonial”, since philosophy forms us “to be, not to seem”, and he allows groaning if the mind keeps its footing. He hopes the disease will finish his reconciliation with death ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch37-resemblance-children-fathers-1]]). In Of Experience his mind addresses his imagination and casts the stone as a school of fortitude and a gentle memento mori: “thou shakest hands with it once a month”, and “thou diest because thou art living”. He labels these arguments “weak and strong” and calls the consolation a rocking to sleep, so the notes warn against reading it as a doctrine of resignation ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-3]]).

## Against premeditation

Of Physiognomy reverses the early programme. During the plague his peasant neighbours left the grapes on the vines and met death with a plain steadiness that learned consolation cannot match. Preparation torments more than the thing; “we trouble life by the care of death”; death is the end of life, not its aim; and nature will teach us to die when the time comes. He leaves open whether the peasants' patience is only stolidity, in which case we should “teach nothing but ignorance” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch12-physiognomy-2]]).

## Dying by degrees, and privately

In old age, decay is death already mixed into life. A tooth falls out without pain; “'tis so I melt and steal away from myself”; “God is favourable to those whom He makes to die by degrees” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-4]]). He wants a private death that shares “in the ease and conveniences of my life”, without theatre and without laying his whole weight on others ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch09-vanity-4]]).

## Hedges

The early readiness is self-report of a disposition, not proof, and Nature's speech is a ventriloquized Lucretian consolation. The notes read its cyclical, indifferent time as rhyming only loosely with the long cycles of [[world-machines]], since it consoles individuals rather than theorising eras ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch19-study-philosophy-learn-die-2]]). Throughout, Montaigne hedges with “I hope”, “methinks” and “peradventure”.

## Death as the assay, and the difficulty of reading it

Several shorter essays treat death as the test of a life. In the chapter that retells Croesus and Solon, Montaigne gives his own reading of Solon: true felicity, a tranquil and well-ordered soul, can be shown only in the last scene, where “there is no more counterfeiting”. Death is the “assay of the fruit of all my studies”, and his chief concern is to die well, patiently and tranquilly. He hedges at once, having seen three execrable men die composed ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch16-18-proceeding-some-ambassadors]]). Elsewhere he answers Aristotle's puzzle about Solon on his own account: once out of being we have no communication with what is ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch03-05-affections-carry-themselves]]). A later essay warns that another's death is hard to read. The dying rarely believe they are dying, because we value ourselves so highly that the world seems to suffer by our dissolution, and of the deaths he has seen, fortune, not design, disposed the countenance. He sets the studied deaths of Socrates, Atticus and Tullius Marcellinus, met by slow abstention, above the blind rush at the blow: it is “far beyond not fearing death to taste and relish it” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch13-15-judging-death-another]]). The composure he admires is ordinary rather than staged. Of Sleep reads Cato's sleep before his suicide as the mark of a soul raised above such accidents, while recording that Augustus's sleep before a sea fight was taken for cowardice ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch44-46-sleep]]). He ranks Cato's way, continuing the “wonted course of life” into death, as the most natural ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch21-26-against-idleness]]), and ends All Things Have Their Season with Cato reading Plato on the night he died as part of his routine, not as preparation ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch27-28-cowardice-mother-cruelty]]).

## Voluntary death

Of the Custom of the Isle of Cea assembles a dossier on self-killing, and opens by leaving final determination to “the authority of the divine will”. It first gives the Spartan and Stoic case, partly in Montaigne's own phrasing: “Living is slavery if the liberty of dying be wanting.” Then it turns. He leans to constancy, finding “more constancy in suffering the chain we are tied to than in breaking it”, ranks Regulus above Cato, and calls suicide from fear cowardice. He argues that self-contempt is a disease particular to man, and offers his own small testimony against haste: “I have seen a hundred hares escape out of the very teeth of the greyhounds.” Pain and fear of a worse death seem to him the most excusable motives. Collective suicides look crueller but are less so, he observes, because the ardour of society carries away particular judgments. The essay closes on custom: Marseilles kept hemlock at public charge for those who gave their reasons to its senate, and at Cea a contented woman over ninety died before witnesses. He adds no verdict on the custom ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch03-custom-isle-cea]]). Of Three Good Women praises three Roman wives who died with or for their husbands as examples unlikely to be imitated, then reverses direction through Seneca's letter: one may rightly stay alive for the sake of someone who loves one, and Montaigne endorses it ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch35-three-good-women]]). The chapter on judging another's death relays a Stoic adviser's speech to Marcellinus without fully endorsing it, and narrates a death taken “not to escape from death, but to essay it” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch13-15-judging-death-another]]). The notes stress that he tests cases rather than ruling on suicide in general.
