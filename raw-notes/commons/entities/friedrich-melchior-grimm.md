# Grimm

This page draws on Volume I (Preface, Chapters I–VIII) of [[john-morley]]'s study [[diderot-and-the-encyclopaedists]]; Volume II material is added in later sections. Morley names him simply Grimm.

## Morley's portrait

Morley's Grimm is hard-edged: sound in judgement, alert to his own interest, and with the best thing known in his favour that he won Diderot's friendship. The asymmetry is the point of the portrait: Diderot weeps and drudges at Grimm's literary correspondence “like any journeyman” while Grimm collects pensions and gold boxes ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-4]]). Diderot accepted Grimm's maxim that time is owed first to friends, spending three days on an acquaintance's discovery while Encyclopædia proofs waited ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-3]]). Grimm was one of the company at Holbach's Grandval, and in a table-talk exchange there called method “the pedantry of letters”, provoking Galiani's fable of the nightingale, the cuckoo and the ass, in which Grimm is the nightingale ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-1]], [[morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-2]]). The hostility between Grimm and Rousseau was part of the background to Rousseau's break with Diderot ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-4]]).

## The literary correspondence

Morley values the Grimm-Diderot circular letters as the “machinery by which the light of Paris was diffused among darker lands”, a record he judges unmatched for any other literary epoch ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-4]]). The notes read this as an early paid, manuscript, subscription-based information channel, a modest piece of knowledge infrastructure that can be compared with [[printing-press-and-information-overload]], with the hedge that its medium was hand-copied letters, not print. Grimm also figures in the transmission of the Voland letters ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-1]]).

## Witness to the Encyclopædia

Morley leans on Grimm repeatedly. After the seizure of Diderot's papers in 1752 Grimm remarked that the Jesuits forgot to take his head and his genius; he feared that Helvétius's De l'Esprit would make the philosophic party pay with new restraints ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-3]]). His account underlies Morley's narration of Le Breton's secret mutilation of the final volumes, and his estimate of Diderot's pay, about three thousand livres a year and sixty thousand in all, is the figure Morley gives ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-4]]).
