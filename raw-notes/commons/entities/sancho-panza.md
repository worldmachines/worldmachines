# Sancho Panza

Don Quixote's squire, and by Part II the novel's second centre of gravity. Ormsby records that he was not in the original design at all: Sancho enters only after a landlord's offhand remark that knights-errant travel with squires, and then grows across the two Parts from a teller of "plain working lies" into the inventor of Dulcinea's enchantment, on whose "matchless mendacity" much of the second volume's plot turns ([[cervantes-don-quixote-front-essay-don-quixote]]).

He is worth a page of his own because he is not a foil. He is a rival epistemology, a rival economics, and — in Part II — a rival author.

## Recruitment and the terms of service

He is bought with an island. The recruitment scene plays the promise for sincerity and comedy at once: kingdoms may fall to him "in the twinkling of an eye," while Sancho immediately worries whether his wife would suit the rank of queen ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p1-06-09]]). The bargain is renegotiated repeatedly and never quite settled — he refuses to be seated as his master's equal, preferring to eat alone and unceremoniously rather than accept honours he cannot use ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p1-10-13]]); he tries to convert the arrangement into wages ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-05-08]]); he needles for the island by claiming twenty years have passed when barely two months have ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-26-29]]); and at home his accounting to Teresa is unsentimental — "out of some I came blanketed, and out of others belaboured," ninety-nine adventures in a hundred end badly, and yet there is "nothing in the world more delightful" than the seeking ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p1-52]]). His wife Teresa is not a comic obstacle but a coherent counter-ethics of staying within one's station, and their marriage runs as an unwon argument between security and advancement ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-05-08]]).

## The empiricist who is never heeded

Sancho supplies, over and over, the falsification test the novel refuses to honour. He names the flesh-and-blood men who tossed him in a blanket and proposes going home for the harvest ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p1-18-20]]); he reports "only a man on a grey ass ... who has something that shines on his head" ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p1-21-22]]); he argues that enchanted people do not eat, sleep or need the privy, and that this devil smells of amber rather than brimstone ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p1-46-48]], [[cervantes-don-quixote-p1-49-51]]); he notices that Queen Micomicona is no more a queen than his own mother ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p1-46-48]]); he checks whether they have crossed the equinoctial line by feeling for lice ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-26-29]]). He is overruled by rank, never refuted by argument.

His counter-discourse is proverbs — a compressed folk archive his master calls dragged in "by the head and shoulders," which nonetheless keeps producing right answers ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-42-45]], [[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-67-70]]). His materialism has a doctrine: "there are only two families in the world, the Haves and the Haven'ts" ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-20-22]]). It also has a floor the text does not flatter — his daydream of selling future "black vassals" for ready money is offered as harmless comic greed and lands as the darkest joke in Part I ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p1-29-32]]).

## The squire as author

The pivot of Part II is that Sancho stops correcting the fiction and starts manufacturing it. His soliloquy in the wood reasons explicitly from his master's established habit of category error — windmills into giants, therefore a peasant girl into Dulcinea — and pre-scripts the enchanter alibi before the deception even begins ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-09-12]]). The lie then closes around him: after Samson Carrasco's unmasking he is trapped into silence rather than correction ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-13-16]]); the Duchess reframes his invention as literal fact and hands it back to him as a sentence of 3,300 lashes ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-33-36]]); and blindfolded thirty feet from where he started, he invents an eyewitness account of tri-coloured heavenly goats, closing the episode on an explicit exchange of mutual fabrication with the Cave of Montesinos ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-37-41]]). He is, by then, the only character who knows the enchantment is false, the person being billed for it, and the person profiting from it — which is why his whipping ends up negotiated at a quarter-real a lash and administered largely to tree bark ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-71-74]]).

He stays, and says why: not belief, but debt and temperament — "I've eaten his bread, I'm fond of him, I'm grateful ... I'm faithful" — while privately holding his master to be stark mad ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-33-36]]).

## Barataria: the fool who governs well

The governorship is a prank, and Sancho is good at it anyway. Don Quixote's counsels beforehand are lucid statecraft — glory in humble birth because "blood is an inheritance, but virtue an acquisition"; let the staff of justice swerve toward mercy; do not govern by "arbitrary law" ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-42-45]]). Sancho already holds the disposition they aim at: he would rather "go Sancho to heaven than governor to hell."

His rulings work without documents, precedent, or literacy: the cane concealing the gold crowns, the purse verdict ("if you had shown as much ... spirit and vigour in defending your body as you have shown in defending that purse"), the five caps split ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-42-45]]); restitution rather than punishment in the gambling case ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-48-49]]); and the bridge-and-gallows paradox, a self-referential edge case that breaks the statute and is resolved only by letting doubt fall toward mercy — a solution the majordomo rates above Lycurgus and Sancho credits to his master ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-50-52]]). Read against [[legibility-as-machine-core]], the joke has a serious edge: an illiterate governor outside the written apparatus repeatedly beats the apparatus, and the case that defeats the code is the one the code cannot classify.

He is not idealised in the role. He collapses back into convention when he sends the cross-dressed girl home with a proverb about broken legs ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-48-49]]), and his first real opponent is not a criminal but Doctor Pedro Recio, who empties every dish at the banquet by citing Hippocrates — expert authority applied as protocol against a body's plain appetite, met with "I can't go on without eating" ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-46-47]]).

## The renunciation

The government ends in a staged night siege in which he is bound between two shields and trampled, and he quits at once, embracing Dapple with more feeling than he ever showed the office: "I was not born to be a governor ... A reaping-hook fits my hand better than a governor's sceptre," leaving exactly as poor as he arrived ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-53-56]]). He refuses Ricote's buried treasure on the same principle, and repeats the formula on the road — "naked I was born, naked I find myself, I neither lose nor gain" ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-57-59]]). This is the clearest [[capture-resistance]] gesture in the novel by someone who is not deluded: he declines the office, the wealth, and the identity that came with them, and keeps the life he can actually operate.

## The reversal at the end

By the final chapters the roles have crossed. Sancho argues against omens and superstition while his master reads a hare and a boy's idle remark as signs about Dulcinea; and when Don Quixote recovers his reason, it is Sancho who begs him to keep believing, proposing they go and be shepherds instead ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-71-74]], [[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-67-70]]). The squire who spent a thousand pages trying to disenchant his master is the last person still holding the fiction open.
