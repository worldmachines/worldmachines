# Helvétius

This page draws on Volume II of [[john-morley]]'s [[diderot-and-the-encyclopaedists]] ([[morley-diderot-v2]]), whose Chapter V treats Helvétius's *L'Esprit* as the first of three books of the Encyclopædic school. The judgments are Morley's unless marked as contemporaries'.

## The burning of L'Esprit

Of all the proscribed books of the century, Morley says, L'Esprit “excited the keenest resentment”, partly because it “came earliest in the literature of attack”. The indictment called it “the code of the most hateful and infamous passions”; the censor was cashiered, the author lost a post in the Queen's household, and the book was burnt along with Voltaire's poem on Natural Religion. Morley adds that the persecution brought Helvétius public vogue and the sympathy of the Encyclopædic circle, which had kept him at a distance ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1]]).

Contemporary verdicts were hostile. Rousseau found the book detestable and was spurred by it in the Savoyard Vicar's Profession of Faith. Diderot ranked it with Montesquieu and Buffon yet saw that it rested on paradox. Turgot called it philosophy without logic, literature without taste, morality without goodness, and argued that attacks on intolerance must rest on just ideas and calm, solid treatment, since declamation gives the strong a good excuse. Morley calls it an open question whether the French monarchy could have reformed itself even in 1758, but thinks Turgot could be excused for hoping to avoid the violence to which Helvétius's “diatribes” pointed ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1]]).

## The man

Morley traces Helvétius's path from farmer-general, geometer and poet of Happiness to social theorist after Montesquieu's success, and reports a charge of vanity made by enemies and friends alike. An intimate attests seven years of laborious composition. Marmontel calls him liberal, generous and benevolent, the opposite of what he professed. Diderot, relaying a lady's account, paints him fearful, hated by his neighbours over his game preserve and guarded by twenty-four men. Morley sets against this well-attested generosity: pensions, and hiding the Young Pretender for two years. Turgot jibed at a farmer-general attacking governments ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1]]).

## The book

Morley finds L'Esprit readable but unscientific, modelled on Fontenelle's elegant popularisation and stuffed with anecdote; Hume valued its composition, not its philosophy. Madame Roland found its “repulsive self-interest” depressing and refused its principles ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1]]). Its quoted formulations make “the Utility of the Public” the single principle of morality and legislation, invariable in principle and pliable in application. Morley's central claim is that Bentham took from Helvétius, and avowed, the idea of general utility as the foundation of morality, though nobody claims Helvétius invented utilitarianism; the notes observe that Bentham's acknowledgement is asserted rather than documented ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-2]]; see [[democracy-of-utility-morley]]).

## The two paradoxes

**Self-love as the sole spring of conduct.** Morley names a classic fallacy, confusing the standard of conduct (utility) with the motive (self-gratification), and a legislator problem: Helvétius assumes a disinterested legislator while denying disinterest to everyone else, vaulting over the selfishness of a governing class. Morley concedes that deterrent penal law presupposes the doctrine, but insists sympathy deserves a place. His charitable reading is that Helvétius was answering theologians who held man predisposed to love evil, and wanted to found probity on interest rather than on an abused religion ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-2]]).

**Circumstance over organisation.** Helvétius holds that intellectual inequality comes from government, times and education: genius is common, the circumstances rare. Morley calls this an “egregious overstatement” of a wholesome half-truth. Diderot objected that it misses the barrier between a man destined by nature for a function and one who brings only industry, and wrote detailed notes against De l'Homme (1774). Morley still thinks this the less mischievous error for France, since it pointed to institutions as the lever of reform; Godwin later recast it ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-2]]).

## Morley's verdict

Morley credits Helvétius with a germ of historical understanding, that even bizarre or cruel customs rest on real or seeming public utility, a doctrine he says Voltaire, Rousseau and Diderot all ignored. He quotes the humane remark that impressed Bentham, “In order to love mankind, we must expect little from them”. His summary: the book unites “most of wholesome truth with most of repellent error”, and its author had a feeble grasp and no gift for accurate thinking ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-2]]). The notes caution that Morley's claims for utility are those of a partisan Victorian liberal-positivist, not a neutral account.
