# Deviant globalization

A concept coined by the editors of [[gilman-deviant-globalization]], Nils Gilman, Jesse Goldhammer and Steven Weber. Everything on this page is the editors' claim, drawn from their Introduction and Conclusion. The volume's chapters are by other authors, who did not write with the concept in mind. The editors themselves say the concept is “a lens”, and “a lens is not quite a theory” ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-intro-2-deviant-globalization]]).

## Definition

Deviant globalization is the set of cross-border economic networks that produce, move and consume illegal, informal or morally repugnant goods and services, from narcotics and wildlife to dirty money, toxic waste and trafficked people ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-intro-1-globalization]]). More precisely, the editors define it as value-adding cross-border activity that violates noneconomic, conventional “Western” norms about human rights, violence, health and the sacred.

## Boundaries

- **Not the same as illegality.** It “cannot, however, be reduced merely to economic activities that violate the law”. Some deviant flows are legal (travel to a jurisdiction with a lower age of consent); some illegal acts are not deviant (monopolistic pricing).
- **Not a distributional harm.** Offshoring call-center jobs hurts some people but is only distributional, not deviant.
- **The test is moral arbitrage:** knowing exploitation of differences in morality and regulation between places, as in the toxic-waste and sex-tourism examples ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-intro-2-deviant-globalization]]).
- **Not separable from the mainstream.** In the editors' four-row table, supply-chain technology pairs with counterfeit logistics, the Internet with malware and scams, capital mobility with laundering, and privatization ideology with “everything for sale”. Every industry has a “deviant doppelgänger”, and in practice transplant surgeons, Mafia-run bakeries and listed waste-dumping companies straddle the line.

## Moral and regulatory arbitrage

The editors' mechanism is that “we” create the market by codifying moral outrage: banning prostitution, methamphetamine or trade in endangered species generates demand that the licit economy cannot meet. Deviant globalization is “the ultimate arbitrage activity, growing at the intersection of ethical difference and regulatory inefficiency”, played on a “morally lumpy field” beneath neoliberal rules ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-intro-1-globalization]]). On the supply side they speak of “regulatory arbitrage” by deviant entrepreneurs and of governments subject to “regulatory capture”. In the Conclusion this becomes a policy principle: since no state can impose uniform standards or prices on moral offensiveness, advocates of different moral standards should bargain through institutions ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-conclusion-2]]). For how the volume's chapter authors describe different gradients of this kind, each in their own terms, see [[prohibition-and-price-gradients-make-the-market]].

## Shared infrastructure

“What enables globalization, enables deviant globalization as well.” The global infrastructure is “dual use and value neutral”: Craigslist, intermodal shipping and Pakistani cell networks serve licit and illicit users alike ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-intro-1-globalization]]). The editors see four drivers of acceleration over the past twenty years: technology, the end of the Cold War, neoliberal ideology, and “power”, meaning the ability to define what is sanctioned. Globalization reinforces the power structures that enable it; deviant globalization works against them. They also insist that space still matters (factor endowments, urban archipelagos, financial nodes such as the City of London) and that the phenomenon is historically old (piracy, the slave trade, the opium trade) though recently accelerated ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-intro-2-deviant-globalization]]). Their dual-use claim sits close to what [[farrell-underground-empire]] and [[weaponized-interdependence]] describe from the state's side, though the editors speak of markets and norms rather than chokepoint power.

## Lenses the editors reject

- **The yuck factor.** Disgust is a legitimate starting signal but varies too much across people, cultures and time, and many non-yucky things (they cite Chinese coalminers) undermine dignity as much.
- **The coercion factor.** The contract-law “meeting of the minds” test is too broad (buying cocaine is voluntary) and too narrow (licit globalization is not free of coercion). Read libertarianly, they note, deviant globalization is “the purest expression of free-market capitalism”.
- **Criminality and moralizing.** In the Conclusion, labelling actors “global criminals” makes governments stop managing and start to “prohibit, target, capture, punish, strike, and kill”, which ignores the system and creates arbitrage opportunities ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-conclusion-2]]).

## Lenses the editors propose

- **The return of the repressed** (demand). The poor, as innovators, found out which demands the rich repress and used globalization's rules and infrastructure to supply them, so deviant supply follows deviant demand and “it's not going away”. The editors present this as analogy, not clinical theory, and the claim that the poor are the innovators is stated, not demonstrated.
- **Regulatory economics** (supply). Prohibitions and policing are control systems that shape illicit markets rather than instruments of a winnable war. The upshot is the management of rules, including talk of “optimal levels” of trafficking and dumping ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-intro-2-deviant-globalization]]).

## Development and the state

The editors' boldest claims:

- **Development.** For the global rich deviant globalization meets unfulfilled demand; for the global poor it is both “an individual's fastest ticket out of poverty” and a mark of exclusion ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-intro-1-globalization]]). In the Conclusion the poor are running “radical experiments in actual development” behind the back of the World Bank, the IMF and the NGOs. The editors' hedges: they deny neither coercion nor externalities, and claim only that participation is rarely the worst available choice. Compared with NGO-style development it is less transparent, less formally organized, weak on contract enforcement and yields no durable public goods, and they reject any Robin Hood reading ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-conclusion-1]]).
- **The deviant entrepreneur.** A third nonstate actor that globalization's enthusiasts and critics both overlook ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-intro-1-globalization]]). These entrepreneurs do marketing, strategy and financial analysis like any others, and the editors compare the Cali Cartel's Rodríguez Orejuela brothers with Brin and Page ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-conclusion-1]]).
- **The state.** The neoliberal hollowing-out of Southern states was a precondition. Deviant entrepreneurs then turn state weakness into a positive feedback loop and prefer autonomous zones to sovereignty. The editors reject the “failed states” vocabulary in favour of a self-reinforcing synergy between weak states and strong deviant entrepreneurs ([[gilman-deviant-globalization-conclusion-1]], [[gilman-deviant-globalization-conclusion-2]]). See [[deviant-entrepreneurs-and-the-hollowed-state]] for how the chapter authors' accounts of this relationship compare.

## Hedges

The editors call their third claim, about a shift in world power, a claim that must survive scrutiny. They say the volume's essays are not a scientific sample. The comparison with mainstream development is asserted rather than measured. The World Machines readings in the Conclusion notes (late [[modernity-machine]], [[divergence-machine]]) are loose: the editors are policy analysts writing about one neoliberal episode and use no machine language.
