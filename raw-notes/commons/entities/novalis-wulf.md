# Novalis (Wulf)

Extends [[Novalis]], which describes his notebooks and the Romantic Encyclopaedia, with the life behind them as Andrea Wulf's [[wulf-magnificent-rebels]] tells it, reporting Novalis's own words and adding a few interpretive claims of her own.

**Fichte revised through love.** Wulf introduces him beside the brutal 1796 surgery on his fiancée Sophie von Kühn. His five-hundred-page notebook dialogue with Fichte finds the Ich circular ('I am for myself the ground of all thoughts'), concludes that the non-Ich must already be there and can only be sensed, declares 'Philosophy is originally a feeling', and makes love the synthesising force that turns the non-Ich into a 'You' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch05-novalis-in-love]]).

**Nachsterben.** After Sophie died on 19 March 1797, and his brother Erasmus a month later, Novalis resolved to follow her by willpower alone, reasoning from Fichte's free Ich that a mind which can lift an arm might end a life. 'Death is self-conquest.' His diary counts the days since her death in 'almost clinical prose'; by July the resolve dissolved ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch08-handful-of-darkness]]). Wulf presents this as his own logic, not her endorsement.

**Freiberg, the encyclopaedia and Magical Idealism.** At the Freiberg mining academy under Werner he began the encyclopaedia discussed on [[Novalis]], deliberately named against Diderot and D'Alembert and meant to unite rather than alphabetise. Wulf sets it against an 'age of classification' (Linnaeus, the metre) and a Prussia run by 'mechanical administration'. 'The sciences must all be poeticised', 'The more poetical, the more true', but, Wulf stresses, for synthesis, not against reason ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch08-handful-of-darkness]]). The mines supplied the imagery by which the *Hymns to the Night* turn darkness into an inward path; Wulf's own claim is that the Jena Set, not Freud, opened the dark regions of the mind ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch08-handful-of-darkness]]). His attempt at an elixir to 'cure physicality' she reads as another attempt to liberate the mind ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch10-symphilosophy]]).

**Fragments and religion.** *Pollen* filled much of the first *Athenaeum*; a fragment on religion as an opiate is, Wulf says, an anticipation of Marx by half a century ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch09-sublime-impertinence-1]]); 'I romanticise' is his definition of the project ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch09-sublime-impertinence-2]]). At Leutragasse in November 1799 ('I produce best in dialogue') he read *Europe*: the Reformation had broken a Europe unified by faith, the Enlightenment had made the world 'a materialistic machine, a gigantic millwheel', and a 'new confederation of minds' should follow. Wulf notes what it ignores (the Inquisition), the friends wondered whether he was going backwards, and Goethe advised against publication ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch15-new-confederation]]). He called Caroline the heart of the Jena Set ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch11-naturphilosophie]]).

**Condemnation, death and myth.** After Auguste's death he condemned Caroline, which Wulf attributes to his aristocratic roots and conventional views of marriage ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-1]]). He died of tuberculosis at Weißenfels on 25 March 1801, aged twenty-eight, leaving *Heinrich von Ofterdingen* unfinished; Tieck called the loss an 'amputation' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-2]]). Wulf argues that Schlegel and Tieck's editing, which suppressed his political and scientific work, made him a Christ-like figurehead, and that because he died before Napoleon's coronation, while Schlegel, Schelling and Fichte turned religious, conservative and nationalistic, he alone stayed the 'First Romantic' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-2]]). Her book's closing moral argument rests on him: self-understanding is the route to understanding others ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-influence-selfish]]; see [[art-of-being-selfish-wulf]], [[poeticising-the-mechanical-world-wulf]]).
