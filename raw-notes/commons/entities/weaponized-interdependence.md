# Weaponized Interdependence

**Weaponized interdependence** is Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman's term — coined in a 2019 academic article, then developed at book length in [[farrell-underground-empire]] — for what happens when a state discovers that its position at the centre of global economic networks can be used as a weapon. Not trade leverage in the ordinary sense, and not blockade: something more specific. The networks in question were built by private firms for private reasons, and the state's power over them derives from where their chokepoints physically and legally sit.

## Preconditions

Weaponized interdependence requires three things to be true at once, and the book's argument is that all three became true of the United States without anyone intending them to.

**Centrality inherited, not built.** The post-1989 global networks were meant to transcend geopolitics but were laid the way medieval roads were laid on Roman ones — "it is easier to build on top of" what already exists. By 2002, less than 1 percent of global Internet bandwidth moved between two non-U.S. regions without transiting the United States. SWIFT's board was dominated by U.S. banks and its data centre sat in Northern Virginia. International dollar transactions ran through U.S.-controlled dollar clearing, and every Eurodollar, however offshore, "had to be backed by a real dollar," sitting in a U.S. bank. The buccaneers of the pirate kingdom still had to "regularly provision themselves in the monarch's ports."

**Chokepoints, produced by the networks' own growth dynamics.** Each infrastructure in the book concentrates as it grows — the commercial Internet into a Northern Virginia corridor, semiconductor fabrication into TSMC, interbank messaging into SWIFT. See [[decentralization-produces-chokepoints]].

**Commercially dependent operators.** The weapon fires through firms, not armies. AT&T's "extreme willingness" to build room 641A; Yahoo! capitulating under a $250,000-a-day fine threat; SWIFT — a nominally independent Belgian cooperative — folding not because a subpoena was irresistible but because its member banks needed continued access to the U.S. financial system; HSBC cooperating against Huawei because it was already under a deferred-prosecution agreement and could not risk dollar clearing. The lever is always the operator's own commercial exposure.

## Two operations

The book documents the capability in two modes, which appear together and reinforce each other.

**Seeing.** The NSA's exploitation of fiber chokepoints (STORMBREW, FAIRVIEW, PRISM), the Treasury's access to SWIFT messaging data, the Biden administration's semiconductor "data hub" demanding supply-chain visibility "from across the federal government." In the book's own image, Ashburn, Virginia became a "vast parabolic mirror, concentrating the Internet into a tiny point that is easy to observe and perhaps to exploit," and the U.S. could "detect the subtle percussions of what enemies and friends were saying."

**Cutting.** Designation by the Office of Foreign Assets Control — once an obscure unit — severs a foreign bank from correspondent accounts at U.S. banks, at which point other banks abandon it preemptively for fear of contagion. Stripping Iran's U-turn exceptions, threatening banks directly rather than governments, and finally forcing SWIFT to expel nineteen Iranian banks. And the technological analogue: the 2020 foreign-produced direct product rule, an obscure footnote in nine-point type at the bottom of a 320-page Entity List, extending U.S. jurisdiction to any foreign-made chip built with U.S.-origin design software or equipment.

Seeing precedes cutting. Brian Hook's blackmail email to an Indian tanker captain works only because his team first started "mapping out … the pressure points" of Iran's oil shipping network. This ordering — legibility first, coercion second — is the same operation [[legibility-as-machine-core]] names in the [[modernity-machine]], redeployed here as covert empire rather than public administration.

## What the weapon actually does

Its effects are consistently disproportionate to their nominal size, because it works through fear and contagion rather than through the sums involved. Banco Delta Asia is the demonstration case: a $25 million freeze against a minor Macau bank produced a bank run, made North Korean money globally toxic, and left banks refusing to touch the money for months after it was unfrozen — an outcome U.S. officials themselves had not anticipated. The corresponding scale case is Huawei, whose global phone market share fell from 20 percent to 4 percent within a year of the FPDP rule reeling in TSMC.

The weapon also does not turn off. Stuart Levey — by then at HSBC — and Christopher Hill described a "sanctions doomsday machine that could not be turned back off": once fear has been manufactured, it cannot easily be un-manufactured. Firms overcomply, and overcompliance persists after the policy that caused it lapses.

## Its own limits

**Asymmetry.** China's countermeasures are structurally weaker at every turn. CIPS processes a fraction of SWIFT's volume. Market-access weapons — banning Norwegian salmon, Australian coal, NBA broadcasts — are self-limiting, hurting China nearly as much as the target; China quietly reimports Australian coal when it needs it. When the U.S. sanctioned Carrie Lam she had to be paid salary in literal cash because no Chinese bank would touch her account; when China sanctioned Gayle Manchin she was merely "flattered." Europe's INSTEX, built to route around the dollar after the JCPOA's destruction, completed one transaction and then essentially nothing.

**Blind spots.** The maps that make the weapon work are financial, and physical supply chains are not on them. Sanctioning Deripaska's Rusal nearly shut down an Irish aluminium refinery that German car factories depended on.

**Blowback through visibility.** Jack Lew's 2016 warning that sanctions power is "inextricably linked" to U.S. economic centrality, and that overreach could "ultimately drive business activity away from the U.S. financial system," is the book's own statement of the constraint. NSA data-centre intrusions provoked Google and Microsoft into pervasive encryption that blinded the agency's own collection. Every successful use advertises the vulnerability to everyone who might next be on the receiving end.

## The boomerang

The term's own history is part of the story. Farrell and Newman coined "weaponized interdependence" as a critical warning; per Chris Miller's *Chip War*, it directly inspired a Trump administration official's use of the FPDP rule — read not as a diagnosis but as "a beautiful thing" and "a good playbook to implement." The concept became an instrument of the thing it described. It also became the vocabulary of the targets: Margrethe Vestager's retrospective — "[W]e have had a hard awakening into the era of weaponized interdependence," followed by "we were just greedy" — adopts the authors' frame as Europe's own self-diagnosis.

## In World Machines terms

Weaponized interdependence is a clean case of [[infrastructure-inheritance]] with a polarity reversal at the input layer: the carrier (cables, messaging standards, correspondent accounts, design toolchains) is unchanged, the cargo inverts from commerce to coercion. The chokepoints function as [[gravitational-attractor]] — they bend the field of possible action for every firm routing through them regardless of what anyone believes about the legitimacy of doing so, which is precisely the priority [[material-power-vs-discursive-power]] argues political theory has inverted.

It is also a dusk signature. The incumbent still commands the plumbing while its relative economic weight declines, and can punish a rising challenger long before that challenger can build parallel infrastructure — the [[gramsci-gap]] condition in which the old order's legitimating story (open markets, peace through trade) has died while nothing has replaced it, and power runs on inherited machinery rather than consent. Whether that is a durable position or a terminal one is the question the book leaves open: "it will crumble, or worse."
