Desire, marriage and the ageing body are among the subjects Montaigne's *Essays* ([[montaigne-complete-essays]]) treat most frankly. The main text is the long Book III essay Upon Some Verses of Virgil, read here in five slices; its fourth slice, on self-invented shame and the book's fidelity, is used on [[custom-as-second-nature-montaigne]] and [[essay-as-self-study-montaigne]]. The reading notes keep three voices apart throughout: Montaigne's self-report, exempla borrowed from ancient writers, and opinions about women that he states flatly in the manner of his age.

## The body that will not obey

Of the Force of Imagination treats impotence brought on by imagination, much of it in the first person. A friend's failure, caught from hearing another man's story, is relieved by confessing it beforehand. Montaigne rigs a ridiculous charm for a bridegroom and explains why such rituals work: “their very inanity gives them weight and reverence”. He then disowns the trick. Hair, skin, voice, bowels and heart all act without our consent, and the will itself often wills what reason forbids. The note reads the self here as a federation of partly autonomous motions rather than a sovereign commanding a body ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch20-21-force-imagination]]). For imagination's wider powers see [[imagination-and-opinion-montaigne]].

## Marriage is not love

Of Moderation, speaking for wives, advises husbands that pleasure in marriage should be “a sober and serious delight”. The essay then turns on the moralists themselves: human wisdom does ill to cut down the few natural pleasures we have ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch29-moderation]]). Upon Some Verses of Virgil develops the distinction. Men marry “for their posterity and family”, matches made by a third hand are preferable, and “furious ardour is worth nothing” in a marriage. A good marriage, if there be any such, resembles friendship ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch05-some-verses-virgil-1]]). Love lives on pleasure inflamed by difficulty, while marriage has “utility, justice, honour, and constancy”. Montaigne reports that he hates all obligation and would not have married Wisdom herself, yet “the most of my actions are guided by example, not by choice”. Having married by custom, he says he kept its laws more strictly than he had promised or expected ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch05-some-verses-virgil-2]]). Of Three Good Women puts the test of a marriage in the whole length of the companionship, not in a widow's display of grief. Its three Roman wives who died with their husbands are borrowed exempla of a kind no longer in use, and the essay closes on Seneca staying alive for his wife's sake, which Montaigne endorses ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch35-three-good-women]]).

## The double standard

The middle of the Virgil essay is an often ironic defence of women against rules that “the men make them without their help”. Governesses imprint the very idea they forbid, as an anecdote about his daughter shows. Men “weigh vices, not according to nature, but according to our interest”. The note cautions that the claims about female appetite are mostly reported from authors and doctors and turned against male hypocrisy, and that his partly tongue-in-cheek advice on courtship sits uneasily with the critique ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch05-some-verses-virgil-2]]). The next slice calls jealousy the most foolish of the passions. Since “there is neither continency nor virtue where there are no opposing desires”, the chastity men want would require women to “become insensible and invisible to satisfy us”. Severity defeats itself, and Venus has made “the laws her bawds”. His counsel is not to seek proof: “Curiosity is vicious throughout; but 'tis pernicious here.” He also paints his own bashfulness in love ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch05-some-verses-virgil-3]]). The essay ends by judging that “males and females are cast in the same mould”, education and usage excepted; the note calls this hedged and borrowed, and it sits beside the male-centred framing that precedes it ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch05-some-verses-virgil-5]]).

## Moderation over abstinence

The Story of Spurina contrasts bodily appetites, which satiety and material remedies can cure, with passions of the soul such as ambition, which grow by fruition. Spurina, the Tuscan youth who scarred his own face so as not to inflame others, earns more admiration than approval, since the design was good but defective in prudence. Montaigne closes that it is easier to abstain from women than to behave rightly in the society of a wife, and that “the well living of Scipio has a thousand fashions, that of Diogenes but one” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch33-story-spurina]]).

## The ageing body

In the Virgil essay Montaigne writes as an old man. “'Tis now my body's turn to guide my mind”, and when the body has the stone the mind seems to have it too; he would have the mind stay green “like mistletoe upon a dead tree” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch05-some-verses-virgil-1]]). In the last slice he mocks an old man's feeble desire as “a true flame of flax”, yet prescribes love in moderation, “as an experienced physician”, to keep an ageing man lively. Philosophy's austerity toward bodily pleasure is too strict, since “there is nothing in us, during this earthly prison, that is purely either corporeal or spiritual”. He reports that in his own affairs he never pretended more affection than he had and was sparing of promises; the note warns that this self-assessment flatters ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch05-some-verses-virgil-5]]).

## Hedges

The notes gloss the silence around sex, which Montaigne treats as a convention, and his entry into marriage by custom, as custom working like invisible machinery ([[habit-and-identity]]), with the hedge that here it governs a taboo rather than law ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch05-some-verses-virgil-1]], [[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch05-some-verses-virgil-2]]). His account of nobility as a customary inheritance, with Sparta's hereditary trumpeters and Calicut's castes, is read as only a loose rhyme with [[Legibility]], since he is arguing about virtue, not infrastructure ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch05-some-verses-virgil-1]]).
