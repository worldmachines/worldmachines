# Nicolas de Condorcet

**Marie Jean Antoine Nicolas de Caritat, Marquis de Condorcet (1743–1794)** — mathematician, secretary of the Academy of Sciences, Girondist, and the second of Frank Manuel's five Parisian prophets ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris]]). Manuel subordinates the mathematician and politician to "the visionary of a utopia of science" ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-preface-prolegomenon]]).

## The man behind the doctrine

D'Alembert called him a "snow-capped volcano." Manuel's portrait insists the public Condorcet — cool, dispassionate, the apostle of reason — concealed a compulsively suffering and submissive romantic, an anxious man "always expecting catastrophe" and who built, out of that anxiety, a doctrine of guaranteed collective happiness ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-condorcet-01]]). The biographical register matters for reading the *Esquisse d'un tableau historique des progres de l'esprit humain*: its serenity was achieved, not given, written clandestinely while he hid from Robespierre's police, and it ends in an Elysium composed by a man awaiting arrest. He died a fugitive in 1794, and the epilogue records the manner of it — clutching a Horace ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-epilogue]]).

He entered the lineage as Turgot's chevalier. Manuel closes his Turgot essay on the generational hinge: Turgot's imperturbable half-smile of resignation at his own fall from power set against Condorcet's furious grief and the way he "girded himself for war" — "the day of the philosophes was drawing to a close, and the men of action were about to take over" ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-turgot-02]]).

## Secularizing history into a science

Condorcet's decisive move is to remove the theology from Turgot's theodicy of progress. Where Vico, Turgot, and Herder needed a rational God to guarantee that history obeyed law as physics does, Condorcet abandons the transcendental sanction and identifies history *completely* with the other sciences, leaning on Hume's demolition of necessary causality: if physical laws are themselves only probabilistic, then history's "general and constant facts" can claim exactly the same epistemic standing ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-condorcet-01]]).

What results is history reconceived as "a science to foresee the progressions of the human species" — the capacity, in his own phrase, "to tame the future" — explicitly modeled on Newtonian calculus applied to "a sufficient number of observations," and explicitly yielding "truths with a high degree of probability" rather than certainties. This is the closest eighteenth-century analogue to what [[psychohistory-and-world-machines]] attempts, and the resemblance extends to the epistemic modesty: probabilistic regularities, not prophecy dressed as physics. The epilogue reinforces the point — Condorcet's private notes anticipate "oscillations" and "passing eclipses" in progress, complicating the caricature of naive linear perfectibility ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-epilogue]]).

The mechanism he imagines driving history is relentlessly utilitarian: men adopt whatever is cheaper or more pleasurable once a genius points out the profit. Even the humane turn away from slaughtering war captives is explained as "a tidy bit of commonsensical bookkeeping" once slave labor outvalued the cost of subsistence — moral advance rewritten as debit and credit.

## Genius made statistical, and equality made the measure of progress

Turgot treated genius as a fixed natural constant, "spread among mankind like gold in a mine." Condorcet's most consequential revision converts it into a statistical proportion of an educated population: expand instruction and the supply of productive scientists multiplies "astronomically." Progress relocates from the heroic individual to society's readiness to *accept* an innovation, and value relocates from elite discovery to its diffusion among "the most numerous portion of each society."

This is the pivot that makes equality the content of progress rather than a moral supplement to it — his three hopes being equality among nations, equality among classes, and the biological perfection of the species. Manuel is sharply critical of what the optimism concealed: an unexamined conviction that European scientific-utilitarian civilization was the sole legitimate form of knowledge, whose worldwide imposition by "altruist white Europeans bearing science" never needed justification ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-condorcet-01]]). The extensive, no-outside logic he inherits from Turgot — no region of the globe can be left outside the illumination of science, since any uncivilized penumbra is a standing threat — is the same civilizing-mission argument passed on intact ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-turgot-02]]). Read against [[weird-bias]], it is an early and unusually candid instance of a particular civilization narrating itself as the universal one.

## Progress as moral absolute

In the late writings, progress stops being a hopeful generalization about history and becomes an ethical imperative with its own logic of sin and virtue ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-condorcet-02]]). Because progress is defined in utilitarian terms, any delay in delivering its bounty is an injustice against those denied it: deceleration becomes "a great iniquity," a "refusal to bestow happiness," ultimately "an act of treachery against humanity." Manuel notes this prefigures the twentieth century's Left/Right tempo debates — how fast to accelerate, and what price in liberty to pay — and that Condorcet, an absolutist defender of individual rights, lets liberty "bend" once progress has become the higher moral principle.

## The republic of science and the state run by mathematics

Against a genuinely atomized eighteenth-century scientific world of prizes, jealous individual genius, and no coordinating body, Condorcet's commentary on Bacon's *New Atlantis* proposes something structurally new: a voluntary but planned, funded, administratively continuous world organization for perpetual data collection — uniform method, dispersed observation posts, decennial reporting, automatic budget allocation to long-term projects, supermajority rules so that no single generation can derail the plan. Its research catalogue runs to vital statistics, meteorology, contraception, eugenics, and artificial insemination.

Beneath it is the conviction, extended from his edition of Pascal, that there is no real boundary between the mathematical and moral kingdoms: the calculus of probabilities is the bridge by which "moral problems would be resolved as scientifically as geometric ones." Carried to the limit, this yields a *mathematique sociale* and a state that could be run "by social mathematics... without debates." Manuel flags the irony precisely: the great protector of individual rights against the state arrives at the all-knowing scientific technician as ruler, a leap that "traversed the age of middle-class parliamentarism" a century early.

Uniform method, quantified observation, and a supreme coordinating body substituting for local idiosyncratic practice make this a clean precursor of [[legibility-as-machine-core]] — an engineering solution to the problem of collective epistemic action across centuries, straining hard against its author's own libertarian instincts. What Condorcet does *not* do is settle whether the counting must serve convergence; see [[legibility-without-convergence]].

## Progress as the new god

The *Esquisse* ends in something close to devotion. Sympathy is stretched from present humanity to all future generations, and contemplation of that future bliss becomes an "asylum" from present persecution — Progress as "the new god of the age," borrowing the old religion's dream of future beatitude while discarding its apocalypse. This is progress functioning as a world-organizing *pull* attractor of the kind [[fiat-progress]] names, at the moment it is being consciously installed as a substitute for the theological guarantee Condorcet himself had removed.

One detail is worth flagging on its own: his anxiety about historical catastrophe — an echo of Boulanger's deluge theory, and the hope that hieroglyph-like steles might shelter the sciences "even from general revolution of the globe" — is an early version of the impulse behind a [[Prime Radiant]]: an archive built to outlast the civilization that made it.
