# August Wilhelm Schlegel (in Wulf's Magnificent Rebels)

The elder Schlegel brother is, in [[wulf-magnificent-rebels]], the orderly, pedantic, well-groomed son of a Lutheran priest, and the counterweight to his volatile brother [[Friedrich Schlegel]] ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch04-intellectual-friction-1]]).

## Recruited through Caroline

In 1795 he left a tutoring post in Amsterdam as French troops advanced and travelled to Braunschweig to court the widowed Caroline Böhmer, who had refused him several times ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch04-intellectual-friction-1]]). Schiller, struggling to keep *Horen* going, found him its most reliable contributor and asked how to bring him to Jena; Humboldt's answer was Caroline ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch03-finest-minds-1]], [[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch04-intellectual-friction-1]]). They married on 1 July 1796 and arrived in Jena on 8 July ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch04-intellectual-friction-2]]).

## The verse Shakespeare

Wulf gives the significance of the translation: prose had lost Shakespeare's dramatic power, the verse versions remain the standard German edition, and (partly on August Wilhelm's own later claim) they led Germans 'to adopt Shakespeare as a national poet'. His line: 'The recurring rhythm ... is the heartbeat of life' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch04-intellectual-friction-2]]). Caroline was his indispensable partner, and Wulf calls his later remark that she 'lacked ambition' 'a little disingenuous' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch06-splendid-circle-2]]). By 1799 they had translated ten plays in four years ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch14-schlegel-clique]]). His *Lectures on Dramatic Art and Literature* later carried the romantic/classical contrast into English Romanticism ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch08-handful-of-darkness]]); in a 1809 retrospect he described the group's aim to 'poeticise the increasingly mechanical clanking of the world' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-2]]).

## Horen, Athenaeum and feuds

Schiller dismissed him from *Horen* because of Friedrich's review, ending the friendship that had brought him to Jena ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch07-little-academy-2]]). He co-founded the *Athenaeum* under Friedrich's pressure, which strained his marriage ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch09-sublime-impertinence-1]]); one of his fragments said language should be 'republicanised through the power of the general will' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch09-sublime-impertinence-2]]). He parodied Schiller's 'The Dignity of Women' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch13-divorce-women-sex]]) and the Lord's Prayer against a hostile critic, and wrote 'We don't make enemies, we already have them' and 'People hate us - good!' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch15-new-confederation]], [[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-1]]).

## Marriage, grief and divorce

He grieved Auguste's death with Caroline, kneeling at her grave ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-1]]); they then agreed he had done his duty and she was no longer his lover, while still working together ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-2]]). His affair with Sophie Bernhardi in Berlin, money quarrels and her failed visit ended the marriage; Wulf hedges the cause ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-1]], [[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-2]]). Despite the separation he defended Schelling against the insinuation that he caused Auguste's death, a continuing friendship Wulf calls amazing ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-2]]).

## Synthesiser and carrier

In Berlin in 1802 he gave public lectures, open to women, presenting the Jena Set's ideas. Wulf's comparison: unlike Enlightenment thinkers for whom progress and the future were watchwords, he studied the past to make sense of the present, connecting across disciplines as [[alexander-von-humboldt]] compared plants across continents ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-2]]). He left with Madame de Staël for Coppet ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch19-current-exodus]]); his letter 'I am proud to be your property' prompts Wulf's comment that she 'wanted nothing less than total submission' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1]]). Through Staël's *Germany* he was one of the channels by which Jena ideas reached Europe ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-influence-selfish]]).
