# Ulama

The **ulama** (singular: *alim*) are Islamic religious scholars who serve as the custodians and interpreters of Islamic law (sharia) and theology. Across the pre-modern Islamic world — al-Andalus, the Ottoman Empire, the Safavid Empire, the Mughal Empire — the ulama held a distinctive form of [[Antimeme|antimemetic]] power.

## The Mechanism of Antimemetic Power

Monarchs in Islamic states issued decrees. The ulama *interpreted* and *applied* those decrees as law in daily life. This interpretive authority was:

- **Continuous**: not episodic legislation but constant, subtle application
- **Unattributable**: "the king decreed" — the interpretation was invisible
- **Adaptive**: allowed legal systems to evolve through interpretation without visible legislative acts
- **Profound**: shaped marriage, inheritance, commerce, criminal punishment, and public morality

As Sathe notes: "This interpretive authority, operating subtly within the legal and religious bureaucracy, allowed for continuous adaptation and influence without the visible, attributable acts of formal legislation, making it profoundly antimemetic."

## Examples Across Empires

- **Al-Andalus**: ulama shaped legal norms and public morality under the Umayyad caliphate
- **Ottomans**: the *mufti* and *qadi* systems applied sharia locally; the Şeyhülislam (Grand Mufti) was enormously powerful
- **Safavids**: Shi'a ulama were central to state legitimacy and legal enforcement
- **Mughals**: ulama interpreted Mughal decrees through Islamic jurisprudence in a predominantly non-Muslim empire

## Comparison to Other Antimemetic Structures

Unlike the [[Ming Dynasty Grand Secretaries]] (who were antimemetic by design — appointed specifically to be invisible), the ulama's antimemetic quality was structural: they occupied the interpretive layer between law and life, a layer that is by nature less visible than the legislative layer.

## Bayly: between public sphere and reforming state

C. A. Bayly's [[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world]] follows the religious scholars of the Islamic world through the long nineteenth century. It shows the interpretive layer this page describes being contested from several directions at once.

- **A public sphere of their own.** Bayly names an “Islamic public sphere” long centred on debate in the mosque square, the *madina*. It was intensified, not invented, by the crisis of the eighteenth century. Its cases include Wahhabi purification, Sufi restatement in Cairo and North Africa, and Shah Wali-Allah's legal and educational reform in Mughal India. He reads these as parallel, not derivative, modernities. The note also records Aurangzeb asserting Sunni sovereignty without doctrinal policing ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch02-critical-publics-conclusion]]).
- **Reform from within.** Before 1857, reformist currents around Shah Abd al-Aziz and Sayyid Ahmed Barelvi mostly avoided declaring holy war on colonial power and pursued moral and social reform “from within”. Bayly generalizes this into a largely pacific stance of the ulama across much of the Muslim world ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch04-losers-legitimacy-and-state]]).
- **Absorbed into the state.** The reformed Muslim states, from the Ottoman Tanzimat to Muhammad Ali's Egypt, welded the ulama and Sufi lineages such as the Qadariyya into the state's structure instead of holding them at arm's length ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch07b-typology-of-eurasian-states]]). Colonial rule absorbed Islamic legal officers into secular law codes, and the Ottoman state pushed to put “men loyal to the state” at the head of the religious hierarchy ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-imperial-religions]]). Secular rulers asserted control over the waqf endowments the ulama had administered ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-new-style-religion]]).
- **Or a rival sovereign.** Bayly's typology of non-centralized “statishness” includes a competing counter-state institution, with Islamic law as one example. In Iran, clerical religious charisma displaced the weaker, merely secular Qajar state ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch07a-defining-the-modern-state]]). Sharia and the modern state coexisted uneasily, most acutely in Iran, without either fully displacing the other ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch07b-typology-of-eurasian-states]]).

Read against the antimemetic framing above, Bayly's material shows the nineteenth century deciding whether the interpretive layer would be absorbed into the state, used as its instrument, or become a rival to it. That framing belongs to this page. Bayly himself writes as a comparative historian of religion and the state.
