# Magic as a spur to science: the will to operate (Yates)

The most explicit and most hedged thesis of [[yates-giordano-bruno]]. [[frances-yates]] does not claim that magic is science, or that the Magus's procedures led to scientific method. Her narrower claim is that the Renaissance Magus changed the will: it became dignified and religious to operate on the world.

## Chapter VIII: the operator in an unchanged cosmos

The cosmos of the Agrippan Magus is still the medieval one. What changes is Man, who is no longer only a pious spectator but one who seeks power. Yates reads Fludd's plate of the ape of nature as the emblem: Man has lost some Dignity but gained power, by learning the chain from earth to heaven (Magia) and the higher chain to the divine Name (Cabala). The Greeks did not want to operate, and the Middle Ages held that operating could only be devilish. Whether or not magic led to scientific procedure, the Magus changed that ([[yates-giordano-bruno-ch08-magic-and-science]]).

She follows three channels, each with caveats:

- **Number as master-key.** Pythagorean symbolism and Hebrew letter-conjuring will not of themselves yield working mathematics. Still, Pico, Reuchlin and Agrippa direct attention to number, and Giorgi's qualitative harmony prepares for mathematical thinking. Agrippa's scheme leaves room for real mechanics: Architas's flying pigeon and moving statues, and the "real artificial magic" that Campanella later names.
- **John Dee.** A serious mathematician who urged applied mathematics on Pico's authority, and also a practical Cabalist who summoned angels. For Yates the two belong together, and Dee shows the will to operate passing from religious magic into applied science.
- **The Hermetic sun.** Copernicus reached heliocentrism by mathematics, not magic, but introduced it in a Hermetic frame and cited Hermes on the sun as visible god. Yates cannot say whether the sun-cult was the emotional driver or a way to make the discovery acceptable ("perhaps both"). Bruno then turns the diagram back into a hieroglyph of divine mysteries.

## The conclusion: a history of motives

Historians of science can trace the stages toward Galileo, Yates writes, but not why interest in the workings of nature surged when it did. She proposes that a movement of the will originates an intellectual movement, and that "Hermes Trismegistus" names the cluster behind it: Ficinian Neoplatonism, Pico's Hermetism and Cabala, the sun as source of power, animated nature, number, the unity of the All, and the Christianising errors that made Hermes licit to study and operate with. She suggests viewing the scientific revolution in two phases, an animistic universe operated by magic followed by a mathematical universe operated by mechanics, and asks whether all science is a gnosis. She calls the long-view story "perhaps too beautiful and coherent to be quite true" ([[yates-giordano-bruno-ch22-fludd-controversies-2]]).

## Where the paths part: two kinds of number

The split between the two phases is sharpest in Kepler's quarrel with Fludd. Kepler was himself steeped in harmonic and Hermetic thought ("aut Pythagoras Hermetiset, aut Hermes Pythagoriset"), yet he saw that his difference from Fludd lay in the attitude to number. He applied quantitative measurement empirically to planetary motion, where Fludd used numerological relations binding macrocosm and microcosm. Yates credits Kepler with releasing mathematics from numerology, and his phrase "more Hermetico" names the other side ([[yates-giordano-bruno-ch22-fludd-controversies-1]], [[yates-giordano-bruno-ch13-hermetic-philosophy-1]]). Bruno is on the wrong side of that line. His *mathesis* reads Mordente's compass as a Pythagorean-Cabalist diagram, and Yates says he is "not at all in the line of the advance of mathematical and mechanical science" but a reactionary pushing the Copernican diagram back toward *mathesis* ([[yates-giordano-bruno-ch16-bruno-second-paris]], [[yates-giordano-bruno-ch17-bruno-germany]]).

## The cost of externalisation

The magician draws the world into himself and the scientist externalises and impersonalises it. Yates suggests that Descartes's dualism, with mind parked in the conarion, was a reaction against the Hermetic internalisation of the world in the *mens*, and that this is why the problem of mind fell so far behind the problem of matter ([[yates-giordano-bruno-ch22-fludd-controversies-2]]).

## World Machines readings, with their hedges

- The notes read the thesis as naming a precondition of the [[modernity-machine]]'s drive to act on and measure the world: a shift in what counts as a worthy human aim. Yates speaks of a religious and psychological source, not infrastructure ([[yates-giordano-bruno-ch08-magic-and-science]]).
- "Number as master-key" rhymes with [[Legibility]], and the will-before-method argument has the shape of [[legibility-as-precondition-not-cause]]. Both links are the notes' own, not Yates's.
- Bruno's Hermetic numerology sits before the separation of mathematics from meaning. It relates to [[Legibility]] only as its opposite: a figure read for revelation, not measurement ([[yates-giordano-bruno-ch16-bruno-second-paris]]).
- The externalisation argument resonates loosely with WMP's interest in what a legibility-optimising machine leaves out. Yates's claim is about epistemology and mind, not social control ([[yates-giordano-bruno-ch22-fludd-controversies-2]]).
