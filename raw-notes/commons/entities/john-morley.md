# John Morley

This page describes John Morley as author and critic, as the notes on *Diderot and the Encyclopædists* show him. It draws on Volume I (Preface, Chapters I–VIII) of that study; it gives no biography beyond what the volume itself reveals.

## The series and the method

Morley presents [[diderot-and-the-encyclopaedists]] as the close of a series of studies on the literary preparation for the French Revolution, following his volumes on [[voltaire]] and [[jean-jacques-rousseau]]. He says the book is more descriptive than those, more “transcript or analysis”, because the reader cannot be assumed to know Diderot's circle, and he sets out to counter Taine's “one-sided exaggerations” by bringing out the social significance and positive quality of the Encyclopædists. The 1878 Preface has an appended note of September 1878 on Franklin, Raynal and the Polly Baker speech ([[morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary]]).

His method mixes narrative, long translated extracts and running judgement. He treats books as symptoms of living social movement rather than causes of the controversies they belong to: Voltaire and Diderot did not become free-thinkers merely because of Bacon, Hobbes and Locke, and speculative literature's force “hangs on practical opportuneness” ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-1]]). He weighs evidence in the open. He gives Diderot the benefit of the doubt against a daughter's memoir he calls full of blunders ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-1]]); he rates as “not decisive” the story that Diderot suggested Rousseau's paradox against the arts and sciences ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-3]]); he corrects Voltaire's Versailles anecdote because Madame de Pompadour was dead before the relevant volumes appeared, while keeping its substance ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-4]]); and he doubts that Goethe's voucher settled the authenticity of Rameau's Nephew ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch8-rameaus-nephew]]).

## A Comtean frame

The book's central diagnosis, that the eighteenth-century insurrection joined the scientific idea to the social idea and lacked only the historic idea, is Comtean in vocabulary, and Morley states it as his own verdict. He cites Comte's praise of Diderot and of the “encyclopædic workshop” that united destructive schools in a constructive task, speaks of a “new spiritual power”, and rejects as a “gross fallacy” the view that only physical science improves the social condition ([[morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary]], [[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-1]]). His closing complaint about the Encyclopædia, that it never rose to a conception of the unity of the intellectual history of man, is the same positivist-historical desideratum ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-3]]). Positivism also shapes his moral sociology: a morality built on theology falls with the faith ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-3]]).

## Quarrels

- **Carlyle.** Morley disputes “our veteran humorist” that the Encyclopædical tree “has borne no fruit”, quotes Goethe's rebuke of those who hold Diderot cheaply, and calls the post-Revolution spiritual reaction of Wordsworth and Carlyle tainted by Philistinism ([[morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary]]). He still borrows Carlyle's verdict on the 1748 tale ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-3]]).
- **Taine.** The book is meant as a corrective to Taine's one-sided picture of the Encyclopædists ([[morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary]]).
- **The siege-engine reading.** He rejects the claim that the Encyclopædia was merely an engine of attack, a response to critics of his own day rather than a settled verdict ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-2]]).

## A Victorian vantage

Morley reads the eighteenth century from 1878. He calls the movement “in full progress” again in his own day, in materialism in the science of man, humanitarian legislation and naturalism in art, and the eighty years of transcendental reaction a “parenthesis” ([[morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary]]). He reads Saunderson's speech in the Letter on the Blind as a rough anticipation of the variability-and-adaptation reasoning that was “the familiar discussion of the day”, and adds his own argument from the “marvels of mis-adaptation” ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-2]]). He finds Cobden anticipated in the Legislator article ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-3]]), contrasts the French Church with English Latitudinarians and later Anglican breadth ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-1]]), and stresses, writing as an Englishman, that both the parent idea (Bacon) and the occasion (Chambers) of the Encyclopædia were English ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-1]]). He sets English prosecutions for heresy, harsh but conducted by law, against the French lettre de cachet, “that vile substitute for prosecution” ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-3]]). He corrects Diderot's science and ethics from the later standpoint too, for example that no perfection of touch could disclose the phenomena of light ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-1]]).

## The moralist

The notes flag Morley's moral register throughout. He judges Diderot's character warmly (pity as his “divine gift”, a man who judged people “wholly to what they were”) and sets it against the culture-affectation with which, in Morley's phrase, Goethe “infected part of the world” ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-1]], [[morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-2]]). He regrets Diderot's dispersiveness, calls his mystifications “odious” and the Liberty article insincere, and condemns the 1748 tale, while declining to blame the household's irregularities on the new opinions ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-1]], [[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-3]], [[morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-2]]). His remarks on women pair a reforming-liberal diagnosis of the narrowness imposed on them with a dated confidence about their inner life ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-3]], [[morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-1]]). As a critic he holds that virtue wins through sympathy, not homily, and that laughter can serve a moral end ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch7-the-stage-1]]); he calls the long controversy over dramatic rules a “grotesque monument of sterility” ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch7-the-stage-2]]). His most sweeping verdict comes with Rameau's Nephew: the old society was “immoral by formula and depraved on principle”, and the Jacobin flood was as indispensable to France as the barbarian flood to Rome, a reading the notes flag as dated ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch8-rameaus-nephew]]).
