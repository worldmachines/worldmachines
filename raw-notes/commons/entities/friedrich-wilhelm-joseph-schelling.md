# Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling (in Wulf's Magnificent Rebels)

Schelling is the Jena Set's philosopher of nature in [[wulf-magnificent-rebels]]. His doctrine is gathered in [[naturphilosophie-wulf]]; this page follows the person as Wulf's narrative shows him.

## Tübingen and the call to Jena

Wulf describes the Tübinger Stift, where Schelling studied with Hölderlin and Hegel, as a will-breaking institution (a student's phrase: 'a miserable cog in a slave machine'), against which the three celebrated the Revolution. Their one-page resolution wanted to unite what had been divided, make philosophy sensual and poeticise the sciences, and opened on the Ich as absolutely free ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch10-symphilosophy]]). Wulf cites Schelling's obsequious petition to his ruler as evidence of petty despotism ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-1]]), and quotes his phrase for a 'revolution brought about by philosophy' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-2]]). Goethe invited him to Jena reluctantly, as a possible mediator between Fichte's idealism and his own realism ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch10-symphilosophy]]).

## Lecturer and 'heartbeat of Romanticism'

From autumn 1798 Schelling lectured that self and nature are identical and nature one universal organism; students received him almost religiously, and Wulf, in her own voice, calls his philosophy 'the heartbeat of Romanticism' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch11-naturphilosophie]]). His *System of Transcendental Idealism* made art the union of self and nature ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch14-schlegel-clique]]); his 1802 lectures on the 'Philosophy of Art', at twenty-seven and at the height of his fame, filled halls ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-2]]). He also shared the circle's combative mood: he demanded to review his own work and wrote a bawdy poem lampooning the friends' new religion ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch15-new-confederation]]). Like Fichte, he read his own life as proof that an empowered Ich could steer its destiny ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch12-atheism-dispute]]).

## Caroline, grief and the break with Fichte

Caroline's attachment to him grows through 1798-99 ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch11-naturphilosophie]], [[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch14-schlegel-clique]]). After her daughter Auguste's death at Bocklet in July 1800 (Dorothea Veit's letters blamed him; the notes treat her claims as hers) he sank into guilt and talk of suicide, and Caroline asked Goethe to draw him out ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-2]], [[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-1]]). Wulf's psychological reading is that success had not prepared him for darkness, so he attacked Caroline instead of opening his heart ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-2]]). In Bamberg he met Brunonian medicine, which treated the body as an organism in balance ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-2]]). His quarrel with Fichte, triggered by Friedrich Schlegel's gossip, hardened into a doctrinal split: a Spinoza-inspired Absolute preceding subject and object, an 'Idealism of Nature' against Fichte's 'Idealism of the Ich' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-1]]). Hegel defended him in print while privately outgrowing him ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-1]]).

## After Jena

After the Schlegels' divorce was granted on 17 May 1803, Caroline left Jena with Schelling and married him a month later ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-2]]). He moved to Würzburg, and students followed ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch19-current-exodus]]). He hoped Napoleon would sweep away despotic monarchies and held that such governments had to fall ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch20-french-in-town-1]], [[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch20-french-in-town-2]]). Caroline died in 1809 while they lived in Munich ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1]]). He later grumbled that without him there would be no Hegelians ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-2]]). Through Coleridge (who absorbed and, by De Quincey's charge, partly plagiarised him, a claim Wulf calls 'a bit of an exaggeration' with 'some truth in it') his ideas reached English and American Romanticism ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-influence-selfish]]). See also [[caroline-schelling]] and [[Naturphilosophie]].
