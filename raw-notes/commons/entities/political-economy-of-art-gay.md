# The Political Economy of Art (Gay)

**Source**: [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5]], Chapters One (*The Political Economy of Art*) and Two (*The Geography of Taste*), with evidence drawn from the rest of the volume. Gay borrows the phrase from Ruskin ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s1-battles-of-the-budgets]]).

## Ruskin's Phrase, Priced

Gay prices the bourgeois hierarchy literally. His verdict is that "the supreme determinant was of course money—or the lack of it" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s1-battles-of-the-budgets]]).

- **Germany.**
  - Craftsmen earned about 1,500 marks a year, so Liebermann's *Die Gänserupferinnen* (3,000 marks) cost them two years' labor.
  - Gymnasium teachers earned about 2,000 marks and were partly paid in honorific rank.
  - Solid Bürger households lived on about 6,000 marks, which was the conductor Hermann Levi's salary.
  - Prussian ministers earned 30,000 marks. Only buyers at that level could commission Makart (14,000 marks) or Anton von Werner (60,000 marks).
  - Status was itself a tax. The 6,000-mark households were "often beset by money worries" because of pianos, servants, stationery and dowries ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s1-battles-of-the-budgets]]).
  - In 1889 a Berlin civil servant's family spent barely one percent of the father's salary on theater, concerts and reading ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s2-affordable-masterpieces]]).
- **Britain and France.** Trollope's post-office clerk rose from £90 to £1,400 over a career. French schoolteachers earned 1,500 francs, prefects 12,000 and presiding judges 25,000, so "bon bourgeois" buying power for culture can be read straight off occupational rank ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s3-limits-and-possibilities]]).
- **The buying elite.** For major art it was "a sport for an elite within an elite," but it was visibly growing ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s1-battles-of-the-budgets]]).

## Why Taste Pluralized

Gay follows the art historian Friedrich Haack. The French Revolution's "stylistic free trade" broke the "dictatorship over taste" that state, nobility and church had long held jointly. Gay adds a cause of his own: the mass entry of the bourgeoisie into cultural consumption. Musset saw only "caprice" at the 1836 Salon. A critic reviewing the 1845 Salon called the same condition a virtue, since "originality is the precondition of art." Baudelaire's "as many sorts of beauty as there are habitual ways of seeking happiness" became "a mainstay of late-nineteenth-century modernism" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s1-battles-of-the-budgets]]).

## Tiered Access

- **One object at several prices.** In 1848 Redgrave's teapot was sold in silver, silver plate and Britannia metal. Opera and theater seats were graded. Dickens sold his novels in shilling monthly numbers ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s3-limits-and-possibilities]]). Munich's ticket ladders ran down to named "poverty-stricken Wagnerites" and a 1-mark ceiling at the new Philharmonic ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s2-affordable-masterpieces]]). The Hallé charged 2s. 6d. reserved and a shilling unreserved, "slouching toward democracy" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch02-s1-dividends-from-commerce]]).
- **Reproductions.** Lithography, engraving and photography put "masterpieces" within reach ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s2-affordable-masterpieces]]). Gambart paid £1,500 and £5,500 for engraving rights, and Fildes's *The Doctor* sold "by the untold thousands." Reproduction extended the market downward "without equalizing it" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s3-limits-and-possibilities]]). Barbey d'Aurevilly attacked photography as "that democracy of the portrait." Gay calls this snobbish but concedes that photography "cheapened" culture ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s2-affordable-masterpieces]]). Later, reproduction became a way to defang modernism: Degas prettified, Winslow Homer "vulgarized into calendar art" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s1-a-profusion-of-alternatives]]).

## Money Is Not Taste

"It would be naive to equate money with taste." A manufacturer's larger culture budget "says little about what the manufacturer ... would do with the cash." Petty bourgeois with no money at all could have "a genuine feeling for what they saw, heard, or read" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s2-affordable-masterpieces]]). Collecting was never "the undisputed monopoly of plutocrats" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s1-a-map-of-motives]]). Chocquet, a modestly paid customhouse clerk, "sacrificed to his obsession all he had" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s2-collecting-as-autobiography]]).

## The Market as the Real Battlefield

- **Prices.** In the 1870s–80s Durand-Ruel bought Sisleys, Renoirs and Pissarros for 200–300 francs. As late as 1881 Monet took 300 francs a canvas, which made an Impressionist "a possible luxury for the middling middle classes." Gérôme, Bouguereau and Cabanel commanded tens of thousands of francs and also sat on the juries. Gérôme had "a compelling voice in French artistic politics" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s3-limits-and-possibilities]]).
- **Advocacy.** Thoré's campaign lifted Vermeer's prices from roughly $200–2,500 in his lifetime to $8,000 by 1888 ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch03-s2-master-craftsmen]]). Caillebotte used his wealth to raise his painter friends' prices ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch05-s3-the-perspiring-philistine-on-trial]]). Critics' reach still ran second to entertainment. The *Constitutionnel* won back its subscribers by paying Eugène Sue 100,000 francs, not through its critics ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch03-s2-master-craftsmen]]).
- **Spare money.** French per capita income nearly tripled between 1825 and 1914, and the birthrate fell from 31.4 to 18.8 per thousand. Together these freed income for "wall decorations, a small private library, or an occasional concert" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s3-causes-and-effects]]).
- **Modernism's dependence.** Modernist architecture needed rich clients before a sketch could become a building: Gaudí's financiers, AEG's commission to Behrens, Wright's Chicago businessmen ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s4-against-the-need-for-probability]]). Some architects simply gave in to the market. Gilbert Scott and Cuypers dropped Gothic for whatever style would win the commission ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s3-causes-and-effects]]).
- **A graduated middle.** Buyers did not have to choose between Bouguereau and Braque. The *juste milieu* let them hold "modernist pretensions" while staying "safely within the accepted canon" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s2-modernists-vs-modernists]]).

## The Geography of Taste

"The geography of taste and the geography of politics and culture were inextricably intertwined" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s1-battles-of-the-budgets]]).

- **Manchester.** There was no court, aristocracy or state to lean on. A circle of "cultured capitalists," many of them German-Jewish manufacturers, brought Charles Hallé over. His orchestra was a profit-seeking business, clearing over £2,000 a year by 1866. The Portico, Owens College, the Rylands Library and the Whitworth came from individual legacies and committees of subscribers. Art lagged behind music. Collectors were gulled by "Picture Jockeys," and the Whitworth showed "crippling uncertainty of touch" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch02-s1-dividends-from-commerce]]).
- **Munich.** Culture was handed down by the Wittelsbachs. The Kunstverein needed a royal charter to exist, and as its membership swelled it "only too often served to lower the taste of its artists" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch02-s2-majestic-dispensations]]).
- **Vienna and Paris.** Hanslick's "purely bourgeois" Musikfreunde turn out to have depended on archdukes. In Paris Thoré diagnosed "governmental logic," and the Salon des Refusés was "a rebellion on imperial command" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch02-s2-majestic-dispensations]]).
- **Gay's credo.** Manchester and Munich are "extreme types." The two civic plaques make the point: Munich's Alte Pinakothek credits "the noble disposition of its rulers," Birmingham's gallery "the gains of Industry" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch02-s2-majestic-dispensations]]).
- **Reception varied by place too.** Courbet was better received in Munich than in Paris, and Mackintosh better in Vienna than in Glasgow ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-bexp-s3-confusing-confrontations]]). Tolerance for the avant-garde differed from city to city ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s2-modernists-vs-modernists]]).
- **The Coda's typology.** Bourgeois who funded culture themselves (Manchester), bourgeois who relied on a ruling house (Munich), and cities where private and state support competed (Paris, Berlin) ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-coda-a-bourgeois-experience]]).

## Resonances (hedged)

- **[[gramsci-gap]].** The fall of the "dictatorship over taste" opened a gap at the scale of one domain. It closed almost at once, "filled ... by a new, blunter organizing principle: money" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s1-battles-of-the-budgets]]).
- **[[Legibility]].** Munich's price ladders sorted a public by income and profession. The note calls this a half-resonance, since it is commercial price discrimination rather than cadastral or statistical legibility ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s2-affordable-masterpieces]]).
- **[[modernity-machine]] and [[divergence-machine]].** Court-directed Munich against commercial Manchester reads as "a miniature machine contest," though Gay himself insists the two types interpenetrate ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch02-s2-majestic-dispensations]]).
- **[[the-mass-as-fiction]].** Berlioz's and Joachim's "public en gros" is a flattening category. It erases the earnest, untrained responses Gay has just documented ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s2-affordable-masterpieces]]).
