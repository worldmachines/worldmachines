# H. G. Wells

**Treated at length in**: John Carey, *The Intellectuals and the Masses* (1992), two consecutive chapters — [[carey-intellectuals-and-masses-ch6-wells-getting-rid-of-people]] and [[carey-intellectuals-and-masses-ch7-wells-against-wells]]. See [[carey-intellectuals-and-masses]].

Wells is the book's central case because he is the only figure Carey treats twice: once as prosecution, once as defense. The two chapters are not a retraction of each other — they are the argument.

## The Origin Wound

Carey traces Wells's hatred of the suburban masses to one biographical fact: Bromley's population nearly tripled in his boyhood as railway-driven development buried the fields and streams of his early memory. The semi-autobiographical *New Machiavelli* passage in which the child realizes "building was the enemy" recurs, transposed, across the novels — *Tono-Bungay*'s suburbs as a "tumorous growth," *Ann Veronica*'s villas like "bright fungoid growth." Carey treats this not as incidental biography but as the psychological engine of Wells's mature politics, and notes that the settings later chosen for annihilation — London's suburbs, by name — make the fiction a repeated, escalating revenge on Bromley.

## The Program

*Anticipations* (1901) fuses Malthusian scarcity with Darwinian selection into something Wells presents as ethics rather than cruelty: the "nation that most resolutely picks over, educates, sterilizes, exports, or poisons its People of the Abyss" will be in the ascendant. Human equality is explicitly denied; extending it to "inferior" masses means being "swamped in their fecundity" — a vocabulary of biological threat rather than political disagreement.

Carey refuses to let euphemism launder the content. The New Republic kills the unfit "with an opiate" rather than brutally, which is a horror precisely because of its bureaucratic gentleness. The logic extends explicitly to race: the "swarms of black, and brown, and dirty-white, and yellow people" who cannot meet the New Republic's efficiency standard must simply "go." Wells's later, milder proposals — means-tested parenthood, forced labour for debt, sterilization on a second offence in *A Modern Utopia* — are shown as softened versions of the same eliminationist premise, not a repudiation of it. Overpopulation is persistently gendered female: unchecked fertility and consumerist vanity are woman's fault, up to fantasies of sex-selection reducing women "to a minority."

## Total Legibility as Precondition

*A Modern Utopia*'s world state administers 1,500 million people through thumbprints, photographs, and a central Paris index tracking births, marriages, deaths and convictions for the entire globe — a fantasy of complete population legibility imagined decades before the computing power to realize it existed. Carey's point, and the WMP one, is that the register is not a side-effect of the utopia but its precondition: who may breed, who is sterilized, who is exiled is decided *through* the index. This is [[legibility-as-machine-core]] in its purest fictional form, and one of the clearest bridges from the [[modernity-machine]]'s administrative drive to [[necropolitics]].

## The Hinge Around 1910: Freedom to System

Carey identifies a turn. Before roughly 1910 Wells's imagination runs on freedom — the open road, the bicycle, science fiction's transgression of natural law, all liberating his lower-middle-class heroes into a dream of the English countryside. After it, population anxiety pushes him toward system: world government, single currency, and crucially categorization. "Once system is accepted, categorization follows, and abstractions like 'the masses' swallow up individual Mr Pollys and Mr Lewishams."

The self-refutation is documented: Wells's own 1903 philosophical paper rejected classification as a "fallacy... in what is quite conceivably a universe of uniques" — a position that, if held, invalidates the racial and eugenic hierarchies his later thought depends on. Forty years later his D.Sc. thesis argues the opposite, that individual identity is illusory and only the collective species is real. The unresolved argument between these two Wellses is a [[Conatus]] problem staged inside one head: the individual's striving to persist as itself against a system — his own — that wants to average it into a type.

## Failures as His Only Successes

Carey's defense is that "despite his contempt for failures, failures are his only successes." Mr Polly, Kipps, Hoopdriver and Lewisham — all victims of "lost educational opportunities" — carry Wells's warmth precisely because they lack the qualities he elsewhere insists should rule the world. His pro-clerk writing (rescuing stockbrokers' clerks) belongs to the small counter-tradition that answered the anti-clerk slur ([[carey-intellectuals-and-masses-ch3-suburbs-and-clerks]]).

Carey does not let the sympathy off the hook: it infantilizes. Polly, Kipps and Hoopdriver are repeatedly described as children, and the narrator's declared love for "my Kippses" as "little pink strips of quivering, living stuff" is, Carey notes drily, "not the way we talk about equals." Wells also displaces his harshest anti-mass positions onto characters marked as sinister (Ostrog) or admirable (Professor Keppel's "I hate common humanity... Clear the earth of them!"), which Carey reads as a way of airing views "but not to be held accountable for them" — evidence that Wells's story-telling gift distrusted the creeds his intellect produced.

## Two Marginal Notes

- It is Wells's testimony that Carey credits for the allegations of Gissing's cruelty toward his second wife, including beatings with stair rods ([[carey-intellectuals-and-masses-ch5-gissing-ineducable-masses]]).
- Wells got the demography exactly backwards: he predicted world population would fall below a billion by 1960; it passed 4 billion by 1975 ([[carey-intellectuals-and-masses-postscript]]). Carey notes that the "solutions" later proposed by demographers — war, plague, famine — echo both Wells's fiction and *Mein Kampf*.
