Across a dozen of Montaigne's *Essays* ([[montaigne-complete-essays]]) one argument recurs: renown and rank are set around a man rather than in him. He holds it firmly while admitting he cannot shake the appetite himself.

## A universal folly

In I.41 the solicitude of reputation and glory is the most universally received of follies: reason accuses its vanity without dislodging it, and even writers who attack glory want their names on their books. Honour, unlike goods and lives, is almost never lent. The exempla of men who handed their credit to someone else, Edward at Crecy among them, are borrowed and offered as rare exceptions ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch41-43-not-communicate-mans]]). Of Glory supplies the governing distinction. Glory belongs to the name, a piece joined from outside, and so is fit for God alone. Montaigne reports the ancient quarrel and sides with the contempt of glory as “very true and rational”. Then, by his own interpretation, he finds even Epicurus's dying letter and will betraying a wish for a name: “we are, I know not how, double in ourselves” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch16-glory]]).

## Why the name is no measure

Of Glory gives four reasons in his own voice:

- **Virtue without witnesses.** If virtue is sought for the honour that attends it, men need be virtuous only in public. Conscience, not reputation, is the true seat of virtue, and he reports that he has himself often returned a trust.
- **Fortune.** Reputation is fortune's work. Glory is a shadow that sometimes outruns the body, and the least lustrous occasions in the wars are often the most dangerous.
- **The judges.** We leave our lives to “the rabble, the mother of ignorance, injustice, and inconstancy”, and strangers see only countenance, never the heart.
- **Oblivion.** Of the valiant men of France over fifteen hundred years not a hundred are known.

His own claim is modest: “All the glory that I pretend to derive from my life is that I have lived it in quiet”. Yet the essay also runs a counter-current it does not resolve. If the false opinion of glory keeps men to their duty, let it be cherished, and every legislator has set a god or fable at the origin of his laws ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch16-glory]]).

## Honour as a currency

Of Recompenses of Honour treats honours as currency. Valueless distinctions (crowns, titles, orders of knighthood) are “a pretty invention” that rewards merit at no cost. Their worth lies in rarity and in being kept apart from money, which pays vices too. The Order of St. Michael has been cheapened by lavish distribution, and a new order cannot be founded in so tumultuous a season. Valour, spread by custom through the civil wars, is only one little ray of the true virtue of the philosophers ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch07-recompenses-honour]]).

## Rank as costume

I.42 asks why we praise a horse for its own qualities and never for its caparison, yet judge a man by his train, palace and revenue. Montaigne's test is to strip him to his shirt. He has often pitied kings rather than envied them, since they are no happier than other men and cannot know whether they are loved: “The honour we receive from those that fear us is not honour” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch41-43-not-communicate-mans]]). The Apology levels the motives of the great: “The souls of emperors and cobblers are cast in the same mould” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-4]]). Of the Inconvenience of Greatness adds that power destroys the honest contest on which pleasure and virtue depend. Everyone lets the prince win, flattery deafens him, and courtiers copy even his vices. Montaigne approves the wise men who yielded to emperors rather than argue ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch07-inconvenience-greatness]]). Of the Art of Conference turns the point on the onlookers. Dignities are conferred more by fortune than by merit, we judge a promoted man “as we do by counters”, and “my reason is not obliged to bow and bend; my knees are” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch08-art-conference-2]]).

## What becomes a ruler

Fine speaking, music or painting is no honour to a prince unless it stands behind the qualities proper to his office. Montaigne reads the begging of historians for a place in the record by Cicero and the younger Pliny as meanness of spirit ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch39-consideration-cicero]]). This is close to the observation [[better-company-than-caesar]] takes from Montaigne about great men wanting to be known outside their competence, though the note does not draw that link. Of Coaches calls it a kind of pusillanimity in monarchs to seek honour by lavish expense among their own subjects. A king has nothing properly his own, liberality is a weak royal virtue that breeds ingratitude, and justice is the true one; the money would be better spent on ports, walls, hospitals and roads ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch06-coaches-1]]). Ranking the most excellent men, he calls glory only a part of the substance of the thing ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch36-most-excellent-men]]), and on Caesar's slowing with age he allows “some moderation, some satiety” in its pursuit ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch34-observation-means-carry-war]]).

## His own refusals

Montaigne declines ambition ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch17-presumption-2]]) and prefers a moderate station, while warning that refusing greatness may hide “worse ambition than even in the desire itself” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch07-inconvenience-greatness]]). As a city officer he defends a quiet term against a public that, “if they hear no noise, they think men sleep” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch10-managing-will-2]]). His self-report is on [[michel-de-montaigne]].

## World Machines resonances, hedged

The legislators' founding fables touch the project's interest in institutions held up by shared fictions ([[world-machines]]), though Montaigne treats them as a tolerated fraud, not as infrastructure ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch16-glory]]). The debasement of the Order of St. Michael is a diagnosis of one institution's loss of function, with no cyclical model behind it ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch07-recompenses-honour]]). Rank as costume, and the preference for visible over silent public work, are read suggestively against [[Legibility]], with the hedge that Montaigne's point is moral, not about institutions as machines ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch41-43-not-communicate-mans]], [[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch10-managing-will-2]]). For how fortune undoes the reading of results as merit, see [[outcomes-are-not-verdicts-montaigne]].
