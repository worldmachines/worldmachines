# Necropolitics: Carey's Literary Prehistory

Extends [[necropolitics]] with material from John Carey's *The Intellectuals and the Masses* (1992); see [[carey-intellectuals-and-masses]].

## The Literary Prehistory: Carey on the Mass-Concept

Mbembe and Foucault analyze necropolitical power once it is operating — the camp, the colony, the register, the zone of exception. John Carey's *The Intellectuals and the Masses* (1992) supplies something the framework mostly assumes: the **discursive preparation**, documented, dated and attributed, that made the administrative act available.

**The theological source of the disposable remainder.** Carey traces "the mass" to Augustine's *massa damnata* / *massa perditionis* — the condemned bulk of humanity minus the elect few — and draws the line forward to the crematoria as "this well-established Christian precedent for disposing of the surplus 'mass' by combustion" ([[carey-intellectuals-and-masses-preface]]). Necropolitics has a doctrinal ancestor in the idea of a human surplus whose destruction costs nothing.

**The "not fully alive" premise.** The operative move in Carey's material is not the assertion that some people should die but the prior assertion that they are already dead: Hardy's division of "souls and machines," Eliot's crowds on London Bridge, Lawrence ranking himself above "the Mexican who drives the wagon for me" ([[carey-intellectuals-and-masses-ch1-revolt-of-the-masses]]). Once the mass is imagined as soulless, its elimination is imaginatively costless — which is precisely the threshold condition necropolitics describes, established rhetorically before any state reaches it administratively.

**The bacterial metaphor as a transmission line.** Le Bon's crowd theory fused with Pasteur's bacterial model of society, passed through Freud's *Group Psychology*, and arrived at Hitler's "Jewish virus" ([[carey-intellectuals-and-masses-ch2-rewriting-the-masses]]). A defensive metaphor at one end of the chain is literal extermination logic at the other. Carey's contribution is to show the chain unbroken and to name its respectable links.

**Register before killing.** Wells's *A Modern Utopia* administers 1,500 million people through thumbprints, photographs and a central Paris index of births, marriages, deaths and convictions — the mechanism by which who may breed, who is sterilized and who is exiled is decided. The euphemism matters too: the New Republic kills the unfit "with an opiate," and the explicitly racial target is "swarms of black, and brown, and dirty-white, and yellow people" who must simply "go" ([[carey-intellectuals-and-masses-ch6-wells-getting-rid-of-people]]). This is bureaucratic gentleness as a necropolitical style — the same organized abandonment, minus the spectacle. See [[h-g-wells]].

**The terminus stated.** Carey's severest claim is that industrialized extermination became thinkable only after it was accepted that Jews were a mass, "not fully alive people," which made transportation, incineration and the manufacture of fertilizer from ashes seem merely proportionate ([[carey-intellectuals-and-masses-ch9b-hitlers-intellectual-programme]]). He explicitly implicates the literary intelligentsia's decades of before-the-fact rhetoric about the soulless, crowd-like, cremation-worthy mass as preparing the ground: "*Mein Kampf* was not, in many respects, a deviant work but one firmly rooted in European intellectual orthodoxy."

**Why it extends the concept.** It adds a stage upstream of both Foucault's dispositif and Mbembe's zone of exception, and it is not merely historical. Aggregation is the enabling operation: populations become manageable, and eliminable, only once rendered as countable figures. Carey's own postscript follows that logic into the present — demographers proposing war, plague and famine as "solutions," the blaming of "irresponsibly procreative" populations, and Bogotá's death squads hunting street children as the point where the statistic becomes an administrative act.
