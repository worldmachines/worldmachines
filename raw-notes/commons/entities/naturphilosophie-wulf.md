# Naturphilosophie (Wulf)

Extends [[Naturphilosophie]] with the account in [[wulf-magnificent-rebels]], where Schelling's philosophy of nature is, in Wulf's own strong phrase, 'the heartbeat of Romanticism' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch11-naturphilosophie]]). The notes carry the claim as hers.

## Seeds: morphology and galvanism

The first Goethe-Schiller conversation turns on Goethe's primordial plant (behind variety, unity) and the men's shared dislike of botany as a fragmentary way of looking at nature ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch01-happy-event-2]]). In spring 1797 Goethe and Alexander von Humboldt electrocuted frogs in Jena's anatomy theatre, inside a European question about whether living matter obeys different laws from inert matter; Wulf presents Newton's inert matter and Descartes's animal-machines as under challenge ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch07-little-academy-1]]). Goethe distinguished an internal force (the Urform) from the external force of environment, and Wulf compares him to Lamarck and Darwin as recognising adaptation without divine plan ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch07-little-academy-1]]). She argues the Goethe-Humboldt influence 'worked both ways' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch07-little-academy-2]]).

## Schelling's move

The Tübingen resolution already wanted to poeticise the sciences, and Schelling's point of departure from Fichte was that nature and the Ich form an interconnected whole ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch10-symphilosophy]]). In his Jena lectures self and nature are identical; nature is 'one universal organism'; 'Mind is invisible nature, while nature is visible mind', so nature is knowable from within, unlike Kant's thing-in-itself ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch11-naturphilosophie]]). Ritter's galvanic frog experiments seemed to the friends to prove the single organism; Novalis recorded Ritter's search for the 'real soul of the world' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch15-new-confederation]]).

## Art as the union of self and nature

Schelling remained, in Wulf's account, an idealist who held that knowledge originates in experience ('every experiment is a question posed to nature'). His *System of Transcendental Idealism* made the objective world the mind's 'original, still-unconscious poetry', revealed in art; Wulf adds her own gloss that this holism anticipates today's interdisciplinary research ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch14-schlegel-clique]]). In the 1802 'Philosophy of Art' lectures he said nature is truly comprehended through artists' work rather than scientific treatises, and mocked the English for valuing mathematics only for 'Spinning Jennies & Stocking-weaving machines' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-2]]).

## The Absolute against the Ich

Schelling's break with Fichte turned on whether the Ich can found the world. Drawing on [[baruch-spinoza]], he posited the Absolute, the unity before it divides into self and nature; Wulf glosses it as an expansion of Goethe's Urform ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-1]]). Goethe's fever dreams in 1801 were dominated by the Naturphilosophie, and Wulf asks, without answering, whether his relief at being 'back in my own self' meant escape from 'the dizzying dangers of an unconstrained self' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-2]]). Hegel's *Phenomenology* is framed as a reckoning with Schelling and his disciples ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-2]]).

## Carriers

- **Humboldt.** Wulf juxtaposes his rainforest vision of 'one great organism in which everything was connected' with the Jena circle's disintegration ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-1]]); she says he 'stayed true to the spirit of Jena', using feeling and imagination alongside forty-two instruments ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch19-current-exodus]]), called Naturphilosophie 'revolutionary' and 'quietly opened the door to subjectivity', and wrote of nature as 'not a dead aggregate' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1]]). See [[alexander-von-humboldt]] and [[wulf-invention-of-nature]].
- **Anglo-American Romanticism.** Coleridge's revised 'The Eolian Harp' is 'Schelling's Naturphilosophie writ in verse'; the plagiarism charge is one Wulf qualifies. Emerson's *Nature*, Thoreau (whose Walden Humboldt reshaped), Wordsworth, Whitman, Caspar David Friedrich and Tyutchev follow. Wulf names the unity of mankind and nature as 'at the core of Romanticism' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-influence-selfish]]).

## Hedges

One note calls Wulf's picture of a distancing, mechanistic Enlightenment 'somewhat one-sided', and reads her claim that travellers began to feel rather than observe nature as her own inference ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch11-naturphilosophie]]). Schelling's encounter with Brunonian medicine is a small echo of organism-thinking that Wulf does not draw ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-2]]). The notes treat the anti-reductive stance as rhyming with [[Legibility]] critique only by loose analogy ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch14-schlegel-clique]]). See also [[friedrich-wilhelm-joseph-schelling]] and [[poeticising-the-mechanical-world-wulf]].
