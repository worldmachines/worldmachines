# Robert Blum

**Robert Blum** was the Leipzig democrat whom Clark follows from the Vormärz to his execution in Vienna after the October rising of 1848, as a case study in how a leader of 1848 was formed and destroyed ([[clark-revolutionary-spring]]). This page is limited to Clark's treatment as the notes record it.

## Formation

Blum rose from poverty and a Catholic upbringing through theatre administration, oratory, publishing and autodidact habits. He became a democrat for whom 'unity' linked freedom, progress and justice, and who read 'divide and rule' as the reactionary state's technique ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch03-political-ferment-in-germany]]). He was tied, with Struve, to the dissenting German-Catholic movement, which had 60,000 members by 1847 ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch02-conservatives-religion]]). Clark also cites his letters about love and duty as an instance of the 'entirely politicized personality' ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch03-clandestinity-national-insurrection]]).

## Leipzig, August 1845

After the Leipzig shootings of 12 August 1845, Blum urged calm and channelled the crowd to City Hall. Clark says the tactic foreshadowed how city governments became 'waystations to revolution' in 1848, and calls the moment 'a foretaste of the alchemy of revolution that would ultimately consume Blum's life'. He frames Blum, with hedges, as one man briefly holding a city's fortunes, a case of contingent individuals rather than a great-man thesis ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch03-political-ferment-in-germany]]).

## A radical loyal to procedure

Blum was unequivocally a radical but valued the procedures of representative democracy. He refused Hecker's walkout and condemned the Baden rising in a letter to his wife as a crime against 'the People' ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch07-radical-breakaway]]). His faction split over the Polish question, and he compared German fragmentation to 'a malignant tumour' ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch07-national-questions-2]]).

## Vienna and death

Blum, a Frankfurt deputy, went to Vienna as to the last chance of the German revolution. He was arrested, court-martialled and executed; Windischgrätz and Schwarzenberg were the architects of both his downfall and the monarchy's renewal under Franz Joseph, and Hübner's portrait of him was fabricated and distorted. Clark reads the farewell letter as a relic that made Blum a private man who entered public life, heroism 'in a bourgeois key'; he and the German revolution were extinguished together ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch08-empire-strikes-back-2]]). The Introduction offers Blum's tear as 'politics in a bourgeois key', against Bismarck's public tears and Haynau's vengefulness ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-introduction]]). He stands among the named dead to whom Clark gives the last word of the counter-revolution chapter ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch08-realism-and-the-dead]]).

## Resonances (hedged)

The notes compare Blum's private, domestic register to the middle-class feeling described in [[the-bourgeois-experience]], though Clark does not draw that link himself. Blum's procedural radicalism is one of the fault lines traced in [[entropy-of-revolution-clark]].
