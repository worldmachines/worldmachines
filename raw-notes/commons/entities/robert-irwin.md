# Robert Irwin

Author of [[irwin-ibn-khaldun]] (*Ibn Khaldun: An Intellectual Biography*, Princeton, 2018). This page is scoped to what the reading notes show of Irwin as the author of that book: his stance, method, voice and interlocutors.

## Stance

Irwin's governing refusal is to make [[ibn-khaldun]] relevant. He allows modern writings to clarify Ibn Khaldun by contrast, but not the reverse, and says the “irrelevance” is what makes him worth reading: a premodern account of society in which causation rests on God's will can give a modern reader “a fresher and perhaps more critical eye” on their own world ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-preface]]). His closing chapter turns this into a charge against earlier accounts, which “exaggerated Ibn Khaldun's rationality and posited an essentially secular frame of mind” by discarding the jurisprudence, occultism, futurology and bizarre science ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch11-ending-up]]). He calls the book a “necromantic pursuit”, a kind of séance.

## Method

- **Frame before comparison.** Irwin places each idea in its genre and institution first: ruins as Qur’anic ‘ibra ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch01-ruins-black-death]]), political thought as mirrors-for-princes ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch03-wisdom-literature-ibar]]), historical method as Maliki ‘usul ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch04-aristotle-theology-maliki-law]]), the Ta‘rif as a fahrasa-style curriculum vitae in a “genealogy of authority” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch05-autobiography-outsider]]).
- **Theory held against evidence.** He tests Ibn Khaldun's claims against what Ibn Khaldun lived through: nomad health against Lawrence, Thesiger and others ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch03-nomads-asabiyya]]); a tribe-free Egypt against the tribes of the 1389 civil war; an elite history that ignores the plagues and famines of his Cairo years ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch05-arrival-in-egypt]]).
- **Source criticism of the sources on him.** Ibn ‘Arabshah's Timur speeches are treated as the chronicler's own style ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch05-office-holding-timur]]), and the Cairene slanders are sorted by the attackers' motives ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch05-autobiography-outsider]]).
- **Reception as evidence about the reader.** Irwin reads each modern Ibn Khaldun as a portrait of its maker: Gellner “created an Ibn Khaldun in his own image” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-colonialist-mahdi-gellner]]), and scholarship shows “many agendas”, each with its own Ibn Khaldun ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-arab-american-novels-orientalism]]).
- **Defence of the despised.** He defends al-Mas‘udi's marvels as literature ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch01-masudi-iram-weird-science]]) and Rosenthal's much-criticized translation as the best substitute for a missing critical edition ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-toynbee-rosenthal]]).

## Voice and characteristic hedges

Irwin marks his own guesses openly: “it seems to me”, “I get the impression” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-preface]]); “there may be something in this” on the political-failure thesis ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch02-ibn-al-khatib-spain-return]]); “possible (though unlikely)” and “only a possibility” on how the Muqaddima and the ‘Ibar were composed ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch03-wisdom-literature-ibar]]); “I believe” on why sorcery fascinated Ibn Khaldun ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-future-last-days]]); “Perhaps” on the Last Judgment as the book's purpose ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch11-ending-up]]). He is dry and occasionally mocking: Timur as “a newly acquired laboratory rat” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch05-office-holding-timur]]), Toynbee's civilizations as automata in a caricature he calls unkind ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-toynbee-rosenthal]]), Adorno's “metaphysics of dopes” for occult wisdom ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-supernatural-occult]]). He sets out Ibn Khaldun's legal severity plainly and without softening ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch04-aristotle-theology-maliki-law]]).

## Interlocutors

- **Endorsed, with qualifications:** Patricia Crone (thinking away modernity; the Confucian cycle; the mamluks as institutionalized tribal conquerors), Hamilton Gibb's Shari‘a reading (“It is hard to contest”), Rosenthal's translation, Taha Husayn's early scepticism ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch04-aristotle-theology-maliki-law]], [[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-colonialist-mahdi-gellner]], [[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-arab-american-novels-orientalism]]).
- **Disputed:** Gellner's secular sociologist, Mahdi's Straussian philosopher, Lacoste's Marxist, Fromherz's Sufi historiography, Dale's Aristotelian lineage, Hodgson's faylasuf, Toynbee's ancestor, and Bruce Lawrence's claim that Ibn Khaldun is “a product of Orientalism” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch06-sufi-mystic]], [[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-toynbee-rosenthal]], [[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-colonialist-mahdi-gellner]], [[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-arab-american-novels-orientalism]]).

On Orientalism Irwin takes a middle line. He concedes the French Ministry of War's commission of De Slane's translation and the selective quotation by colonial scholars in Algeria, but argues that religious motives dominated until well into the twentieth century and that most Ibn Khaldun scholarship is by Arabs ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-arab-american-novels-orientalism]]).

## Self-implication

Irwin includes himself, parenthetically, among readers who may remake Ibn Khaldun ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-arab-american-novels-orientalism]]). He admits he failed to understand parts of the Muqaddima (the za’iraja, physiology, dreaming, soothsaying) ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch11-ending-up]]), and quotes his own earlier formulation that Ibn Khaldun “believed that one was what one had committed to memory” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch09-teaching-writing]]). For World Machines readers the practical upshot is that Irwin supplies the evidence for a retrofitting critique ([[terminal-medievals-vs-early-moderns-irwin]]) while declining to offer a replacement grand theory of his own.
