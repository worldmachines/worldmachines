# D'Alembert

This page draws on Volume I (Preface, Chapters I–VIII) of [[john-morley]]'s study [[diderot-and-the-encyclopaedists]]; Volume II material is added in later sections. Morley names him simply D'Alembert.

## Portrait

Diderot, admitting his weakness in mathematics and physics, recruited D'Alembert as co-editor of the [[encyclopedie]]. Morley's portrait: a foundling of Madame de Tencin, a Jansenist-educated geometer, an independent man who refused offers from Frederick and Catherine, a stylist and correspondent of [[voltaire]], sickly and irritable, a “precisian” where Diderot was “exuberant, prodigal, and disordered”. The details are narrative; the evaluative adjectives are Morley's ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-1]]).

## The independent man of letters

For Morley, Diderot and D'Alembert together made the profession of letters “definite and independent”, rejecting suitorship and patronage. He quotes D'Alembert on men of letters living “united and almost shut up among themselves” and giving “the law to the rest of the nation in all affairs of taste and philosophy” ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-1]]; see [[men-of-letters-as-spiritual-power-morley]]). Morley nonetheless lists D'Alembert, with Voltaire, among those who took pensions from Frederick, a comparison he makes in discussing Diderot's dependence on Catherine ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-4]]).

## The Preliminary Discourse

The Preliminary Discourse, mostly D'Alembert's, acknowledges [[francis-bacon]] as the parent of the scheme and surveys progress from Bacon and Descartes to Newton, Locke and Leibnitz, conveying “ordered growth and measured progress”. Its two conditions of success are disregard of authority and avoidance of premature system ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-1]], [[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-2]]). D'Alembert's contempt for antiquarian erudition shaped the work's preference for positive knowledge, and Morley registers a mild dissent: studying the pathology of the past mind is not useless ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-1]], [[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-2]]).

## Under censorship

D'Alembert conceded that the Encyclopædia's theology and metaphysics articles were bad and blamed the censors and the privilege; his line that readers must distinguish “what we have thought from what we have said” is the one Morley calls bitter and humiliating ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-2]]). After the suppression of 1752 he answered malice in the preface to the third volume with the fable of the grasshoppers. His article on Geneva in the seventh volume (1757), praising the Socinian pastors, was read, in Morley's account, as a veiled reproach to French churchmen and helped bring on the crisis that ended with the withdrawal of the privilege in 1759 ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-3]]). Rousseau's Letter to D'Alembert on Stage Plays (1758) is for Morley the opening of a reaction inside the illumination ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-4]]).

## Withdrawal

D'Alembert's letters to Voltaire, as Morley quotes them, give his reasons for retiring: affronts, satires protected or even commanded by those in power, sermons at Versailles and new censors. Morley adds dissatisfaction with the booksellers' finances. Diderot carried the work on alone ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-4]]). In a 1759 dialogue that Diderot reported, he scolded D'Alembert for dealing unfairly with the booksellers; Morley notes that the telling is Diderot's and favours him ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-4]]).

## Volume II: sceptic, interlocutor and witness

This section draws on Volume II ([[morley-diderot-v2]]). When Holbach's System of Nature appeared in 1770, D'Alembert is reported as a sceptic who nonetheless thought experience proved a material soul and a limited material deity ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-1]]). Frederick's distaste for Diderot's “self-sufficiency” and “arrogance” reaches Morley through D'Alembert ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-2]]). He is a speaker in Diderot's 1769 dialogues, the conversation with D'Alembert and D'Alembert's Dream, in which Diderot figures definitely as a materialist ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-2]]). In Romilly's account of a 1781 visit, D'Alembert, more cautious than Diderot, observes that the people who were “drunk with joy” at a Dauphin's birth in 1729 now regard the birth of “another master” with indifference, which Morley reads as proof that the change the philosophers had worked for was at hand. He is among the dead Morley lists in Diderot's last years, tended by [[nicolas-de-condorcet]] ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch08-closing-years-2]]). In Rameau's Nephew, He imagines mocking D'Alembert among other great names if he were rich ([[morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-3]]).
