# Cide Hamete Benengeli

The fictitious Moorish chronicler to whom Cervantes attributes the "true history" of Don Quixote. He is a joke, a legal shield, and the novel's working theory of historical evidence, in roughly that order of appearance.

## An afterthought that became the frame

Ormsby is explicit that the device was not planned: the chapter divisions and the fictive source-manuscript were bolted on partway through, in imitation of the chivalric romances' own trick of claiming a recondite source ([[cervantes-don-quixote-front-essay-don-quixote]]). The joke about contested chronicle is already running in the first chapters, where "authors who write on the subject" disagree about the hidalgo's surname and about which adventure came first ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p1-01-05]]) — but it acquires its machinery only in Chapter IX, when the narrative stops dead with swords raised over the Biscayan and the narrator admits the history "came to a stop and stood cut short." He then reports buying Arabic pamphlets in the Alcana of Toledo and paying for a Castilian translation, revealing the source as an Arab historian — and immediately flagging its unreliability ("lying is a very common propensity with those of that nation") while insisting "no history can be bad so long as it is true" ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p1-06-09]]).

The structure that results has at least four hands between reader and event: Cide Hamete, the anonymous Morisco translator, the compiling "second author," and the printed book — every one of them explicitly fallible.

## What the device is used for

**Refusing to certify.** After the Cave of Montesinos, Cide Hamete appends a marginal note disclaiming his own ability to believe or disbelieve his protagonist, and passes on a rumour that Don Quixote recanted the episode on his deathbed. No voice in the chapter is allowed to close the question — not the narrator, not the translator, not Sancho, not the divining ape, which answers only that the events are "part of them false, part true" ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-23-25]]). The device exists precisely so that the text can decline to adjudicate.

**Marking the seams.** The translator interrupts to call Sancho's eloquence in one chapter "apocryphal" because it exceeds his limited intelligence; Cide Hamete opens Chapter VIII with a ritual invocation of Allah before promising the true adventures ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-05-08]]). Elsewhere he swears "as a Catholic Christian" though a Moor, a formula the narrator flags rather than smooths ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-26-29]]).

**Exposing stagecraft.** It is Cide Hamete who lays bare the speaking-tube mechanism inside Don Antonio Moreno's bronze oracle in Barcelona ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-60-62]]), and Cide Hamete who steps outside the narrative to judge the Duke and Duchess as scarcely less foolish than the two men they spend a month mocking — a rare, unhedged moral verdict from a chronicler otherwise committed to non-certification ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-67-70]]).

**Supplying philosophical drapery.** The meditation on time's ever-turning wheel that opens Sancho's fall from Barataria is delivered in Cide Hamete's voice, and the chapter then undercuts it by showing exactly how contrived the "siege" was — the chronicler's cosmic register set against the plain mechanics of a prank ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-53-56]]).

## Poet against historian

Part II converts the device into an in-world argument about historiography. When the characters learn their first sally is already in print — Sancho garbling the author's name as "Cide Hamete Berengena" — the bachelor Samson Carrasco states the principle the whole apparatus depends on: "it is one thing to write as a poet, another to write as a historian ... the historian has to write them down, not as they ought to have been, but as they were" ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-01-04]]). A self-consciously fictional "true history" thus argues for documentary fidelity, and the reader is invited to hold both.

The same argument is fought elsewhere in the novel without Cide Hamete's name attached. The Canon of Toledo indicts the romances not as fiction but as *counterfeit history* — fluent in genealogies, itineraries and day-by-day chronicles, and therefore dangerous — and proposes verifiable chronicle as the corrective; Don Quixote answers with relics, unbroken genealogy and his grandmother's eyewitness memory, so that the debate turns on what counts as evidence at all ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p1-49-51]]). Part I closes with a parody of authentication in its purest form: a leaden box of worm-eaten parchment containing epitaphs by fictitious "Academicians of Argamasilla" ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p1-52]]). Compare [[chaucer-legibility-machines]], where an intact apparatus of proof is likewise shown certifying a fabrication; here the apparatus certifies nothing and says so.

## The closing seal

Cide Hamete's last act is to address his pen: no third sally, a warning off the "Tordesillesque writer," and a declaration that pen and knight are one ([[cervantes-don-quixote-p2-71-74]]). The chronicler invented to make the history deniable is used, at the end, to make it exclusive — the fiction's own guard posted against unauthorised continuation. Read alongside Cervantes's vow in the Part II preface to leave his hero "dead and buried" ([[cervantes-don-quixote-transition-part2-dedication-preface]]), it is the same manoeuvre performed twice: once by the author in his own voice, once inside the fiction by the source he made up.
