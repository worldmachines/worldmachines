# Walter Wriston

Walter Wriston, chairman of Citibank, is the figure Farrell and Newman use to open [[farrell-underground-empire]] — not as a villain but as the book's most instructive wrong prophet. He correctly identified that something enormous was happening to the world economy and misdiagnosed, completely, what it meant politically.

## Formation

Wriston inherited a Hayekian faith in market freedom from his father Henry Wriston, a co-founder of the Mont Pèlerin Society, and spent his career arguing that information technology and mobile capital would "decentralize power" and erode the "choke points" of state sovereignty. His signature phrase — capital was "shifting the tectonic plates of national sovereignty" — is quoted almost verbatim at the chapter's opening and then immediately undercut with the observation that "when novel ideas succeed, they decay into stale clichés." The chapter sustains that ironic register throughout: Wriston is taken seriously as a prophet of real change while being shown, repeatedly, to have misread its direction.

## The inversion

The book's thesis sentence about him is that Wriston and his peers were "centralizers by their nature." They built world-spanning networks that "centered on a few key choke points," and those chokepoints sat on U.S. soil, subject to U.S. surveillance and U.S. law. The escape from the state produced the state's best instrument.

**The Eurodollar market.** Wriston's Citibank helped build a market that arose to solve a real problem — dollar demand outside the U.S., interest-rate caps inside it — and grew into "a legal gray zone" that made the dollar the de facto global currency "without anyone really planning it." But the escape was never complete: every Eurodollar, however offshore, "had to be backed by a real dollar," sitting in a U.S. bank. The pirates still had to "regularly provision themselves in the monarch's ports."

**MARTI and SWIFT.** Wriston tried to make Citibank's proprietary MARTI system the mandatory standard for interbank messaging. Other banks revolted, and the revolt catalysed a multilateral, Belgium-based consortium — SWIFT — created precisely to prevent that kind of capture. The irony is exact: SWIFT became an obligatory passage point for the entire global financial system, so the consolidation Wriston wanted for Citibank was achieved collectively instead, and proved just as exploitable once the cops noticed.

**Herstatt, 1974.** The chapter's sharpest dramatisation of Wriston's blind spot. A failing German bank's frozen clearing account cascaded through the interbank system until "the global financial system went into heart failure," and Wriston personally reassembled a working payments network out of a phone tree of elite bankers. It emerged that "twenty to thirty private banks had become the 'de facto payments mechanism of the world.'" His own dry aside — "and it also makes us nervous" — is the moment the system's chief architect admits he had not understood what he built.

## Afterlife

Wriston's dream outlived him as the default self-understanding of global business. The book's case-study chapter on firms opens by naming it directly: Wriston imagined companies sailing "piratical realms beyond the reach of sovereigns," and traces how that dream soured as "clashing powers—the United States, China, Europe, and even Russia—press companies into their service." Microsoft's aspiration to be a "trusted and neutral Digital Switzerland," TSMC's deliberate construction as the "Switzerland of semiconductors," and the crypto movement's attempt to build statelessness into code are all recognisable as Wriston's premise pursued by other means — and all of them fail in the same direction he did.

## Reading him in World Machines terms

Wriston is describing, in his own vocabulary, what he takes to be the death of one world machine — state-centred, convergent, legibility-optimising — and the birth of another built on market and information flow. That much of the diagnosis the [[world-machines]] framework can accept. What he misses is [[infrastructure-inheritance]]: the new apparatus is the old apparatus, and a carrier does not stop being jurisdiction-bound because the ideology flowing through it has changed. The flows he thought had escaped the state re-encode [[legibility-as-machine-core]] at exactly the points where they must pass through identifiable, law-bound nodes — clearing banks, messaging cooperatives, colocation facilities, foundries.

He is, in this sense, the purest available specimen of [[globalisation-as-inevitability-rhetoric]] argued sincerely from the inside: not a propagandist claiming markets are a force of nature, but a builder who believed it and acted on it, and whose actions produced the opposite of what the belief predicted.
