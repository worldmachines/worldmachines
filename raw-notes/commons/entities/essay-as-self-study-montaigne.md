What the *Essays* ([[montaigne-complete-essays]]) say about their own enterprise: the self as subject, and the essay as the instrument for studying it. This page collects Montaigne's statements of method across the three books. For the wider concepts see [[personal-essay-as-form]] and [[self-construction-through-writing]]; the reading notes warn that the second fits some passages better than others.

## Beginnings: a memorial and an idle mind

The preface is modest and commemorative. The book is for kin and friends, and he paints himself only as far as “public reverence hath permitted”. The notes call the self-construction claim merely implicit here ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch01-02-to-the-reader-various-ways]]). The earliest account of why he writes is half-comic. On retiring, his idle mind bred “chimaeras”, running like a horse that has broken from its rider, and he wrote them down “hoping in time to make it ashamed of itself” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch06-10-hour-parley-dangerous]]).

## The book that made its author

In Of Giving the Lie the claim becomes reflexive. Composing the portrait made him “temper and compose” himself, and “I have no more made my book than my book has made me”. He also concedes that, painting himself for others, he shows “a better colouring” than his natural complexion ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch18-20-giving-lie]]). In Use Makes Perfect he admits he is “perpetually tricking myself out”, so that self-description is also self-presentation. He presents the work as an undertaking almost no one had attempted: “This is not my doctrine, 'tis my study”; “I do not write my own acts, but myself and my essence” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch06-use-makes-perfect]]).

## Passage, not being

“Others form man; I only report him.” Everything is in motion, so “I do not paint its being, I paint its passage”, and the ordinary private life holds “the whole human condition”. In the book “who touches the one, touches the other” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch02-repentance]]). The essay on inconstancy gives the logic of this: “If I speak variously of myself, it is because I consider myself variously”, and “'Distinguo' is the most universal member of my logic” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch01-inconstancy-actions]]). Hence the editorial rule “I add, but I correct not”, because “I now, and I anon, are two several persons” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch09-vanity-2]]). He leaves each piece “as it came from the forge” to show “the progress of my humours” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch37-resemblance-children-fathers-1]]).

## Against the custom that forbids self-talk

The prevailing custom condemned all talk of oneself as boasting. Montaigne answers that this is like condemning wine because some get drunk, and that “My trade and art is to live” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch06-use-makes-perfect]]). Ceremony forbids both self-praise and self-blame (“We are nothing but ceremony”), and the self-portrait pushes against that rule ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch17-presumption-1]]). He sets out his deficiencies without apology: he cannot tell cabbage from lettuce. The standard is fidelity: “provided it be such as I really am, I have my end” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch17-presumption-3]]).

## Fidelity as an editorial rule

The book's “principal end and perfection is to be exactly mine”. He corrects “the faults of inadvertence, not those of custom”, keeps his Gascon words, and writes at home, away from books, so that “all the world knows me in my book, and my book in me”. He also confesses an “apish, imitative” mind that picks up whatever it reads, which qualifies the confident portrait ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch05-some-verses-virgil-4]]). Publication then works back on the man. The public image becomes “a rule” that obliges him to keep his way. A confession full enough leaves slanderers “fighting with the wind”. And a sympathetic reader learns in three days what acquaintance would take years to show ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch09-vanity-4]]). Those who speak of themselves lose the licence to split word from deed: “I must march my pen as I do my feet” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch09-vanity-5]]).

## Form: grotesques, stabs and digressions

He calls his writings “grotesques and monstrous bodies, made of various parts, without any certain figure”, set around a borrowed centrepiece ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch27-28-friendship]]). He takes subjects as fortune presents them, gives “a stab, not so wide but as deep as I can”, and names ignorance and doubt as his method. He holds that “All motion discovers us”, so table-talk or chess reveals a man as well as battle does ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch50-55-democritus-heraclitus]]). He defends leaps and skips as a method: “it is the indiligent reader who loses my subject, and not I” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch09-vanity-5]]). He asks to be judged on how he chooses and weighs his borrowings, since the invention is always his own ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch10-books]]). He even offers his faults as a public example, as “we correct others” by a hanged man ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch08-art-conference-1]]).

## Self-study as a school of doubt

The self does not deliver certainty. In the Apology, watching his own moods and revisions is the empirical ground of his scepticism ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch12-apology-raimond-sebond-12]]). He has “never seen greater monster or miracle in the world than myself” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch11-cripples]]). Of Experience makes self-study his whole curriculum: “I study myself more than any other subject; 'tis my metaphysic, my physic” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-1]]). Its infinite depth teaches ignorance and modesty, and the book becomes “a register of the essays of my own life” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-2]]). For the body the register is literal: “For want of natural memory, I make one of paper”, a log of symptoms reread for precedents ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-3]]).

## World Machines resonances, hedged

The notes use [[self-construction-through-writing]] for the Giving the Lie passage, but mark its limits elsewhere. The preface's motive is commemorative. In the Apology self-observation dismantles confidence rather than building a stable self. In the Experience regimen passages the writing is an inventory or a clinical log, not self-making. In Vanity the stress falls on being held to one's own truthfulness, which only half-fits the modern idea of self-curation. Against the prestige of print, Montaigne rejects those who trust only what is in a book. The notes link this to [[eisenstein-printing-revolution]], with the hedge that his target is the quotation's prestige and that he quotes heavily himself ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch13-experience-2]]).
