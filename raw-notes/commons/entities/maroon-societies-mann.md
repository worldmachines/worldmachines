# Maroon Societies (Mann)

Communities of escaped slaves, almost always joined by Indians, that Mann treats as the counter-current of the human exchange in [[mann-1493]]. He counts thousands across the hemisphere, from Brazil to the US Southeast, known as quilombos, mocambos, palenques and cumbes, and glosses 'maroon' as apparently from a Taino word for the flight of an arrow ([[mann-1493-ch09-in-calabar]]).

## Mann's thesis

Against a schoolbook picture in which Africans and Indians have 'no agency, as social scientists say', Mann insists that people 'always seek ways to exert their will'. For centuries most newcomers to the Americas were African and the land was full of indigenous people, so the encounter of the two halves of the world was largely between Africans and Indians, 'largely conducted out of sight of Europeans' and only now being unravelled by researchers ([[mann-1493-ch09-in-calabar]]). He lists maroon victories 'if only because slaves' prospects for autonomy are all too often portrayed as completely dependent on the goodwill of their masters' ([[mann-1493-ch09-capitulations]]).

## Why they formed

- **Labor hunger imported its own enemies.** Spain brought enslaved Africans to run the Panama silver road. By 1565 they outnumbered Europeans seven to one, and runaways formed multiethnic villages that robbed travellers and threatened the silver line. Spanish sources show a community of escaped Africans already in the mountains when Balboa crossed in 1513 ([[mann-1493-ch09-in-the-isthmus]]). In Hispaniola, after the crown's 1501 attempts to bar Africans, the governor pleaded that 'They flee to the Indians' ([[mann-1493-ch08-new-world-born]]).
- **Anonymized captives, many of them soldiers.** Plantation chattel slavery turned people into 'pieces' on a balance sheet, so owners did not know that many captives were prisoners of war. Mann reads revolt as structurally predictable: European labor hunger imported people who wanted to destroy the colony ([[mann-1493-ch09-africans-in-charge]]).
- **Everyday resistance.** Drawing on James Scott's 'weapons of the weak', Mann moves from plantation sabotage to escape to the heights ([[mann-1493-ch08-new-world-born]]).

## Cases

- **Panama.** Bayano's mixed community, his betrayal at a peace feast, and Drake's raids of 1572-73, which Mann reads as triumphs for the maroons rather than the English ([[mann-1493-ch09-in-the-isthmus]]).
- **Palmares.** A polity led by Angolans but including Tupi-speaking Indians and European outcasts, with American and African crops, African forges and African-style military organization. It repelled Portuguese and Dutch attacks for decades and fell in 1694 to a hybrid force under the bandeirante Domingos Jorge Velho. Aqualtune's biography and some titles are flagged by Mann as uncertain or legendary ([[mann-1493-ch09-africans-in-charge]]).
- **Capitulations.** Spain's treaty with the Panamanian maroons, Yanga's town near Veracruz, the Miskitu, the Seminole and Black Seminole, Haiti ('an entire maroon nation') and Suriname's 1762 treaty ([[mann-1493-ch09-capitulations]], [[mann-1493-ch09-in-calabar]]). Winning freedom also brought 'the later anonymity that was its concomitant' ([[mann-1493-ch09-capitulations]]).
- **Amazonia.** After Palmares, ten thousand or more small villages formed a 'flexible, shifting network', a lesson Mann presents as learned from Palmares's centralized failure. At Vila Nova Mazagão the maroons and the Portuguese administration each kept up the other's convenient fiction ([[mann-1493-ch09-shake-it-ox-dona-rosario]]).

## Leverage and entanglement

Maroons bargained from strength: terrain and booby traps ([[mann-1493-ch09-in-calabar]]), raids that menaced the silver road, and disease. The Seminole waited for yellow fever and malaria to kill northern soldiers, and Toussaint's retreat-and-wait strategy let fevers destroy the French ([[mann-1493-ch09-capitulations]]). They were never pure enclaves: the Portuguese hunted runaways while trading with them ([[mann-1493-ch09-in-calabar]]), and planters bought food from Palmares ([[mann-1493-ch09-africans-in-charge]]). Indian-African cooperation was not automatic; the Creek hunted maroons ([[mann-1493-ch09-capitulations]]).

## A made forest

Mann's closing claim rests on Susanna Hecht's research and Dona Rosario's own practice rather than on quantitative evidence. Two centuries of maroon tending, mixing West African shrimp traps with Indian manioc processing and fish poison, 'structured the forest', so that 'forest' is the wrong word for much of the Amazon ([[mann-1493-ch09-shake-it-ox-dona-rosario]]).

## The present

Calabar, now surrounded by Salvador's high-rises, never became legally part of the city ([[mann-1493-ch09-in-calabar]]). Brazil's 1988 constitution and Provisional Law 458 (2009) finally gave maroons title, and many researchers now think as many as five thousand quilombos survive ([[mann-1493-ch09-shake-it-ox-dona-rosario]]). Suriname leased about 40 percent of the country for logging without consulting the Saramaka and others, and had not complied with an Inter-American Court ruling at the time of writing ([[mann-1493-ch09-capitulations]]).

## World Machines resonances, with their hedges

- **[[Legibility]] and [[legibility-as-machine-core]].** Quilombos were places the state could not or would not read. Brazil's military government saw 60 percent of the map as blank and drove highways through it, yet legal recognition later became the maroons' protection, so legibility is 'both threat and shield' here ([[mann-1493-ch09-shake-it-ox-dona-rosario]], [[mann-1493-ch09-in-calabar]]). Mann never frames it so.
- **[[divergence-machine]].** Bottom-up polities forming in the interstices of an emerging world system have some affinity with proliferating local forms nobody designed, but Mann is 'a historian of agency, not a machine theorist', and the connection is the notes' ([[mann-1493-ch09-in-calabar]], [[mann-1493-ch09-capitulations]]).
- **[[tropical-island-eden-grove]].** The note on the Amazonian quilombos connects the made forest to the line of argument that colonial observers misread managed landscapes as untouched nature, adding that Mann does not cite Grove ([[mann-1493-ch09-shake-it-ox-dona-rosario]]).
