# The old woman (Candide)

The unnamed old woman of [[voltaire-candide]] appears at the end of the Lisbon chapters ([[voltaire-candide-ch05-06-lisbon-earthquake-auto-da-fe]]) and stays with Cunégonde to the end. She is first a silent go-between, then the narrator of the book's longest inset story, then its most practical adviser. Her claims are hers; the notes read the satire in them by interpretation.

## The go-between

She feeds Candide, dresses his wounds, and leads him by night to Cunégonde without explaining who she is ([[voltaire-candide-ch07-08-cunegonde-found]]). She gives him the sword that kills Issachar, organizes the flight on three Andalusian horses, suspects the Cordelier of the theft, and answers Cunégonde's catalogue of paired woes by saying she would "suspend your judgment" if she knew her birth and rank ([[voltaire-candide-ch09-10-inquisitor-and-cadiz]]).

## Her history

She was the daughter of Pope Urban X and the Princess of Palestrina, raised in dazzling luxury, her beauty described in absurd hyperbole. Her fiancé is poisoned ("a mere trifle"); Salé pirates take her galley while the Pope's soldiers beg the corsair for absolution; she is searched for diamonds (a custom, she explains, of "civilised nations that scour the seas", the Knights of Malta included), raped by the captain ("common things; they are hardly worth mentioning"), and sees her mother torn apart in Morocco's civil wars, fought without missing the five prayer times. The note flags her climatic generalisations about Africans and Europeans as the character's stereotyping in the period's idiom, delivered with heavy irony, which the text does not endorse ([[voltaire-candide-ch11-old-womans-history]]). A castrato sells her in Algiers; she survives the plague, is resold across the Mediterranean, and at the siege of Azov the janissaries, on an imam's "excellent sermon", cut a buttock from each woman. A boyard works and whips her; she ends as an innkeeper's servant in northern Europe ([[voltaire-candide-ch12-old-womans-adventures]]).

## Her argument

The comic engine of her tale, per the notes, is the gap between what happens and her flippant tone, which mocks any frame that absorbs suffering quickly, though she has lived the suffering rather than explained it away. She ranks miseries (an earthquake is "a trifle compared to" the plague), and she calls her clinging to life, after a hundred near-suicides, a "ridiculous weakness", "perhaps one of our worst instincts", like caressing the serpent that devours us. She closes with a wager: let Cunégonde ask every passenger his story, and throw her into the sea if any has not "cursed his existence many times". The note reads this as turning the novel's episodes into a test of a hypothesis, empirical rather than systematic ([[voltaire-candide-ch12-old-womans-adventures]]). Candide recalls the bet when he holds his contest of misery at Surinam ([[voltaire-candide-ch19-surinam-and-martin]]).

## Counsel and the farm

At Buenos Ayres she urges Cunégonde to accept the Governor, since "seventy-two quarterings" are worth little without a penny, and reduces Cunégonde's history to bargaining leverage: "People take advantage of misfortunes". She then tells Candide to flee at once ([[voltaire-candide-ch13-14-buenos-aires-and-paraguay]]). She is found with Cunégonde hanging out tablecloths on the Propontis, and the farm is hers ([[voltaire-candide-ch28-29-reunion]]). There, grown ill-natured, she puts the final chapter's question: whether all their miseries are worse than "remain here doing nothing" ([[voltaire-candide-ch30-conclusion-garden]]).
