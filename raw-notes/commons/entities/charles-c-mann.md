# Charles C. Mann

Author of [[mann-1493]]. This page records only what the reading notes on that book show about him: his method and his stance.

## Method

- **Travel reportage.** Mann writes in the first person from places he visits: the ruins of La Isabela and the Columbus Lighthouse ([[mann-1493-ch01-seams-of-pangaea]], [[mann-1493-ch01-to-the-lighthouse]]), Manila's Legazpi-Urdaneta monument ([[mann-1493-ch01-shiploads-of-silver]]), Yongding in Fujian ([[mann-1493-ch05-malthusian-interlude-mountains]]), the Loess Plateau ([[mann-1493-ch05-unlearning-from-dazhai]]), a rubber monoculture in Yunnan and villages in Laos ([[mann-1493-ch07-no-birds-grease-chemistry]], [[mann-1493-ch07-wickham-end-of-world]]), Salvador da Bahia and Amapá, where he walked with the geographer Susanna Hecht ([[mann-1493-ch09-in-calabar]], [[mann-1493-ch09-shake-it-ox-dona-rosario]]), the Serra da Barriga ([[mann-1493-ch09-africans-in-charge]]), and Bulalacao and the Ifugao terraces ([[mann-1493-ch10-on-the-boat]], [[mann-1493-ch10-fractured-cerebration-staircases]]).
- **Synthesis of specialist scholarship.** The book's frame is Alfred Crosby's ([[mann-1493-prologue]]). Much of the epidemiology is J. R. McNeill's, whom Mann also interviewed ([[mann-1493-ch03-yellow-jack-war-mosquitoes]]). The silver argument leans on Dennis Flynn and Arturo Giráldez ([[mann-1493-ch04-treasure-of-the-world]]), Chinese monetary history on Richard von Glahn ([[mann-1493-ch04-out-of-money]]), the extractive-state argument on Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson ([[mann-1493-ch03-extractive-states-seasoning]]), Asians in New Spain on Edward Slack ([[mann-1493-ch08-shook-up-city]]), and African agency on John Thornton ([[mann-1493-ch09-africans-in-charge]]). He also interviews historians such as Li Jinming ([[mann-1493-ch04-wooden-noses]]).
- **Personal and family threads.** His tomato garden opens the book ([[mann-1493-prologue]]), and his blighted garden of 2009 closes the beetle chapter ([[mann-1493-ch06-war-upon-the-beetles]]). A portrait of his ancestor Neville Craig, an engineer on the failed 1878 Madeira railroad expedition, opens the Amazon rubber story ([[mann-1493-ch07-bather-in-the-bubbly]]). His grandfather's Philippine mahogany table leads into the Ifugao worm puzzle ([[mann-1493-ch10-fractured-cerebration-staircases]]).
- **Hedging as practice.** Mann labels speculation as such. One word in Colón's chronicle 'is not enough to make a diagnosis' of malaria ([[mann-1493-ch03-extractive-states-seasoning]]); Rolfe's role in bringing earthworms is 'just possible' ([[mann-1493-ch02-lowly-creatures-strange-land]]); the blight 'most likely' came on a guano ship ([[mann-1493-ch06-modern-famine-lazy-beds]]). He reports disputes he does not settle, such as how much silver went to China ([[mann-1493-ch01-reversals-of-fortune]]) and Britain's culpability for the Irish famine ([[mann-1493-ch06-modern-famine-lazy-beds]]).
- **Care with names.** An appendix explains why he labels people by modern geographic origin and gives indigenous peoples ethnic names, and admits the scheme courts anachronism ([[mann-1493-appendix-a-fighting-words]]).

## Stance

- **Ecology before great men.** He separates Colón from his consequences: the Homogenocene is something Colón 'almost accidentally created', and Mann sides against the critics on ecological, not moral, grounds ([[mann-1493-ch01-to-the-lighthouse]], [[mann-1493-ch01-seams-of-pangaea]]). Jamestown's fate was decided 'by impersonal ecological forces', not John Smith's heroics ([[mann-1493-ch02-risk-pool]]).
- **Against monocausal claims.** Malaria did not cause slavery but strengthened its economic case ([[mann-1493-ch03-no-distempers-villa-plasmodia]]). American crops were not 'entirely responsible' for China's population surge ([[mann-1493-ch05-hidden-passengers]]). The potato's effect is calibrated against a single 2010 estimate ([[mann-1493-ch06-potato-wars-sea-of-genes]]).
- **Against victim-only history.** Africans and Indians 'always seek ways to exert their will' ([[mann-1493-ch09-in-calabar]]).
- **Ambivalence about globalization.** He suspects free-traders and their opponents are both partly right ([[mann-1493-prologue]]), catches himself 'simultaneously promoting and denouncing the Columbian Exchange' ([[mann-1493-ch10-fractured-cerebration-staircases]]), and borrows the economists' idea of externalities while conceding that the remedy is hard in practice ([[mann-1493-ch10-on-the-boat]]).

## World Machines note

The notes repeatedly caution that Mann is a narrative journalist-historian, not a machine theorist. Any reading of his cases through [[Legibility]], [[history-machine]] or [[divergence-machine]] is the wiki's, not his ([[mann-1493-ch01-seams-of-pangaea]]).
