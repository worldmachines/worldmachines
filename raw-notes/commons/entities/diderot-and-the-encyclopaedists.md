# Diderot and the Encyclopædists

*Diderot and the Encyclopædists* is [[john-morley]]'s two-volume study of Denis Diderot and the circle around the Encyclopædia, first published by Macmillan in 1878 and reissued as a New Edition in 1886. This page describes the work as a whole; each volume has its own section below.

## What the work is

Morley's Preface presents the book as the close of his series of studies on the literary preparation for the French Revolution, companion to his volumes on [[voltaire]] and [[jean-jacques-rousseau]]. It is more descriptive than those (more “transcript or analysis”), because Diderot's circle cannot be assumed known to readers. It also sets out to counter Taine's “one-sided exaggerations” by bringing out the social significance and the positive quality of the Encyclopædists ([[morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary]]).

## Overall design

The opening chapter states the frame for the whole work. The Gallican Church of about 1750 looked secure; within some forty years Paris held the Feast of the Supreme Being. Earlier heresy had only the scientific idea; the new insurrection joined it to the social idea, and “the great symbol of this new comprehensiveness in the insurrection was the Encyclopædia.” Morley then makes the case for [[denis-diderot]] as the one real thinker among the great illuminators, and so as the figure through whom the movement is best told ([[morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary]]). The design is a biography of Diderot that widens at each stage into the history of the [[encyclopedie]], of the men of letters as a new social order, and of the society around them ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch2-youth-1]], [[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-1]]).

## Volume I

Volume I holds the Preface and Chapters I–VIII. It follows Diderot from Langres and his Paris poverty through his early sceptical writings, the Letter on the Blind and the imprisonment at Vincennes ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-1]], [[morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-3]]). It then gives the Encyclopædia's history, general contents and Diderot's own articles at length ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-1]]), before turning to his private and social life in the encyclopædic years, his theory of the drama, and Rameau's Nephew ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch6-social-life-1]], [[morley-diderot-v1-ch7-the-stage-1]], [[morley-diderot-v1-ch8-rameaus-nephew]]).

Its argument centres on two claims: that spiritual authority passed from the Church to the men of letters, and that the philosophers' attack turned from speculation to a practical, social assault on the old order. The volume summary is [[morley-diderot-v1]].

## Volume II

This section draws on Volume II, which holds Chapters I–IX and an Appendix. Where Volume I is built around the Encyclopædia, Volume II follows Diderot's other work and the books of his circle, then closes his life. Chapter I treats three moral dialogues, among them the Supplement to Bougainville's Travels ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch01-other-dialogues-1]], [[morley-diderot-v2-ch01-other-dialogues-2]]). Chapter II, Romance, covers Diderot's Eloge of Richardson, The Nun and Jacques le Fataliste ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch02-romance-1]], [[morley-diderot-v2-ch02-romance-2]]); Chapter III, Art, the Salons he wrote for Grimm from 1759 to 1781 ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch03-art-1]]). Chapter IV follows the 1773–74 journey to Catherine II and the months at the Hague ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-1]], [[morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-2]]). Chapters V to VII give a short account of three books of the Encyclopædic school, commonly credited to Diderot's inspiration: Helvétius's L'Esprit, Holbach's System of Nature and Raynal's History of the Indies ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1]], [[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-3]], [[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-1]]). Chapter VIII narrates Diderot's last years, the dying-off of the circle and his death in 1784 ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch08-closing-years-2]]). Chapter IX, the Conclusion, reads the Interpretation of Nature through Comte and surveys D'Alembert's Dream, the University plan for Russia and the Falconet correspondence ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-1]], [[morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-2]]). The Appendix is Morley's own abridged translation of Rameau's Nephew ([[morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-1]]; see [[rameaus-nephew]]).

The volume's arc moves from Diderot as writer and critic, through the philosophers' dealings with power, to the Encyclopædic school's substitutes for theology and their reception on the eve of the Revolution, and ends in Morley's Comtean appraisal of Diderot as a thinker. The volume summary is [[morley-diderot-v2]].
