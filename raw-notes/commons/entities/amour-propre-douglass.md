# Amour-propre and amour de soi-même (Douglass)

[[robin-douglass]] reads [[jean-jacques-rousseau]]'s distinction between *amour de soi-même* and *amour-propre* as the point where Rousseau's anti-Hobbesian argument and his Augustinian inheritance meet ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes]]). The bare concept is much older and broader than Rousseau's usage. This page follows Douglass's framing.

## The distinction

The famous note to the *Discours sur l'inégalité* is the first statement of the distinction. Douglass says it was made in direct response to Sorbière's French *De cive*, which placed both utility and glory under amour-propre. For Rousseau, advantage belongs to amour de soi-même, which is pre-social and shared with animals. Comparison-dependent sentiments (vanity, pride, envy, glory, honour) belong to amour-propre. Douglass notes that Hobbes's glory, which "consists in comparison and pre-eminence", is close to Rousseau's amour-propre. He approves Nicholas Dent's view that amour-propre can be directed to virtue or to vice ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-natural-goodness-golden-age]]).

## The Augustinian background

- **Amour-propre as vice.** In a strong French Augustinian tradition (Augustine, Senault, Pascal, Esprit, La Rochefoucauld, Nicole), amour-propre, which became the pejorative French rendering of *amor sui* around the 1640s, was the root of man's depravity. In Senault and Pascal it was natural and righteous in Adam and disordered only by the Fall. Nicole held that enlightened amour-propre could imitate charity, and he combined this Augustinian anthropology with a Hobbesian account of civil society ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-neutralizing-amour-propre]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-nicole-bayle]]).
- **Neutralization.** Malebranche made amour-propre "neither virtue nor vice" and equated it with the desire to be happy, with Descartes possibly in the background. Vauvenargues and Duclos later attacked the neo-Augustinians openly. Yet Malebranche himself, followed by Barbeyrac's Pufendorf, blamed Hobbes for grounding justice on amour-propre ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-neutralizing-amour-propre]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-malebranche]]). Douglass notes that for Malebranche the word "means nothing more specific than self-love", unlike Rousseau's later, more technical sense.
- **Rousseau as "half Jansenist".** Rousseau read widely in this literature in the 1730s. He gave amour-propre a more specific sense but kept its neo-Augustinian moral and theological connotations ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-nature-free-will-passions]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-neutralizing-amour-propre]]).

## A secularized Fall

Douglass's central interpretive claim is that the *Discours sur l'inégalité* is a secularized Augustinian fall. Original Sin is recast as entrenched inequality and luxury, which inflame amour-propre against amour de soi-même. Because the distinction first appears in a note criticizing Hobbes for making man naturally evil through an inflamed amour-propre, Hobbes is read as sharing the Augustinian account Rousseau repudiates ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-neutralizing-amour-propre]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-of-love-and-fear]]). Douglass is careful here. Hobbes himself denied that man is naturally evil. The point is that Hobbes's state of nature resembles the post-lapsarian condition, and that Rousseau read him that way through Nicole.

## Genealogy, pity and the golden age

In the *Discours*, pity precedes amour-propre. When amour-propre stirs, pity is at full force and tempers it, so no Hobbesian war follows, and the small societies of this stage are the golden age ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-natural-goodness-golden-age]]). With agriculture, metallurgy and inequality of fortunes, amour-propre makes worth a matter of others' opinion, and "to be and to seem" come apart. It becomes a vice only when it drives man to live outside himself, in constant opposition to amour de soi-même ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-harmony-contradiction-hobbesian-moment]]). Against Pettit, Douglass holds that for Rousseau nothing requires speech to make amour-propre displace amour de soi ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-rousseaus-critique-reappraised]]).

## Harmonizing the two loves

In *Emile* amour-propre is the extension of amour de soi-même once man is social. It "becomes good or bad only by the application made of it" ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-neutralizing-amour-propre]]). Rousseau's 1755 hope was to combine "the force of amour-propre with all the beauty of virtue" ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-ordering-the-passions-opening]]). In the republic, public education generalizes amour de soi into love of fatherland. It activates amour-propre instead of delaying it as in *Emile*, and turns its positive branch, pride (*orgueil*) as opposed to vanity (*vanité*), toward the glory of the fatherland. With both passions aimed at one object, the contradiction between them is overcome ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-love-of-fatherland]]). Douglass's claim is that Hobbes and the neo-Augustinians saw only the negative branch of amour-propre, and that only Rousseau reversed its association with vice. See [[love-and-fear-douglass]].
