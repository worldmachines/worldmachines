# Johann Gottlieb Fichte (in Wulf's Magnificent Rebels)

Fichte is the philosopher whose Ich-philosophy supplies the intellectual centre of [[wulf-magnificent-rebels]]. This page keeps to what Wulf's narrative shows, as the reading notes record it, and distinguishes her explanatory glosses from Fichte's own words and from what critics and gossips said.

## From poor tutor to Kant's anointed successor

Wulf gives Fichte's poor Saxon origins, the baron who paid for his schooling, his years as a badly behaved and perpetually broke tutor, and his conversion by Kant's Critiques. His anonymous *Attempt at a Critique of All Revelation* was taken for Kant's own work until Kant publicly named him, which made him famous and led to the Jena chair ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-1]]). He arrived in May 1794, on Goethe's recommendation ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch01-happy-event-1]]). Wulf reads his life as an enactment of his idea: a boy who left a predestined path by will, and a man who abandoned his fiancée Johanne because marriage would 'clip his wings' (the note records the abandonment without comment from Wulf; Johanne later appears as his wife) ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-1]], [[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-2]]).

## The Ich

In Wulf's gloss, Kant had made the mind something like a lawgiver of nature. Fichte discards the thing-in-itself: the Ich posits itself and, through that act, the non-Ich. Wulf adds that this does not mean the Ich creates the world, only our knowledge of it ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-1]]). Where Kant's Ich carried the burden of compliance with the Categorical Imperative, Fichte's acted from free will; there were no absolute truths or laws created by God, only the self ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-2]]). In his own reported words, 'The source of all reality is the Ich' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch01-happy-event-1]]), and a person 'should be self-determined, never letting himself be defined by anything external' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-1]]). He also held that only those who try to make others free are free ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-2]], [[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-influence-selfish]]).

## Revolution and reception

Fichte called the French Revolution the 'breaking dawn' and said his system analysed the concept of freedom; Wulf's claim is that the Ich as first principle was 'as revolutionary as any of the political changes witnessed in France', promising freedom to students in states that regulated marriage and private theatricals ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-1]], [[wulf-magnificent-rebels-prologue-2]]). Students flocked to him (Hölderlin called him the 'soul of Jena'); a Sunday-lecture row and a five-month fraternity campaign of broken windows followed ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch03-finest-minds-2]]). He said he developed his system 'with the listeners, and before their eyes' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-2]]). Critics predicted egotism and despotism, Herder punned against him, Goethe joked 'Dear non-Ich', and the *Xenien* mocked the Ich in a couplet ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-2]], [[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch04-intellectual-friction-2]]). Wulf judges much of the criticism unfair but notes his precarious balance between the empowered self, narcissism and equality, and his volatile temper ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch02-priest-of-truth-2]]). His quarrel with Schiller over a *Horen* essay lasted years ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch03-finest-minds-2]]).

## The atheism dispute

Fichte's article identified God with the 'living and active moral order'. The elector of Saxony complained; Fichte answered with the combative *Appeal to the Public*, then bluffed a resignation that Voigt accepted. Wulf argues the affair was never really about religion: in the duke's letters the worry was Fichte's revolutionary leanings and influence over students. In Berlin, Kant called the *Wissenschaftslehre* untenable and Fichte published Kant's private admission of the weakness of his old age ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch12-atheism-dispute]]).

## Marriage, women and the free-love circle

Wulf argues Fichte 'seemed to forget his celebration of free will when it came to women': his Ich was only male ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch13-divorce-women-sex]]). His attitude to marriage was 'resoundingly traditional', and he withdrew, hurt, from the Caroline-Schelling arrangement and the failed 'Jena Colony' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-1]]).

## The break with Schelling

Schelling objected that the Ich cannot found the world (is there light only so that rational beings can see each other?) and charged Fichte with an 'annihilation of nature'; his Absolute preceded subject and object ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-1]]). The split went public, and adversaries jeered 'when philosophers start eating one another like starving rats' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-2]]). See [[naturphilosophie-wulf]].

## The Ich of a nation

In his 1807 Berlin *Addresses to the German Nation* Fichte argued the Germans formed a nation by shared, living language. Wulf's retrospective judgement is that he 'paved the way for a bigger Ich - the Ich of a nation', 'a dangerous idea, and one that would be exploited in Germany in the future' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1]]). Friedrich Schlegel later accused him of 'idolising the Ich' and confusing the self with the divine ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1]]). Wulf gives this link a single paragraph ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1]]); see [[inwardness-without-a-state-wulf]], [[nationalism]] and [[romanticism-and-political-danger]]. Her Epilogue nonetheless defends him against 'Ich-fetishism' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-influence-selfish]]; see [[art-of-being-selfish-wulf]], [[invention-of-the-self-wulf]]).
