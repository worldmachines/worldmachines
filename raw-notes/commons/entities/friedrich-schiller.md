# Friedrich Schiller (1759–1805)

## Biographical Context

- Son of a gardener
- Playwright, poet, philosopher — contemporary of Goethe and Kant
- Key texts for [[Kulturkritik]]: *Letters on the Aesthetic Education of Man* (1794), "On Naïve and Sentimental Poetry" (1795), "Concerning the Sublime" (1801)
- Strong interest in horticulture: reviewed garden calendar for 1795; identified Hohenheim Park near Stuttgart as his horticultural ideal

## Schiller's Kulturkritik (per Kempf)

Schiller positions his work as "an answer to the French Revolution gone awry." Where [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]] blamed cultural overrefinement in general, Schiller identifies **overspecialization** as the specific culprit of self-alienation. Like Rousseau, he sees the Enlightenment as both cause and cure.

His norm, however, is not original nature (which he sees as a wretched "troglodyte" state) but **the Greeks of antiquity**, whose "wholeness of being is irretrievably lost" ("AE," p. 102).

## The Drive Theory

Schiller's *Aesthetic Education* proposes that modern humans are split between two drives:
- **Sensuous drive** (Sinntrieb): the material, sensory, particular
- **Formal drive** (Formtrieb): the rational, universal, abstract

The **play drive** (Spieltrieb) sublates both, producing "living form" (lebende Gestalt) — the incarnation of beauty. Key claim: "It is only through beauty that man makes his way to freedom" ("AE," p. 90).

This is Schiller's reformulation of the [[Nature-Culture Dialectic]]: the synthesis is achieved not through nature (Rousseau) nor through pure reason, but through *art*.

## The Garden as Integrative Ideal

Schiller's horticultural ideal is a "middle way between the rigidity of the French style of garden and the anarchy of the so-called English garden" — explicitly attributed to Hirschfeld's *Theory of Garden Design* (1779). The Hohenheim Park "combines such disparate elements into a whole" — it is "soulfully inspired and artistically exalted nature, which satisfies not just the untutored person but even the person spoiled by culture."

This garden is an "integrative ideal" — it appeals to both intellect and feeling. But Schiller is "disregarding actuality for the sake of appearance and effect": the ideal is a postulate, not a description.

## The Aesthetic Semblance Problem

In the "Twenty-Second Letter," Schiller writes that the truly successful work of art requires an artist "who can make his form consume the material" — the beautiful semblance of art is a "calculated effect in which culture keeps nature in check."

Schiller's own admission: his ideal is "aesthetic semblance" — a beauty that is only an illusion, a freedom that exists only in fiction. This makes his Kulturkritik self-consciously a move from "renaturalization" (Rousseau) to "refinement" — an "*éducation esthétique*" rather than an "*éducation naturelle*."

## The Sublime Paradox

"Concerning the Sublime" (1801) reveals the deepest contradiction in Schiller's position. He prefers the "inspiring disorder of a natural landscape" to the "insipid regularity of a French garden." But to *use* this nature as a source of the sublime, it must be "brought under the control of culture." In the sublime, man becomes "conscious of his independence from nature" — and what the sublime generates *is* culture, not nature.

Kempf's summary: "Schiller criticizes the sedative culture of the French garden and, more broadly, harmony and beauty, in favor of an experience of nature that delivers ... more culture."

## Key Distinction from Rousseau

| Rousseau | Schiller |
|----------|----------|
| Man in natural state = innocent, wholesome | Man in natural state = "troglodyte" (miserable) |
| Reflection is "contrary to nature" | Reflection *begins* humanity |
| Thinking human being = "depraved animal" | Taking "delight in semblance ... ornamentation and play" |
| Cure: return to nature or nascent society | Cure: aesthetic education through art |
| Model: the Swiss peasant | Model: the Greeks |

## Wulf's Schiller: the enabler who left (Magnificent Rebels)

Andrea Wulf's [[wulf-magnificent-rebels]] gives the life and circle around the *Aesthetic Education* discussed above.

**Before Goethe.** Formed under the despotic Duke Karl Eugen, author of *The Robbers* (frontispiece 'In Tirannos'), Schiller fled Württemberg for a poorly paid Jena chair; in 1794 he was ill and afraid that Kant had killed his poetry ('cold reason my poetry') ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch01-happy-event-1]]). His friendship with Goethe began in a disagreement between his Kantian idealism and Goethe's realism, and became a complementary method ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch01-happy-event-2]]).

**Horen and the Letters in context.** Once an honorary French citizen, Schiller was horrified by the king's execution and the guillotine; in his own words the French were like 'wild animals' needing 'salutary chains', and the Revolution 'had happened too soon'. Wulf presents *Horen* as 'a writer's response', a deliberately apolitical journal of the German Kulturnation, and says it made the Jena Set a group for the first time ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch03-finest-minds-1]]). In her summary of the *Letters*, 'Utility is the great idol of our time', and beauty, not reason alone, is the route to freedom; she calls the *Letters* a founding document for the Romantics, who would raise imagination above reason ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch03-finest-minds-1]]). The notes read him as a critic from within the Enlightenment via Kant.

**Social thinking and quarrels.** With Wilhelm von Humboldt and Goethe he formed 'a three-leaf clover of critical thought' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch03-finest-minds-2]]). He feared the 'vortex' of Fichte's Ich and broke with Fichte after four drafts of a rejection letter, over whether the artist or the scholar is humankind's teacher ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch03-finest-minds-2]]). Overwhelmed by *Horen*, he recruited August Wilhelm Schlegel ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch04-intellectual-friction-1]]), then decided to 'close up the philosophical shop' for poetry ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch04-intellectual-friction-2]]). Friedrich Schlegel's reviews made him dismiss August Wilhelm; privately he called Alexander von Humboldt a show-off of 'naked, analytical reason', which Wulf attributes to jealousy, and judged the young generation 'too self-centred', having 'taken Fichte's Ich-philosophy too literally' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch06-splendid-circle-2]], [[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch07-little-academy-2]]). The *Athenaeum* punished him with silence ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch09-sublime-impertinence-2]]).

**Withdrawal and death.** He left Jena for Weimar to escape the Schlegels, still finding 'all that individuality shimmering on every page so repulsive' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch14-schlegel-clique]], [[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-1]]). No one from the Schlegel house visited while his wife Charlotte lay gravely ill ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch15-new-confederation]]). He wondered why Goethe kept their company ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-2]]). Famous, ennobled and comfortable, he found life 'monotonous and empty' and remembered being 'electrified by our intellectual friction'; he died on 9 May 1805, and Wulf reads his complaints as the cost of pushing a weakened body, not hypochondria ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch19-current-exodus]]).

**Wulf's verdict.** She calls him the 'unsung hero': through *Horen* and his invitations he 'made possible' the 'Alliance of Minds', and the *Letters* underlie the Schlegels' beliefs even as they disowned him. His own line that posterity would turn them 'from contemporaries into neighbours' is his doubt that they shared a creed ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-2]]; see [[jena-set-wulf]]).
