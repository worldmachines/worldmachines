# The Two Currents of Love (Gay)

In *The Tender Passion* ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2]]), [[peter-gay]] takes Freud's distinction between the tender (affectionate) and the sensual currents of love as the volume's organizing frame. The bare idea, that love joins affection to desire, is ancient and broad. This page records how Gay uses it: as a historical consensus, a bourgeois ideal, and an interpretive yardstick.

## Freud's formulation

A normal love unites the two currents. The affectionate current is older, rooted in early childhood. The sensual current arrives with puberty after a long latency, and the incest barrier forces it onto new objects that are still chosen on the infantile model. Freud thereby "threw his bridge between physiology and psychology": libido is biological yet expressed in feelings ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch1-3-physiology-psychology]]). The neurotic split is the other side: men who "where they love ... do not desire, and where they desire ... cannot love" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch1-1-conflicting-legacies]]). Don Juan, far from a triumph, is read as unable to unite the currents ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch1-3-physiology-psychology]]). Love as "refinding" an early parental object belongs to the same account ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-counterpoint-2-new-objects-old]]).

## A consensus with conflicting legacies

Gay presents the union of concupiscence and affection as the one point "on which cynics, metaphysicians, researchers, and ordinary bourgeois could cheerfully unite," from Plutarch and scripture to Freud. The real disputes concerned the proper places of eros and affection. Christian asceticism subordinated concupiscence to affection within lifelong marriage as a "regulative if not always an attainable ideal." The Enlightenment had "a far more intense interest in sex than in love." The Romantics purified sensuality and subordinated it to the spiritual ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch1-1-conflicting-legacies]]). Stendhal's reformist *De l'amour* also joins affection to passion ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch1-2-cynics-enthusiasts]]).

## The ideal, and Gay's claim that it was lived

Critics disagreed on whether the bourgeois erred toward sexless sentiment or toward sensuality, but both sides wanted the strands combined. Gay claims the synthesis was "a matter not only of hope but of experience" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch2-2-desire-holy-pure]]). His witnesses include Beatrice Webb, whose verdict on her marriage was "Work and Love", so that the confluence of the two currents "was not a Utopia" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch2-1-sober-realities]]). Mabel Loomis Todd "would not have known how to segregate affection from passion" ([[mabel-loomis-todd]]; [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch2-2-desire-holy-pure]]). The Roes' Civil War letters call married desire "holy & pure" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch2-2-desire-holy-pure]]). Fanny Kingsley reasoned her way, by Scripture, to a Christian version of love's two interwoven strands ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch5-3-glandular-christianity-part-2]]; see [[charles-kingsley]]). The respectable physician George Napheys defined true love as the union of love's physical and moral natures in marriage, and Gay maps Geefs's *The Lion in Love* (raw sexual power tamed by beauty) onto that definition as a cherished ideal, a statement of an ideal rather than a description of practice ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-epilogue-2-lion-love-last-thoughts]]).

## Split lovers as self-diagnosing witnesses

Gay's failures are read in their own words rather than through imposed theory. Holtei's "the angel in me" sought another angel while "the animal demanded its like." Amiel set spiritual love against "nocturnal" imagination. Mill wished to find "a sincere friend" and "a sincere Magdalen" in one woman ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch2-2-desire-holy-pure]]). The critic Statham charged that Wagner separates "the two currents of love," cultivating passion over affection ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch5-1-food-love]]). Thackeray's letter about "investing a trull in the street with that priceless jewel my heart" shows lust and affection running in "divergent directions", and Gay reads prostitution as the institutional form of that split, which rescuers aimed to rejoin ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch6-2-hysteria-remorse-part-3]]).

## Gay against Freud's pessimism

For Freud, perfect fusion is "fragile, rather uncommon." Gay replies that this pessimism "outran his evidence and his experience" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch1-3-physiology-psychology]]). He also finds that Freud's 1908 claim that modern marriage makes the fusion rare rests on patients who were "the casualties of their culture" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch6-1-modern-malady-love]]). The frame is Freud's; the verdict that the union was often reached is Gay's, drawn from diaries and letters he admits are a tepid record of what was lived ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch2-2-desire-holy-pure]]).

## Hedges

- The theory belongs to Freud and to the sources. The diarists supply evidence, not the concept ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch2-2-desire-holy-pure]]).
- Gay's witnesses are weighted toward the literate and the documented, and his readings of dreams and letters are marked as inference ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-counterpoint-2-new-objects-old]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch2-2-desire-holy-pure]]).
- As a World Machines matter the page is about a class's emotional ideal and its gap from practice. It makes no structural claim.
