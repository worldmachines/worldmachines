# Raynal

This page draws on Volume II of [[john-morley]]'s [[diderot-and-the-encyclopaedists]] ([[morley-diderot-v2]]), whose Chapter VII treats the abbé Raynal's *Philosophical and Political History of European settlements and commerce in the two Indies*. Merits and faults are Morley's judgments; Grimm, Walpole, Gibbon, Franklin and others are quoted contemporaries.

## The man

Born in 1711, Raynal was in Morley's sketch a needy priest turned bookmaker who grew rich and founded academy prizes, including one on whether the discovery of America had helped or harmed humanity. He had a habit of cross-examining dinner guests at Holbach's table ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-1]]).

## A many-handed book

Grimm predicted immortality for the History, yet Morley says “not one hundred persons now living have ever read two chapters” of it. It has “no unity of composition” and reads like “half a dozen writers”; Diderot was popularly credited with at least a third. Morley generalises that the French insurgents suppressed authorial vanity, as the Jesuits did, because of their constant need for secrecy ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-1]]).

## Reception

It ran to twenty-odd editions and was read by Franklin, Gibbon, Robertson and Toussaint Louverture, who found there “the first definite expression” of thoughts the brutalities of slavery had stirred. Frederick the Great admired it until he met a passage rebuking him; when Raynal later visited Berlin the king answered “I don't know them” ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-1]]).

## Merits and faults

Morley judges that “the instinct of the gaping crowd was sound”. Raynal is below Voltaire and Robertson in judgment but has “a luminous breadth of outlook”, as when he notes that the weightiest effect of the Portuguese conquests was the check on Ottoman power. Against this: no dates, no authorities, no criticism; the Manco-Capac legend turned into sober narrative; savage and distant life idealised into idylls. Raynal's plea for Polly Baker, an unmarried mother in Puritan New England, was Franklin's invention as a newspaper filler, and Raynal's reply (“I would rather relate your stories than other men's truths”) becomes Morley's emblem of the book's attitude to fact ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-1]]).

## Why it caught fire

Morley gives surface causes: variety, a new and childlike curiosity about the world beyond France, a concrete form compared with Montesquieu's, and national mortification after the loss of Canada and India in 1763, with Guiana as a consolation utopia ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-1]]). The deeper source, he argues, was that it fed two “master emotions” of the age, hatred of religion and the passion for justice and freedom. He reads its account of the Spanish conquest as an indictment of papal Christianity, hedged by Aztec sacrifice and by Las Casas being a Catholic ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-2]]).

## Slavery

In Morley's view the book brought “the lower races finally within the pale of right and duty” in French opinion; the notes flag the phrase as Morley's Victorian hierarchy. Where Montesquieu gave a rational account of slavery's growth, Raynal refused to treat it as anything but an offence to reason; Morley reads this as a revolt against the historic method. Raynal appeals to kings for redress and prophesies a black Spartacus (“how terrible will the White Code be!”), words Morley says acted upon Toussaint ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-2]]).

## The recoil and the end

Raynal's letter of 31 May 1791 to the National Assembly attacked a government “enslaved by popular tyranny” and defended persecuted priests; Robespierre excused it as the apostasy of age. Morley contrasts him with [[nicolas-de-condorcet]], who kept faith, and calls him “of weaker mould”, with a “stratum of cant and borrowed formulas”. He lost his property and died in poverty in 1796; Morley closes by calling him “a powerful pleader for noble causes” ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-2]]). The bishops' memorials of Diderot's last years asked for repression of Raynal's History along with Voltaire's Kehl edition, and Morley links Figaro's laugh to Raynal's saying that the law is a sword levelling what rises ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch08-closing-years-2]]).

## World Machines resonances, with the notes' hedges

The many-handed book rhymes with the [[history-machine]] idea of a paradigm assembled from many hands, though Morley's point is about publication conditions and vanity ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-1]]). Its idylls overlap the [[noble-savage]] motif, which Morley judges a historiographical vice with one modest merit, teaching that savage life is coherent, not chaos ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-1]]). Its circulation shows ideas moving through a print network, compare [[eisenstein-printing-revolution]], and its author's recoil is a small case of a precursor turning from the revolution he helped prepare ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-2]]).
