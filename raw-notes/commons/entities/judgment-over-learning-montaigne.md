A principle that runs from Book I's essays on schooling to Book III's on conversation in Montaigne's *Essays* ([[montaigne-complete-essays]]): what matters is the formation of judgment, not the storing of learning.

## Better learned, not more learned

Of Pedantry asks why the farces make the pedant the fool. The answer is that schooling aims at stocking the head, not at judgment and virtue. “We should rather examine, who is better learned, than who is more learned.” Judgment can get along without knowledge, but knowledge cannot get along without judgment. Pedants are birds that carry grain home without tasting it. Montaigne turns the charge on himself, since his own book culls sentences that are “no more mine than in their first places”, and he names the borrowing without retracting it. Learning must “give it a thorough and perfect dye”, or be left alone. Adrian Turnebus, learned without pedantry, shows the fault lies in formation, not in study ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch24-pedantry]]).

## Digestion and the bees

In Of the Education of Children the tutor should have a well-made rather than a well-filled head. Pedagogues pour knowledge “into a funnel”; to “disgorge what we eat in the same condition it was swallowed” shows indigestion. Bees cull their sweets and then make honey that is their own, “that is to say, his judgment”. “To know by rote, is no knowledge.” The pupil should hear diverse opinions and may “remain in doubt” if he cannot choose ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch25-education-children-1]]).

## The world as a book

The tutor should teach the manners of Hannibal and Scipio, not the date of Carthage's fall. Plutarch is praised for pointing “with his finger” and leaving the reader to “grope out the rest”. “This great world” is “the mirror wherein we are to behold ourselves”. Philosophy should come early and cheerfully, with virtue placed on a “fair, fruitful, and flourishing plain” and not on a precipice. And “'Tis not a soul, 'tis not a body that we are training up, but a man”, which is why the rod-wielding colleges are condemned ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch25-education-children-2]]).

## Conference

Conversation is “the most fruitful and natural exercise of the mind”. A rough disputant stirs him “to something above myself”, and he is prouder of beating himself in argument than of beating an opponent. The schools' logic has produced no understanding: learning is “in the hand of some 'tis a sceptre, in that of others a fool's bauble”. “To hunt after truth is properly our business”; possession “belongs to a greater power”. Experience counts only when the holder can “weigh, sort and distil” it ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch08-art-conference-1]]).

## Reading for judgment

Of Books asks the reader to attend to Montaigne's method and to how well he chooses what he borrows. He weighs his borrowings and does not count them. He names the confession of ignorance one of the surest testimonies of judgment. He ranks historians by how much they leave the reader to judge ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch10-books]]). Even his weak memory becomes an argument: without a stocked memory he is forced to use his own judgment, and he observes that a strong memory is often coupled with infirm judgment ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch06-10-hour-parley-dangerous]]).

## Natural wisdom and the two ignorances

Socrates drew on carters, joiners and cobblers, “nothing in all this borrowed from arts and sciences”. “We are all of us richer than we think we are; but we are taught to borrow and to beg.” Books “have not so much served me for instruction as exercise” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch12-physiognomy-1]]). An “Abecedarian ignorance” comes before knowledge and a “doctoral ignorance” after it. The half-educated “mongrels” in between, among whom he places himself, “trouble the world” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch50-55-democritus-heraclitus]]). Education teaches us to “decline Virtue” but not to love it ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch17-presumption-3]]).

## Hedges

The principle carries some strains the notes keep visible. Of Pedantry sounds elitist and fatalistic about natures (“Degenerate and vulgar souls are unworthy of philosophy”). It closes with an unreconciled claim that letters soften courage ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch24-pedantry]]). Montaigne is himself a heavy borrower throughout. The notes link the principle to [[socratic-thinking]], and the early reflection on stitching borrowed passages into one's prose to [[printing-press-and-information-overload-eisenstein]], with the hedge that his concern is authorial honesty, not the volume of print ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch25-education-children-1]]). For how this trust in judgment sits beside the Apology's attack on reason, see [[reason-demolished-judgment-trusted-montaigne]].

## Conduct, things and plain speech

The closing third of the education essay tests the pupil by conduct: “The conduct of our lives is the true mirror of our doctrine.” The young body should be bent to all fashions, and the rule for the will is “Let him be able to do everything, but love to do nothing but what is good.” Things come before words. Montaigne suspects that inability to express a conception is a failure of conception, and he wants a style “natural and plain, the same in writing as in speaking”, since “force and sinews are never to be borrowed”. He then tells his own schooling: Latin as his mother tongue under his father's scheme, its corruption at the College of Guienne, and Ovid read by appetite under a tutor who connived at truancy. Forced books breed hatred of books, and the essay ends on children as asses laden with books ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch25-education-children-3]]). A Consideration upon Cicero asks readers to praise his matter rather than his language, since his stories and quotations carry “the seed of a more rich and a bolder matter” ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b1-ch39-consideration-cicero]]).

## Testing borrowed sufficiency

Of the Art of Conference adds a method for conversation. Men are rich in borrowed sufficiency, and a good word may be said by someone who does not see its force, which Montaigne admits may hold of himself. So he presses speakers for the why or how, and treats vague praise of a page of Virgil as the safe refuge of the cunning ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch08-art-conference-2]]). Of Physiognomy calls his book a nosegay of foreign flowers. He mocks gatherers of commonplaces and says his own practice is to steal a passage and alter it for new service, so that the ornaments do not hide him ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b3-ch12-physiognomy-3]]). Of Presumption ties this to his failing memory: “I turn over books; I do not study them”, and what stays is what his judgment has used ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch17-presumption-2]]). The Defence of Seneca and Plutarch shows judgment applied to authorities themselves. It discounts Dion as an inconsistent witness and reads Plutarch's parallels as comparing manners piece by piece, not ranking nations ([[montaigne-complete-essays-b2-ch32-defence-seneca-plutarch]]).
