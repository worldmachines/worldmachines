# Archaic Globalization (Bayly)

In [[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world]], “archaic globalization” names a real prehistory of globalization, older than any system of nation-states. It had three engines: universal kingship, cosmic religion and humoral bodily practice. Bayly's summary contrast is sharp: archaic globalization strategically *consumed* diversity, whereas the modern world's globalization is market-driven uniformity ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch01-fringes-and-archaic-globalization]]). The suffix marks the term as Bayly's.

## The three engines

- **Universal kingship.** Kings prized “men from afar” and honorific goods such as Kashmiri shawls, Chinese silks and Arab horses as tools of legitimacy. The Alexander legend and Aristotelian ethics circulated as a shared moral currency from Christendom to the Indo-Islamic world.
- **Cosmic religion.** Pilgrimage to Jerusalem, Rome and Mecca, and the expansion of Sufism, moved people across vast distances for reasons of faith.
- **Humoral bodily practice.** A shared logic of humoral medicine drove the movement of spices and precious substances. Tea, tobacco and opium were first medicines and only later mass commodities ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch01-fringes-and-archaic-globalization]]).

The political world that carried these networks was porous and often ruled by outsiders. Bayly adopts the Indian term *pardeshi*, a familiar outsider from just over the fuzzy border, and argues that pardeshi rule was closer to the eighteenth-century norm than any bounded, ethnically self-ruling polity ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch01-fringes-and-archaic-globalization]]). The agrarian empires beside them deliberately cultivated religious and ethnic pluralism. The note on that span says explicitly that it does not yet use the phrase “archaic globalization”; it lays the old-regime groundwork on which the concept is built ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch01-old-regime-society-and-difference]]).

## Caste as the shared grid

Before race or nationality “as understood at the end of the nineteenth century” dominated, caste in the original Portuguese sense supplied an interlocking ranking by purity and lineage. It was crystallized in the Mexican manual *Las Castas Mexicanas*, and it was portable enough that Indian, Islamic and Chinese observers could each map their own idioms of status onto it. Bayly keeps it a “flexible and indistinct” idiom, not yet a state technology ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch01-fringes-and-archaic-globalization]]).

## From archaic to proto-capitalist

Borrowing Appadurai's word, Bayly describes the new chartered-company trade in tea, tobacco, opium and slaves as “cannibalizing” the older networks of kingship, religion and the body rather than replacing them. Slave capture, for instance, had been a strategy for building the archaic African or Ottoman household; it became a brutal proto-capitalist industry. A shared millenarian anxiety bridged the eras as well. A sixteenth-century Christian-Muslim millenarian conjuncture recurred after 1720, when Buddhist, Muslim and Sikh millennial currents met the secular millenarianism of the French Revolution ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch01-fringes-and-archaic-globalization]]). The introduction insists the mutual cannibalizing of global and local long predates the contemporary phase of globalization ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-intro-thematic-problems-of-history]]).

## Captured, not erased

After 1815 the nation-state, defined above all by existing among rival nation-states, pressed international connections into national form and eroded the old ecumenes, honorific exchange and trade diasporas ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch06c-new-imperialism-and-internationalism]]). Yet the older networks persisted much as before 1780. They included:

- common-law lineages and evangelical networks;
- coolie migration and migration descended from slavery;
- the “charismatic” non-market demand for gold in India and the Middle East;
- hypogamy across racial lines, and temporary Muslim marriages linking the Arab, Indian and Muslim East;
- royal and aristocratic intermarriage.

What changed was that nation-states increasingly represented and controlled these networks in national terms. Bayly's point is paradoxical rather than a story of decline. Three cases show the capture:

- The passport moved from a royal letter for elite travellers to an instrument of state surveillance.
- The Red Cross became “the obverse of the Swiss flag” and split into national committees and the Red Crescent.
- At the 1893 World Parliament of Religions, nationality controlled who could “represent” a religion, and Swami Vivekananda turned the universalist platform into an assertion of Indian nationhood ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch06c-new-imperialism-and-internationalism]]).

Archaic religious travel was retooled in the same way. Steamships, railways and the telegraph made the Hajj cheap and safe enough for middle-class pilgrims, and it fed the pan-Islamic consciousness of the later century ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-pilgrimage-print-nation]]).

## World Machines resonances, with their hedges

- **[[history-machine]].** A new order assembling itself out of redirected, pre-existing components is a loose but genuine rhyme. Bayly's builders, however, are chartered companies and slavers, not a silent historiographic process, and the parts being cannibalized are moral and religious ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch01-fringes-and-archaic-globalization]]).
- **[[legibility-as-machine-core]].** Caste reads as a rough forerunner in miniature of classificatory ambition, but it worked through honour, blood and purity rather than census and statistics ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch01-fringes-and-archaic-globalization]]). The later renationalization is a standardizing dynamic, but here uniformity comes from rivalry between states rather than from a single administrative apparatus ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch06c-new-imperialism-and-internationalism]]).

## Mann: a first pass at globalization in Fujian

Charles C. Mann's [[mann-1493]] offers a neighbouring periodization in two passes, without citing Bayly.

- **The first pass.** Mann calls the medieval Eurasian system, overland and by sea, 'a first pass at globalization' that 'reached its apogee in the fourteenth century'. Its eastern terminus was Zaytun (Quanzhou), which Ibn Battuta called the biggest port in the world ([[mann-1493-appendix-b-globalization-in-beta]]).
- **Bulk, not boutique.** Against Marco Polo's luxury-focused picture, Fujianese traders 'made most of their money from items that Polo would have found mundane', such as bulk copper and iron for Southeast Asian temples ([[mann-1493-appendix-b-globalization-in-beta]]). This sits in some tension with the honorific goods this page places at the centre of archaic exchange, though the two accounts may describe different layers of trade. The tension is this section's reading.
- **'Foreign' as a legal category.** Chinese converted to Islam to register as foreigners with privileges, so foreignness was a fiscal and legal status as much as an ethnic one ([[mann-1493-appendix-b-globalization-in-beta]]).
- **Continuity of people.** After the Ming let Zaytun silt up, its trading families resurfaced in the Yuegang silver trade, so the sixteenth-century 'birth of globalization' inherited personnel and know-how from the first pass ([[mann-1493-appendix-b-globalization-in-beta]]).
- **What was new after 1492.** In the body of the book Mann concedes the Silk Road and Indian Ocean networks but claims nothing before joined both hemispheres at a scale able to disrupt societies on opposite sides of the planet. The notes read this as sitting in tension with this page's stress on older integration, while noting that Mann does concede the earlier networks ([[mann-1493-ch01-seams-of-pangaea]], [[mann-1493-ch01-shiploads-of-silver]]).

The hedge the notes carry: Mann offers a narrative, not a theory, and his two passes are not a model of how one globalization cannibalizes another.
