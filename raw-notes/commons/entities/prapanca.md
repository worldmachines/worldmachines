# Prapanca

Prapanca was the Majapahit court poet who wrote the *Nagarakertagama* (*Depiction of
the Districts*) in 1359 — the text that became, five centuries later, the primary
documentary foundation for everything known about the empire at its height.

**Source**: [[vanderlinde-majapahit]] (Herald van der Linde).

*Correction note.* The wiki's existing [[majapahit-empire]] page and
[[history-machines-in-the-margins-lohse]] describe this figure as an anonymous
"oddball court official." He is named, dated and biographically drawn in this batch,
and his book is used as the book's own evidentiary spine.

## The man

A bookish, drink-loving, unlucky-in-love poet, son of a Buddhist *dharmadhyaksa*
(bishop), who joined King Hayam Wuruk's three-month, 900-kilometre royal progress
through eastern Java in 1359 deputizing for his aged father
([[vanderlinde-majapahit-ch01]]). He was a childhood playmate of Hayam Wuruk before
becoming court scribe ([[vanderlinde-majapahit-ch09]]).

## The book nobody wanted

He broke the rules of epic composition by writing about ordinary life instead of
gods and battles, and contemporary critics mocked the result as dull and lacking in
magic or heroism ([[vanderlinde-majapahit-ch01]]). What offended contemporary taste
is exactly what makes the text valuable now. His journey notes work as a field
survey: salt harvesting, forest trade in ceramics, dye, cloth and dried fish, the
*akuwu* petty landowners granted estates for military service, and the bondsmen who
worked their fields after being sold into servitude for unpaid fines.

This is the cleanest instance of [[history-machine]] logic in the batch — a source
that means nothing to its own era's judges and only lurches into visibility
generations later — and it is the concrete case behind
[[history-machines-in-the-margins-lohse]]'s "notes in the margins" figure.

## Survival by accident

The text's transmission is a chain of near-losses. Javanese *lontar*-leaf
manuscripts had to be hand-copied every few decades against humidity and ants
([[vanderlinde-majapahit-ch01]]). The *Nagarakertagama* survived through Balinese
manuscript copying, and the sole surviving copy was pulled from a fire at Lombok in
1894 — the philologist J.L.A. Brandes grabbing "bunches of dried leaves tied
together with a string" out of a burning treasury during the Dutch conquest while
his comrades stuffed their pockets with gold
([[vanderlinde-majapahit-introduction]]). The epilogue calls what he saved "the best
primary historical source for the Majapahit empire at its peak," and treats the whole
record of Majapahit as assembling in fits and starts from independent, near-accidental
survivals across three centuries ([[vanderlinde-majapahit-ch16]]).

## The chronicler as accomplice

The book's most pointed use of Prapanca is not as a witness but as a curator of the
official past.

At the Darbaru monastery in 1359 the abbot Ranangsa reads him the *Chronicle of
Kings*, and Prapanca discovers that the dynasty's actual founder was Ken Arok —
"a brutal robber, a liar, a cheat, a rapist and a murderer" — rather than
Wisnuwardhana, the founder named in the official genealogy he was taught as a child
([[vanderlinde-majapahit-ch02]]). He carries the story back to court, where Gajah
Mada cross-examines it and a *puspa* ceremony at Kagenengan converts it into ritual
proof that the king is "of divine origin." Prapanca's reflexive aside — "Not that I
ever thought otherwise" — is the tell that the belief is being performed into
existence rather than simply held. The same abbot who supplies the flattering myth
withholds the family's real record of fratricide and cursed daggers
([[vanderlinde-majapahit-ch03]]).

And Prapanca himself buries the Ken Arok revelation. Van der Linde's gloss is blunt:
the empire's first great chronicler was "the early equivalent of a brand ambassador"
([[vanderlinde-majapahit-ch09]]). The regime's official memory is curated rather than
recorded, with the historian complicit in the founding myth's preservation — a
different mechanism from the conqueror-on-rival destruction catalogued at
[[deliberate-archival-erasure]], and one worth holding alongside it.
