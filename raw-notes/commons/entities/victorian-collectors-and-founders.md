# Victorian Collectors, Patrons and Founders (Gay)

**Source**: [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5]], Chapters Four (*Hunters and Gatherers*) and Five (*Movers and Shakers*).

## Why Collect

- **Buying as proof.** The chapter opens with Dumas fils: buying a painting is "the only way of proving" you like it ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s1-a-map-of-motives]]).
- **A near-clinical list of rewards.** Collecting "gives control ... power over a selected domain." It summons memories. It builds "a close-knit fraternity among fellow specialists." It licenses boasting. For the lonely, objects become "affectionate, uncomplaining companions." Pure speculators, who lack "impassioned engagement," do not count as collectors ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s1-a-map-of-motives]]).
- **Conversion.** In 1887 Charles Freer saw a friend's Whistler etchings, and Gay likens the evening to the road to Damascus. It ended in near-adoration of Whistler and the purchase of the Peacock Room. Many great collectors were unmarried and made their collections into surrogate families ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s1-a-map-of-motives]]).
- **Freud.** Freud owned more than two thousand antiquities. He "found sublimated erotic satisfaction in his acquisitions" and defined the collector as one who "directs his surplus libido at something inanimate: love of things." Gay pointedly separates this from Marx's commodity fetishism: money is only "some of the guts" of pleasure ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s1-a-map-of-motives]]).
- **Autobiography and obsession.** "Collecting is a kind of autobiography": Liebermann's slow conversion to Pissarro and Morisot is legible in what he bought. Degas said "I can't stop myself." Chocquet, a clerk, "sacrificed to his obsession all he had." Berlin's unbaptized Jewish collectors James Simon and Eduard Arnhold collected under real pressure to assimilate, yet status-seeking was "dwarfed by the lust after objects for their own sake": "they bought art because they loved art" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s2-collecting-as-autobiography]]).

## Patrons

The Maecenas survived "in the modern manner," with roles "no longer so clear-cut." Examples include von Meck and Tchaikovsky, and Fiedler and Marées, whose break Fiedler called "simultaneously a tragedy and a comedy." Artists were "glad to be free of [such alliances] yet eager for the security they promised." Constable dreaded that "the publick is hard, cruel and unrelenting" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s3-the-victorian-maecenas]]). Patronage also paid "narcissistic dividends." Willet's mansion held costumed self-portraits, and Bruyas had some thirty images of himself made. Courbet's *The Meeting*, which Bruyas commissioned, makes the painter the superior figure ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s3-the-victorian-maecenas]]).

## The Education to Modernism, and Its Limits

- **Conversions.** Havemeyer, Camondo, Faure, Koehler and the Fischers all move from Old Masters, Barbizon or Salon painting toward Impressionism and beyond. Shchukin commissioned *Dance* and *Music* from Matisse and suggested themes: "The customer had become a patron." Louisine Havemeyer was the venturesome half of her marriage, begging her husband for Courbet's *La Femme au perroquet* ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s3-the-victorian-maecenas]]). Koehler funded Marc and Kandinsky's *Blue Rider* ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s3-the-victorian-maecenas]]).
- **Rescues.** The Steins, the Cone sisters, Shchukin and Morosov bought Picasso out of poverty ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s4-against-the-need-for-probability]]). Cézanne's first buyers (Tanguy, Gachet, Chocquet) were vindicated ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s2-collecting-as-autobiography]]). The Rouen magnate Depeaux's fifty-three Impressionists were rejected by the city once and celebrated a few years later ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-bexp-s1-flaubertus]]).
- **Those who never converted.** Walters paid record prices for Meissonier and Gérôme into the 1880s. Frick and Gardner ranged widely without committing. Gay's verdict: bourgeois collectors "acted as agents of cultural change," and "if, as the avant-garde charged, they all too often followed the fashion, there were times when they made it" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s3-the-victorian-maecenas]]).

## Founders

- **Motives.** Founders' motives included revenge on heirs, memorials to spouses, status, patriotism, the wish for a name that lasts, and guilt. Carnegie's private 1868 memorandum against the worship of money is the proof text. Gay closes on the Freudian-inflected aside that for many "the love of art was just a love of art" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch05-s1-founders]]).
- **A triangle of donors, publics and managers.** Friction among the three could push toward tradition (the Frankfurt Museum, with its hushed audiences and banned dogs) or toward innovation: "Not all movers were shakers." Higginson founded the Boston Symphony as a refusal of the capitalist "iron cage" a generation before Weber coined the phrase. The Berlin Philharmoniker was a "peaceful revolution" by its own musicians. After 1871, newly emancipated Jewish wealth filled the Städel's Museums-Verein ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch05-s1-founders]]).
- **Riskless taste.**
  - Fodor bought "picturesque, entertaining, beautifully done" French works, with "no Daumiers."
  - Schwabe's bequest had "not one undraped figure."
  - Wagener's 262 paintings became the core of Berlin's National-Galerie. He was exacting with painters, waiting "like a child for the Christmas tree," and self-abasing before the crown. Gay adds: "In an age of nationalism, art was rarely untouched by politics" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch05-s2-buttresses-for-the-establishment]]).
- **Fights inside the institutions.**
  - Lichtwark's Hamburg Kunsthalle kept Liebermann's portrait of Mayor Petersen behind a curtain until 1905. Gay calls this "an intrabourgeois conflict" driven by "the narcissism of minor differences" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch05-s3-the-perspiring-philistine-on-trial]]).
  - Caillebotte forced his Impressionists on the French state by will. Gérôme called the bequest "excrement" and "anarchy." But the administrators' hesitation also reflected a real rule against hanging more than three or four canvases by one artist. The affair foreshadows "the breakup of a great if shaky bourgeois compromise" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch05-s3-the-perspiring-philistine-on-trial]]).

## Resonances (hedged)

- **[[Legibility]].** "Power over a selected domain" is a private, small-scale version of the impulse to render a domain orderable, "a display cabinet and a card catalogue rather than a cadastre" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s1-a-map-of-motives]]).
- **[[carey-intellectuals-and-masses]].** Gay's Maecenases show the despised bourgeois class as modernism's "patrons and midwives, not only its target." That complicates Carey's dichotomy rather than matching it ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s3-the-victorian-maecenas]]). The riskless taste that Fodor, Schwabe and Wagener enshrined is the culture Carey's intelligentsia would scorn. Here, though, it is the wealthy bourgeoisie itself doing the choosing ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch05-s2-buttresses-for-the-establishment]]).
- **[[history-machine]].** Galleries and orchestras arose independently across cities.
