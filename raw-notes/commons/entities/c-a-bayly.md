# C. A. Bayly

Christopher Alan Bayly was the historian who wrote [[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world]] (2004). This page records only what that batch of reading notes shows about his method and stance. It adds no wider biography.

## Method

- **Connective, comparative world history.** He designed the book in two modes. “Lateral” chapters trace linked chronological crises across regions, and “vertical” chapters follow one institution across places. He places himself in a lineage of historians already writing empires and nations as global history, and credits the Annales school, led by [[fernand-braudel]], with pioneering global social and economic history for the early modern period ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-intro-thematic-problems-of-history]]).
- **A parallelogram of forces.** No single prime mover: economy, ideology and the state penetrate one another differently by period and place ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-intro-thematic-problems-of-history]], [[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch13-liberalism-crisis-and-motors-of-change]]).
- **Theories as tools.** He sets out rival accounts and weights each case by case instead of choosing one. He does this for theories of nationalism ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch06a-theories-of-nationalism]]) and for the causes of the new imperialism ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch06c-new-imperialism-and-internationalism]]). On industrialization, he holds that neither pure diffusion nor pure endowment explains the divergence ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch05-industrialization]]).
- **Healing the breach.** He wants to reconcile materialist histories of economic process with the newer historiography of representation ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch03-climacteric-sapping-legitimacy]]).
- **Suspicion of hindsight and of archives.** He writes that art history is “particularly unilinear, because we know visually ‘what happened’” ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch10-world-literature-and-conclusion]]). He reports how readings of the Qing and Ottoman empires as “hollow crowns” were revised as illusions of hindsight ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch11-historiography-and-gender]]), and he refuses to treat 1914 as inevitable ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch13-liberalism-crisis-and-motors-of-change]]). He warns that reliance on state documents makes it “very easy to assume that states are therefore responsible for any and all social change” ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch07a-defining-the-modern-state]]).
- **Global history as a heuristic.** For him the genre is a way to cross-check regional narratives, not a device for dissolving all difference ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch13-liberalism-crisis-and-motors-of-change]]).

## Stance

- **Against both exceptionalism and relativism.** Northwestern Europe really was more “dynamic” by 1780, but that dynamism makes sense only when set against comparably vigorous Asian and African debate and commerce ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch13-liberalism-crisis-and-motors-of-change]]). He explicitly refuses the Victorian “backwardness” verdict, and says of [[david-s-landes]] that “the weakness of Landes's position lies in its reversion to old ideas of Asian stagnation” ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch02-critical-publics-conclusion]]). He still grants a qualified distinctiveness to eighteenth-century European thought: it was willing to treat the past as fully redundant ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch08-liberalism-land-trade]]).
- **Against the secularization thesis.** He calls it an ideological artifact shared by philosophes, liberals, Marxists and later historians ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-new-style-religion]]), and finds the divide between a secular Europe and a religious rest “definitely too stark” ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch08-righteous-republics]]).
- **For grand narrative.** He answers postmodernist and postcolonial objections by showing that those critics also rely on meta-narratives ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-intro-thematic-problems-of-history]]).
- **Modernity as aspiration.** He argues that “thinking you are modern” is part of being modern, and he answers Latour on one side and Gellner, Macfarlane and Landes on the other with that claim ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-intro-riddle-of-modernity-and-the-body]]).
- **Unsentimental.** He denies that native peoples were the world's “first conservationists” ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch12-deluge-in-practice-and-marginalization]]). He also reads imperial campaigns for women's reform as largely “discursive offensives by Europeans against ‘depraved natives’” ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch11-historiography-and-gender]]).

## Interlocutors named in the notes

Arjun Appadurai (the global and the local “cannibalizing” each other), Jan de Vries (industrious revolution), Kenneth Pomeranz, [[benedict-anderson]], Ernest Gellner, Eric Hobsbawm, James Scott and [[michel-foucault]], Jürgen Habermas, Arno Mayer, Robinson and Gallagher, Edward Said, and Max Weber. On conversion he follows Susan Bayly, asking how Christianity itself “was converted” by the people who received it ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch09-new-style-religion]]).

## How the World Machines notes read him

The notes consistently hedge: Bayly is a historian of connections and comparisons, not a machine theorist. His resonances with [[legibility-as-machine-core]], [[gramsci-gap]] and [[history-machine]] are rhymes that the notes point out; he does not make them himself.
