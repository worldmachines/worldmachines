# Scottish Enlightenment

**Period**: roughly 1740–1790, centered on Edinburgh and Glasgow
**Principal figures in this account**: [[david-hume]], [[adam-smith]], Francis Hutcheson, William Robertson, Hugh Blair, Adam Ferguson, Dugald Stewart, Alexander Carlyle

## The Puzzle of Sudden Emergence

Within living memory Scotland had been, in a contemporary's phrase, "the rudest... of all European Nations." Within decades it was an intellectual powerhouse that contemporaries compared to Periclean Athens or Renaissance Italy. Dugald Stewart described genius as having seemed to "have sprung up in this country by a sort of enchantment."

Rasmussen refuses both the enchantment and the great-man account. He attributes the transformation instead to a convergence of independently developing structural components:

- a parish school system producing unusually broad literacy
- universities that had already modernized their curricula and teaching
- a dense layer of clubs and debating societies
- a domestic publishing industry
- the 1707 union with England, opening markets and careers
- reforming "Moderate" clergy pushing back against a repressive Kirk

None of these was built to produce an Enlightenment; each was doing something else. This is a paradigm cohering out of distributed, uncoordinated components and only then becoming visible as a named era — the pattern the [[history-machine]] concept describes, and one instance of the broader institutional construction WMP frames as the [[modernity-machine]]'s work.

## Institutional Ascent, and Its Contrast

Smith supplies the era's sharpest statement of what made the difference, by describing its opposite. Balliol's professors, he wrote, had "given up altogether even the pretence of teaching," and richly endowed universities generally become "sanctuaries in which exploded systems and obsolete prejudices found shelter and protection, after they had been hunted out of every other corner of the world." Against this he set the fast-modernizing Scottish universities: "without exception the best seminaries of learning that are to be found any where in Europe." The Oxford verdict is simultaneously autobiography and an early draft of the theory of institutional decline he later formalized in *The Wealth of Nations*.

The contrast has a personal edge. Balliol's dons confiscated Smith's copy of Hume's *Treatise* and reprimanded him for reading it; the Scottish institutions, by contrast, put Hume's freshly published *Political Discourses* into the Glasgow jurisprudence curriculum within a year or two of publication.

## Sociability as Infrastructure

The era's characteristic institution is not the university but the club. The Select Society, founded in Edinburgh in 1754 with Hume as treasurer and Smith explaining its charter at the first meeting, drew (by one report) more general curiosity in Edinburgh than the House of Commons did in London. Around it sat the Literary Society of Glasgow, the Advocates' Library (whose keepership gave Hume the research base for the *History of England*), and, after the Select Society's decline, the Poker Club.

This infrastructure was portable and continuous with the wider republic of letters: the Paris salons of the Comtesse de Boufflers and Baron d'Holbach, the correspondence with Benjamin Franklin, and the letter itself as the primary medium of a friendship conducted across thirteen coach-hours. Compare the [[lunar-society]] of Birmingham as a parallel informal network, and [[print-culture-and-the-individual-author]] on the print substrate all of it ran on.

## Respectable Heretics

What most distinguished the Scottish literati from the Parisian *philosophes*, on Rasmussen's account, is that they were respected professionals embedded in the institutions of their own society rather than alienated outsiders attacking it. Robertson was Principal of the University of Edinburgh and Moderator of the General Assembly; Blair held a pulpit and a chair; Smith held two chairs at Glasgow in succession.

The price of that embedding was a negotiated silence. When the Kirk's Popular Party under George Anderson pressed the General Assembly between 1755 and 1757 to excommunicate Hume on six enumerated heresies, the campaign's real targets were not Hume — excommunication carried no civil penalty and would only have martyred him — but the Moderate ministers who socialized openly with an avowed skeptic. Hume eventually proposed to Hugh Blair that they stop discussing religion altogether so the friendship could continue on other terms: "I have, long since, done with all inquiries on such subjects, and am become incapable of instruction." The literati's protection of Hume rested on deliberate conversational management, not on shared belief. See [[calibrated-candor]].

## Jurisdictional Reputation

The same corpus that made Hume a candidate for excommunication in Edinburgh made him the idol of Paris — "I eat nothing but Ambrosia, drink nothing but Nectar" — where the d'Holbach circle found him insufficiently radical (the famous dinner of eighteen guests, fifteen confirmed atheists and "the other three haven't yet made up their minds"). Reputation was a function of which public was judging, not a property of the work. The Enlightenment's informal networks partly operated as arbitrage across those local prices, surviving wherever local orthodoxy tolerated them — and Hume worried explicitly that Edinburgh's religious "narrowness" would "hurt" his friends by association.

## Grove: the Scottish-trained colonial surgeons

Richard Grove's *Green Imperialism* ([[grove-green-imperialism]]) follows one output of the reformed Scottish universities out of Scotland: the physicians and surgeons who carried desiccation theory into East India Company forest policy. The notes hedge the link. This is a colonial medical-botanical training network adjacent to the Edinburgh literati, not the same circle or doctrine ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s5-anderson-critique]]).

**The climatic current.** Grove names [[david-hume]]'s 1741 essay on climate, society and population as a Scottish parallel to Montesquieu ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch04-s2-climate-theories-france]]). Lord Kames drew on the Cook voyages to argue that a balmy climate hindered progress ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s2-forsters-resolution]]).

**The medical pipeline.** Roxburgh trained under John Hope at Edinburgh and so inherited Hales's and Duhamel du Monceau's plant physiology ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s2-roxburgh-1]]). By the late 1830s most surgeons working in India were Scottish-trained. The *Edinburgh New Philosophical Journal*, which printed Boussingault's desiccation paper in translation, was “widely read” among them, and Scottish teaching had primed them to read disease environmentally ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s3-lobbying]]). The Introduction names Gibson, Balfour and Cleghorn, trained at Edinburgh, Glasgow and Aberdeen, as the men who won a forest-conservancy system by the 1850s ([[grove-green-imperialism-introduction]]). Grove calls Alexander Anderson of St Vincent “the first in a long line of Scottish colonial experts who linked together their biological, religious and social insights” ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s5-anderson-critique]]).

**Why Scots.** Banks's professional network deliberately relied on Scottish and German rather than English expertise ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s1-banks-professionalisation]]). Grove suggests that the Scottish and Central European origins of many company scientists, socially peripheral to the imperial establishment, reinforced their pairing of environmental and social critique ([[grove-green-imperialism-conclusion]]). Balfour, nephew of the radical MP Joseph Hume, took the same physiocratic inheritance toward statist protection where his uncle took it toward free trade ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s5-balfour]]). Grove sums up the surgeons' intellectual make-up as “physiocratic, Priestleyite, Scottish Hippocratic and Humboldtian” ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s6-dalhousie]]).

This page explains the Enlightenment through convergent institutions rather than great men. Grove's surgeons show one of those institutions, the medical school, producing effects half a world away, where its graduates, among the more than 800 East India Company surgeons Grove counts by 1838, became a lobby inside a trading company's administration ([[grove-green-imperialism-introduction]]).
