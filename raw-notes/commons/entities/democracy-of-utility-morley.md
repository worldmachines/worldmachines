# The democracy of utility (Morley)

This page draws on Volume II of [[john-morley]]'s [[diderot-and-the-encyclopaedists]] ([[morley-diderot-v2]]). The concept is Morley's, a partisan Victorian liberal-positivist verdict, and the notes treat it as such. It is narrower than utilitarianism in general: it names Morley's story of a utility doctrine that could have guided France and was displaced.

## The claim

Morley holds that despite its shallowness, Helvétius's L'Esprit contained the one principle that might have allowed France's transition without a Reign of Terror ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1]]). That principle is public utility: “the Utility of the Public” as the single principle of morality and legislation, invariable in principle and pliable in application. Bentham, Morley says, took it from Helvétius and avowed it ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-2]]; see [[claude-adrien-helvetius]]).

## Why it was lost

Helvétius buried the principle under the paradox that self-love is the only motive, confusing the standard of conduct with its motive. Bentham succeeded where he failed because he came as a practical lawyer and classified motives, pleasures and pains objectively. The book's discredit let [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] “sweep into the field”: “the democracy of sentimentalism” displaced “the democracy of utility” in the literature preparing the Revolution, and Morley calls the Sovereignty of the People an “arbitrary and intrinsically sterile” rendering of Helvétius's truth ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-2]]).

## Holbach's universalistic version

Morley defends Holbach against the charge of egoism, using Sidgwick's classification: his real base is “the happiness of the greatest number”, and “no man can be happy alone”. In a society kept miserable by a privileged few, this principle was “the indispensable weapon” and “the ethical side of every popular and democratic movement”. The Church, nominally built on the Golden Rule, had become “the great organ of sinister self-interest”, while the Atheists taught a public spirit that “could only mean revolution” ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-3]]; see [[baron-dholbach]]).

## Tensions inside Morley's account

Morley's two halves pull against each other. In Helvétius's case, utility is the road to reform without terror; in Holbach's, the same ethic of the greatest number is the ethical side of a movement that “could only mean revolution”. Morley also limits both versions. Helvétius assumes a disinterested legislator while denying disinterest to everyone else, vaulting over “the crucial problem of political constitutions” ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-2]]), and Holbach fuses distinct questions under one “basis of morals” and ignores the moral inheritance of generations ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-3]]).

## World Machines resonances, with the notes' hedges

The notes read utility as a legislation theme in embryo, social machinery for governing conduct, though Helvétius's calculus is psychological and moral rather than the infrastructural sense of the [[modernity-machine]] ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-2]]). Morley's framing of the Enlightenment as having two possible engines of revolution, one sentimental and one utilitarian, can be compared with [[history-machine]] accounts of how ideas assemble into revolutions, though his is a great-book, great-author story ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-2]]). The reform-or-revolution question also sits near [[intellectual-vs-revolutionary]] ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1]]).
