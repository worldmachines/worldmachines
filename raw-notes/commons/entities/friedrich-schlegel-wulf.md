# Friedrich Schlegel (Wulf)

Extends [[Friedrich Schlegel]], which treats his ideas, with the life around them from Andrea Wulf's group biography [[wulf-magnificent-rebels]]. Its notes mostly report his words and other people's judgements rather than Wulf's own verdicts.

**Arrival and temperament.** Friedrich reached Jena on 6 August 1796, brilliant, broke, arrogant and given to 'constant and merciless self-analysis'; 'a rare and dangerous beast' is his own self-description ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch06-splendid-circle-1]]). He styled himself a 'Dictator-Critic', held that only Goethe, Shakespeare and Dante were worth reading, and linked the fragmentation of German poetry to the absence of a unified nation ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch06-splendid-circle-2]]). His review of Schiller's *Musen-Almanach* began a feud, and his review of *Horen* led Schiller to dismiss his brother ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch06-splendid-circle-2]], [[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch07-little-academy-2]]). He credited Caroline with opening his mind to the Revolution and held that revolutions must be spoken as well as carried out ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch05-novalis-in-love]]). With Novalis he turned Fichte's closed self-reflection into an endless 'thinking about thinking' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch05-novalis-in-love]]).

**The Athenaeum plan.** In Berlin in 1797-98, sharing lodgings with Schleiermacher and in love with Dorothea Veit, he wanted an 'aesthetic anarchy' to bring a 'happy catastrophe' like the French Revolution, fought 'with words rather than swords'; his sister suspected vanity too ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch09-sublime-impertinence-1]]). The journal was to be of 'sublime impertinence'. He called himself 'a system of fragments' and hoped radical lines buried among milder ones would slip past Prussian censors ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch09-sublime-impertinence-1]]). His long fragment on romantic poetry anchors Wulf's account of what 'romantic' meant in Jena ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch09-sublime-impertinence-2]]); 'make Euclid singable' was his joke ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch08-handful-of-darkness]]); an 'ur-assembly of humanity' his phrase for the friends' gatherings ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch10-symphilosophy]]).

**Lucinde.** Wulf reads the 1799 novel as provocation and as an allegory of equality between the sexes ('petals of a single flower'), with deliberate role reversal and a fragmentary, generically mixed structure; reviewers were abusive and Novalis wrote 'you lose yourself in a dizzy whirl' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch13-divorce-women-sex]]).

**Doctrine and conduct.** He cast himself as the prophet of a new bible with poets as priests, which his brother found preposterous ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch15-new-confederation]]). Wulf notes that he advocated women's equality in his writing while pursuing Sophie Mereau ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-1]]); he turned against Caroline for motives Wulf lists only as possibilities ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch16-republic-of-despots-1]]); his gossip helped set Schelling against Fichte, and partisan correspondents portray him as debt-ridden and self-absorbed ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-1]]).

**Novalis's legacy.** At Novalis's death he called it 'divine' and denounced any completion of *Heinrich von Ofterdingen* by Tieck as 'sacrilegious, heinous, godless, unholy' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch17-black-fog-2]]). With Tieck he then edited Novalis's works, cutting fragments and suppressing the political and scientific writing, which Wulf argues created the legend of the otherworldly poet ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-2]]).

**Paris, the Orient and the conversion.** He left for Paris to launch a journal called *Europe* ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch18-starving-rats-2]]), watched Napoleon centralise power until the Revolution's ideals 'seemed to disappear', declared himself 'stubbornly and idiotically German' and turned to Sanskrit and Persian ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-ch19-current-exodus]]). Wulf reads the 1808 conversion to Catholicism, noted on [[Friedrich Schlegel]], as the culmination of the wish for a 'religion filled with feeling and imagination' voiced in Jena in November 1799 and of growing conservatism. He discarded his youthful ideas and the Jena Set, accused Fichte of 'idolising the Ich' and confusing the self with the divine, and called Goethe an 'old worn-out demigod'; Dorothea's translations appeared under his name ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-after-jena-1]]). Wulf tells this as personal biography, not a theory of reaction. His formal programme outlived him: a critic Wulf quotes calls *Finnegans Wake* 'Schlegelian' ([[wulf-magnificent-rebels-epilogue-influence-selfish]]).
