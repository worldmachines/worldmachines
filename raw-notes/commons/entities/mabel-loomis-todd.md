# Mabel Loomis Todd

Diarist, poet and painter, wife of the astronomer David Peck Todd, and the central informant of [[peter-gay]]'s *Education of the Senses* ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1]]). Her “exhaustive” diaries and journals record a courtship, a marriage and a long affair with an erotic candor Gay calls an evidentiary gift he can only “quote at length” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-erotic-record-1-mabel-loomis]]).

## Why she matters to Gay's argument

Gay insists she was typical in experience and exceptional only in documentation. Her diary-keeping, reading, piano lessons and social fantasies are “typical of her class and age”; what sets her apart is “introspective candor and fine lucidity” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-erotic-record-1-mabel-loomis]]). He defends the diary's use despite its rarity ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-communions-1-paradise-two]]) and uses Clelia Mosher's survey of educated wives of her generation to conclude that her distinction was “her eloquence, not her experience” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-communions-2-dubious-certainty-numbers]]). Her diary stands, in the notes, for genuine private sexuality beneath the century's “gospel of female sexual anesthesia” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-communions-3-problematic-sex]]).

## Courtship

Her diaries trace adolescent sexual awakening, repeated self-questioning (“if I am a flirt”), and extensive premarital intimacy with David Todd; she went to her 1879 wedding “at least technically a virgin,” while her mother praised the “rapt expression of purity” on her face — innocence, Gay remarks, was “a relatively elastic thing” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-erotic-record-1-mabel-loomis]]). Her moral certainties bent under experience; her social certainties — scorn for “vulgar” money, pride in “good blood” and a family coat of arms — did not ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-erotic-record-1-mabel-loomis]]). An 1878 journal entry wrestles with whether a woman can keep both “talent” and marriage ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-fortifications-2-social-science-cultural-symptom]]).

## Marriage

The Todds' “appetite grew with its regular and lawful satisfaction.” Mabel's homemade theory of contraception was disconfirmed nine months later by her daughter Millicent; she met pregnancy with candid ambivalence, ranking wife-love as “air & food & water” and mother-love as “incidental”; and David's letter on the pregnancy praised her body frankly while calling her “sacred, holy mother” — for Gay, counter-evidence to the angel/whore split ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-erotic-record-2-mabel-loomis-todd]]). David, avoiding the word “milk,” nonetheless performed the physical labor of nursing crises ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-carnal-knowledge-2-lessons-body]]).

## The diary code

She kept a private cipher: marks for menstruation, “#” with a running numeral for intercourse, “f.m.” and “o.” for her own orgasm. The shifting sense of “Sem.” — petting before the wedding, intercourse after, masturbation during David's absences — supports Gay's virginity claim, and David's request that she log his own masturbation exposes the shared anxiety of the “spermatic economy” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-appendix-sexual-symbols-todd-diaries]]). The couple relied on rhythm and coitus interruptus ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-appendix-sexual-symbols-todd-diaries]]). Her diary surviving into marriage is one of Gay's exhibits that reserve and candor coexisted ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-fortifications-4-private-experience]]).

## Amherst and Austin Dickinson

After the move to Amherst in 1881 she began a decade-plus affair with Austin Dickinson, Emily Dickinson's brother, lasting until his death in 1895 — “a secret that everyone shared, a test case for the middle-class social style.” David encouraged it and warned the lovers of his return by whistling; Millicent practiced a lifelong refusal to “wonder,” “witness and sacrifice.” Gay reads Mabel's self-justifying doctrine that “the spirit is greater than the letter” as narcissistic exceptionalism, traces her erotic choices to idealization of her father Eben Loomis, and concludes that bourgeois hypocrisy offered “routes of evasion” at a price she “could afford to pay” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-erotic-record-3-mabel-loomis-dickinson]]).

## In World Machines terms

The notes read the Todds' dated tallies as a private, “near-actuarial” legibility regime applied to eros — a cousin of the [[modernity-machine]]'s record-keeping impulse but turned inward and kept secret from any authority, a partial fit only ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-erotic-record-2-mabel-loomis-todd]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v1-appendix-sexual-symbols-todd-diaries]]).
