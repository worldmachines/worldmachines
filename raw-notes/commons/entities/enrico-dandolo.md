# Enrico Dandolo

**Doge of Venice** during the Fourth Crusade; blind, and by his own crusade's reckoning over ninety years old, when six French envoys arrived in the lagoon in 1201 "about a crusade." He is the central human figure of the first third of [[crowley-city-of-fortune]], and Crowley treats him less as a biographical subject than as an aggregation point for how contemporaries narrated Venetian character ([[crowley-city-of-fortune-ch02-the-blind-doge]]).

## A contested text

No reliable physical portrait survives, and the judgments diverge exactly as judgments of Venice itself diverge: "most worthy man and wise" to Robert of Clari; "treacherous and hostile," consumed by "lust for glory," to Niketas Choniates. The blindness itself became myth-material — rumours of Byzantine mutilation, of sight feigned, of cunning concealed.

## The contract

Dandolo's signature act is the Treaty of Venice (1201): a holy war costed with the instruments used on the Rialto every trading day — "quantity, price and delivery date." Four thousand five hundred horses, 4,500 knights, twenty thousand foot, nine months of provisions, four marks per horse and two per man, ninety-four thousand marks in total, and half of all future conquest. Buried inside it, known to a handful of signatories, was a destination the rank and file never agreed to: Egypt rather than the Holy Land, justified strategically and driven equally by Venice's hunger for a share of the Alexandria spice trade that Genoa and Pisa then dominated. Villehardouin admits the split — "secretly agreed in closed council," while the public story remained "we were going overseas."

Ratification was staged rather than debated: ten thousand people in St Mark's, weeping envoys, a roaring "We agree!", and Dandolo sealing the pact from the pulpit — consent engineered as spectacle for a decision already made.

## Debt as steering mechanism

When only about twelve thousand of the contracted thirty-three thousand crusaders mustered, the shortfall was thirty-four thousand marks — nine tons of silver — against a Venice that had suspended its entire mercantile economy for a year to perform its side ([[crowley-city-of-fortune-ch03-thirty-four-thousand-marks]]). Dandolo's fury is specifically mercantile: "we shall henceforth always be considered as rogues and cheats." And the same contractual literalism that made him inflexible on repayment is what produced the escape: deferring the debt against future conquests preserved the contract's letter while redirecting its substance entirely — first onto the Christian city of Zara, negotiated in secret against explicit papal warning, and then, after the pretender Alexius Angelus's offer of two hundred thousand marks, onto Constantinople ([[crowley-city-of-fortune-ch04-dog-returning-to-its-vomit]]). His cross-taking at ninety, with the cross sewn visibly to his corno "because he wanted people to be able to see it," is simultaneously sincere piety and the single most effective piece of crisis management in the affair.

Against papal sanction he is blunt — "not even for the pope!" — and Innocent III's escalating condemnations (the Zara interdict, the general excommunication of the Venetians, the "dog returning to its vomit" letter) become rhetorically maximal precisely as they become practically powerless.

## At the walls

On 17 July 1203, with the land assault stalled against the Varangian Guard and the Venetian galleys balking under missile fire, Dandolo ordered his own galley run ashore under the banner of St Mark and shamed the fleet into following ([[crowley-city-of-fortune-ch05-at-the-walls]]). Crowley immediately steps back from the set-piece to note both its contingency — "if that day the Venetians had failed by sea, as the French had by land, the whole expedition would probably have collapsed" — and its afterlife, calcified into the Republic's founding heroic image and re-enacted by Tintoretto four centuries later in the doge's palace. It is the batch's clearest case of a near-run thing retrospectively narrated as destiny.

His confrontation with the emperor he had installed closes that alliance: rowed out under armed guard to curse Alexius IV from the shore — "Contemptible boy… we hauled you out of the dung heap and we'll drop you back in it" ([[crowley-city-of-fortune-ch06-four-emperors]]).

## What the gamble produced

Before the second assault the leaders drafted the March Pact: a formal, quantified division of booty — three quarters to Venice until its 150,000-mark debt was retired, then equal shares — a mechanism for electing an emperor, and a clause locking out Pisan and Genoese trade ([[crowley-city-of-fortune-ch07-the-works-of-hell]]). The gap between that legible paperwork and the three days of sack that followed is the chapter's central irony. Where Frankish soldiers melted centuries-old statuary for coin metal, Venetians looted "like connoisseurs": bronze horses, relics, marble columns, and sculpted reliefs carried home to build St Mark's Square and Venice's imperial self-image out of the legitimating icons of the empire it had just helped destroy.

Dandolo outlived nearly every other protagonist of the crusade — three emperors dead or blinded — and left Venice with "a Quarter and Half a Quarter" of Byzantium and an empire it had never set out to win ([[crowley-city-of-fortune-ch08-a-quarter-and-half-a-quarter]]). The Dandolo name recurs at the far end of the story in an unflattering register: it appears among the great Venetian colonial families — with Gradenigo and Venier — who rebelled against the mother city in Crete's Revolt of St Titus in 1363 ([[crowley-city-of-fortune-ch11-the-flag-of-st-titus]]).

## Reading him

The notes converge on a reading in which Dandolo is neither the villain of Byzantine historiography nor the hero of the doge's palace, but the point where a commercial polity's ordinary instruments — a contract, a debt schedule, a deferral clause — turn out to be capable of steering a continent's holy war. What his career demonstrates is less personal genius than the leverage available to whoever holds the ledger; see [[venetian-legibility-and-its-blind-spot]] and [[stato-da-mar]].
