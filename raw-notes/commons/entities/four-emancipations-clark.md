# The four emancipations (Clark)

Clark's comparative treatment of four emancipations that 1848 put on the agenda: of enslaved Africans, women, Jews and Roma. His argument is that they shared a chronology and a rhetoric but never fused, and that proclamation was not implementation ([[clark-revolutionary-spring]]).

## A word 'through which history flowed'

'Emancipation' underwent semantic inflation. Heine's phrasing merged the liberation of races, classes and nations into one 'universal emancipatory process', and the word became 'a channel through which history flowed' ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch06-abolitionist-black-1848]]). The idea promised linear progress; the reality was 'faltering, tortuous and strewn with pitfalls'. For Jewish emancipation Clark borrows Rürup's image of 'tortuous and thorny' paths and rejects a 'forward-driven, unitary transformation defined teleologically' ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch06-liberty-and-risk]]).

## Before 1848: the metaphor without the thing

Slavery was 'one of the root metaphors of western political philosophy and language', invoked for women, Poles, Italians, Jews and workers, yet almost never tied to the chattel slavery expanding in continental colonies. Continental abolitionism was thin and elite (Bissette's *Revue des Colonies* had 250 subscribers). February 1848 suddenly joined 'the metaphor and the thing' ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch02-free-and-unfree-places-in-history]]).

## The four cases

- **Abolition.** Schoelcher's commission argued that a government born of revolution had the legitimacy to proclaim liberation. The decree of 27 April 1848 was immediate and unconditional and deferred the hard questions. In Martinique the enslaved acted first, and the governor signed a local emancipation on 23 May. Afterwards contracts, vagrancy law, workbooks and a head tax rebuilt a controlled labour force, and 'nowhere in the Europe of 1848' did the revolutions seriously challenge imperialism ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch06-abolitionist-black-1848]]).
- **Women.** Women were in virtually all the tumults but were excluded from clubs, parliaments and the franchise. Frankfurt greeted votes for women with guffaws. Nothing like the abolition act happened for women. Clark's explanation is that in emergent bourgeois society gender norms had become more polarized, so wider participation carried men where women could not reach ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch06-waving-from-windows]]). Before 1848, Claire Démar had already named the power of the father as the deepest inequality, and Clark judges sexual inequality 'the most resistant to change' ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch02-its-a-mans-world]]).
- **Jews.** A spring of emancipation laws coincided with a Europe-wide wave of anti-Jewish violence that was economic, ideological and political at once. Most of the legislation was reversed after the revolutions, and emancipation arrived durably only between 1861 and 1871. The slogan 'emancipation from the Jews' bridged left and right critiques of capital ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch06-liberty-and-risk]]).
- **Roma.** The Wallachian Provisional Government proclaimed manumission on 26 June 1848 and built a commission, liberation tickets and compensation machinery. The machinery choked, owners resisted, and the Ottoman–Russian intervention annulled everything on 28 September: 'Roma emancipation was the first casualty of the counter-revolution in Wallachia' ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch06-roma-slaves-time-of-emancipation]]).

## Proclamation is not implementation

Clark separates abolition as a discrete executive act from emancipation as a slow, uneven and reversible social process ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch06-abolitionist-black-1848]]). The same gap appears among peasants. Hungary's April laws abolished labour services but left grey areas resolved only by 1896. Clark generalizes: emancipation by stroke of pen versus emancipation as a generations-long social process ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch07-town-and-country]]).

## Connected but not reducible

Garrison, Heinzen and Douglass saw the struggles as one. Clark argues they did not merge because racial or ethnic difference, sexual difference and the Jewish predicament were 'separate fundaments', deeply built into European culture and made to seem natural. They shared a chronology: stirrings in the 1790s, an advocacy boom in the 1830s–40s, a push in 1848, then narrowing. Elite culture rehearsed emancipation in opera, ballet and fiction, which 'allowed European cultural elites to keep chewing on emancipation without ever quite swallowing it'. Clark refuses the verdict of failure and points to Fürst's remark that 'what matters is not the result, but rather the activities that gave birth to this outcome' ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch06-roma-slaves-time-of-emancipation]]).

## World Machines resonances (hedged)

The notes read the Roma tickets, which doubled as identity cards and recorded the former owner's name, as a case where the instrument of freedom was also an instrument of registration that carried the old hierarchy forward. That fits [[legibility-cuts-both-ways]] and [[legibility-hardens-hierarchy-bayly]], but Clark only notes the detail ('tellingly') ([[clark-revolutionary-spring-ch06-roma-slaves-time-of-emancipation]]). The finding that one liberating logic did not carry all excluded groups at once presses on [[modernity-machine]] assumptions of convergence: Clark describes plural, non-convergent rationales of exclusion, closer to divergence than to one arc.
