# Dividends of Denial (Gay)

"Dividends of Denial" is the title of the section of *The Tender Passion* ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2]]) in which [[peter-gay]] treats homoerotic attachment in nineteenth-century bourgeois culture. His paradoxical claim is that the culture's refusal to name such love protected it.

## The argument

The bourgeois ideal of love left whole territories unnamed. This reticence was "not hypocrisy" but one way of handling "a familiar bourgeois dilemma": the middle classes "could never quite decide whether denial was a sounder cultural defense than candor," and "most bourgeois chose the spurious safety of ignorance over the risky benefits of knowledge." The result was that lovers were safer before the 1880s. Bachelors and women sharing quiet households drew praise, and impunity rested on a "sheer failure of sexual imagination" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-1-dividends-denial]]). Passionate friendships (the Yale student Albert Dodd's journal, the Hunt–Millais "sacred friendship," schoolgirl crushes) were felt and written about without shame. Gay calls the innocence thesis "complacent and self-serving," resting on the cult of friendship and the denial of infantile sexuality. Dodd's "impunity is a tribute to the unsuspected openness of his culture," though "we shall never know how expensive this victory must have been to him" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-1-dividends-denial]]). Discretion worked as a bargain: C. J. Vaughan of Harrow, surprised with a pupil, was "fenced in but not exposed" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-1-dividends-denial]]).

## The irony of exposure

After the 1880s, hesitant openness "invited blackmail, ruined careers, drove some into suicide." Gay reads the press response to the Wilde trials as less outrage at homosexuality than fury at a violator of "the supreme social rule of reticence," with calls for "a dash of wholesome bigotry" and a return to "protective ignorance." The arc runs from "vices which have no names" to "the love that dare not speak its name" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-1-dividends-denial]]). Proust could publish the Montjouvain scene in 1913 partly because he made his homosexuals joyless: "A fate is not a vice" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-1-dividends-denial]]).

## The return of the repressed in culture

Gay applies Freud's account of failed defense to law and science. The patchwork of legislation, from decriminalization in France to Paragraph 175 and the Labouchere Amendment, and its capricious enforcement are a compromise formation between the need to know and the need to stay ignorant, "the return of the repressed in culture" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-2-return-repressed]]). The new candor had several unintended causes: Darwinian and sociological secularization of sex, purity crusaders who publicized what they attacked, and a medical profession expanding its jurisdiction. It stayed "shame-faced", with physicians retreating into Latin. The vocabulary of "homosexuality" and "contrary sexual feeling" came from physicians and was adopted, half defiantly, by writers such as Symonds. Gay's remark that the 1870s to 1890s "invented homosexuality" is hedged: "too much to say, but not by a great deal" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-2-return-repressed]]).

## The Greek alibi and Freud's way out

Homosexual men used the Greek inheritance as cover, dignity and program. Scholars bowdlerized and changed pronouns, while Pater and Symonds wrote in euphemism as policy so that only the equipped would understand ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-3-antique-persuasion]]). Open advocacy, from Carpenter to Kuzmin, sharpened a dilemma the bourgeoisie preferred to treat as gossip about decadent aristocrats or bohemians. The honest public airing that might have resolved it was something bourgeois culture "could never quite adopt" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-3-antique-persuasion]]). Freud's theory (the unconscious, bisexual endowment, homosexuality as a localized developmental arrest with no implied moral or artistic deficit) made exclusive heterosexuality "a problem requiring elucidation" and pleased neither reformers nor moralists. Gay notes that Freud kept the normative hierarchy ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-3-antique-persuasion]]).

## World Machines resonances (hedged)

The notes read this as a case of a class holding a large zone of tolerated practice open by *not naming* it. A transition in the infrastructure of naming (newspapers, trials, clinical categories, from about the 1880s) converted tolerated ambiguity into a regulated category. That fits [[legibility-as-machine-core]] only in inverted form: "here legibility is the danger, and illegibility the shelter." Gay never theorizes it that way ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-1-dividends-denial]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-2-return-repressed]]). The convergence of unrelated causes behind the new candor rhymes with the distributed assembly of a [[history-machine]], though Gay describes a discourse shift, not a paradigm ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-2-return-repressed]]). Against [[michel-foucault]], Gay stresses concealment and tolerated exceptions rather than the production of a homosexual category, while still tracing how the physicians' vocabulary was adopted ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v2-ch4-3-antique-persuasion]]).
