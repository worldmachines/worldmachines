# Ibn Khaldun (1332-1406)

## Standard Reading vs. Rao's Reading

**Standard reading**: Ibn Khaldun as proto-sociologist, pioneer of social science, ahead of his time — the *Muqaddimah* as inaugurating a new science of society.

**Rao's reading** (from *Modernity Machine III*): "Ibn Khaldun: An Intellectual Biography should not be read as the story of a proto-sociologist ahead of his time. That role is largely retrofitted by modern interpreters."

In his own context, Ibn Khaldun appears more plausibly as "a kind of depressed Arab Petrarch: a brilliant chronicler of decline and defender of tradition, lamenting the absence of an Islamic renaissance rather than inaugurating one."

## The Diagnostic Reading

His cyclical theory of dynasties (*asabiyyah* — group cohesion — rising, peaking, and declining) "does not launch a new science of society; it records the exhaustion of an old civilizational form."

His importance in Rao's framework is **diagnostic, not genealogical**: "He documents a world failing to enter the Modernity Machine at all, despite being in possession of many of the necessary components."

This is a pointed claim: the Islamic world of the 14th century had the raw materials for modernity (cities, trade networks, sophisticated legal systems, philosophy, mathematics) but something prevented assembly. Ibn Khaldun's cyclical pessimism is *evidence of that failure*, not a contribution to its solution.

## Intellectual Lineage

- Compare [[Giordano Bruno]]: another figure retrofitted by modernity as an early-modern prophet, but actually a *terminal medieval outlier*
- Compare [[Petrarch]]: the Petrarch parallel is apt — Petrarch also looked back at a classical golden age with longing, constructing the "dark ages" concept in the process
- In Rao's Spinoza essay: Adam Smith's approach to economics was "ontologically different from (say) the approach of Ibn Khaldun" — Ibn Khaldun worked within an existing cosmological ontology; Smith worked from noisy empirical reality data

## What's Missing from Rao's Account

- No engagement with Marshall Hodgson's account of Islamic civilization's structural constraints
- The reason *why* the Islamic world possessed the components but failed to assemble them is left unexplained in Rao's framework — he gestures at "delayed arrival and diffusion of print" as a factor in the Indian case, but the Islamic case is left open
- The *Muqaddimah*'s sociology of knowledge and concept of *asabiyyah* are more sophisticated than a simple "cyclical history" framing suggests

## Relevance to Royalty and Administration

Ibn Khaldun's cyclical theory directly informs Sathe's essay on [[royalty-administration-and-antimemetics|Royalty, Administration, and Antimemetics]]. The *asabiyyah* cycle — desert cohesion → state-building → luxury → diffusion → collapse — maps onto the observation that antimemetic bureaucracies are a late-cycle phenomenon: the machinery outlasting the original driving force that built the state.

Robert Irwin's *Ibn Khaldun: An Intellectual Biography* is cited as a source in Sathe's essay.

## A Parallel Late-Cycle State: Ortega's Rome

The observation above — antimemetic bureaucracy as a late-cycle phenomenon, “the machinery outlasting the original driving force that built the state” — has an independent statement in Ortega y Gasset's *The Revolt of the Masses* ([[ortega-revolt-of-the-masses]]).

Ortega narrates Rome's end as a State-machine consuming its host: bureaucratization under the Antonines gives way to militarization under the Severi, the army must be recruited from foreigners, and those foreigners “take possession of the State.” His summary image — “The skeleton eats up the flesh around it. The scaffolding becomes the owner and tenant of the house” — is offered as the historical proof that State intervention, once it absorbs spontaneous social effort, leaves the State “bloodless, a skeleton, dead with that rusty death of machinery” ([[ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch13-the-greatest-danger-the-state]]). The endnotes add Septimius Severus's deathbed advice, “Remain united, pay the soldiers and take no heed of the rest,” as a specimen of naked late statecraft (n. 42), and a thirty-year generational rhythm of revolutionary and restorationist cohorts (n. 32) as Ortega's own periodizing model ([[ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-endnotes]]).

The fit is partial. Ibn Khaldun's cycle is dynastic and turns on the decay of group cohesion; Ortega's is a single arc driven by the demands of a population that wants the State to do everything, and he insists Europe's crisis inverts Rome's — Rome “came to an end for lack of technique,” Europe fails because men cannot keep pace with their own civilization ([[ortega-revolt-of-the-masses-ch10-primitivism-and-history]]). What the two share is the late-cycle figure of an apparatus that persists and intensifies after the vitality that built it has gone. See [[civilization-undone-by-its-own-success]].

## Bayly: the last round of the Khaldunian cycle

C. A. Bayly's [[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world]] gives the dynastic cycle discussed on this page a historical terminus. In the eighteenth century, nomadic and forest polities still met agrarian states on near-equal terms. Examples include Manchurian herdsmen, Arabian camel tribes underwriting Wahhabi resistance, the Zand and Qajar tribal dynasts in Persia, and forest chieftain-marauders across India, Burma and Siberia. Bayly frames this explicitly as the closing round of the process Ibn Khaldun described, in which tough frontier peoples periodically broke in to revive and purge settled governments. It was a cycle that 1780 was about to foreclose for good ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch01-fringes-and-archaic-globalization]]).

The later chapters show how it closed. Before about 1820, states recruited the frontier peoples they distrusted as cavalry and elite corps, and warrior-nomads still became ruling dynasties: the Timurids, the Manchus, the Bedouin Saudi rulers and the Fulani founders of the Sokoto caliphate ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch12-native-peoples-context-and-hiatus]]). A last “great domestication” then pushed cultivation into forest and steppe ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch02-old-regime-industrious-revolutions]]). After 1840, land hunger, railways, steamships and firearms turned this “Age of Hiatus” into a “White Deluge” ([[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch12-native-peoples-context-and-hiatus]], [[bayly-birth-of-the-modern-world-ch12-deluge-in-practice-and-marginalization]]).

This sits beside Rao's reading rather than against it. Bayly invokes Ibn Khaldun briefly, as the describer of a pattern, and does not engage the question of whether he was a proto-sociologist. What the notes add is that the cycle ended from outside. The settled side acquired technologies that frontier peoples could not match; there is no sign that frontier cohesion simply failed.

## Irwin's portrait: a man of his own world

The book this page says Rao and Sathe cite, Robert Irwin's *Ibn Khaldun: An Intellectual Biography* ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun]]), has now been read in full. Its Ibn Khaldun is assembled from the fourteenth-century evidence before any modern comparison is allowed, and Irwin's stated rule is that “the contrasts matter more than the comparisons” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-preface]]).

**A scholar-politician in a violent court culture.** In Irwin's account Maghribi rulers displayed scholars as insignia of royalty, and Ibn Khaldun worked as a “bureaucratic condottiere” who changed sides, plotted and was imprisoned. His intrigues “had been successful only in the sense that, at the end of them all, he was still alive” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch02-topography-early-career]], [[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch02-ibn-al-khatib-spain-return]]). In Cairo he signed a fatwa against his patron Barquq and later glossed over it in his memoir; Irwin calls him “always more interested in winners than in losers” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch05-arrival-in-egypt]]).

**A Maliki jurist first.** Irwin argues that the principles of history (‘usul al-ta’rikh) were modeled on the principles of jurisprudence (‘usul al-fiqh), so that the celebrated critique of credulous historians has a legal template rather than a modern empiricist one ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch04-aristotle-theology-maliki-law]]). The Muqaddima's “philosophy” is hikma, wisdom literature, and Ibn Khaldun disowned falsafa ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch04-philosophy-cyclical-history]]). Irwin's closing judgment is that on many issues the Qur’an and the Maliki law books “did his thinking for him” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch11-ending-up]]).

**Sufi, and a believer in the unseen.** Circumstantial evidence “overwhelmingly” indicates he was a Sufi, though a jurist who policed mysticism ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch06-sufi-mystic]]). He held sorcery, the evil eye and dream-divination to be real but forbidden ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-supernatural-occult]]), and Irwin thinks his deepest occult interest was in knowing the future ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-future-last-days]]). See [[muqaddima-enchanted-frame-irwin]].

**A bounded theory.** ‘Asabiyya is treated seriously but limited. The cycle fits the Almoravids and Almohads but not the Merinids, Hafsids or ‘Abd al-Wadids; Ibn Khaldun himself exempted the eastern lands and the Mamluks; and its importance “has been overstressed in some modern readings” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch03-nomads-asabiyya]]). See [[asabiyya-irwin]]. Irwin also credits the causal palette as broader than a nomad theory (economy, climate, kinship bonds and the supernatural) and grants that the models, built from medieval data, bear comparison with Darwin, Marx and Durkheim even though they “cannot apply to modern societies” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch11-ending-up]]).

**A text in revision, not a system.** The Egyptian recension is half as long again as the first draft and grew partly through occult material ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch05-arrival-in-egypt]]). Irwin lists inconsistencies in ‘asabiyya, badawi, “Arab”, the scope of the cycle and the causes of plague, and suspects the Muqaddima's ideal reader was its author ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch11-ending-up]]).

## Irwin and the retrofitting reading: support, complication, and what goes beyond

Irwin's book is the main scholarly warrant for the claim quoted at the top of this page, that the proto-sociologist is “largely retrofitted by modern interpreters”. Read in full, it supports that claim strongly, complicates the “depressed Arab Petrarch” image in specific ways, and answers part of what this page lists as missing from Rao's account.

### Where Irwin supports the reading

The whole reception half of the book is a catalogue of retrofits: the Ottomans' ageing-empire diagnostic, the “Arab Montesquieu”, De Slane's “esprit de corps”, Gautier's Renaissance Frenchman, Von Kremer's Hegelian, Toynbee's “long-lost relative”, Lacoste's Marxist, Mahdi's Straussian, Gellner's value-free sociologist, Reagan's supply-sider ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-afterlife-french-germans]], [[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-toynbee-rosenthal]], [[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-colonialist-mahdi-gellner]], [[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch08-economics]]). “Defender of tradition” also fits Irwin's picture of a culture of transmission where “originality was at a discount” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch09-teaching-writing]]). See [[terminal-medievals-vs-early-moderns-irwin]].

### Where Irwin complicates it

- **The pessimism has a named source.** Irwin endorses Gibb that the cycle of rise and fall follows from infractions of the Shari‘a through pride, luxury and greed, and that the pessimism is religious and moral, “not sociological” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch04-aristotle-theology-maliki-law]]). He also traces it to an inherited chroniclers' trope of austere early Arab conquerors ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch03-bedouin-berbers-genealogy]]), and speculates, hedged, that political failure, the murders of his brother and Ibn al-Khatib, the Black Death and the surrounding ruins deepened it ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch04-philosophy-cyclical-history]], [[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch01-ruins-black-death]]). “Depressed” is a temperament; Irwin's account is of a genre, a law school and an eschatology.
- **What he awaited was an ending, not a renaissance.** The notes carry no claim by Irwin that Ibn Khaldun lamented a missing Islamic renaissance. Irwin's Ibn Khaldun thought the Islamic sciences were at their peak, the Arab age over and power drifting north to Franks and Turks. He had no idea of progress or reform and expected the End of Time ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch07-future-last-days]]). Irwin's tentative reading of the book's ultimate purpose is to prepare Muslims for the Last Judgment ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch11-ending-up]]).
- **The “depressed man in an age of decline” is itself one of the readings.** Irwin reports that the novelist Bensalem Himmich reads Ibn Khaldun that way, while also finding a materialist and positivist in him ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-arab-american-novels-orientalism]]). The image is not Irwin's.
- **Irwin does not pose Rao's diagnostic question.** Nothing in the notes frames Ibn Khaldun's world as failing to assemble modernity. Irwin grants some forward-leaning qualities: the capacity to generalize about society makes him “almost unique among medieval historians” ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch04-aristotle-theology-maliki-law]]), and he has “the perhaps delusive appearance of modernity” beside Froissart ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch03-wisdom-literature-ibar]]). But he rejects the one reading that comes closest to Rao's question, Engels's account of cyclical Islamic stasis against Christian progress. Irwin says Ibn Khaldun would not have recognized that contrast ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch08-economics]]).

### What Irwin adds to the listed gaps

- **Hodgson.** Irwin does engage Hodgson, but as a reader rather than as an account of structural constraints. Hodgson objected to Rosenthal's “Arab” and “supernatural”, and Irwin argues his motive was to present a secular faylasuf. Irwin calls Hodgson's Granada philosopher-king story unevidenced ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-toynbee-rosenthal]]).
- **The sophistication of ‘asabiyya.** Irwin shows a concept with “a life of its own”, used by God, reinforced by religion but not dependent on it. It can be engineered through the mamluk system and generated by proximity rather than only by kinship, against Gellner ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch03-nomads-asabiyya]], [[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch05-arrival-in-egypt]], [[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-colonialist-mahdi-gellner]]). See [[asabiyya-irwin]].
- **The geography of the cycle.** Beside the Bayly section above, Irwin records that Ibn Khaldun described Egypt and Syria as free of tribes, which Irwin calls “nonsense” given the 1389 civil war ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch05-arrival-in-egypt]]). Toynbee, for his part, objected that most empires were not founded by nomads ([[irwin-ibn-khaldun-ch10-toynbee-rosenthal]]). The cycle was always regional.
