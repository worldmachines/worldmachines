# Adam Smith (1723-1790)

## Biographical Context

- The "professor" in *The Infidel and the Professor* — held an institutional position while remaining an undeclared agnostic
- Close friend and intellectual collaborator of [[david-hume]]
- Key figure of the Scottish Enlightenment, along with Hume
- More diplomatically circumspect than Hume — took religion and theology out of his intellectual work quietly rather than combatively

## Rao's Reading

Smith is best understood as an intellectual descendant of [[baruch-spinoza]], with [[gottfried-wilhelm-leibniz]] (as philosopher) already faded into irrelevance by his time. He represents "an equally radical continuation of Spinoza's thought in an entire new reality domain that was just beginning to acquire modern contours — economics."

His *approach* to economic phenomena was "ontologically different from (say) the approach of Ibn Khaldun, whose life and ideas we encountered in last year's book club. Or even the approach of the Venetian merchants who inaugurated the reality-scope expansion that eventually required an Adam Smith to make sense of."

## The Invisible Hand as Empirical Stance

The "invisible hand" is not primarily a claim about markets or efficiency — it is an epistemological stance. The discipline of economics that Smith inaugurated was "fundamentally *about* noisy, messy reality data, with only weak edifices of emergent constitutive laws built on top. The opposite of divine design and direction."

This is the Spinozan move applied to economic life: don't look for divine design or moral order in economic outcomes; look at what actually happens and build weak heuristics from the data.

> "A unified Hume-Smith theory of reality would be: Shit happens, but it's not entirely unpredictable and disorderly. There are laws, but they're just handy, contingent heuristics, not 'reality' itself."

## The Hume-Smith Collaboration Pattern

Where Spinoza and Leibniz were in a partially adversarial relationship, Hume and Smith were "collaborating allies who influenced each other in their campaigns on different parts of the frontier of expanding reality." This is a different mode of participating in the [[Argument of Progress]] — collaborative frontier expansion rather than adversarial divergence from a common starting point.

Both were "primarily oriented towards reality rather than theology, compared to their predecessors." Both paid attention to moral sentiments — "rugging religious or institutional understandings of morality" — continuing Spinoza's project to separate secular and religious philosophy.

## The Darwin Connection

The Hume-Smith assault on traditional moral and natural philosophy represents "the beginnings of the intellectual iconoclasm that reach its peak with Darwin." Darwin's *real* accomplishment was not disproving literalist religion (Hume had done that effectively) but "assaulting the *secular* philosophical foundations of the modernity machine" — making divergence and variety load-bearing features of reality itself.

## Rasmussen's *The Infidel and the Professor* (2017)

Dennis Rasmussen's joint biography of Smith and [[david-hume]] ([[rasmussen-infidel-and-professor]]) confirms the sketch above of "the Professor" who held his Glasgow chair and kept his religious views to himself. It sharpens Rao's picture of collaborating allies: Smith's thought turns out to circle Hume's, indebted almost everywhere and revising it at almost every point. It also adds an early astronomy essay that makes Smith's naturalism more explicit than anything he published.

### The Biographical Record

**Early life.** Born 1723 in Kirkcaldy; fatherless, an only child, raised by a devout Presbyterian mother — a set of coincidences Rasmussen stacks deliberately against [[david-hume]]'s. He studied at Glasgow under Francis Hutcheson and the mathematicians Robert Simson and Robert Dick.

**Oxford as counter-example.** Six dispiriting years at Balliol, capped by a nervous breakdown, produced the era's most quotable indictment of ossified institutions: the professors had "given up altogether even the pretence of teaching," and richly endowed universities generally become "sanctuaries in which exploded systems and obsolete prejudices found shelter and protection, after they had been hunted out of every other corner of the world." It is autobiography and an early draft of the theory of institutional decline he later formalized in *The Wealth of Nations*. Balliol's dons also confiscated his copy of Hume's *Treatise* — the two men's first contact was textual, years before they met, probably in autumn 1749.

**Career.** Public lectures in Edinburgh; the Chair of Logic and then of Moral Philosophy at Glasgow (which he later called "by far the happiest and most honourable period of my life"); resignation in 1764 to tutor the young Duke of Buccleuch across Toulouse, Geneva, and Paris on a lifelong pension — aristocratic patronage, not the university, funding what became *The Wealth of Nations* ("I have begun to write a book in order to pass away the time"). Then six grinding, solitary years in Kirkcaldy, comparing his progress to the endlessly-undone "web of Penelope"; London to see the book through the press; and finally Edinburgh, where he took a customs commissionership — history's most celebrated free-trader collecting tariffs, an irony he was fully aware of and which his own theory (which allowed moderate revenue duties) partly absolves. He died in 1790, having ordered his unpublished manuscripts burned, to near-silence: a mean-spirited London obituary and a few bland lines in the *Scots Magazine*.

### Circling Hume: Where Smith Revised His Friend

The characterization of Hume and Smith as collaborating allies (see *The Hume-Smith Collaboration Pattern* above, and [[venkatesh-rao-the-divergence-machine-ii]]) holds, but Rasmussen's granular record gives it a sharper texture: Smith's thought, in Samuel Fleischacker's phrase, "circles around" Hume's — indebted almost everywhere, and revising incessantly. Smith never names Hume in *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759), yet the questions, arguments, and even the examples are saturated with him.

**Four divergences in *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*:**

1. **Sympathy: projection, not contagion.** Hume treats sympathy as passive transmission, emotions passing between minds the way vibrating strings convey motion. Smith argues for active imaginative projection into another's *situation* — which alone explains blushing for someone oblivious to his own rudeness, pitying a person who has lost her reason, or grieving for the dead. He reworks an example from Hume's own *Treatise* to make the point.
2. **Utility and the appearance of fitness.** Smith accepts that perceived utility makes objects beautiful, then adds his own proudest insight: the appearance of contrivance is often valued *more than the end it serves* — tidying askew chairs costs more effort than the convenience gained; a watch's precision is prized beyond its use. He extends this to the pursuit of wealth and power that drives civilization itself: people mistake the machinery of comfort for comfort. The point that a system can be valued for its orderliness independent of what it delivers connects directly to [[legibility-as-machine-core]].
3. **Justice as natural, not artificial.** Where Hume grounds justice in reflection on its social utility, Smith grounds it in the resentment we feel on a victim's behalf, arising "antecedent to all reflection upon the utility of punishment" — an instance of Smith accusing Hume, in a memorable formulation, of being *not sufficiently Humean*.
4. **Religion, ambivalently.** Against Hume's view of religion as almost wholly pernicious, Smith treats belief sympathetically without making it foundational — his framework needs no divine premise, and his God arrives hedged with "circumlocutions... and frequent use of the verb 'to seem.'"

**In *The Wealth of Nations* (1776):** Smith's most important claim is also his most Humean. Book 3's argument that commerce "introduced order and good government, and with them, the liberty and security of individuals" credits Hume as the only writer to have noticed it, and the underlying narrative — feudal lords dissipating their power on "trinkets and baubles" until they could no longer maintain armed dependents — comes near-intact from Hume. Anti-mercantilism and free trade were already Hume's causes; Smith's additions are the division of labor and vastly greater system.

**Where Smith broke hardest.** He catalogued costs Hume waved away: merchants' collusive "mean rapacity"; the mental "torpor" of extreme division of labor, which can leave a worker "as stupid and ignorant as it is possible for a human creature to become"; and structural inequality, whose teaching of admiration for the rich and neglect of the poor he called "the great and most universal cause of the corruption of our moral sentiments." And on religion he was the *more* radical: against Hume's cynical defense of an established church (a subsidized clergy can be bribed into indolence), Smith quoted that case at length and rebutted it, arguing that competition among sects defuses fanaticism as free markets defuse monopoly — full disestablishment being a genuine outlier position in 1776.

Both men were also early and isolated in urging British withdrawal from America.

### The Astronomy Essay: Science and Religion as Self-Soothing

Smith's earliest surviving work, the astronomy essay of *The Principles Which Lead and Direct Philosophical Enquiries*, written at Oxford in his twenties, argues that **science and religion are both products of the imagination soothing itself**. Frightening or awe-inspiring irregularities — storms, eclipses, rainbows — generate first polytheism, then monotheism, then scientific law, each superseding the last only by leaving fewer explanatory gaps in the "chaos of jarring and discordant appearances." Order is imposed on nature by the mind rather than discovered within it.

Two things make the essay significant beyond its content. First, it anticipates Hume's *Natural History of Religion* (1757) by several years, which Rasmussen reads as evidence of two minds converging independently rather than one influencing the other. Second, Smith soft-pedaled its obvious implications for a designing God — plausibly for reasons of "prudence" — and never published it; it appeared only after his death. That decision is the first entry in a lifelong pattern of managing exposure rather than belief, traced in [[calibrated-candor]].

The essay's treatment of a paradigm's coherence as something successively constructed and then felt as necessary sits close to the [[history-machine]] account of how paradigms acquire their retrospective air of inevitability, and its deflationary, gap-closing genealogy of explanation belongs to the family [[skyhooks-and-cranes]] describes.
