# Hobbism (Douglass)

In [[robin-douglass]]'s usage ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes]]), "Hobbism" is the image of [[thomas-hobbes]] current in late seventeenth- and eighteenth-century France and Geneva. It was a caricature that often misrepresented Hobbes, and it mattered as much as Hobbes's own texts, because influence usually travelled indirectly. The Hobbes/Hobbism distinction governs the whole book. [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] sometimes engaged Hobbes directly, sometimes attacked Hobbesian legacies in contemporaries, and sometimes subverted the prevailing image of Hobbism in order to criticise them ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-mirabeau-letter]]).

## How the caricature was assembled

- **A rhetorical weapon.** Hobbes's name carried great weight, perhaps surpassed only by Spinoza's, and the two were paired as the most subversive philosophers. Responses tended to be authoritative dismissal, not detailed critique. Douglass suggests the protests sometimes concealed real affinity ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-french-reception-opening]]).
- **From science to morals.** Bayle's *Dictionnaire* article shifted attention from Hobbes's mechanism to his morals and politics. Bayle's "one person" may have helped seed the misreading that Hobbes's sovereign must be a single monarch. "Man is a wolf to man", which concerned commonwealths, was read as describing individuals. Nicole absorbed Hobbes's state of war into an Augustinian picture of fallen man ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-nicole-bayle]]).
- **Amour-propre as the source of justice.** Malebranche charged Hobbes with making amour-propre the foundation of justice, and his materialism with destroying freedom. Douglass says the charge "recurs throughout eighteenth-century French thought" ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-malebranche]]).
- **Barbeyrac's four charges.** Epicurean self-preservation as the origin of society, the state of nature as a state of war, unlimited royal authority over state and religion, and justice determined by the sovereign's will ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-barbeyrac-burlamaqui-natural-law]]). The *Encyclopédie* (Boucher d'Argis, Jaucourt) recycled the list. After the 1752 Prades affair, contributors had a new urgency to denounce Hobbes ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-diderot-encyclopedie]]).
- **Justice as convention.** Montesquieu called Hobbes's a "terrible system" that, "like Spinoza", overturns religion and morality ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-montesquieu-against-hobbes]]). The later charge of "Hobbesian positivism" was tied to divine voluntarism. Douglass calls the Hobbes it targets "largely a straw Hobbes, although one widely accepted at the time" ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-law-nature-denaturing]]).
- **A condemnatory label.** By 1758 the label could condemn an author, as in Archbishop Beaumont's condemnation of Helvétius ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-unity-civil-religion]]).

## What the caricature got wrong, per Douglass

- **Justice versus the laws of nature.** The central error was conflating Hobbes's definition of justice with his account of the laws of nature. Hobbes held that the laws of nature operate in the state of nature, binding *in foro interno*. This let the philosophes evade the real problem of their own tradition: the obligatory force of natural law without civil sanctions ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-diderot-encyclopedie]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-state-of-nature-to-political-society]]).
- **An artificial gulf.** Anti-Hobbism made Pufendorf look less Hobbesian than he was. Pufendorf's fundamental law of sociability was not far from Hobbes's "seek peace", and his account of why civil society arose stayed "very Hobbesian" ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-barbeyrac-burlamaqui-natural-law]]).
- **Natural evil.** Hobbes explicitly denied that man is naturally evil ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-natural-goodness-golden-age]]).

## Rousseau's use of Hobbism

Douglass treats Rousseau as one of Hobbes's "most sophisticated commentators", not a naive transmitter of the caricature ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch1-french-reception-opening]]). Rousseau's distinctive move was to turn the charge of Hobbism back on the anti-Hobbists. Pufendorf's weak, miserable natural man ended in a condition virtually identical to Hobbes's, so Rousseau exposed affinities Barbeyrac had tried to hide ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-state-of-nature-state-of-war]]). He attached Grotius to the reputation usually reserved for Hobbes ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-free-will-slavery-obligation]]). And he charged the doux commerce theorists with a covert Hobbism: justifying commerce and luxury as remedies for man's natural barbarism implicitly accepts the Hobbesian state of nature ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-engagement-in-context]]). Rousseau collapsed the accepted Hobbes/Pufendorf bifurcation ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch2-rousseaus-critique-reappraised]]). He was not thereby siding with Hobbes: "to disagree with Hobbes's critics is not necessarily to agree with Hobbes" ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-hobbes-and-rousseau]]).

Rousseau also fell partly into the caricature. He may not have grasped the nuances of Hobbes on natural law ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-state-of-nature-to-political-society]]). Douglass concedes that the charge that Rousseau missed Hobbes's point is partly valid, but says it misses the point of Rousseau's critique, which was to show Hobbes's pervasive influence on contemporaries ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-engagement-in-context]]).

## Later echoes

Douglass notes that the Straussian reading of Hobbes, whatever its fairness, resembles how Hobbes was read in eighteenth-century France ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-intro-hobbes-and-rousseau]]). Some Straussian commentators repeat the old charge of Hobbism against Rousseau, and in Douglass's view this misconstrues both men ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-sovereignty-and-law-opening]]).
