# From speculative to social attack (Morley)

This page draws on Volume I (Preface, Chapters I–VIII) of [[john-morley]]'s study [[diderot-and-the-encyclopaedists]]; Volume II material is added in later sections. The argument is Morley's, a Comtean-flavoured causal story that the notes treat as contestable.

## The puzzle

Why did a metaphysical agitation (can matter think? are ideas innate?) turn after mid-century into a political and social one? Morley's answer is that metaphysical conclusions shape civil sentiment, and that the theological persecutors of De Prades and [[joseph-de-maistre]]'s later assault on Bacon were “guided by a right instinct of self-preservation” ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-1]]). His opening chapter states the same thesis from the other side: earlier heresy had only the scientific idea, while the new insurrection added the social idea ([[morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary]]).

## Books as symbols, ideas by opportuneness

Morley warns that histories of literature mistake a book for the cause of a controversy of which it is “only a symbol”. Speculative literature's force “hangs on practical opportuneness”: on the economic evils of monasticism, superstition, ecclesiastical factiousness and bigoted tribunals ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-1]]). The attack weighed more in France than in England, he argues, because French institutions were “too deeply rooted in absolutism and privilege” to be reformed and the Catholic Church was identified with repression, while English deism stayed impotent as a merely intellectual movement. He quotes D'Argenson (1753) that hatred of priests, not English philosophy, was driving France toward revolution, and adds that hope, not only hate, drove the movement ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-1]]).

## Why empiricism tends to a social spirit

Newton over Descartes, Bacon, and Locke's sensational psychology, on Morley's account, expel “Magic” in favour of ordered cause and effect. As knowledge widens, so does recognised nescience, and attention turns to forces that can be controlled; man becomes “the centre of the world to himself”; and sensationalism heightens belief in the power of education and institutions. Morley sets aside whether the philosophy was true and concedes its metaphysic was “imperfectly thought out” ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-1]]). Diderot's Letter on the Blind belongs here: Morley says its doctrine of relativity was used “as a weapon of combat against the standing organisation” ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch4-new-philosophy-1]]; see [[relativity-against-catholic-metaphysics-morley]]).

## Political ideas as instruments

For Morley economic conditions and administration matter more to national well-being than the form of government (his clothes-versus-blood analogy). The Encyclopædists attacked colonial tyranny, the slave trade, the fiscal system, venal justice and the penal code, and first grasped “the honour that is owed to productive industry”. They did not originate these ideas, owing peace-ideas to Saint Pierre and political method to Montesquieu; what changed is that “political ideas have been grasped as instruments” ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-1]]). The honour paid to labour and the turn from words to things are, for Morley, a social and an intellectual revolution in one ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-2]]). Hence his rejection of the siege-engine reading: the aim was “practice, not literature but life”, and the tools built for attack would serve disinterested research in a happier season ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-history-2]]).

## The same movement on the stage

Morley extends the thesis to Diderot's dramatic theory. As the Encyclopædia glorified pacific industry and civil justice, the theory of the drama glorified private virtue and domestic life: “two great sides of a single movement”. He hedges that Diderot need not have consciously surveyed his time, only thought sincerely “in the spirit of reality” ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch7-the-stage-1]]; see [[diderot-theory-of-the-drama-morley]]).

## Morley's limits on the thesis

Morley qualifies the movement in two ways. It lacked the historic idea, which he holds would have let the Encyclopædia treat religious and other systems as aspects of intellectual effort ([[morley-diderot-v1-preface-ch1-preliminary]], [[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-3]]). And the habit of measuring every conclusion by its presumed social effect carries its own danger, the same one ecclesiastics fall into when they resist free discussion; Diderot's Liberty article, in Morley's reading, forgets that truth is the best of the things good for society ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-diderot-contributions-3]]).

## World Machines resonances, with the notes' hedges

The notes connect the thesis to [[intellectual-vs-revolutionary]] and to the [[counter-enlightenment]] (De Maistre as the acute Catholic opponent), but stress that Morley frames it as ideas gaining force through practical opportuneness, not as a machine theory ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch5-encyclopaedia-contents-1]]). Its sociology of ideas is close to a social-causation view and can be compared with the [[history-machine]] preference for distributed, silently assembling movements, though Morley makes no structural argument ([[morley-diderot-v1-ch3-early-writings-1]]).

## Volume II: Holbach and the coalition of two revolts

This section draws on Volume II ([[morley-diderot-v2]]), which carries the thesis into the books of the Encyclopædic school. Morley's division of labour runs: Voltaire pierced the Church's dogma, Rousseau shamed its irreligious temper, Diderot exposed its philosophy's absoluteness, and then Helvétius and Holbach came “not merely with criticism, but with substitutes” ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1]]). Holbach's System of Nature is where speculation turns most directly political. Unlike Voltaire, who abstained from politics, and Rousseau, who was vague, Holbach attacked government itself, calling society a state of war between ruler and ruled; its premise, as Morley states it, is that social deliverance depends on intellectual deliverance, Naturalism for Theism. Revolt against the impotence of society and revolt against the omnipotence of God made a “firm coalition” that lasted until Hébert was guillotined as an atheist ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-1]]). In Holbach's indictment of kings, Morley hears Robespierre, Desmoulins and Danton already speaking ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-2]]), and he reads Holbach's ethic of the greatest number as the ethical side of every popular movement, in a society where public spirit “could only mean revolution” ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-3]]). Raynal's History shows the positive side Morley requires of the movement: by then attacks on the Church were “cheap”, and the book needed zeal for humanity, better laws and the amelioration of the common lot ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-2]]). Morley's limit on the thesis recurs here too. The school lacked the historic idea, offering reasons for changing convictions without asking what the old ones were rooted in ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-1]]); the notes flag his prophecy-readings as retrospective framing, not demonstrated causation.
