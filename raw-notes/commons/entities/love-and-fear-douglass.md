# Love and fear (Douglass)

[[robin-douglass]] ends his chapter on the passions with a compressed contrast ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes]]). Both [[thomas-hobbes]] and [[jean-jacques-rousseau]] held that political unity depends on ordering the passions. Hobbes ordered **fear**; Rousseau ordered **love** of fatherland. Douglass traces the difference to their opposed accounts of human nature ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-hobbes-and-fear]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-of-love-and-fear]]).

## The shared premise

Douglass claims that both thinkers, "perhaps" more than any other modern political philosopher, made ordering the passions to secure political unity a central problem. Both wanted a unified political will driven by passion, because interest in obeying the laws, grasped by reason, cannot bind citizens alone ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-ordering-the-passions-opening]]). Both deny a strict combat between reason and passion. Against Gert, Douglass argues that Hobbes's reason reckons consequences and does not supply its own ends. Rousseau's reason is a faculty of judgement that orders the passions without mastering them, so only passions can check passions ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-hobbes-and-fear]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-reason-and-the-passions]]). Douglass places both nearer Hume than Kant, with the hedge "at least to the extent" that they deny conflict (compare [[david-hume]]).

## Hobbes: fear redirected

Fear of death is the passion Hobbes most needs. Whether by institution or by acquisition, men covenant out of fear. Hobbes's project is to show that such covenants are valid and to direct perpetual fear toward its "rightful object", the state of nature, by shaping the imagination. The last books of *Leviathan* dispel the fear of "Spirits Invisible", which breeds sedition. Hell becomes a second death, so that, as Bramhall put it, there are "no devils to be feared, but devils incarnate". Douglass doubts, against Tuck, that Hobbes thought fear could be overcome. Its aim was reorientation. Pride, "glory disliked", is answered by the law of natural equality. Education, such as teaching *Leviathan* in the universities, still works through the imagination, above all fear ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-hobbes-and-fear]]).

## Rousseau: love cultivated

Rousseau concedes that fear has uses, but its purpose is "not to lead them to do good but to keep them from doing evil" ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-of-love-and-fear]]). His republic instead makes love of fatherland the citizen's dominant passion. Public education generalizes amour de soi-même to the fatherland, and festivals, honours and public esteem turn pride toward civic glory ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-love-of-fatherland]]; see [[amour-propre-douglass]]). Politics must "speak to the heart". Rousseau faults the Abbé de Saint-Pierre's "false principle of perfected reason" and modern political language for becoming "more precise and less passionate" ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-reason-and-the-passions]]). In a well-ordered republic virtue is love of fatherland, where duty is also inclination, and it is no longer a "state of war" between them ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-free-will-and-virtue]]).

## Different threats, different natures

Hobbes fears citizens who make themselves judges of right and wrong. Rousseau fears civic disengagement and lack of virtue ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-of-love-and-fear]]). The choice between fear and love turns on whether human nature is pre-lapsarian goodness or post-lapsarian war. Through Pierre Nicole, who used Hobbes's state of nature and taught that fear of death is the first brake on amour-propre, Rousseau read Hobbes's politics, like Pufendorf's morality, as the politics of fallen man: peace as the best achievable without the right love. Douglass keeps the qualification that Hobbes himself denied natural evil. This is the Hobbism of the French moral tradition ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-of-love-and-fear]]).

## Negative and positive unity

The same contrast appears in civil religion. Hobbes's reuniting of "the two heads of the eagle" is primarily negative: removing ecclesiastical challenges to peace. Rousseau's civil profession of faith is positive: it cultivates citizens who love laws, duties and fatherland ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch3-unity-civil-religion]]). Douglass's summary of the whole book puts it the same way: agreement on the problem of disordered passions, divergence on the passion politics should engage ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-oppositions-and-affinities]]).

## The limiting condition

A politics of love presupposes natural goodness not yet corrupted by luxury and entrenched inequality. So the love-governed republic suits only the naturally good, and Douglass judges it no live option for large commercial states ([[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-ch4-of-love-and-fear]], [[douglass-rousseau-and-hobbes-conclusion-republics-naturally-good]]).
