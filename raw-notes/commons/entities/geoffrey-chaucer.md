# Geoffrey Chaucer

**Source**: prefatory biography and edition of [[chaucer-canterbury-tales]] (ed. D. Laing Purves)

Geoffrey Chaucer (born, "generally believed," London c. 1328; died 1400) — poet, and by day a working functionary of the late-medieval English state. The edition's biographical essay reconstructs him almost entirely from the paper trail of a legible administrative order in formation: customs controllerships whose accounts he kept "in his own hand," pensions drawn twice yearly at the Exchequer, diplomatic commissions, letters of protection, a wardship, a dated lease ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-life-of-chaucer]]).

## The linguistic unifier

The essay's largest claim is infrastructural rather than aesthetic: Chaucer fused England's two "half-hostile" strands — the Teutonic speech of the people and the French-derived speech of the court — into a single vernacular literature, closing a rift open since the Conquest. He does not merely write well; he changes what a shared English idiom can do ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-life-of-chaucer]], [[chaucer-canterbury-tales-editors-preface]]).

## A career run on patronage weather

Chaucer's material fortunes track his patron's faction, not his merit. He rises under John of Gaunt and Lancastrian ascendancy, is stripped of both customs offices in December 1386 when Gloucester's commission purges Lancastrian appointees, and is restored the moment Richard II resumes power in 1389 — the essay careful to insist this was factional weather, not misconduct. His late *Complaint to His Purse*, a courtly love-complaint redirected onto an empty coin-purse and capped by a petition to the new king Henry IV, reportedly succeeded in getting his annuity confirmed ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-life-of-chaucer]], [[chaucer-canterbury-tales-miscellaneous-poems]]). A possible 1373 meeting with Petrarch at Padua is argued by literary inference rather than record — the Clerk's mournful digression on Petrarch's death read as the poet's own voice — a method the essay flags as its own weakest, in contrast to the archival rigor applied elsewhere ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-clerks-tale]]).

## Two evidentiary regimes

The biography stages a small [[history-machine]] episode of its own: it contrasts the "old biographers," who built a vivid Wycliffite-conspiracy-and-exile drama out of inferred autobiographical allusions in a literary text, against Sir Harris Nicolas's sober counter-history assembled from customs rolls, pension disbursements, and Parliamentary attendance. The dramatic personality-driven narrative is displaced by a boring, distributed, record-based one — the same [[legibility-as-machine-core]] apparatus that structured Chaucer's civil-service life now structuring how his life can be known. Both sit inside the Dawn of the [[modernity-machine]] ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-life-of-chaucer]]).

## The self inside the work

Chaucer rarely appears in his poems except as a deflected, self-mocking figure. In the *House of Fame* the eagle needles him for finishing his customs accounts only to go home and read alone "as a hermite" — a rare direct glimpse of his working life inside an allegory ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-house-of-fame]]). In *Sir Thopas* the Host describes him as an aloof, portly, "elvish" man staring at the ground, then hands him the floor for a deliberately terrible tail-rhyme romance — the only physical self-portrait in the work, delivered as mockery ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-sir-thopas]]). He also curates his own canon within the fiction: the *Legend of Good Women* prologue stages him arraigned by the God of Love for *Troilus* and the *Rose* translation, with Alcestis defending him by reciting his bibliography, and the *Bukton* envoy cites "the Wife of Bath" as an authority in a private verse letter ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-legend-of-good-women-prologue]], [[chaucer-canterbury-tales-man-of-laws-tale]], [[chaucer-canterbury-tales-miscellaneous-poems]]).

## The signature and its apocrypha

Chaucer's name became a strong enough attractor to accrete texts he did not write. The edition's own notes flag *Chaucer's Dream*, the *Court of Love*, the *Cuckoo and the Nightingale*, and the *Flower and the Leaf* as probably not his, yet each travelled for centuries under his name and acquired a Chaucerian (often biographical-allegorical) reading — a corpus gathering apocrypha by resonance and utility rather than provenance ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-chaucers-dream]], [[chaucer-canterbury-tales-court-of-love]], [[chaucer-canterbury-tales-cuckoo-and-nightingale]], [[chaucer-canterbury-tales-flower-and-leaf]]). This is the practical face of the [[belatedness]] and [[anxiety-of-influence]] that a strong authorial signature generates: works cluster around the name that can give them staying power.

## The Retraction

Chaucer's closing prayer applies his own Parson's penitential logic to his oeuvre, keeping his devotional translations and revoking his "worldly vanities" — *Troilus*, the *Book of Fame*, and "the Tales of Canterbury, all those that sounen unto sin." Whether sincere or a monastic interpolation, it collapses author and penitent into one figure and ends the whole collection on an act of self-cancellation ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-parsons-tale]]).
