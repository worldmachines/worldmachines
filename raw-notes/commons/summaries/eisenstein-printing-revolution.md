# The Printing Revolution in Early Modern Europe (Eisenstein)

[[elizabeth-eisenstein]], *The Printing Revolution in Early Modern Europe* (Cambridge University Press, 2nd ed., 2005). The book is the abridged *The Printing Press as an Agent of Change* followed by a 2005 Afterword in which she answers two decades of critics.

## The question

Eisenstein begins with two overload narratives she found symptomatic rather than diagnostic. One is Carl Bridenbaugh's lament over historians' “collective amnesia”, which she reframes as a problem of *recall* under unmanageable volume. The other is [[marshall-mcluhan]]'s *The Gutenberg Galaxy*. From McLuhan she takes only a well-posed question: what did the fifteenth-century shift from script to print actually change? To her surprise, “not even a small literature” had surveyed it ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-introduction]]). Her scope is deliberately narrow and unfashionable. She studies a shift *within* literate culture (scribal to print), not the oral-to-literate shift, among the Latin-reading elites of the Commonwealth of Learning, in Western Europe. Her governing caveat is that printing was “an agent, not the agent, let alone the only agent, of change”.

## Structure

- **Part I, the features of print culture.** Historians depend on print yet are silent about it, partly because a “veil of print” projects print-era uniformity back onto scribal culture. The fifteenth-century shift is defended as “cumulative and irreversible” where a twelfth-century commercialization of copying was not ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch01-unacknowledged-revolution]]). The “advent of printing” is defined as presses spreading beyond the Rhineland from the 1460s. Output rose by revolution, not evolution, through a new institution, the master printer's workshop ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch02-defining-initial-shift-1]]). Consumption effects (literacy, reading publics, “image to word”) resist every neat formula ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch02-defining-initial-shift-2]]). Chapter Three itemizes six features: wide dissemination and altered intake ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s1-wide-dissemination]]), standardization ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s2-standardization]]), the reorganization of texts and reference guides ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s3-reorganizing-texts]]), data collection through improved editions ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s4-data-collection]]), preservation and fixity ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s5-preservative-powers]]), and amplification and reinforcement ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s6-amplification-reinforcement]]). Chapter Four follows the rise of the reading public ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch04-s1-reading-public]]) and of a new class of men of letters ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch04-s2-men-of-letters]]).
- **Part II, three movements re-read.** The classical revival made permanent ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch05-permanent-renaissance-1]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch05-permanent-renaissance-2]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch05-permanent-renaissance-3]]). Western Christendom disrupted ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch06-christendom-disrupted-1]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch06-christendom-disrupted-2]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch06-christendom-disrupted-3]]). The Book of Nature transformed, through the Copernican revolution and Galileo's trial ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s1-great-book-of-nature]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s2-copernican-revolution]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s3-galileos-trial-1]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s3-galileos-trial-2]]).
- **Conclusion and final remarks.** She trades “modernity” for “a definite communications shift”, sets Scripture and Nature on diverging paths ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-conclusion-1]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-conclusion-2]]), and defends irreversibility against grand dialectical narratives ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-final-remarks]]).
- **Afterword (2005).** She answers book historians, gradualists and the charge of technological determinism ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-1]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-2]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-3]]).

## Throughlines

- **Fixity is the master feature.** Print preserved through multiplied quantity on a *worse* substrate than parchment. That turned transitory revivals, heresies and observations into permanent, cumulative ones: “The preservation of the old, in brief, was a prerequisite for a tradition of the new.” See [[typographical-fixity-eisenstein]].
- **Standardization has a dialectical twin.** Sufficiently uniform copies, errata sheets, alphabetized indexes and repeatable images sharpened perceived diversity and a felt individual self: “The more standardized the type, indeed, the more compelling the sense of an idiosyncratic personal self.” See [[standardization-and-individuation-eisenstein]].
- **A new institution sits at the centre.** The master printer's shop, not the university, the artist's studio or the secluded study, is where scholars, artisans and merchants met. See [[master-printer-workshop-eisenstein]].
- **Printing, not Protestantism.** Print was both precondition and precipitant of the Reformation. It outmoded the Vulgate, made liturgy uniform for the first time, fixed a permanent schism, and built through profit-seeking printers a cosmopolitan “third force” neither church intended ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch06-christendom-disrupted-1]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch06-christendom-disrupted-2]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch06-christendom-disrupted-3]]).
- **A revolution in transmission, not in data.** Against Thomas Kuhn, what changed between the young Copernicus, who could barely see an *Almagest*, and the teenage Tycho, who could buy one, was “the transmission of old observations” ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s4-data-collection]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s2-copernican-revolution]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-conclusion-1]]). Publication also had a confessional geography that decided who could print Copernican work ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s3-galileos-trial-1]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch07-s3-galileos-trial-2]]).
- **Overload is old.** Print inaugurated an information overload that every later medium has intensified rather than resolved. See [[printing-press-and-information-overload-eisenstein]].
- **Opposite effects, left standing.** The same press “laid the basis both for literal fundamentalism and for modern science”. It fanned bigotry while fostering toleration, and it fixed national divisions while creating a cosmopolitan Commonwealth of Learning. Eisenstein refuses to force these toward a dialectical end ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-conclusion-2]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-final-remarks]]). See [[scripture-and-nature-parting-eisenstein]].

## The Afterword's corrections

In 2005 Eisenstein drops “impact” and its billiard-ball image for a “cooperative transition” or “change of phase”. She unpacks “revolution” into an abrupt replacement and a “long-range irreversible process”. She argues that print exploded in politically fragmented Western Europe, not despite fragmentation ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-2]]). She concedes that speech and script persisted and that standardization was relative, not absolute, while holding that the shift ran “all in one direction” ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-1]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-3]]).

## In World Machines terms, with the notes' hedges

- **[[modernity-machine]].** The spread of printing workshops sits inside the 1200–1600 Dawn window WMP already assumes, and the notes read the workshop, the reference apparatus and fixity as Dawn-period infrastructure. Eisenstein, however, is a historian of one medium. She calls print an “agent”, never a sole cause, and would resist any claim that one technology built the machine ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-introduction]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch05-permanent-renaissance-1]]). Her substitution of a traceable communications shift for the label “modernity” resonates with WMP's habit of naming infrastructure rather than zeitgeist, a structural rather than doctrinal resonance ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-conclusion-1]]).
- **[[Legibility]] and [[legibility-as-precondition-not-cause]].** Standardized maps, calendars, indexes and costume books read as legibility avant la lettre. Yet her own material shows this infrastructure doing divergent work, which supports the precondition-not-cause reading rather than a determinist one ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s2-standardization]]).
- **[[history-machine]].** Several notes see a partial rhyme: a revolution that assembled silently in workshops, whose preconditions had been “incubating” for a century before locking together, and which was a “business of time” no one agent authored. Eisenstein's version is a historian's multivariable causal analysis, not a claim about paradigm-level shock ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch01-unacknowledged-revolution]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-2]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-3]]).
- **[[world-machines]].** Her refusal to resolve standing contradictions half-rhymes with eras defined by unresolved friction. Her contradictions are two effects of one process, though, not three coexisting machines ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch08-final-remarks]]).
- **[[benedict-anderson]] and [[nationalism]].** She supplies an earlier, more mechanical layer beneath print capitalism (differential repetition, printers' choices of which vernacular to typeset, nationalized Scriptures). She also complicates it: nation-building “worked at cross purposes” with print's early expansion ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s6-amplification-reinforcement]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-afterword-2]]).
- **[[print-culture-and-the-individual-author]].** Fixity is what produces authorship, plagiarism and copyright, and the professional man of letters is its social class ([[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch03-s5-preservative-powers]], [[eisenstein-printing-revolution-ch04-s2-men-of-letters]]).
