# Candide, or, Optimism (Voltaire, 1759)

Voltaire's *Candide, or, Optimism* (1759), read here in the Henry Morley translation (Barnes & Noble Classics, 2003). The edition's introduction and notes were not read; everything below comes from the thirty chapters as the reading notes record them. It is a short picaresque satire in which a simple-minded young man, taught by his tutor Pangloss that this is the best of all possible worlds, is driven across Europe, South America and the Ottoman Mediterranean through war, earthquake, the Inquisition, slavery and swindles, until the surviving company settles on a small farm near Constantinople.

The notes keep one rule throughout, and this page follows it: *Candide* is a satire with an ironic narrator, so each claim belongs to whoever voices it (Pangloss, Martin, the old woman, Cacambo, a passing character, or the narrator's deadpan). Readings of what the satire targets are the notes' interpretations, not statements the text makes.

## The arc by episode

**Westphalia and the Bulgarians (Ch. I–II).** In the castle of Thunder-ten-tronckh, Pangloss teaches "metaphysico-theologo-cosmolonigology": noses exist for spectacles, so the baron's castle is the best of castles. Candide is kicked out for kissing Cunégonde, toasted into the Bulgarian army by recruiters who talk Pangloss-like mutual aid, flogged, and pardoned just before being shot ([[voltaire-candide-ch01-02-castle-and-bulgarians]]).

**War, Holland and the diseased tutor (III–IV).** A battle narrated in optimist vocabulary; a Dutch orator on charity refuses Candide bread because he will not call the Pope Antichrist; the Anabaptist James takes him in. Pangloss reappears ruined by syphilis, traces it through a chain of lovers to Columbus's crew and justifies it by chocolate and cochineal, while James answers that people "were not born wolves" ([[voltaire-candide-ch03-04-war-and-pangloss-found]]).

**Lisbon (V–VI).** James drowns while Pangloss proves a priori that the coast was made for it; the earthquake; the University of Coimbra's finding that slow burning prevents earthquakes; Pangloss hanged, Candide flogged, and Candide's first doubt: "If this is the best of all possible worlds, what are the others like?" ([[voltaire-candide-ch05-06-lisbon-earthquake-auto-da-fe]]).

**Cunégonde, the Inquisitor, Cadiz (VII–X).** An old woman nurses Candide and brings him to Cunégonde, alive, who has been raped, sold, and shared on a weekly schedule between the banker Don Issachar and the Grand Inquisitor; she concludes that Pangloss "deceived me most cruelly" ([[voltaire-candide-ch07-08-cunegonde-found]]). Candide kills both men; the three flee to Cadiz, lose their jewels to a Cordelier, and sail for the New World arguing over who has suffered most ([[voltaire-candide-ch09-10-inquisitor-and-cadiz]]).

**The old woman's story (XI–XII).** A pope's daughter, she tells of a poisoned fiancé, pirates, rape, Morocco's civil wars, the plague at Algiers, sale after sale, and the siege of Azov, where the janissaries cut a buttock from each woman on an imam's advice. She ends by wagering that every passenger has cursed his existence ([[voltaire-candide-ch11-old-womans-history]], [[voltaire-candide-ch12-old-womans-adventures]]).

**Buenos Ayres, Paraguay, the Oreillons (XIII–XVI).** The Governor claims Cunégonde; pursued for the Inquisitor's murder, Candide flees with his valet Cacambo to the Jesuits of Paraguay, whose commandant proves to be Cunégonde's brother. When Candide says he will marry her, the Baron cites her "seventy-two quarterings" and strikes him, and Candide runs him through. Captured by the Oreillons, who want to eat a Jesuit, they are freed by Cacambo's appeal to the law of nature ([[voltaire-candide-ch13-14-buenos-aires-and-paraguay]], [[voltaire-candide-ch15-16-baron-killed-and-oreillons]]).

**El Dorado (XVII–XVIII).** A mountain-walled country where children play quoits with gold and gems, inns are free, and there are no courts, prisons or monks but a palace of sciences. Candide and Cacambo leave by their own choice with a hundred and two sheep laden with "pebbles" ([[voltaire-candide-ch17-arrival-in-el-dorado]], [[voltaire-candide-ch18-el-dorado]]; see [[el-dorado-voltaire]]).

**Surinam and Martin (XIX–XXI).** A mutilated slave tells Candide "it is at this cost that you eat sugar in Europe", and Candide renounces optimism, defining it as "the obstinacy of maintaining that everything is best when it is worst". Robbed by the skipper Vanderdendur and fined by a magistrate, he advertises for the most miserable man in the province and takes a persecuted scholar, Martin, who at sea declares himself a Manichaean ([[voltaire-candide-ch19-surinam-and-martin]], [[voltaire-candide-ch20-21-at-sea-with-martin]]).

**Paris (XXII).** Physicians, false friends and a priest swarm the rich stranger; a pamphleteering wit at the theatre; a gaming house; a salon supper; a Marchioness and an Abbé who extract his diamonds with a forged Cunégonde; an arrest bought off with more diamonds ([[voltaire-candide-ch22-paris-1]], [[voltaire-candide-ch22-paris-2]]).

**Portsmouth and Venice (XXIII–XXVI).** An admiral shot "to inspire the others to fight"; in Venice the cheerful-looking Pacquette and Friar Giroflée turn out to be wretched; the senator Pococuranté is bored by everything he owns; six dethroned kings are in Venice "to spend the Carnival", and Cacambo reappears as a slave ([[voltaire-candide-ch23-24-portsmouth-paquette-giroflee]], [[voltaire-candide-ch25-pococurante]], [[voltaire-candide-ch26-six-kings]]).

**Constantinople and the reunion (XXVII–XXIX).** Cunégonde is a dishwasher and has grown ugly; two beaten galley slaves are the Baron and Pangloss, both alive. Ransomed, Pangloss says he holds his first opinion, "since Leibniz could not be wrong". The Baron again refuses the marriage on grounds of rank ([[voltaire-candide-ch27-voyage-to-constantinople]], [[voltaire-candide-ch28-29-reunion]]).

**The farm (XXX).** See *The ending* below ([[voltaire-candide-ch30-conclusion-garden]]).

## Recurring satirical targets (the notes' readings)

- **Optimism as a system that explains harm away.** The core target in every note: Pangloss's reasoning from effect to purpose absorbs each disaster. The notes read its target as Leibniz-style theodicy, by interpretation; the one place they record Leibniz named is Pangloss's own line in Ch. XXVIII. See [[panglossian-optimism-voltaire]] and [[pangloss]].
- **Church and Inquisition.** The Coimbra auto-da-fé, the Grand Inquisitor's share of Cunégonde, the thieving Cordelier, the Inquisitor's magnificent burial beside Issachar's dunghill, Jesuits who (in Cacambo's account) fight Spain and Portugal in America while confessing their kings in Europe, a parish priest's "note payable to the bearer in the other world", Giroflée's forced vows. The notes mostly mark the anti-clerical reading as interpretation of what the narrator's flat tone shows.
- **Rationales for cruelty beyond Pangloss.** The imam's "charitable" sermon, the surgeon's "laws of war", the admiral shot because it is "necessary" now and then, the prize-winning proof of why the sheep must be red; see [[institutional-theodicies-candide]].
- **War.** Drill and counted blows in the Bulgarian army, a battle of "heroic butchery", Morocco's civil wars fought without missing a prayer time, Martin's "million regimented assassins".
- **Aristocratic pride.** The baron's castle with "not only a gate, but even windows", quarterings counted and refused, the Governor's string of names, and the Baron's refusal, which survives massacre, priesthood and the galley unchanged.
- **Colonial cruelty and greed.** The Surinam slave, Vanderdendur's swindle, the arbour where the Jesuit commandant eats from gold while Paraguayans eat corn in the sun, and the El Dorado elder's conviction that Europeans would "murder us all to the very last man" for the gold.
- **Venal society.** Paris's predators and salon judges, and an officer who arrests "suspected foreigners" and takes a bribe in one breath.

## The voices

- **Pangloss** states the doctrine and keeps restating it, through syphilis, hanging and the galleys ([[pangloss]]).
- **Martin**, the Manichaean, answers with an equally total pessimism; after fifteen days at sea the two are "just as far advanced as the first moment they began", and the notes read the book as letting neither win ([[martin-voltaire]]).
- **The old woman** narrates atrocity as "trifles", ranks miseries, and calls the clinging to life "perhaps one of our worst instincts" ([[the-old-woman-candide]]).
- **Cacambo** is the practical, cheerful opportunist who keeps the party alive by talking ([[cacambo]]).
- **Cunégonde** gives the earliest plain rejection of the doctrine from a victim's mouth, narrates her own ruin with ironic piety, and ends as a sun-burnt, headstrong wife whom Candide marries out of obligation.
- **Candide** oscillates. Doubt grows with suffering (the Lisbon lament; at the Oreillons' cauldron, "Everything is right. It may be so: but I must confess it seems harsh"; the Surinam renunciation) and recedes with comfort (leaning to Pangloss's doctrine "especially after meal times"; taking the six kings as proof that "All is for the best").
- **The narrator's irony** is the book's main instrument: deadpan counting, mock praise ("a prince of great genius"), bathetic juxtaposition. The notes repeatedly caution that it yields no stated thesis.

## The ending

On the farm the household settles into disappointment and boredom. The old woman sets every misery they have suffered against "remain here doing nothing" and asks which is worse; Martin answers that people live in convulsions of misery or the lethargy of boredom. A dervish shuts the door on Pangloss's questions about the origin of evil; an old Turk who never learned the names of mufti or vizier works twenty acres with his children and says labour keeps off "boredom, vice, and want". Candide's "we must cultivate our garden" is a brief, undefended remark: Pangloss absorbs it into his system (Adam was put in Eden to work), Martin takes it as the "only way to make life bearable". Pangloss, who by now "maintained it, and at the same time didn't believe it all", still recites the chain of events that led to "preserved citrons and pistachio-nuts", and Candide replies "Excellently observed… but we must cultivate our garden." The note reads this as a courteous stalemate between a system that explains everything and a practice that does not try, and leaves the garden's meaning open ([[voltaire-candide-ch30-conclusion-garden]]).

## World Machines resonances (hedged)

These are the notes' glosses, and several warn that the book is a farce and a satire of optimism, "not a theory of institutions or world machines".

- **Explanation that ratifies the order of things.** The Ch. I–II note observes that Pangloss's doctrine is "a social ratifier: the existing hierarchy is declared the best end." That is the clearest bridge to [[panglossian-optimism]] as the vault uses it, and to Lohse's essay [[the-best-of-all-possible-worlds]], which uses *Candide* as a lens on techno-optimism.
- **Legibility, each time marked as a stretch.** Status registered by counting quarterings ("a faint rhyme" with [[modernity-machine]], "though nothing in the text develops that"); Paris reading strangers by their visible signs ("a stretch, as the text is a farce"); cheerful surfaces hiding misery ("Voltaire's point is moral, not about measurement"); Martin asking what "scales" could weigh sufferings ("the text itself makes no such claim"). See [[Legibility]].
- **Institutions that legitimize extraction by ritual.** The Inquisitor-and-banker episode is offered as "only a loose rhyme" with [[world-machines]], "not an argument the text makes" ([[voltaire-candide-ch07-08-cunegonde-found]]).
- **Whether human nature changes.** Martin's hawks against Candide's interrupted "free will—" resonate "loosely" with the [[argument-of-progress]], though the text says nothing about progress and the connection is only analogical.
- **The colonial Atlantic.** Surinam's sugar, Jesuit Paraguay and El Dorado touch [[mann-1493]], [[columbian-exchange-mann]] and [[grove-green-imperialism]] only loosely: the novel gives "no systemic analysis, only a single exemplary body".
- **Utopia.** El Dorado sits in the tradition of [[more-utopia]], comic where More's is argued ([[el-dorado-voltaire]]).
