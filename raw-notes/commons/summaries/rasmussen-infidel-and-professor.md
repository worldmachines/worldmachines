# The Infidel and the Professor (Dennis C. Rasmussen, 2017)

**Author**: Dennis C. Rasmussen
**Publisher**: Princeton University Press, 2017
**Subject**: The twenty-seven-year friendship of [[david-hume]] (1711–1776) and [[adam-smith]] (1723–1790), from their probable first meeting in autumn 1749 to Hume's death on 25 August 1776, and its afterlife in Smith's last revisions.

## What the Book Is

Rasmussen writes a dual biography organized not around two bodies of thought but around the relationship between them. The stated premise is that the bond between "the greatest philosopher ever to write in the English language" and "history's most famous theorist of commercial society" is arguably the greatest philosophical friendship in the Western tradition — and that, unusually, it is recoverable. Fifty-six surviving letters between the two men (out of roughly one hundred seventy Smith exchanged with anyone in that period), Hume's autobiography *My Own Life*, Smith's *Letter to Strahan*, and testimony from Dugald Stewart, James Boswell, and the Edinburgh Moderates give enough material to narrate influence as it was actually transmitted: through commentary on manuscripts, career-boosting, shared clubs, consolation in crisis, and running jokes.

Both men worked against exactly this book — Hume feared his letters falling into "idle People's hands," Smith suppressed the publication of Hume's correspondence and had most of his own papers burned — and Rasmussen reads that reticence as itself evidence of how much both staked on reputation and posterity.

The organizing tension, named in the preface and never dropped, is that the two held **broadly similar private views but adopted very different public postures** on religion. The title stages this as infidel versus professor.

## The Two Lives Before the Friendship

Rasmussen builds the parallel deliberately. Both men grew up fatherless, raised by devout Presbyterian mothers; both were effectively only children; neither married.

**Hume** (born 1711 at Ninewells) lost his faith young — involuntarily and cyclically, and, ironically, by reading Locke's and Clarke's *defenses* of theism. He set out in *A Treatise of Human Nature* to "march up directly to the capital or center of" the sciences — human nature itself — and the book "fell dead-born from the press." He pivoted to essays, was rejected in 1745 for the Edinburgh chair of Ethics and Pneumatical Philosophy over "Scepticism, Heterodoxy & other hard Names" (his friend Francis Hutcheson among the opponents), tutored an insane marquess, served twice as a military secretary, and wrote the *Philosophical Essays* (the First Enquiry) with its arguments against miracles and against design left uncastrated and published under his own name.

**Smith** (born 1723 in Kirkcaldy) studied at Glasgow under Hutcheson, then endured six years at Balliol whose dons had "given up altogether even the pretence of teaching" — and who confiscated his copy of Hume's *Treatise*. That confiscation is the narrative hinge: the two men's first contact was textual, years before they met. Smith's earliest surviving work, the astronomy essay of *The Principles Which Lead and Direct Philosophical Enquiries*, already argues that science and religion alike are the imagination's devices for soothing the discomfort of the unexplained — anticipating Hume's *Natural History of Religion* by several years without having read it.

## The Friendship's Infrastructure

Glasgow and Edinburgh are thirteen coach-hours apart, and Hume claimed a near-phobic dread of the short sail across the Firth of Forth ("mortally sick at Sea... a kind of hydrophobia"). The friendship therefore ran on institutions and paper: the Advocates' Library, the Literary Society of Glasgow, the Select Society founded in 1754 (Hume as treasurer, Smith explaining its charter at the first meeting), the later Poker Club, the Paris salons of the Comtesse de Boufflers and d'Holbach, correspondence with Benjamin Franklin — and above all the letters, whose dominant register is teasing reproach about visits not made. The surviving record of their actual meetings is thin enough that most of the intimacy has to be inferred from the mail. See [[scottish-enlightenment]] for the wider institutional setting.

## The Intellectual Traffic

Rasmussen's structural claim, borrowed from Samuel Fleischacker, is that Smith's thought "circles around" Hume's — indebted almost everywhere, revising incessantly.

- **Into the lecture room.** Smith taught Hume's freshly published *Political Discourses* at Glasgow almost verbatim: the specie-flow mechanism, free trade, and the demolition of social-contract theory (down to the impressed sailor who cannot be said to have consented to his own kidnapping), reproduced "blow by blow" by a student note-taker.
- **Into *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759).** Smith never names Hume, but the book is a sustained dialogue with him, diverging on four points: sympathy as active imaginative *projection* rather than passive *contagion*; the appearance of "fitness" being valued above the end it serves; justice as a *natural* virtue grounded in spontaneous resentment rather than an "artificial" one grounded in reflected utility; and a sympathetic-but-non-foundational treatment of religion against Hume's view of it as almost wholly pernicious. Hume's response — a comic letter piling up delays before revealing the book was already a hit, plus an anonymous laudatory review that quietly flags his disagreements — shows the friendship functioning as real philosophical collaboration.
- **Into *The Wealth of Nations* (1776).** Rasmussen nominates Book 3's claim that commerce "introduced order and good government, and with them, the liberty and security of individuals" as the book's single most important passage — and Smith immediately credits Hume as the only writer to have noticed it. The underlying narrative (feudal lords dissipating their power on "trinkets and baubles" until they could no longer maintain armed dependents) comes near-intact from Hume's *Political Discourses* and *History of England*. Anti-mercantilism and free trade were already Hume's causes.
- **Where Smith broke from Hume.** Smith catalogued commercial society's costs that Hume waved away — merchants' "mean rapacity," the mental "torpor" bred by extreme division of labor, and the structural inequality that corrupts moral sentiment by teaching people to admire the rich and neglect the poor. And on religion Smith was the *more* radical: against Hume's cynical defense of an established church (a subsidized clergy can be bribed into indolence), Smith argued for full disestablishment and competition among sects — a genuine outlier position in 1776.

## The Religion Problem

Hume never softened. The Kirk's Popular Party, led by George Anderson, tried repeatedly between 1755 and 1757 to have the General Assembly excommunicate him on six enumerated heresies; Rasmussen judges the charges a largely fair summary of his actual views, and notes the real irony — excommunication carried no civil penalty and would only have martyred him, so the exposed parties were the Moderate ministers (Blair, Robertson, Carlyle) who socialized with an avowed skeptic. Hume's response was to publish the *Natural History of Religion* in 1757, and privately to ask Blair simply to stop discussing religion with him so the friendship could continue on other terms.

Smith never declared. His Glasgow natural theology was thin enough to draw suspicion that his "principles were not sound"; his astronomy essay went unpublished until after his death; his God appears in *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* hedged with "circumlocutions... and frequent use of the verb 'to seem.'"

The book's most argued-over episode tests the difference. Hume's January 1776 will left Smith all his manuscripts, two hundred pounds, and a request to publish the *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*; Smith refused, repeatedly. Rasmussen argues against the standard reading of a rift: Hume's letters stayed as affectionate as ever, Smith called the work "finely written" and circulated it privately, and every other friend counseled against publication too. The sharper puzzle, he suggests, is the reverse one — why the dying Hume abruptly reversed twenty-five years of his own caution and tried to hand the risk to his closest friend weeks after *The Wealth of Nations* appeared. The 1779 reception, uniformly scandalized, vindicated the caution. See [[calibrated-candor]] for the pattern this episode belongs to.

## The Death and the Backlash

Hume's terminal illness (probably colon cancer) made his dying a public test: would the famous infidel flinch? He was determined, in Rasmussen's phrase, "to die as he thought a philosopher should — cheerfully and tranquilly, without undue hopes or fears — and to do it for a public audience." He wrote *My Own Life*, a spare ten-page self-portrait that omits both his intimate life and every professional cost of his irreligion; he parried Boswell, who reported against his own hopes "a tranquillity of mind and a clearness of head which few men possess at any time"; and he joked about having no excuse to offer Charon for a delay. He died on 25 August 1776 in what Joseph Black called "such a happy composure of mind that nothing could have made it better."

Smith completed the record with the *Letter to Strahan*, which uses the word "friend" seventeen times and closes by calling Hume as near "to the idea of a perfectly wise and virtuous man, as perhaps the nature of human frailty will permit" — a deliberate echo of Phaedo on Socrates. That sentence, not skepticism in the abstract, was the trigger. Horne, Johnson, Boswell, Temple, Burke and (a decade later) Wesley all answered; the rumor that Hume secretly died in anguish persisted into the 1850s. Smith reckoned this "harmless Sheet of paper" brought him "ten times more abuse" than his attack on "the whole commercial system of Great Britain." He never retracted, never explained, and never replied.

## Afterlife

Smith moved to Edinburgh — the move Hume had spent twenty years urging — took a customs commissionership (history's most celebrated free-trader collecting tariffs), and revised. Rasmussen's closing argument is that the 1790 sixth edition of *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, finished months before Smith's death, is an oblique elegy: its wholly new Part 6 tracks Hume's positions on faction, commercial virtue, and suicide; it replaces the doctrine of atonement with the Tartarus/Elysium passage that contemporaries read as proof of "the infection of David Hume's society"; its crowning virtue, self-command, is illustrated by a man who "upon the approach of death, preserves his tranquillity unaltered"; and its new discussion of friendship distinguishes natural sympathy from the "constrained sympathy" of relatives and "partners in trade." D. D. Raphael's phrase for the whole is "a libation to Hume's ghost." Smith died in 1790 to near-silence — a final asymmetry in a book built on quiet, distributed influence.

## World Machines Resonances

- **Distributed assembly.** The introduction's account of the Scottish Enlightenment refuses any great-man explanation, attributing it instead to a convergence of parish schools, reformed universities, clubs, a publishing industry, the 1707 union, and Moderate clergy — a paradigm assembling from independent components and then lurching into visibility as a named era, in the manner of a [[history-machine]].
- **A machine dissolved by another.** Smith and Hume's shared account of commerce dissolving feudal dependency by "silent and insensible operation" — no force, no design, visible only in hindsight — is a compact theory of one deep institutional order being displaced by a rival infrastructure, and their brief for commerce, luxury, and the "middling rank of men" is a foundational statement of [[modernity-machine]] optimism. Compare [[the-relentless-revolution]] on capitalism as contingent rather than inevitable.
- **Interregnum.** Hume's *History of England* locates liberty's survival in an accident — Puritan fanatics feared God more than any king, and so kept "the precious spark of liberty" alive — with a balanced constitution emerging "only from the resulting ashes" of civil war: an interregnum between a dying absolutist legitimacy and an unborn constitutional one, in the shape of a [[gramsci-gap]].
- **A receding container, defended.** The backlash against the *Letter to Strahan* is an old legitimating order attacking not an argument but an exemplar, at the moment its monopoly on the good life was publicly contested — the register in which [[receding-containers]] describes containers thinning rather than breaking.
- **Rao's frame, complicated.** [[venkatesh-rao-the-divergence-machine-ii]] (and, following it, [[david-hume]]) reads Hume and Smith as collaborating allies advancing the [[argument-of-progress]] on different frontiers. Rasmussen's granular record supports the alliance but corrects its texture: Smith revised Hume incessantly, sometimes for being insufficiently Humean, and dissented sharply on commercial society's costs.
