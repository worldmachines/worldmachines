# The Bourgeois Experience, Vol. V: Pleasure Wars

**Author**: Peter Gay (see [[peter-gay]])
**Published**: 1998. This is the fifth and final volume of *The Bourgeois Experience: Victoria to Freud* (see [[the-bourgeois-experience]]). It ends with a Coda that serves as Gay's apologia for the whole series ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-coda-a-bourgeois-experience]]).

## The Argument

Volume V is about taste. It asks how the nineteenth-century bourgeoisie paid for high culture, argued about it, collected it, turned it into institutions, and finally met modernism. Its target is what Gay calls the "modernist myth": the story of noble avant-gardes defending "honest art" against a philistine, money-loving middle class. He calls that story "self-serving and partisan," with only "a measure of truth" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-bexp-s1-flaubertus]]).

Every chapter restates his counter-thesis in some form:

- The bourgeoisie was "a deeply cleft class." Most of it barely noticed the pleasure wars, and an active minority fought them ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s1-a-profusion-of-alternatives]]).
- The fiercest taste battles ran *inside* the bourgeoisie. Gay's term for this is "intrabourgeois conflict" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch05-s3-the-perspiring-philistine-on-trial]]).
- "The middle classes ... nourished their sworn enemies," because "avant-gardes could not have made their way without massive bourgeois patronage" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s1-a-profusion-of-alternatives]]).

The Coda generalizes the point to the whole series. The philistine who "loved money and hated art" is not a neutral historical finding. It is what remains of a polemical tradition that later historians took literally ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-coda-a-bourgeois-experience]]).

## Method

Gay works with two tools throughout.

- **He checks what people said about themselves against the record.** Flaubert's "bourgeois" is tested against the stratified real bourgeoisie of Rouen and against the cotton magnate Depeaux's collection of Impressionists ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-bexp-s1-flaubertus]]). Hanslick's claim that the Vienna Musikfreunde were "purely bourgeois" is tested against its archducal protectors and subsidies ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch02-s2-majestic-dispensations]]). Morris's war on philistinism is tested against what his products cost ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s3-limits-and-possibilities]]).
- **He reads taste psychoanalytically, as mixed feelings.** Flaubert's phobia is a defense against recognizing himself, sharpened by the "narcissism of minor difference" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-bexp-s1-flaubertus]]). Taste is never "wholly free," and aesthetic pleasure is conservative by nature ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch03-s1-guides-to-pleasure]]). Collecting is sublimated eros, "a kind of love" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s1-a-map-of-motives]]).

Gay calls himself "a historian informed by psychoanalysis," not a psychohistorian ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-coda-a-bourgeois-experience]]).

The Introduction lays the groundwork in two steps. First, bourgeois identity is an ideology of the "middling" sort rather than a census category ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-intro-a]]). Second, bourgeois selfhood rested on managed anxiety and a "well-fashioned will," and the word "Victorian" itself turned from compliment into insult ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-intro-b]]).

## Throughlines

### Bourgeoisophobia and its limits
The book's framing theme is the artists' crusade against the bourgeois public. Gay borrows its name from Flaubert's self-signature "Bourgeoisophobus." He traces it from Werther through Nietzsche to the modernists, and then takes it apart as a curse word, a screen and a defense mechanism ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-bexp-s1-flaubertus]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-bexp-s2-six-pack-philistines]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-bexp-s3-confusing-confrontations]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-coda-a-bourgeois-experience]]). See [[bourgeoisophobia]].

### The political economy of art
Gay borrows Ruskin's phrase and turns it into a literal ledger. He sets German, British and French income ladders against the prices of paintings, opera seats and serial novels. His governing distinction is that money decided *access* but did not decide *taste*: "it would be naive to equate money with taste" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s1-battles-of-the-budgets]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s2-affordable-masterpieces]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s3-limits-and-possibilities]]). Chapter Two adds a geography. Commercial Manchester, courtly Munich and state-run Paris each produced a different bourgeois culture, and "definitions of bourgeois culture ... must start from scratch city by city, almost decade by decade" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch02-s1-dividends-from-commerce]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch02-s2-majestic-dispensations]]). See [[political-economy-of-art-gay]].

### Critics as legislators
The professional critic rises to become the century's chief guide to taste. Borrowing Shelley's formula, critics called themselves "the acknowledged legislators of taste," and they also confessed they had no lasting effect. Gay reads their clashing verdicts as freeing the bourgeois public rather than dominating it ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch03-s1-guides-to-pleasure]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch03-s2-master-craftsmen]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch03-s3-from-zola-to-wilde]]). See [[critics-as-legislators-of-taste]].

### Collectors, patrons and founders
Collecting is read as autobiography and as compulsion. Patronage shades into a recurring "education to modernism," in which "the customer had become a patron." Private fortunes became museums and orchestras, many of which enshrined safe taste rather than new art ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s1-a-map-of-motives]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s2-collecting-as-autobiography]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s3-the-victorian-maecenas]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch05-s1-founders]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch05-s2-buttresses-for-the-establishment]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch05-s3-the-perspiring-philistine-on-trial]]). See [[victorian-collectors-and-founders]].

### Modernism as a moving frontier
Modernism arrives as a "profusion" of competing isms rather than a single revolution. Its landmark works provoked riots. Conservative taste tamed it by reproducing it as something pretty ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s1-a-profusion-of-alternatives]]). Its factions fought one another as hard as they fought the bourgeoisie: "One generation's unconventionality was the next generation's conformism," and in Liebermann's words, "No one can leap over his shadow" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s2-modernists-vs-modernists]]). Gay's explanation has several causes: spare income, cities, quasi-religious fervor and political extremism, all resting on a Freudian substrate of ambivalence ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s3-causes-and-effects]]). Franz Marc's lament that "there is no style left" is, for Gay, a misreading. The real problem was that there were so many styles ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s4-against-the-need-for-probability]]).

## In World Machines Terms

The notes test several WMP concepts against the text and hedge each one.

- **[[gramsci-gap]], four times and always qualified.**
  - Mill's 1831 remark that mankind has "outgrown old institutions ... and not yet acquired new ones" has the phenomenology of an interregnum. It is not a world-machine transition ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-intro-a]]).
  - When the "dictatorship over taste" fell, a gap opened at the scale of one domain. It closed almost at once, and money filled it ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s1-battles-of-the-budgets]]).
  - Modernism's riots are an aesthetic interregnum, "far shorter, more reversible" than a civilizational one ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s1-a-profusion-of-alternatives]]).
  - Late-century anxiety is diffuse and internal to one era's Day, not the gap between two machines ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s3-causes-and-effects]]).
- **[[Legibility]] and [[legibility-as-machine-core]], loosely.**
  - Contemporaries kept inventing new words to sort the bourgeoisie, and the category would not hold still. This was legibility-seeking that was "contested and multi-authored rather than administratively imposed" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-intro-a]]).
  - Weber's iron cage of timetables is the same phenomenon WMP names, seen through a psychological and moral frame rather than an institutional one ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-intro-b]]).
  - Munich's ticket ladders sorted the public by income. That is commercial price discrimination, not state legibility ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch01-s2-affordable-masterpieces]]).
- **[[modernity-machine]] and [[divergence-machine]].** Munich's single-willed, court-directed order set against Manchester's competing private societies is "a miniature machine contest," though Gay himself calls the two cities "extreme types" that interpenetrate ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch02-s2-majestic-dispensations]]). Modernism's proliferation rhymes with the divergence machine. But the isms compete inside one contested field over a few decades, which is not a civilizational push machine ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s2-modernists-vs-modernists]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s4-against-the-need-for-probability]]).
- **[[history-machine]].** Bourgeois cultural institutions assembled piecemeal and independently in city after city. Gay's own narration, meanwhile, leans on great men ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch02-s1-dividends-from-commerce]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch02-s2-majestic-dispensations]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch05-s1-founders]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch05-s2-buttresses-for-the-establishment]]).
- **[[decay-sequence]].** In 1905 Gerald Cumberland attacked the Hallé's staple oratorios: "They are no longer musical works; they have become religious services." This is functional practice hollowing into ritual within two generations ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch02-s1-dividends-from-commerce]]).
- **[[carey-intellectuals-and-masses]] and [[the-mass-as-fiction]].** These are the most frequent comparison in the batch, and they are always drawn with explicit hedges. See [[the-philistine-bourgeois-as-fiction]].

## Reading Cautions

- **Gay's revisionism is advocacy as well as finding.** He concedes the cases where the caricature fits: Carnegie's indiscriminate gallery, the Vanderbilts' "competitive ostentation," the stuffed horse at Krupp's Villa Hügel ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch04-s2-collecting-as-autobiography]]). He also concedes the donors who made safe, "riskless" taste permanent ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch05-s2-buttresses-for-the-establishment]]).
- **Bourgeoisophobia turned ugly.** It absorbed anti-Semitism, with "the modern Jew" cast as "bourgeoisdom incarnate" ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-bexp-s2-six-pack-philistines]]). The Dreyfus affair split modernist circles, and Degas and Renoir sided with prejudice ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s3-causes-and-effects]]).
- **The subject is an active minority.** Most bourgeois kept breeding pigeons and reading family weeklies ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch06-s1-a-profusion-of-alternatives]]).
- **Method and evidence pull apart.** Gay narrates through kings and named patrons, while his comparative evidence points to distributed assembly ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch02-s2-majestic-dispensations]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v5-ch05-s1-founders]]).
