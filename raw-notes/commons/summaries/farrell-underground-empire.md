# Underground Empire

*Underground Empire: How America Weaponized the World Economy* (Henry Holt / Allen Lane, 2023), by political scientists Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman, is an account of how the infrastructure of globalisation became an apparatus of American coercion — not by design, but by accretion.

The book's governing image is Rome. Beneath the visible monuments of American power — the White House, the Pentagon, Foggy Bottom — runs a hidden system of fiber-optic backbones, bank-messaging networks, dollar-clearing arrangements, and chip supply chains that functions the way Rome's roads and aqueducts once did: built for commerce, usable for domination. "The plumbing is political." These "subterranean machineries" are not neutral technical substrate; a state sitting at their centre can turn them into tools of coercion.

## The argument by negation

Farrell and Newman stage their thesis against two mirror-image errors. Vladimir Putin's claim that the Internet was always a "CIA project" sees deliberate plot everywhere. Thomas Friedman's flat world — the Web replacing the Wall, "an emporium, not an imperium," reinforced by Bill Clinton's "Jell-O" line and James Carville's wish to be reincarnated as the bond market — sees the end of power politics altogether. Both are rejected. The truth is more mundane and more interesting: **no one planned the underground empire.** Private firms built the networks pursuing efficiency and profit; U.S. officials "believed that their job was to get out of the way." Power accrued as an unintended by-product of infrastructure that happened to route through American territory. "Governments, too, can follow paths without anticipating where they lead."

This makes the book a study of drift rather than conspiracy — and, in [[world-machines]] terms, a case study in how a paradigm assembles out of independent components until observers realise all at once that the pieces were always one system. See [[history-machine]].

## Structure

**[[farrell-underground-empire-introduction]]** — the underground-empire thesis, the two false narratives, and the path-dependence claim: by 2002 less than 1 percent of global Internet bandwidth moved between two non-U.S. regions without transiting the United States; SWIFT's board was U.S.-bank-dominated with a data centre in Northern Virginia; international dollar transactions ran through U.S.-controlled dollar clearing. A network order meant to transcend geopolitics instead "froze a moment in time" — the peak of American Cold War victory — and preserved it past its justification.

**[[farrell-underground-empire-ch01-wriston-world]]** — the paradox of origins, told through the Citibank chairman who believed information technology and mobile capital would dissolve state power. Four infrastructures, one arc each: the Eurodollar market, the SWIFT/MARTI standardisation fight, the physical Internet at Ashburn, and the fabless/foundry split in semiconductors. Each grows decentralised and ungovernable, then collapses into a handful of critical nodes. See [[walter-wriston]].

**[[farrell-underground-empire-ch02-stormbrew-map]]** — the weaponisation itself, braided in two strands: Michael Hayden's NSA exploiting fiber chokepoints after 9/11 (STORMBREW, FAIRVIEW, PRISM, Mark Klein's AT&T room 641A), and Treasury officials — David Aufhauser, Stuart Levey, Juan Zarate — discovering that SWIFT data and dollar clearing could freeze and excommunicate entire countries. Banco Delta Asia (2005) is the proof of concept; Iran (2006–2015) the full-scale test.

**[[farrell-underground-empire-ch03-war-without-gunsmoke]]** — Huawei and Meng Wanzhou as the paradigm case against a single firm: bank fraud against a dollar-dependent HSBC as the legal wedge, then the 2020 foreign-produced direct product rule as the kill shot. Huawei's global phone share fell from 20 percent to 4 percent within a year. The chapter's second half catalogues China's structurally weaker countermeasures.

**[[farrell-underground-empire-ch04-waking-into-winter]]** — the EU's founding creed of peace through trade collapsing in stages, from Trump's destruction of the JCPOA through Nord Stream 2 to the February 2022 sanctions, and the discovery that new capacity revealed new dependency.

**[[farrell-underground-empire-ch05-hooks-captain]]** — the view from inside the firms: a coerced tanker captain, Microsoft, TSMC, and the crypto ecosystem, each losing the neutral ground it had assumed.

**[[farrell-underground-empire-ch06-empire-of-wind-and-light]]** — the survey and the prescription: a digital-currency arms race, a catalogue of the improvisations that hardened into structure, and a closing argument for Schelling-style strategic frameworks over unbounded rivalry.

## Throughlines

**Legibility precedes coercion.** The book's operative sequence is always the same: render a commercial network visible — find the chokepoints, the captains, the insurers, the correspondent accounts — and only then squeeze. Brian Hook "mapping out … the pressure points" of Iran's oil shipping is the miniature; the STORMBREW slide is the emblem. The chapter on the NSA opens not with a program but with Pope Alexander VI's 1493 bull and John Dee's "iust Arthurien clayme," arguing that maps do not describe territory so much as manufacture a basis for dominion over it (see [[maps-as-technologies-of-power]]). This is [[legibility-as-machine-core]] operating as covert empire rather than public bureaucracy — the [[modernity-machine]]'s signature operation performed on flows that were supposed to have escaped it.

**Compliance, not command, is the mechanism.** The empire runs on regulated firms choosing — or being made — to cooperate. AT&T's "extreme willingness" to build room 641A; Yahoo! capitulating under a $250,000-a-day fine threat; SWIFT's Lenny Schrank going from "We don't do subpoena" to "What took you so long?"; HSBC cooperating against Huawei because a deferred-prosecution agreement had already put its dollar access at risk. In every case the lever is commercial dependency on U.S. financial or market access, not a warrant. See [[infrastructure-as-political]].

**Precedent is the propagation mechanism.** "Whenever a new use was discovered, it created a possible precedent for others." Banco Delta Asia's $25 million freeze produced a bank run and made North Korean money globally toxic — an effect officials themselves had not anticipated — and inadvertently modelled the technique later used to sever Iran. Once-off emergency measures hardened into standing policy. Christopher Hill's phrase for the result: a "sanctions doomsday machine that could not be turned back off."

**Economic scale is not network power.** The book's sharpest analytic claim is the asymmetry between the two. "Judged by the size of its economy, China was a global power. Judged by its influence over global economic networks, it was at best an also-ran." CIPS processes a fraction of SWIFT's traffic; China's market-access weapons (Norwegian salmon, Australian coal, NBA broadcasts) are self-limiting, hurting China nearly as much as the target. When the U.S. sanctioned Carrie Lam she had to be paid in literal cash; when China sanctioned Gayle Manchin she was merely flattered. In the terms of [[iemp-model]], the book is an argument that economic power converts into political and military leverage only through infrastructural position, and that position is built over decades, not bought with GDP.

**Visibility, not restraint, ends an underground empire.** Snowden's leaks, Jack Lew's 2016 warning that overuse could drive transactions "outside of the United States entirely," Trump's undisciplined escalation against allies and Huawei alike, Macron's warning about becoming "somebody's vassal," Margrethe Vestager's "we were just greedy" — together they trace an arc from concealment to exposure. The empire "was able to retain its empire so long because it was hidden in the shadows." Now exposed, "it will crumble, or worse." Every demonstration of the weapon supplies every other state a reason to build something less mappable.

**Improvisation, then feedback.** From outside, the empire looks like "a relentless machine of domination, the product of decades of careful engineering." From inside it is "a haphazard construction lashed together from ad hoc bureaucratic decisions and repurposed legal authorities" that "emerged half by accident, as officials tried to deal with one damn problem after another." NSA data-centre intrusions provoked Google and Microsoft into pervasive encryption that blinded the agency's own secret mirrors; sanctioning Deripaska's Rusal nearly shut an Irish aluminium refinery load-bearing for German car factories, a physical-supply-chain blind spot the U.S.'s financial maps could not see. The Libra/CBDC episode shows the same dynamic at the level of fear itself: Chinese officials pattern-matched Facebook's Libra to Sputnik, accelerated a digital yuan, and triggered American panic about a "Sputnik moment" — an escalation spiral driven entirely by mutual misreading, with neither side accurately modelling the other's constraints.

## The prescription

Farrell and Newman do not call for disarmament. They call for thinking about economic weapons the way Thomas Schelling taught Cold War strategists to think about nuclear ones: game-theoretic, systematic, aimed at crisis management rather than victory. "The United States needs a framework like Schelling's today... the players are both blindfolded and are playing on a board whose shape they do not know." Concretely: a Manhattan-Project-scale mapping of global economic networks; a dedicated U.S. economic-security institution on the model of Japan's 2021 ministry; and eventually direct dialogue with adversaries, not only allies — a shift from unilateral network imperialism toward a negotiated commonwealth.

The darker frame is Nicholas Mulder's *The Economic Weapon*. The relevant analogy may not be the Cold War but the interwar period, when "fear of sanctions helped spur Nazi Germany to embark on conquest so that it could secure the raw materials that the Third Reich feared it would be denied." The authors state the parallel bluntly — "Like the World War II Axis powers, China is nationalistic, militaristic, and ruthlessly nondemocratic. It has reason to be paranoid: the world economic order is being retooled to try to hold it down" — and refuse to resolve whether China acquiesces, retaliates, or turns to force over Taiwan.

The closing gesture is a possible repurposing rather than an escape: Sputnik produced a missile race but also satellites, and the same instruments built for economic warfare — tariffs, investment restriction, sanctions designations — could in principle be aimed at carbon-intensive economies and at corruption, producing a feedback loop "in which national power reinforced global legitimacy and global legitimacy reinforced national power." The book's final image is a tunnel: "There is no visible exit from the underground empire. Every tunnel that seems to lead out ends up turning back in on itself." Its last line is the only ascent it allows: "The roots of imperium go far too deep to ever fully be torn out. Yet even if we cannot escape the darkness underneath, we can strive to bend the growth of empire upward, toward the sun and air."

## Why this book matters for the World Machines Project

Three things recommend it. First, it is a fully documented case of [[infrastructure-inheritance]]: an apparatus built under one regime's canonical direction (commerce, efficiency, the escape from politics) carrying, without modification, an incompatible cargo (surveillance, exclusion, coercion). Second, it is an unusually well-sourced instance of [[history-machine]] emergence — a paradigm assembling out of a Treasury subpoena here, an NSA program there, a leaked ZTE document, a nine-point footnote, and an academic article, until it lurches into visibility all at once. Third, it supplies a rare empirical test of [[capture-resistance]]: the book examines several serious, well-funded attempts to build resistance to instrumentalisation directly into infrastructure, and watches nearly all of them fail. See [[decentralization-produces-chokepoints]] and [[weaponized-interdependence]].

It also inverts the [[globalisation-as-inevitability-rhetoric]] the same period produced. Globalisation was sold as a force of nature admitting no alternative; Farrell and Newman show it was a specific, jurisdiction-bound geography, and that the people insisting it had abolished politics were standing on top of the machinery that made politics newly decisive.
