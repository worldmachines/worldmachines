# Erewhon; or, Over the Range (Samuel Butler)

*Erewhon* is [[samuel-butler]]'s satirical traveller's tale, first published in 1872 and read here in the text of his revised 1901 edition. An unnamed young Englishman, working on a sheep station in an unnamed colony, crosses a glaciered range and finds Erewhon: a handsome, courteous, "quasi-European" people some centuries behind Europe in appliances, who punish illness as a crime, treat fraud and violence as maladies, keep a splendid but hollow religion of "Musical Banks", train their young in Colleges of Unreason, and centuries earlier destroyed almost all their machinery after a professor's treatise argued that machines were evolving toward supremacy. The narrator escapes by balloon with Arowhena, the daughter of his host, and the book ends on his prospectus for "converting" the Erewhonians by shipping them to Queensland as contract labour.

## Who is speaking

The book is built of nested voices, and every claim drawn from it has to say whose it is.

- **Butler in his own voice** appears only in the three prefaces ([[butler-erewhon-prefaces]]): a one-line 1872 first-edition preface on pronunciation, the second-edition preface of June 1872, and the revised-edition preface of August 1901. There he denies that the Machines chapters were meant to make Darwin's theory absurd, denies that the book absolves people of responsibility for their actions, blames its inconsistencies on the Erewhonians, and says plainly that "there is no central idea underlying Erewhon".
- **The narrator** is not Butler's mouthpiece but a satirical target: pious, mercenary, vain and self-satisfied. He wants to monopolise sheep country ([[butler-erewhon-ch04-the-saddle]]), baptises his guide Chowbok from a pannikin to offset his own sins ([[butler-erewhon-ch05-the-river-and-the-range]]), decides the Erewhonians are the lost ten tribes of Israel ([[butler-erewhon-ch06-into-erewhon]]), and ends with a joint-stock evangelisation-and-labour scheme ([[butler-erewhon-ch29-conclusion]]). He often admires what the reader is meant to doubt, as when he calls the consumptive's sentence "solemn, not to say majestic" ([[butler-erewhon-ch11-some-erewhonian-trials]]).
- **Erewhonian voices** that the narrator reports or "translates": the judge, the malcontents, Mrs. Nosnibor and Arowhena, the Professors of Unreason, the wiser unborn, the professor who wrote the Book of the Machines and his rebutter, the prophet of animal rights and the botany professor who undid him. None of these is Butler speaking *in propria persona*.

Identifying the satire's targets (the Victorian church, the universities, respectability, criminal justice, missionary and colonial enterprise) is a reading. The reading notes offer it as a likely one, but the text mostly leaves the narrator to condemn himself. The one firm external anchor is the January 1872 *Times* paragraph on the Polynesian labour trade that the narrator quotes in the last chapter.

## How the book was made

Butler's 1901 preface gives a genealogy ([[butler-erewhon-prefaces]]). The machine material came first: "Darwin among the Machines", signed Cellarius, in the Christchurch *Press* (13 June 1863), written in New Zealand's Upper Rangitata district, which also supplied the opening chapters' landscape. A second Machines article followed, then a rewrite in 1865 as "The Mechanical Creation", then the World of the Unborn, the Musical Banks and the trial of a man for being in a consumption. Only in autumn 1870, at a friend's suggestion, were the pieces strung together into a book. Chapman & Hall turned it down. Butler credited its success to two reviews and to "the sound of a new voice". He padded the 1901 text by about sixty pages for copyright reasons and called the book patchwork, not an organic whole. He also says that the second Machines article led to the theory of *Life and Habit*, which he believed "quite sound" and put into the mouth of an Erewhonian philosopher.

## Structure

- **Prefaces**: Butler's disclaimers and genealogy ([[butler-erewhon-prefaces]]).
- **I–VI, the approach**: the colonial sheep frontier and the narrator's motives ([[butler-erewhon-ch01-waste-lands]]); Chowbok's terrified pantomime in the wool-shed ([[butler-erewhon-ch02-in-the-wool-shed]]); the gorge, the glaciers and Chowbok's desertion ([[butler-erewhon-ch03-up-the-river]]); the saddle, solitude and the organ dream ([[butler-erewhon-ch04-the-saddle]]); the raft, the baptism recalled and the ring of hollow-headed statues that moan in the wind ([[butler-erewhon-ch05-the-river-and-the-range]]); first contact and the lost-tribes fantasy ([[butler-erewhon-ch06-into-erewhon]]).
- **VII–XIV, arrival and the inverted moral economy**: the medical examination, the confiscated watch and the museum of broken machines ([[butler-erewhon-ch07-first-impressions]]); prison, language lessons with Yram, and the discovery that illness is a crime ([[butler-erewhon-ch08-in-prison]]); the journey to the capital and the embezzler Senoj Nosnibor ([[butler-erewhon-ch09-to-the-metropolis]]); the straighteners ([[butler-erewhon-ch10-current-opinions]]); the trials ([[butler-erewhon-ch11-some-erewhonian-trials]]); the malcontents ([[butler-erewhon-ch12-malcontents]]); death, statues and the concealment of childbirth ([[butler-erewhon-ch13-views-concerning-death]]); Mahaina's "poor dipsomania" ([[butler-erewhon-ch14-mahaina]]).
- **XV–XXII, religion, family and schooling**: the Musical Banks ([[butler-erewhon-ch15-the-musical-banks]]); Arowhena, the King's distrust of machines and the personified gods ([[butler-erewhon-ch16-arowhena]]); Ydgrun, the real religion ([[butler-erewhon-ch17-ydgrun-and-the-ydgrunites]]); birth formulae ([[butler-erewhon-ch18-birth-formulae]]); the World of the Unborn ([[butler-erewhon-ch19-the-world-of-the-unborn]]); the narrator's commentary on parents, schools and money ([[butler-erewhon-ch20-what-they-mean-by-it]]); the Colleges of Unreason ([[butler-erewhon-ch21-colleges-of-unreason-1]], [[butler-erewhon-ch22-colleges-of-unreason-2]]).
- **XXIII–XXV, the [[book-of-the-machines]]**: the narrator's "translation" of the treatise that led to the abolition of machinery, and of its one serious rebuttal ([[butler-erewhon-ch23-the-book-of-the-machines]], [[butler-erewhon-ch24-the-machines-continued]], [[butler-erewhon-ch25-the-machines-concluded]]).
- **XXVI–XXVII, the rights of animals and of vegetables**: a prophet's ban on eating animals, its evasion, and a botany professor's reductio that wins its repeal through an oracle ([[butler-erewhon-ch26-rights-of-animals]], [[butler-erewhon-ch27-rights-of-vegetables]]).
- **XXVIII–XXIX, escape and conclusion**: prosecution "for measles" over the watch, the balloon escape, and the Queensland prospectus with Chowbok's reappearance as a celebrated native missionary ([[butler-erewhon-ch28-escape]], [[butler-erewhon-ch29-conclusion]]).

## Throughlines

**Inversion as a mirror.** Erewhon reverses the English moral map closely enough to make the English arrangement look arbitrary. Illness and misfortune are tried and punished, while embezzlement and forgery are treated by "straighteners". The book's whole machinery of luck-as-desert (courts, birth formulae, the myth of the unborn, gods who punish the deed and not the motive) is traced across chapters in [[misfortune-as-crime-butler]]. Butler's 1872 preface rejects the reading that the book denies moral responsibility.

**Official forms and real belief.** The Musical Banks are the recognised religion, yet nearly empty. Their coin is displayed for respectability and not spent, and plain criticism of them is met by calling the speaker unwell ([[butler-erewhon-ch15-the-musical-banks]]). The working faith is Ydgrun, respectability or custom ([[butler-erewhon-ch17-ydgrun-and-the-ydgrunites]]). Erewhonians "very often do not believe or mean things which they profess to regard as indisputable" ([[butler-erewhon-ch20-what-they-mean-by-it]]), and the Colleges of Unreason teach Inconsistency and Evasion as subjects ([[butler-erewhon-ch21-colleges-of-unreason-1]]). How this habit makes Erewhon's absolute prohibitions (on illness, meat and machines) livable but porous is the subject of [[prohibition-breeds-evasion-butler]].

**Reasoning by analogy and from first principles.** The narrator reasons confidently and wrongly, from the watch to Paley's design argument, from the broken machines to the exhaustion of coal and iron ([[butler-erewhon-ch07-first-impressions]], [[butler-erewhon-ch08-in-prison]]). The Erewhonians are "easily led by the nose" by a philosopher's logic ([[butler-erewhon-ch26-rights-of-animals]]). The Book of the Machines piles analogy on analogy, and the botany professor's reductio turns the method against itself ([[butler-erewhon-ch27-rights-of-vegetables]]). Butler's preface hints that the book which most deserves the charge of "the specious misuse of analogy" is left unnamed.

**Heredity, habit and memory.** The botany professor argues that germ and parent form one chain of identity that remembers what it did "in the persons of its ancestors", and that skill becomes unconscious once it is perfected ([[butler-erewhon-ch27-rights-of-vegetables]]). The narrator speculates that religion guards "the unfathomable and unconscious instinctive wisdom of millions of past generations" ([[butler-erewhon-ch15-the-musical-banks]]). This is the strand the preface links to *Life and Habit*, the one machine-adjacent idea Butler says he held to be sound. It resonates loosely with [[habit-and-identity]], though Butler's version is a claim about literal inherited memory.

**The narrator as the final target.** The book closes not on Erewhon but on England. The narrator's evangelisation company, with its gunboat, Queensland contracts and catechism "to put a stop to any uneasy feeling", turns the mirror round ([[butler-erewhon-ch29-conclusion]]).

## In the World Machines frame (hedged)

The Book of the Machines is the book's centre for this project. It offers an Erewhonian argument that machines are a new kingdom evolving faster than their makers, that humans already feed and breed them, and that "it is the machines which act upon man and make him man". Against it stands the rebutter's view of machines as man's "extra-corporeal limbs". The argument rhymes with [[world-machines]] (infrastructure that enrols people and cannot simply be exited), with [[extended-mind]] and [[tools-that-shape-thought]], and with design-without-a-designer as in [[dennett-darwins-dangerous-idea]]. Erewhon's abolition, and the museum of broken engines that survives it, form a rare fictional case of a society deliberately halting a technological line (compare [[useless-machine]] only distantly). The book also shows how such a halt decays into compromise and antiquarianism. See [[book-of-the-machines]] for the argument and its hedges.

What does not fit should be said as plainly. Butler is a satirist, not a machine theorist. He denies ridiculing Darwin but hints at a parody of analogy, says the book has no central idea, and the treatise is voiced by an Erewhonian whose victory the book does not endorse. The Erewhonian institutions resemble WMP's interest in forms that outlive their function (the ornamental stages of a [[decay-sequence]]), but Butler's targets are moral hypocrisy and respectability, not infrastructure or millennial cycles. Utopian-mirror lineage: compare [[more-utopia]].
