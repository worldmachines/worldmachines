# The Prophets of Paris (Manuel)

**Frank E. Manuel**, *The Prophets of Paris: Turgot, Condorcet, Saint-Simon, Fourier, and Comte* (1962).

A study of five Parisian thinkers who, across roughly a century — from Turgot's 1750 Sorbonne discourses to Comte's death in 1857 — invented the modern secular philosophy of history and then progressively hardened it. Manuel's organizing claim is that the five belong together not by discipline (philosophe, mathematician, speculator, traveling salesman, Polytechnician) but by *type*: they are prophets in the Hebrew sense — moral teachers "sparing neither kings nor commoners" and "revealers of a divine historical design" — operating without a God ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-preface-prolegomenon]]).

## Method: portraiture, not disembodied tradition

Manuel's preface defends a history of ideas that still feels "the need to introduce the bearer of the idea even when he disturbs the flow of abstractions" — biography and intellectual history braided together, with an avowed taste for psychological and sociological determinants. The method is load-bearing rather than decorative, and the book cashes it repeatedly: Condorcet's serene *Esquisse* is read as the work of a "snow-capped volcano" who was "always expecting catastrophe," composed in hiding under sentence of death ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-condorcet-01]]); Fourier's phalanstery is traced to compulsive measuring, a fourteen-sou apple, and unappeased personal need ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-fourier-01]]); Comte's law of the three states is grounded in his 1826 psychosis, in which he felt himself regress through metaphysics to fetishism and then recover forward again ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-comte-02]]). Manuel will call the group "schizophrenic, megalomaniac, and paranoid" and in the same breath mount "a frank attempt at rehabilitation."

## The type: what makes a secular prophet

The Prolegomenon defines the shared profile that the rest of the book fills in.

- **Crisis-consciousness.** "They were all crisis philosophers," each rehearsing the whole of human history to prove that "the moment of their speech was the instant of the great divide."
- **Future-intoxication.** They were "intoxicated with the future," treating the present as unendurable and to be destroyed as fast as possible — permanently adolescent men "panting to grow up," Moses figures born too soon who might glimpse but not enter the promised land.
- **A deliberate turn away from the state.** None of the five, in Manuel's reckoning, regarded man as "primarily a political animal." The state is "a scaffolding, a tool, a superstructure, a covering — not the heart of the human condition," to be circumvented rather than seized.
- **Discipleship and blood feud.** Condorcet as Turgot's chevalier; Comte's Judas-like break with Saint-Simon; Fourierists and Saint-Simonians warring over "the alienated souls of Europe and America."

## The five

**Turgot** ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-turgot-01]], [[manuel-prophets-of-paris-turgot-02]]) produced, in two Sorbonne orations delivered as a nominal eulogy of Christianity, "the first important version in modern times of the ideology of progress" — born, as Manuel relishes, inside a theological faculty, so that "in the very bosom of the old religion of sin, death, and salvation was born the new religion of earthly immortality." His system: a sensationalist drive to innovate at war with an institutional "spirit of routine"; genius as a natural constant "spread among mankind like gold in a mine"; language as the vessel and fossil record of thought (with an embryonic anticipation of Comte's three states); contemporary "savage" peoples read as frozen earlier stages of one universal path; and four unequally-developing progressions (technology, science, fine arts, morals). Regression has become structurally impossible once knowledge is globally diffused and encased in mathematical symbols. Underneath it all sits a theodicy: crime and ambition are the "leading strings" by which Providence advances the species without the actors' knowledge or consent.

**Condorcet** ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-condorcet-01]], [[manuel-prophets-of-paris-condorcet-02]]) strips out the Providence and keeps the machinery. History becomes "a science to foresee the progressions of the human species" — the capacity "to tame the future" — resting on Hume's demolition of necessary causality: if physical law is only probabilistic, history's "general and constant facts" can claim the same status. Genius is demoted from rare endowment to a statistical proportion of an educated population, which makes equality the *measure* of progress rather than an add-on. The late fragments sketch a self-governing international republic of science and a state that could in principle be run by "social mathematics... without debates." See [[nicolas-de-condorcet]].

**Saint-Simon** ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-saint-simon-01]], [[manuel-prophets-of-paris-saint-simon-02]]) converts the doctrine into an organicist and technocratic program: three unequal, physiologically grounded human types; aggression rerouted from domination over men to "the action of man on things"; government reduced "to nothing, or almost nothing," surviving only as administration on the model of a bank or canal company; and finally the *Nouveau Christianisme*, Christianity reduced to a single commandment of labor for "the poorest and most numerous class." His disciples ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-children-01]], [[manuel-prophets-of-paris-children-02]]) build this into a church with a priesthood of love, a doctrine of organic and critical epochs, a motto of expert-assessed capacity, and a utopia of finance capital under one directing bank — before the 1832 trial and a long dissolution into Second Empire respectability. See [[henri-de-saint-simon]].

**Fourier** ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-fourier-01]], [[manuel-prophets-of-paris-fourier-02]], [[manuel-prophets-of-paris-fourier-03]]) is the outlier and, for Manuel, "the greatest and the most neglected." His method of *ecart absolu* refuses reform outright: civilization is "a prison" and philosophy's whole project of amelioration is worthless, since millennia of moralizing produced no useful invention. The passions are fixed and God-given; repression never destroys them but deforms them into "counterpassions" "as malignant as the natural passion would have been benign." The phalanstery is therefore built not to discipline desire but to be combinatorially capacious enough to gratify every type of it — including the sadist, routed into a butcher's trade rather than a throne. Manuel's psychobiographical closing reads Fourier's fantasies of vengeance on a France that mocked him as evidence that the utopia of total love grew out of fantasies of total destruction.

**Comte** ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-comte-01]], [[manuel-prophets-of-paris-comte-02]]) is, in Manuel's framing, the priest who succeeds the prophets: where his predecessors "stammered" and left their systems half-built, Comte freezes them into "hard and sharp lines" and "ironclad categories" — the law of the three states correlated with three modes of activity and three ages of humanity, a hierarchy of the sciences running up to sociology and morality, and then a positivist calendar, catechism, and church, with Comte signing himself "Grand Prêtre de l'Humanité." The Religion of Humanity's cult of love is generated directly out of his passion for the dying Clothilde de Vaux; the endpoint of the system dissolves labor and science alike into fêtes and a "gaie science."

## Throughlines

**From tentative essay to closed system.** The epilogue's opening opposition ([[manuel-prophets-of-paris-epilogue]]): Turgot and Condorcet distrusted the *esprit de systeme* — Turgot likened a closed system to "a bier in which a cadaver is contained" — while the nineteenth century built "mammoth structures" and turned strident. This curdling of essay into system is the book's clearest developmental arc.

**Progress loses its guarantor and acquires a church.** Turgot's progress is still underwritten by Providence. Condorcet removes the transcendental sanction and, at the end of the *Esquisse*, has to reinstall Progress itself as "the new god of the age," an Elysium of the mind offering asylum to a man awaiting arrest. Saint-Simon demands on his deathbed that "religion must bring itself into harmony with the progress of the sciences"; the Saint-Simonians build the church; Comte writes its liturgy. The epilogue names this manufacture of a substitute religion as the group's most ambitious and most linguistically strained creative act.

**Dawn, not twilight.** Manuel's sharpest contrast is with Hegel's owl of Minerva, which flies only at dusk: none of the Paris prophets were content to understand history after the fact — "action was always bound up with theory... They were of the dawn, not the twilight." Manuel traces Marx's activist thesis on Feuerbach to this French lineage rather than to Hegel, and finds the actual phrases of the *Critique of the Gotha Programme* prefigured in the prophets' doctrines of property and labor.

**Determinism that never quite closes.** Each grounded historical necessity in a different agency — Providence for Turgot, nature for Condorcet and Saint-Simon, the Great Being for Comte — yet all insisted the philosopher-historian was himself an actor. Manuel is careful to show the determinism was looser than caricature allows: Turgot's marginalia ("truer, but not always," "depending on the circumstances") and Condorcet's own notes anticipating "oscillations" and "passing eclipses."

**The missing tragic sense.** The book's final verdict: for all their personal acquaintance with catastrophe — disgrace, the guillotine, botched suicides, Condorcet's fugitive death clutching Horace — none of the five confronted "the blind, meaningless destructiveness of fate." A compensatory "blue heaven" of the future was always available, and that escape hatch is at once their optimism's secret and its limit.

## Why this source matters to the World Machines Project

The book is a close-range record of a [[modernity-machine]] telos being manufactured, argued over, and institutionalized. Several strands connect directly:

- **Progress as *pull* attractor with a theological pedigree.** Turgot's doctrine is explicitly a transposed eschatology, and Manuel's account of it as theodicy makes it a documented ancestor of [[fiat-progress]] and of [[panglossian-optimism]] — history's crimes redeemed as necessary steps.
- **Psychohistory's first draft.** Condorcet's "tame the future" — extrapolable historical regularities held as "truths with a high degree of probability" rather than certainties — is the eighteenth-century version of what [[psychohistory-and-world-machines]] attempts, and shares its refusal of determinist guarantees. His hope that steles could shelter the sciences "even from general revolution of the globe" is a striking early [[Prime Radiant]] impulse.
- **The legibility apparatus.** Condorcet's social mathematics, the Saint-Simonian sorting of every person into an expert-assigned "cubbyhole," and Comte's classification of all knowledge and every day of the year are among the purest available statements of [[legibility-as-machine-core]] — including the fork Fourier introduces, treated in [[legibility-without-convergence]].
- **An interregnum with an unusually articulate cast.** The post-Napoleonic "religious and moral vacuum" the prophets set out to fill is a [[gramsci-gap]] whose inhabitants left a full record of what it feels like to build a successor legitimacy from inside the gap.
- **A caution about the frame's own optimism.** Manuel's indictment of Condorcet's unexamined conviction that European scientific-utilitarian civilization was the sole legitimate form of knowledge is a period-specific instance of [[weird-bias]], and a reminder that a universal history is usually somebody's particular history.
