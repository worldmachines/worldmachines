# The Cultivation of Hatred — Peter Gay (1993)

*The Cultivation of Hatred* is Volume III of [[the-bourgeois-experience]], [[peter-gay]]'s five-volume psychoanalytic history of the nineteenth-century middle classes. Its subject is aggression. Gay starts from a broad nineteenth-century consensus (Büchner, the Goncourts, Spencer, Darwin, William James, Simmel) that humans are aggressive by nature. He then asks how a respectable, self-disciplined bourgeoisie managed a drive it both enjoyed and feared ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-front-introduction]]). The title is a pun that runs through the whole book. Aggression was *cultivated* in two senses: individuals indulged it for its pleasures, and societies fenced it in with rule-bound institutions ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-front-mensur-1-jerome-fraternities]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-apotheosis-conflict-spencer-darwin]]). The appendix states the thesis without ornament. The nineteenth century was an age of “aggressive destruction and construction both,” and its respectable culture worked harder than any before it to cultivate hatred, “and needed to” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-appendix-theories-of-aggression]]).

Gay frames the whole inquiry inside Freud's drive theory, in which civilization is “an interminable contest, only intermittently peaceful, between Eros and its great adversary, aggression.” His key coinage is [[alibis-for-aggression]]: the religious, political, and above all scientific rhetoric that let the bourgeoisie treat its own “attacks” on markets, nature, and empire as purely constructive work.

## Structure

- **Front matter.** The Introduction sets out aggression as a universal, adaptive, often reactive endowment (frustration-aggression). It then opens on the German student duel, the Mensur. Jerome K. Jerome's growing fascination as a spectator (“I wanted more”) serves as clinical evidence of the savage under the starched shirt. The duelling fraternities turn out to be bourgeois status machinery wearing a feudal pedigree that was “possibly forged,” and the duel itself was a compromise formation for adolescent men caught between defying and obeying their fathers ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-front-mensur-1-jerome-fraternities]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-front-mensur-2-politics-adolescence]]).
- **Part One: the three alibis.** The first alibi is competition. What came to be called “Social Darwinism” was older than Darwin (Hobbes, Smith, Malthus, Spencer), was disowned by Darwin himself, and spread through Nietzsche, Sumner, the naturalist novelists, the Carnegie–Rockefeller philanthropies, and finally the *Lochner* decision ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-apotheosis-conflict-spencer-darwin]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-apotheosis-conflict-nietzsche-sumner]]). The second is [[the-convenient-other]], which runs from Dreyfus and Freudian projection through craniometry to colonial atrocity ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-convenient-other-race-theory]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-convenient-other-colonial-violence]]). The third is manliness: democratized chivalry that proved too imprecise to work reliably as an alibi, set against the harsher cult of honor in which “society was the superego” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-manliness-ideal-ambiguity]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-manliness-hughes-honor-code]]). A chapter on Theodore Roosevelt shows all three alibis “made flesh” in one man, and also shows them harnessed ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p1-tr-extremist-of-the-center]]).
- **Part Two, “Pathologies.”** This part covers punishment as an alibi for revenge ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-civilized-rationales]]), prison reform's humane motives and harsh machinery ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-prisons-pathos-reform-disillusionment]]), and the M’Naghten rules and Lombroso's born criminal ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-mnaghten-insanity-plea]]). It follows public execution from carnival spectacle to Victor Hugo's crusade ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-spectacle-execution-victor-hugo]]) and traces the century-long fight over the death penalty ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-bourgeois-conscience-capital-punishment]]). It ends with flogging and the naming of sadism and masochism ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-pleasures-pain-flogging-sadism-masochism]]) and with rape, wife-beating, and suicide's move from sin to statistic ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-domestic-violence-rape-suicide]]).
- **Part Three, “Demagogues and Democrats.”** Here Gay defines politics as aggression redefined and sublimated: “A pogrom may serve politicians, but it is not politics” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p3-redefinitions]]). He traces the slow invention of legitimate opposition ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p3-long-birth-political-culture]]) and the franchise as a referendum on human nature ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p3-human-nature-suffrage-debate]]). Britain's Reform Acts appear as the procedural alternative ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p3-human-nature-britain-reform]]) to [[caesarism]] under Napoleon III and Bismarck ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p3-modern-caesars-napoleon]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p3-modern-caesars-bismarck]]).
- **Part Four, “The Powerful, Weaker Sex.”** Two alibis supported the male monopoly on aggression: separate-spheres naturalism, and the paradox of woman's secret domestic “rule” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p4-domesticity-alibis-power-paradox]]). The women's magazine spread that ideology at mass scale ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p4-domesticity-education-womens-magazines]]). The appeal to “Nature” kept the doctrine standing while it “crumbled around the edges first” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p4-time-for-tentativeness]]). Competence, redefined, became women's route to constructive aggression ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p4-competence-womanliness-redefined]]). Gay reads women writers' pseudonyms, guilt, and sublimated ambition through “identification with the aggressor” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p4-scribbling-women-market-anxiety]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p4-scribbling-women-motives-reputation]]).
- **Part Five, “The Bite of Wit.”** This part treats humor as licensed aggression. It covers theories of laughter from Hobbes to Freud's 1905 jokebook ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p5-varieties-of-laughter]]), the satirist as “physician to society” who can become its “pander” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p5-physicians-to-society]]), Wilhelm Busch's cruel, beloved comedy ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p5-laughing-cruel-sage]]), and the regime-set threshold for political caricature ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p5-victim-as-executioner]]).
- **Part Six, “Uncertain Mastery.”** This part turns to aggression's constructive face. It covers moral equivalents and safety valves ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p6-moral-equivalents-safety-valves]]), sport and the referee ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p6-sport-referee-olympics]]), and the Victorian “empire of fact” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p6-empire-of-fact-bacon-science]]). It follows specialization and professionalization ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p6-end-of-renaissance-man]]), pairs Brentano and Booth ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p6-brentano-booth-social-science]]), surveys the advice literature of self-control ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p6-age-of-advice-self-control-thrift]]), and ends on character and obsessional neurosis ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p6-character-obsessional-neurosis-freud]]).
- **Epilogue and Appendix.** August 4, 1914 marks the collapse of the century's “great compromises” under nationalism, “the alibi of alibis” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-epilogue-august-4-1914]]). A theoretical appendix surveys ethological, environmentalist, and Freudian theories of aggression and rehabilitates the “drive toward mastery” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-appendix-theories-of-aggression]]).

## Throughlines

### Alibis, not absence
The century did not abolish aggression. It dressed it in respectability: competition, the Other, manliness, honor, punishment, Nature, humor, Caesarism, nationalism. Gay treats these alibis as plural, contested, and leaky. They could provoke guilt in the people they licensed, and they could contradict themselves, as with Spencer's own late humanitarianism or Carnegie's monopolies built to escape competition. See [[alibis-for-aggression]].

### Channelling, not suppressing
The volume's institutions all route aggression rather than end it: the Mensur, legitimate opposition, the referee, the licensed joke, the penitentiary, and the forensic expert. Reform itself re-channels. By century's end the bourgeoisie was trading “the savage thrill of acting out their vengeful impulses” for “less intense but more refined pleasures” ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-mnaghten-insanity-plea]]). Each channel also shows a way of turning back into violence. That cross-cutting pattern is drawn out in [[channels-for-aggression-and-their-inversion]].

### The bourgeois conscience at work
Gay repeatedly stages a real humanitarian current against a persistent retributive or punitive one. Examples include Byron's “I would have saved them if I could,” Hugo's fifty-year campaign, the abolitionist lobbies, Bentham's “Men punish because they hate,” and James Fitzjames Stephen's frank demand that punishment “express that hatred.” He never lets the humane side read as simple progress: statute outruns practice, reform produces new cruelties, and the gallows is kept in reserve ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-bourgeois-conscience-capital-punishment]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-prisons-pathos-reform-disillusionment]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-spectacle-execution-victor-hugo]]).

### Aggression and Eros entangled
Across the book aggression carries an erotic charge. The scar is a “passport” to love, and hangings arouse. Chamberlain “seduces” a crowd, and the Caesarist bond is a “dual amorous bond.” Flogging leads to Krafft-Ebing's sadism and masochism, and Freud's 1905 joke categories (obscene and hostile) anticipate his later dualism of Eros and aggression ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-front-mensur-2-politics-adolescence]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-spectacle-execution-victor-hugo]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p3-human-nature-britain-reform]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-pleasures-pain-flogging-sadism-masochism]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p5-varieties-of-laughter]]).

### Mastery, aggression's constructive face
Part Six and the appendix argue that the same drive, redirected, built the century. It shows in tools, statistics, medicine, professions, and the self-controlled “character.” Freud's sardonic clinical lens undervalues this, Gay says, because the sublimation that produced neurotics also collaborated with Eros to build cities and lengthen life ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p6-character-obsessional-neurosis-freud]]). See [[mastery-drive]].

### Gender and the male monopoly on aggression
Part Four reads the separate-spheres ideology as an alibi that reserved overt aggression for men. Two apparently opposite clichés, woman as “omnipotent mother” and woman as “perpetual child,” did the same work. Resistance to feminist claims, including Reichstag laughter and Moebius's “physiological feeblemindedness,” was “really naked aggression.” Women's own aggression found sublimated routes through competence, authorship, and self-sacrifice ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p4-domesticity-alibis-power-paradox]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p4-competence-womanliness-redefined]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p4-scribbling-women-motives-reputation]]).

### Contingency and the liberal temper
The epilogue names the disposition able to check aggression: tolerance for “the many shades of gray,” and a “gift for testing and accepting reality.” It stresses how fragile that disposition is. Gay refuses to read 1914 as fated, crediting blunders and “sheer stupidity” as much as structural forces. He also notes that Freud's own circle cheered the war before recoiling ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-epilogue-august-4-1914]]).

## World Machines resonances (with the notes' hedges)

- **Legibility recast as aggression.** Many notes read the century's measuring, classifying, and codifying projects as channelled aggression. Examples are the census and Quetelet, Booth's poverty maps, craniometry, Lombroso, civil-service examinations, codified flogging rules, and sport's rulebooks. Together they suggest a psychological account of [[legibility-as-machine-core]]. The notes consistently mark the fit as partial: Gay's frame is drive and motive, not administrative control. See [[legibility-as-cultivated-aggression]].
- **Gramsci-gap readings.** Several notes use [[gramsci-gap]] loosely: for 1914, where the old accommodations die and morbid symptoms fill the gap ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-epilogue-august-4-1914]]); for Caesarism, a term Gramsci himself coined for the charismatic arbiter of an interregnum ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p3-modern-caesars-napoleon]]); for France's repeated constitutional relapses ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p3-long-birth-political-culture]]); and for the suffrage compromise machinery ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p3-human-nature-suffrage-debate]]). The notes insist that Gay's account is psychological and moral and works at a timescale of decades or weeks. It is not the millennium-scale transition between machines, and Gay explicitly rejects the inevitabilism a clean Dusk narrative might invite.
- **Decay sequence.** The Mensur keeps “the compelling force of an obsessive habit” after losing “the persuasiveness of a living tradition.” The death penalty persists in statute while its practice hollows out. Both loosely rhyme with the [[decay-sequence]], though they play out within one social order rather than across a machine's arc ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-front-mensur-1-jerome-fraternities]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-bourgeois-conscience-capital-punishment]]).
- **History machine, and its foils.** Criminological reform accreting through journals, congresses, and societies is a good match for [[history-machine]] ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p2-mnaghten-insanity-plea]]). The Bismarck chapter and Bismarck's social-insurance decision are explicit counter-cases of great-man causation ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p3-modern-caesars-bismarck]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p6-brentano-booth-social-science]]).
- **Psychohistory.** Gay's Bismarck is psychohistory in the clinical, individual sense, where one family drama explains a career. It only half-rhymes with [[psychohistory-and-world-machines]], which forecasts civilizational patterns from many variables ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p3-modern-caesars-bismarck]]).
- **Conatus.** Aggression as healthy self-enlargement, “the love of power,” and the mastery drive all half-rhyme with [[Conatus]]. The notes flag each time that Gay's drive is relational, other-directed, and compatible with cruelty, and that the Victorian regime *redirected* striving rather than averaging it away ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-front-introduction]], [[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p6-age-of-advice-self-control-thrift]]).
- **Fear of the masses.** The suffrage debate's “swinish multitude” anticipates the anxiety behind [[mass-man]] and [[carey-intellectuals-and-masses]]. The resonance is loose: Gay's subjects feared the masses' political judgment, not their cultural claims ([[gay-bourgeois-experience-v3-p3-human-nature-suffrage-debate]]).
