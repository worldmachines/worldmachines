# Green Imperialism — Richard H. Grove (1995)

**Source**: Richard H. Grove, *Green Imperialism: Colonial Expansion, Tropical Island Edens and the Origins of Environmentalism, 1600–1860* (Cambridge University Press, Studies in Environment and History, 1995)
**Author**: [[richard-grove]]
**Level**: L2 summary over 49 L1 reading notes (Introduction, Chapters 1–8, Conclusion).

## The book by its own account

Grove's Introduction stakes out a revisionist claim: a scientifically informed critique of “development” and a programme of state conservation were fully established by the early nineteenth century, and their origins lie not in Romantic-era metropolitan Europe or in the American pantheon of Marsh, Thoreau and Roosevelt, but at the colonial periphery. Above all they lie on small, previously uninhabited tropical islands (the Canaries, Madeira, the West Indies, St Helena, Mauritius), where deforestation, soil erosion and species loss became visible within decades ([[grove-green-imperialism-introduction]]). Three things fused there: island-scale ecological crisis, an old European fantasy of the tropics as Eden, and emerging climatic “desiccation” theory. The result was the first systematic colonial conservation programme, on Mauritius under Pierre Poivre, Philibert Commerson and Bernardin de Saint-Pierre (1768–1810). Company surgeons and botanical gardens then carried its logic to the Caribbean and, after 1820, to India, where Scottish-trained East India Company surgeons won a forest-conservancy system by the 1850s.

The method is stated at the start of Chapter 1. Grove declines to hunt for the earliest “conservationist” act, since such practices cannot be separated from the ordinary web of economic, religious and cultural arrangements for safeguarding resources. He traces instead how an integrated Western discourse about nature and resource use was constructed ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s1-early-conservation]]). The Conclusion restates the thesis at its most general: environmental policy arose from “highly structured tensions between colonial periphery and metropolitan centre,” and modern environmentalism was a response to colonial ecological destruction rather than a metropolitan invention ([[grove-green-imperialism-conclusion]]).

## Structure

**Chapter 1 — deep history and the discourse of islands.** Deforestation and social collapse from Paleolithic fire-clearing to Mesopotamia; Theophrastus's deforestation–rainfall theory and its 1483 republication; the displacement of Eden onto distant forested lands; a Sanskrit–Arab–Venetian chain of medico-botanical learning ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s1-early-conservation]]). Medieval German and Venetian forest law, the Atlantic islands, and Columbus's claim that clearing forests reduces rain ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s2-island-environments-1400-1660]]). The island as Eden in *The Tempest*, chapbooks and verse ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s3-discourse-of-islands]]); Mandelslo and Le Guat on St Helena, Mauritius and Rodriguez ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s4-st-helena-mauritius-insular]]); seventeenth-century secularisation, mechanistic science and Dutch landscape culture ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s5-aesthetic-transitions]]); and the centre–periphery asymmetry of tempo, with Barbados deforested within a generation ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s6-ecological-pressures]]).

**Chapter 2 — indigenous knowledge and tropical botany.** Garcia da Orta's *Coloquios* and Hendrik van Reede's *Hortus Malabaricus* as “profoundly indigenous texts,” the Ezhava physician Itti Achuden and low-caste botanical knowledge, and the Leiden garden model that French and British botany copied ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch02-s1-south-west-india]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch02-s2-low-caste-epistemologies]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch02-s3-botanical-networks]]).

**Chapter 3 — St Helena, the Cape and Dutch Mauritius.** St Helena as the East India Company's first ecological crisis: goats, arrack distilling, Governor Roberts's negotiated 1709 enclosure rule, the 1731 Forest Act, and the Company's belated 1794 conversion to desiccation theory ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch03-s1-st-helena-1]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch03-s1-st-helena-2]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch03-s1-st-helena-3]]). The interventionist Dutch Cape, its placaats and hedges, and van Reede's Malabar-trained reforms, against the ebony plunder and dodo extinction on Dutch Mauritius ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch03-s2-dutch-cape-mauritius]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch03-s4-het-paradijs]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch03-s5-mauritius-importance]]).

**Chapter 4 — the science.** Woodward's forest–rainfall correlation, Stephen Hales's Newtonian mechanism in *Vegetable Staticks* (1727), and its rapid uptake at the Jardin du Roi ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch04-s1-hales-newtonian]]); French climatic social theory and Poivre's 1763 Lyons speech as the hinge from theory to policy ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch04-s2-climate-theories-france]]).

**Chapter 5 — French Mauritius.** Early regulation driven by felt insular finitude ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s1-french-mauritius]]); the gardens and first anxieties ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s2-labourdonnais-anxieties]]); Poivre's cosmopolitan and physiocratic formation, his 1767–72 programme, and the 1769 Réglement économique built on the Commerson–Cossigny survey ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s4-poivre-origins]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s5-poivre-programmes]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s6-poivre-commerson]]); the Utopian and robinsonnade lineage from More through Defoe to Rousseau and Commerson ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s7-defoe-rousseau-commerson]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s8-crusoe-rousseau]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s9-commerson-utopian-science]]); Bernardin de Saint-Pierre's philosophy of island nature ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s10-bernardin]]); and Decaen's 1804 laws and the global afterlife of Poivre's model ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s11-revolution-global-context]]).

**Chapter 6 — the Caribbean.** Forest reserves in the Ceded Islands “to preserve the seasons,” Soame Jenyns as the conduit from Hales to statute, the black Caribs' defeat of the St Vincent survey, and Alexander Anderson's King's Hill Forest Act of 1791 and private crisis of conscience ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s1-caribbean-forests]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s2-soame-jenyns]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s3-carib-resistance]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s4-anderson-advocacy]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s5-anderson-critique]]).

**Chapter 7 — globalisation, 1768–1838.** Banks and the professional colonial naturalist ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s1-banks-professionalisation]]); the Forsters' island comparisons ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s2-forsters-resolution]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s3-forster-st-helena]]); famine and the Calcutta garden ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s4-famine-calcutta-garden]]); Burchell and Beatson on St Helena ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s5-burchell-beatson]]); and Humboldt's universalised desiccation theory, carried into empire by Boussingault ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s6-humboldtian-diffusion]]).

**Chapter 8 — India.** Pre-colonial forest management and timber-driven expansion ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s1-company-forests-1]]); the first Malabar conservancy and its 1823 abolition ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s1-company-forests-2]]); Roxburgh and Wallich ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s2-roxburgh-1]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s2-roxburgh-2]]); Gibson's Bombay campaign and the Court of Directors' Despatch No. 21 of 1847 ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s3-lobbying]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s4-bombay-department]]); Balfour's Madras campaign ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s5-balfour]]); Dalhousie and the Sind amirs' shikargahs ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s6-dalhousie]]); and the wider perspective to the 1865 Indian Forest Act, with Marsh decentred ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s7-wider-perspective]]).

## Throughlines

- **Periphery before metropole.** Pressure on the European environment built up gradually, while at the periphery it was felt “far more rapidly and catastrophically” ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s6-ecological-pressures]]). Grove ends by arguing that Marsh's *Man and Nature* (1864) reinforced an Indian and Cape desiccationist consensus that already existed, and that the US Congress's adviser F. B. Hough held up Indian methods as a model ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s7-wider-perspective]]).
- **The island as Eden and as laboratory.** See [[tropical-island-eden-grove]].
- **Desiccation theory as the scientific engine.** See [[desiccation-theory-grove]].
- **Gardens and surgeons as the carriers.** See [[colonial-botanical-garden-grove]].
- **Indigenous knowledge as a structuring input, not a footnote.** See [[indigenous-knowledge-in-colonial-environmentalism-grove]].
- **A colonial state “peculiarly open” to scientists.** Grove rebuts “monolithic theories of ecological imperialism.” The colonial state was “heterogeneous and ambivalent,” and at times overrode short-term capitalist interest for long-term security ([[grove-green-imperialism-introduction]]). Scientists moved it by making “death, disease, famine and economic ruin” credible, and some of them were “actively anti-colonial” ([[grove-green-imperialism-conclusion]]). See [[diagnosis-waits-for-crisis-grove]].
- **Environmentalism twinned with social critique, and with control.** Poivre, Bernardin de Saint-Pierre, Anderson, Kyd and Balfour were also critics of slavery, the treatment of the Caribs, or famine-inducing revenue policy ([[grove-green-imperialism-conclusion]]). The same apparatus later became “a highly convenient form of social control” ([[grove-green-imperialism-introduction]]). See [[conservation-as-control-grove]].
- **Newtonian and Rousseauist as allies.** Grove resists the story that mechanistic science simply destroyed an ecological holism: the same measuring habit that objectified nature measured its degradation ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s5-aesthetic-transitions]]). Early environmentalism fused Hales's Newtonian science with Rousseauist and physiocratic language ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s11-revolution-global-context]]).
- **Anxiety about nature as anxiety about the settler self.** Fear of ecological collapse mirrored colonists' fears for their own survival and the loss of biblical chronological certainty ([[grove-green-imperialism-introduction]]). Anderson's crisis over Soufrière's geology ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s5-anderson-critique]]) and Burchell's displaced deep-time anxiety ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s5-burchell-beatson]]) are its cases.

One reading here stands against another in the canon. [[wulf-invention-of-nature]] credits [[alexander-von-humboldt]] with the first theory of human-caused climate change. Grove places Humboldt as the systematiser of an older, island-derived desiccationist tradition that reached him through the Forsters, and he stresses Humboldt's debt to Hindu monism via Herder ([[grove-green-imperialism-introduction]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s6-humboldtian-diffusion]]). Both readings stand.

## Where it meets the World Machines frame

Grove is an environmental historian, not a machine theorist, and the notes mark every resonance as a reader's. His own vocabulary is risk, evidence and “centres of calculation,” not legibility.

- **Legibility, but from the periphery.** Notes across the book flag [[Legibility]] and [[legibility-as-machine-core]]: Colbert's survey-based 1669 ordinance ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s6-ecological-pressures]]), Poivre's Land Court and felling caps ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch05-s5-poivre-programmes]]), the Ceded Islands survey ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch06-s2-soame-jenyns]]), Despatch No. 21's circulars ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s4-bombay-department]]). The Introduction note records Grove's inversion of the usual direction: here the colonial periphery is *more* capable of environmental intervention than the metropole ([[grove-green-imperialism-introduction]]).
- **Precondition, not cause.** The single most frequent reading in the notes is [[legibility-as-precondition-not-cause]]: surveys, maps and laws that existed and still did not produce control. The fifth series on that page draws the book's cases together.
- **The garden network as infrastructure** of the [[modernity-machine]], hedged in every note because Grove's gardens are also paradisal, medical and prestige institutions ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch02-s3-botanical-networks]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch07-s4-famine-calcutta-garden]]).
- **[[history-machine]].** The 1855 Dalhousie memorandum and the 1865 Act crystallise out of the separate careers of six surgeon-naturalists, which rhymes with a paradigm assembling from independent parts. The notes call the rhyme partial, since Grove's agents are a small named network ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s6-dalhousie]], [[grove-green-imperialism-ch08-s7-wider-perspective]]).
- **Bayly.** The multi-centred revision of Wallerstein rhymes with [[c-a-bayly]] and [[archaic-globalization-bayly]], though Grove's frame is narrowly ecological and institutional ([[grove-green-imperialism-ch01-s6-ecological-pressures]]).

**What does not fit.** Grove has no lifecycle argument and no Dawn/Day/Dusk. His causal picture is institutional and diffusionist, running through named people, gardens, societies and despatches.
