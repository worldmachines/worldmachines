# Diderot and the Encyclopædists, Volume II (Morley)

[[john-morley]]'s *Diderot and the Encyclopædists* first appeared in 1878; this is Volume II of the 1886 New Edition. It holds Chapters I–IX and an Appendix containing Morley's own English translation of Diderot's dialogue Rameau's Nephew. Volume I is summarised at [[morley-diderot-v1]], and the two-volume work is described at [[diderot-and-the-encyclopaedists]]. Volume II follows [[denis-diderot]] through his other writings (moral dialogues, fiction, art criticism), his journey to Catherine II, three books of the Encyclopædic school (Helvétius, Holbach, Raynal), his last years and death, and a Conclusion on his scientific and late philosophical works. Morley's verdicts run through every chapter. They are those of a Victorian liberal with positivist sympathies, and the notes keep them apart from the views of the authors he expounds and of the contemporaries he quotes.

## Structure

- **Chapter I, other dialogues.** The Conversation of a Father with his Children (published 1773) sets the supremacy of law against the sage's private judgment. On the Inconsistency of the Public Judgment on our Private Actions tells the Desroches story ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch01-other-dialogues-1]]). The Supplement to Bougainville's Travels (written 1772, published 1796) gives Orou's naturalist attack on European monogamy, which Morley rebuts at length ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch01-other-dialogues-2]]).
- **Chapter II, Romance.** Diderot's Eloge of Richardson, read by Morley as a sign of the passage from feudal to middle-class private life; The Nun, born of the Croismare hoax ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch02-romance-1]]); Sterne in Paris and Jacques le Fataliste, which Morley finds heavy and pointless while granting why Goethe and Schiller relished it ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch02-romance-2]]).
- **Chapter III, Art.** The nine Salons written for Grimm between 1759 and 1781, and French painting as the mirror of a sociable, idle society ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch03-art-1]]); Greuze, Boucher and the critic's digressions ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch03-art-2]]); the Essay on Painting against Goethe's commentary, the Encyclopædia essay on Beauty, the anticipation of Lessing, and music ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch03-art-3]]).
- **Chapter IV, St Petersburg and the Hague.** Diderot, at sixty, goes to Catherine II in 1773; Morley argues that she courted the philosophers for vogue, fame and cover ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-1]]). Five months at her court, her verdict that his principles “would make fine books, but very bad business”, and months at the Hague with Hemsterhuys and Princess Galitzin ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-2]]).
- **Chapter V, Helvétius.** The burning of L'Esprit in 1758 and Turgot's counsel on how to attack despotism ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1]]); the principle of public utility that Bentham took up, and the two paradoxes that buried it ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-2]]).
- **Chapter VI, Holbach's System of Nature (1770).** Pseudonymous publication and the alarm it spread among the philosophers' own public ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-1]]); naturalism, necessity, free will and punishment ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-2]]); the attack on theism and the antithesis of Nature and Religion ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-3]]).
- **Chapter VII, Raynal's History of the Indies (1772).** A forgotten bestseller of collective authorship, its merits and its invented facts ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-1]]); slavery, Toussaint Louverture, and Raynal's 1791 recoil from the Revolution ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-2]]).
- **Chapter VIII, closing years.** Diderot's meditations on death, the Essay on the Reigns of Claudius and Nero and the quarrel with Rousseau ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch08-closing-years-1]]); the single meeting with Voltaire, Naigeon, Romilly's 1781 visit, the clergy's appeals to repression, the dying-off of the circle, the refusal to retract and Diderot's death on 30 July 1784 ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch08-closing-years-2]]).
- **Chapter IX, Conclusion.** The Thoughts on the Interpretation of Nature (1754), read through Comte ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-1]]); D'Alembert's Dream (1769), the Plan of a University for Russia, the Conversation with the Maréchale and the Falconet correspondence on posterity ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-2]]).
- **Appendix.** Morley's abridged translation of Rameau's Nephew, in five notes ([[morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-1]], [[morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-2]], [[morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-3]], [[morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-4]], [[morley-diderot-v2-appendix-rameaus-nephew-5]]). The words are Diderot's, in Morley's English; see [[rameaus-nephew]].

## Throughlines

- **Morley the moralist.** Morley traces the moral corruption of old-regime France partly to Jesuit casuistry ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch01-other-dialogues-1]]). He holds that progress in the relations of the sexes means more self-control, not less ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch01-other-dialogues-2]]). He reads The Nun “under protest” ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch02-romance-1]]), ranks Diderot's humour “in the second class” ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch02-romance-2]]), credits Helvétius with only a feeble grasp ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-2]]), calls Holbach a preacher more than a seeker ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-2]]), finds Raynal “of weaker mould” ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-2]]), and condemns the form of D'Alembert's Dream while granting its sincerity ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-2]]). See [[john-morley]].
- **Sentiment and private life.** For Morley, passion for Richardson signalled the social transition from feudal to middle-class private life, with Diderot the “half-conscious oracle” of domestic ideals ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch02-romance-1]]). The thread runs through Sterne's reception in Paris ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch02-romance-2]]), Greuze's village picture in the year of the New Heloïsa ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch03-art-2]]) and English sentimentalism in Dutch letters ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-2]]). Morley also notes the irony that this great exalter of domestic sentiment wrote Orou's attack on constancy ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch01-other-dialogues-2]]).
- **Philosophers and power.** Catherine, Frederick, Seneca under Nero and Raynal's appeal to kings recur across the volume; see [[philosophers-and-enlightened-despots-morley]] and [[catherine-the-great]].
- **Substitutes for theology.** In Morley's division of labour, Voltaire pierced the Church's dogma, Rousseau shamed its irreligious temper and Diderot exposed its philosophy's absoluteness. Helvétius and Holbach then came “not merely with criticism, but with substitutes”: a theory of character and morals, and a dogma of matter and motion ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1]]). See [[claude-adrien-helvetius]], [[baron-dholbach]] and [[democracy-of-utility-morley]].
- **Necessity and punishment.** Holbach, Helvétius and Diderot's own D'Alembert's Dream all turn praise, blame and punishment into means of acting on motives. See [[necessity-and-punishment-morley]].
- **Opinions have a history.** Morley's recurring complaint is a missing historic sense. Holbach disregards what old convictions were rooted in ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-1]]) and ignores the moral inheritance of generations ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-3]]). Raynal gives no dates, authorities or criticism ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-1]]) and refuses Montesquieu's historic method on slavery ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-2]]). And French political thought treated social truths like Euclid's propositions ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-1]]). Against this Morley credits a Helvétius passage, that even cruel customs rest on real or seeming public utility, as approaching “the modern view” ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-2]]).
- **The approach of the Revolution.** In Holbach's indictment of kings Morley hears Robespierre, Desmoulins and Danton already speaking ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-2]]). Raynal's humanitarian sentiment, he says, shaped the youth of those who carried it into revolution, and acted on Toussaint ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-2]]). Romilly's 1781 report has D'Alembert noting the people's indifference to the birth of “another master”, and Morley reads Figaro's laugh as “pregnant with menace” ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch08-closing-years-2]]). The notes flag all of this as Morley's retrospective framing, not demonstrated causation.
- **Manuscripts and late publication.** Much of what the volume treats circulated privately or appeared late. The Nun (written about 1760) and the Supplement were published in 1796 ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch02-romance-1]], [[morley-diderot-v2-ch01-other-dialogues-2]]), as was the Essay on Painting ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch03-art-3]]). Jacques le Fataliste reached France only in 1796, through Prince Henri of Prussia, after German versions ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch02-romance-2]]). D'Alembert's Dream was not published until 1830 ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-2]]). Morley generalises that the French insurgents suppressed authorial vanity because they constantly needed secrecy ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-1]]).

## Method and stance

The Conclusion makes Morley's Comtean frame explicit. He reads the Interpretation of Nature as containing the germ of Comte's “subjective synthesis”, notes that Comte placed it beside Descartes's Discourse on Method and Bacon's Novum Organum in his Positivist library, and argues that France's misfortune was that the supremacy of geometry coincided with the age of political discussion ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-1]]). He reads from his own day: by 1880, he thinks, a thousand people would agree with Holbach that non-theological motives can serve ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-2]]); he contrasts French denunciation of Society with the English appeal to the Individual, Diderot and Victor Hugo with Milton and Mill ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch01-other-dialogues-1]]). The notes flag his use of “lower races” as Victorian hierarchy left intact ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-2]]).

## World Machines resonances, with the notes' hedges

The notes draw several loose rhymes and qualify each. Raynal's many-handed History, Holbach's flood of tracts and Romilly's glimpse of shifting opinion are read against the [[history-machine]] idea of a paradigm assembling unseen, though Morley's point is about publication conditions, irony or a Victorian progress narrative, not historical mechanism ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch07-raynal-history-indies-1]], [[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-3]], [[morley-diderot-v2-ch08-closing-years-2]]). Russia as a laboratory of forced modernisation resonates with the [[modernity-machine]], though Morley's frame is linear civilisation ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-1]]). Turgot's counsel against declamation and Morley's reading of Holbach as prophecy sit near [[intellectual-vs-revolutionary]], as a contemporary's policy argument and a literary-historical judgment respectively ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch05-helvetius-1]], [[morley-diderot-v2-ch06-holbach-system-of-nature-2]]). The geometer's treatment of social complexity rhymes with [[Legibility]]-style simplification, though Morley's complaint is about method, not states and scale ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch09-conclusion-1]]). French painting's descent into boudoir decoration loosely echoes the [[decay-sequence]], a partial fit Morley does not draw ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch03-art-1]]). The bishops' appeals to censorship against a print network invite comparison with [[eisenstein-printing-revolution]], though Morley does not theorise media ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch08-closing-years-2]]). Hemsterhuys's reaction against the philosophy of sensation resonates with the [[counter-enlightenment]], seen by a positivist watching a rival tradition rise ([[morley-diderot-v2-ch04-st-petersburg-hague-2]]).
