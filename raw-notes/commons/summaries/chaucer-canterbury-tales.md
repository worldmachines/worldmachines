# The Canterbury Tales, and Other Poems

Geoffrey Chaucer's works (late 14th c.), read here in the Rev. D. Laing Purves's 19th-century popular edition — spelling modernized, the two long prose pieces and much epic bulk abridged, and the volume conceived as a companion to Spenser's *Faerie Queene* under the banner of "our two early poetic masterpieces" ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-editors-preface]]). The batch spans the whole Purves Chaucer: editorial front matter, the *Canterbury Tales* entire (General Prologue plus twenty-four tales), the five books of *Troilus and Criseyde*, the major dream-visions, a cluster of dream-visions now doubted to be Chaucer's, and the shorter lyrics and envoys.

## What the volume is

The edition is itself an artifact of legibility-work. Purves is explicit that his task is to strip "the spelling of the monkish transcribers" so a mass readership can reach Chaucer "in a time of little leisure," and the modern e-text adds a second such pass — footnotes relocated, Latin translated. Two layers of re-cutting a canonical text for wider, less specialist audiences, a small concrete instance of the [[legibility-as-machine-core]] logic ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-editors-preface]]). The prefatory life of [[geoffrey-chaucer]] then reconstructs the poet from customs rolls, Exchequer pensions, and Parliamentary records — a record-based biography quietly displacing an older romantic "great men" legend, itself a [[history-machine]] in miniature ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-life-of-chaucer]]).

## Structure

**The frame.** The General Prologue installs the work's engine: an April pilgrimage to Canterbury becomes a storytelling contest, judged by the Host for "best sentence and most solace," after a portrait-gallery survey of some thirty pilgrims ranked down the estates ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-general-prologue]]). The contest generates a running game in which tales answer and "quite" one another — the Miller quiting the Knight, the Reeve the Miller, the Summoner the Friar — and no teller is exempt from the mockery he deals.

**The tales.** They sort loosely by kind: the Boethian chivalric romance of the Knight; the fabliaux (Miller, Reeve, the unfinished Cook, Shipman, Merchant, Summoner); moralized exempla and legends (Man of Law's Constance, Clerk's Griselda, Physician's Virginia, Pardoner's rioters, Prioress's boy-martyr, Second Nun's Cecilia, the Monk's *de casibus* catalogue); the beast-fable and fable of the Nun's Priest and the Manciple; the two prose pieces (Melibee, the Parson's penitential treatise); the marvel-romances of the Squire and the Franklin; the Wife of Bath's prologue-and-tale; the paired anti-clerical satire of Friar and Summoner; the Canon's Yeoman's confession of alchemical fraud; and Chaucer's own self-parody, *Sir Thopas*.

**Troilus and Criseyde.** The five-book tragedy of love won by a go-between and lost to a prisoner-exchange, framed by the editor as Chaucer's most finished and elevated work and closing in a Boethian *contemptus mundi* turn that repudiates the courtly love it spent four books building ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-troilus-book-1]], [[chaucer-canterbury-tales-troilus-book-5]]).

**The dream-visions and the apocrypha.** The *House of Fame*, the *Parliament (Assembly) of Fowls*, and the *Legend of Good Women* prologue sit beside four dream-visions the edition's own notes flag as probably not Chaucer's — *Chaucer's Dream*, the *Court of Love*, the *Cuckoo and the Nightingale*, and the *Flower and the Leaf* — which travelled for centuries under his name. The shorter poems (the Marian *A.B.C.*, ballades, the political *Lak of Stedfastnesse*, the *Complaint to His Purse*, *Truth*) close the volume, several likewise doubted.

## Major throughlines

**Estates satire and the self-indicting portrait.** The Prologue's method is to let each pilgrim's own praised attributes convict him, idealized figures (Knight, Parson, Ploughman) bracketing a graduated sequence of venality. The clergy in particular trace a movement from functional vocation through performance to naked extraction — the Monk who holds his rule "not worth an oyster," the Friar who prices absolution, the Pardoner whose relics are visible frauds — close to the World Machines [[decay-sequence]] running inside a single institution ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-general-prologue]]).

**Apparatus of proof, counterfeited.** Across the fabliaux and satires Chaucer builds instruments of measurement, record, and verification — a counting-house, a walled garden and its single key, seals and papal bulls, a mock donor-ledger, an astrolabe, a confessor's exhaustive audit of the soul — and then shows each gamed, erased, or hollowed out. The cross-cutting argument is developed in [[chaucer-legibility-machines]].

**Marriage, mastery, and self-governance.** The Wife of Bath, Clerk, Merchant, and Franklin conduct a distributed debate over sovereignty in marriage that the tales explicitly route through one another; read with the Manciple's caged bird and the *Court of Love*'s imposed vows, it becomes a study of a self's striving to keep governing itself against apparatus built to contain it. Developed in [[chaucer-marriage-group]].

**Fortune, providence, and a great-men cosmos.** The Monk's wheel of Fortune, the Knight's Boethian Destiny and First Mover, and the Man of Law's providence-over-astrology all locate historical causation in personal luck, virtue, and divine will rather than in infrastructure — an instructive foil to [[world-machines]] historiography, which places the mechanism at civilizational scale rather than in a goddess turning a crank on individuals ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-monks-tale]], [[chaucer-canterbury-tales-knights-tale-part-2]], [[chaucer-canterbury-tales-man-of-laws-tale]]).

**Books, belatedness, and the accretion of a canon.** The poems are unusually self-aware about arriving after the *auctores*: the *Parliament* insists "out of olde bookes... cometh all this new science," the *Legend* prologue defends old books as the only key to the unwitnessed, *Troilus* invents an authority ("Lollius") to stand between itself and Boccaccio, and the Monk defers his Ugolino to "the greate poet of Itale, that Dante hight." That the *House of Fame* makes renown itself a matter of pure caprice — and that four apocryphal dream-visions drifted into Chaucer's canon by resonance rather than provenance — reads as a live case of the anxiety Harold Bloom names ([[anxiety-of-influence]], [[belatedness]]) and of a [[history-machine]] assembling a canon out of accident and utility.

**Fiction retracted.** The collection ends by breaking its own game: the Parson refuses to tell a "fable" and delivers a prose treatise on penitence, after which Chaucer's closing Retraction revokes "the Tales of Canterbury, all those that sounen unto sin" — the whole storytelling pilgrimage folded, at the last, into the pilgrimage of the soul ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-parsons-tale]]).

## Translation note

Purves's modernized verse is the vehicle throughout, with the two prose tales (Melibee, the Parson's sermon) heavily compressed. Chaucer's own compiler's ethic — fidelity to "sentence" over verbatim form, defended via the four Evangelists retelling the Passion differently ([[chaucer-canterbury-tales-tale-of-melibeus]]) — sits one layer beneath the editor's, so several texts here are compressions of compressions of translations.
