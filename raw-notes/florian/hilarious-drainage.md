# Canonic infrastructure decays in hilarious drainage

## The idea

Convergent canonicity — the kind of institutional or conceptual structure that used to just *work*, and got treated as permanent because of it — doesn't collapse. It drains. Not through revolution or paradigm shift, but through a long decay sequence that runs identically across completely unrelated domains: Functional → Performative → Explicitly Allegorical → Ornamental → Invisible → Absent.

The mechanism is simple once you see it: nobody decides to kill the thing. It keeps getting kept. First because it still half-works (Functional). Then because everyone quietly agrees to act as though it works, even once it mostly doesn't (Performative). Then it gets openly acknowledged as symbolic rather than load-bearing (Explicitly Allegorical) — the stage where a culture can joke about it without threatening it. Then it becomes decoration nobody registers as decoration anymore (Ornamental). Then it disappears from notice entirely while still technically present (Invisible). Then it's simply gone (Absent), with no single moment anyone could point to as the death.

The strongest evidence is the physical moat, because you can walk the stages: Kenilworth Castle's moat still does defensive work (Functional). Plessis-Bourré's moat is defense-shaped but exists mostly to project the *idea* of defensibility (Performative). Chambord's moat is explicitly ornamental-symbolic, referencing kingship rather than repelling anyone (Explicitly Allegorical). Dampierre's moat is a garden feature (Ornamental). The ha-ha — the sunken ditch engineered specifically to vanish from the sightline of the house — is a moat built to be unseen (Invisible). The Tower of London's moat was drained and grassed over; there's no ditch left to notice (Absent).

The same six beats recur outside architecture. Leibniz's monads were working metaphysics (Functional), got ridiculed by Voltaire's Pangloss into a punchline (Explicitly Allegorical, skipping straight past Performative), then got quietly repurposed by Deleuze as a live philosophical tool again — which is itself evidence the sequence isn't strictly one-way, structures can be pulled back from ornamental use into working use. AI "competitive moats" show the same drift on a much faster clock: real compute and data barriers (Functional) → the leaked "We Have No Moat" memo puncturing the confidence in them (Performative, the point where everyone still uses the word while half-believing it) → "moat" surviving today mostly as a pitch-deck word, a lifestyle brand for a strategic claim that no longer holds (Ornamental).

The claim isn't just "things decay." It's that decay takes this specific six-stage shape, and that the shape reproduces across a medieval fortification, a seventeenth-century metaphysics, and a 2020s tech-strategy meme without anyone coordinating it.

## Where it comes from

Written for *Shitty Castles* (Notefields, March 2026), where it describes the mechanism behind what the essay calls the Dusk Machine Conversion — the general process by which a Modernity Machine institution loses function without anyone formally retiring it.

## How settled this is

Established, in the sense of well-evidenced: the six stages themselves, and that all three cross-domain cases actually move through recognizable versions of them. My own extension, not yet tested outside these three examples: treating the sequence as a *general* diagnostic — something you could run on an arbitrary institution today and get a real read on its stage. The Leibniz case also complicates the "irreversible drain" framing, since it shows a structure pulled back toward function from allegory; that's noted rather than resolved.

## Open

Untested against the one transition the sequence is implicitly modeled on: medieval canonical structures draining into modern ones. If that transition doesn't show the same six beats, the claim is weaker than the cross-domain evidence here suggests. There's also an unexplored connection to how liveness returns after a structure goes fully Absent — the divergence-age question the essay gestures at but doesn't answer.
