# Decay and descent are two routes to the same invisibility

## The idea

Two different processes end in the same place, and telling them apart is worth more than either one on its own.

The first is drainage. A canonical structure loses function while being kept: it still half-works, then everyone agrees to act as though it works, then it is openly symbolic, then decorative, then present but unnoticed, then gone — with no moment anyone can point to as the death. The endpoint is invisibility through failure.

The second is descent. Temporal coordination, to take the case I know best, starts as something people visibly do together — one shift greets the next and hands over the work. It hardens into an object that materialises the boundary, a punch clock. It becomes a structure, a roster coordinating roles and dependencies. Then it becomes infrastructure, a plant operations system the factory continuously runs on. At each step it works better and shows less. The endpoint is invisibility through success.

Opposite causes, identical result: something once arguable in public is no longer perceptible as a thing anyone chose.

The claim worth testing is that these are often not two processes but one, observed at two layers. When a practice drains into performance, the function it carried has frequently gone somewhere — usually one layer down. The shift-change greeting becomes a pleasantry because the boundary it enforced is now enforced by the operations system. The moat goes ornamental because defence has relocated into standing armies and treaty systems. The ha-ha is the cleanest case, because it is both at once: a fortification drained to a garden feature *and* a boundary that descended into the property and legal system — exactly why the ditch could afford to disappear.

If that holds, the six-stage sequence is not only a decay curve. It is a *relocation signature*, and the Ornamental stage becomes the most informative rather than the most pathetic: the moment the surface has fully stopped working and the forwarding address is freshest.

Which gives the existing diagnostic something it lacks. Counting the ornament tells you the function has left, not where it went — and a framework whose function relocated is a different object from one whose function simply ceased. The first has an heir you can go find and argue with. The second has nobody.

## How settled this is

Both parent claims carry their own confidence tiers elsewhere; nothing here re-argues them. What this note adds is the crossing, and the crossing is a hypothesis with three worked cases, all chosen after the pattern was noticed — the standard way to fool yourself.

The falsifier is at least clean. If the crossing is real, most structures at the Ornamental stage should have an identifiable lower layer that inherited the function; if plenty go ornamental with nothing picking up what they dropped, decay and descent are just two independent roads to invisibility, and the interesting claim collapses into an observation about endpoints. I have not run that check systematically — it is a survey, not an argument, and it is the actual next piece of work.

One asymmetry I cannot explain and should not paper over: drainage is reversible in at least one documented case, Leibniz's monads pulled back from allegory into working use. I know of no case of a function climbing back up the stack once descended. If descent is one-way where drainage isn't, that needs a mechanism, not a shrug.

## Open

Does anything ever climb back up? A function returning from infrastructure to visible practice would be the sharpest test of whether the stack is a direction or only a gradient — standards reform, where a technical parameter briefly becomes public argument before sinking again, is the likeliest place to look.

Prior to that: whether "layer" means the same thing in both parent claims. Drainage moves through stages of *function*; descent moves through levels of *embedding*. I have treated those as one axis in order to cross them, and they may not be.
