# A temporal regime is not inhabited until it has a practice

## The idea

A temporal regime — a standing arrangement for how a society organises, distributes and coordinates time — does not arrive with instructions. People have to develop a repertoire for living inside it. By practice I mean something specific and fairly narrow: a socially learnable, repeatable way of dealing with a temporal order. Not an app, not a private habit, not a theory, not a policy. You can be taught it, you can be visibly bad at it, other people can recognise you doing it, and it outlives whoever taught you.

Three regimes have filled that slot. Calendar Time organises recurrence, and its practice is ritualization: making a cycle inhabitable through repeated collective form, aligning bodies and attention and memory with a return rather than merely marking a date. Clock Time organises synchronization, and its practice is punctuality — which is not the ability to read a dial but an acquired social capacity for arriving, beginning, handing over and sequencing action against a shared standard. Capitalist Time organises optimization, and its practice is personal time management: allocating finite attention and effort among competing demands.

What makes this a claim rather than a tidy taxonomy is what the practice actually does. It converts a regime from something done *to* people into something people can participate in, contest and shape. Without one, the temporal order still runs — it just runs on you. Punctuality is the whole difference between being summoned by a bell and coordinating with others through a common reference. Personal time management is the difference between having your day allocated and allocating it. In both cases the regime was there first and the repertoire arrived afterwards, and the arrival is what changed the relation from subjection to participation.

That suggests a test. A regime whose practice slot is empty should produce a characteristic kind of complaint: people experiencing its effects as ambient pressure or personal inadequacy rather than as something with a name, a technique, and other people who are better at it than they are. Where there is no practice, there is also no shared vocabulary for being bad at it — and so the failure gets read as a private defect.

## Where it comes from

Developed for "Time Moves Down the Stack," a talk given at the Protocol Institute Symposium, 2026, out of the Contraptions Bookclub and the Special Interest Group for Psychohistory. The four-regime schema is my own synthesis of the temporal-infrastructure research field, particularly Axel Volmar and Kyle Stine's edited collection on media infrastructures and the politics of digital time — it is not a table reproduced from them. The material under punctuality rests on David S. Landes on the social distribution of clock time, which the bookclub read directly.

## How settled this is

The three filled slots are descriptive and I would call them established: nobody disputes that ritualization, punctuality and personal time management exist as learned, transmissible repertoires. What is mine, and unsettled, is the claim that the *pairing* is structural rather than coincidental — that each regime needs a practice and gets one, rather than three separate historical developments that happen to line up neatly when you draw them in a row.

The mapping is a heuristic, not an anthropology. Regimes contain more than one practice, practices cross regime boundaries, and none of them stopped when the next arrived — punctuality is not obsolete, and neither is ritual. The schema earns its keep only if it makes an absence legible that would otherwise look like nothing at all.

## Open

Can a regime run indefinitely with no practice at all, or does something always occupy the slot badly? I suspect the second, but a genuinely empty slot and a slot filled by something useless would look identical from outside, and I have no way to tell them apart yet.

Separately, and more damaging: [[ritual-versus-protocol|Ritual versus protocol]] raises the possibility that a heavily protocolised culture looks practice-poor without actually being empty — that the repertoire got automated rather than lost. If that is right, reading an absence off the surface is exactly the move this note licenses and should not.
